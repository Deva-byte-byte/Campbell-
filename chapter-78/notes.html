Chapter 78 — Surgical, Radiographic, and Endoscopic Anatomy of the Retroperitoneum

DEFINITION, BOUNDARIES AND ORGANS

  • Why it matters: safe entry into the retroperitoneum underpins open, laparoscopic and robotic surgery and percutaneous access for endoscopy.
  • Etymology: retro (“behind”) + peritoneum (the lining of the abdominal cavity), so it is the space behind the peritoneum.
  • Boundaries: anterior is the posterior reflection of the peritoneum; posterior is the abdominal wall; cranial is the diaphragm; caudal is the extraperitoneal pelvic structures.
  • Boundaries are debated: there is no consensus on whether psoas and quadratus lumborum are boundaries or contents.
  • Retroperitoneum ≠ extraperitoneal space. The extraperitoneal space is the retroperitoneum plus the space circumferentially surrounding the abdominal cavity.

POSTERIOR ABDOMINAL WALL

Flank Muscles

Superficial → deep:

  • External oblique (beneath subcutaneous fascia) arises from ribs 5–12. Fibres run inferomedially to the iliac crest and linea alba. The inferior border of its aponeurosis forms the inguinal ligament.
  • → Internal oblique arises from the lumbodorsal fascia and iliac crest. Fibres run superomedially to the lower ribs and linea alba.
  • → Transversus abdominis, named for its transverse fibres.
  • → Transversalis fascia crosses the midline anteriorly and fuses with the lumbodorsal fascia posteriorly.
  • Each muscle layer is invested in its own fascia. Together the layers flex, extend and rotate the trunk and compress the abdominal contents.
  • Examinable contrast: external oblique fibres run inferomedially (“hands in pockets”), internal oblique fibres run superomedially.

Psoas, Iliacus, Quadratus Lumborum and Erector Spinae

  • Posterior compartment: psoas, iliacus and quadratus lumborum are sometimes grouped as the posterior compartment of the retroperitoneum.
  • Psoas major: arises from T12–L5, passes along the pelvic brim behind the inguinal ligament to the lesser trochanter. It flexes the hip. Nerve supply is the anterior rami of L1–L3.
  • Psoas minor: may be absent. Arises from T12–L1 and inserts at the pelvic brim and iliopubic eminence.
  • Iliacus: arises from the iliac fossa and lateral sacrum and inserts at the lesser trochanter. It flexes the hip together with psoas major.
  • Quadratus lumborum: lies posterolateral to psoas. It runs from L5 and the iliac crest to the 12th rib and the L1–L4 transverse processes. It stabilises the 12th rib and bends the trunk laterally.
  • Erector spinae (sacrospinalis): a large group of back muscles that extend the spine.

  • What the table is for: it separates the flank layers, which compress the abdomen, from the posterior compartment, whose muscles move the hip and spine.
  • Pattern: psoas major and iliacus share the lesser trochanter and act together as hip flexors. Transversus is the only flank layer that does not flex the trunk.
  • Trap: psoas minor is inconstant and inserts at the pelvic brim, not the femur.

Spine

  • Vertebral count: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, then sacrum and coccyx.
  • Vertebral structure: each vertebra has a weight-bearing body and a posterior and lateral arch forming the vertebral foramen. The spinous process projects posteroinferiorly and the transverse processes posterolaterally.
  • Lumbar vertebrae matter most here: they are the largest and have long, thin transverse processes.
  • Vertebral level ≠ spinal cord segmental level: the sacral cord segments begin at vertebral T12–L1 in adults. In spinal cord injury, always specify which level is meant.

10th, 11th and 12th Ribs

  • Protective role: the lower ribs shield the retroperitoneum. A lower rib fracture → high suspicion of retroperitoneal injury.
  • Shape: lower ribs are shorter and less angulated than the upper ribs.

Concept — The posterior wall is a layered barrier that each approach crosses differently. Flank incisions cut muscle, while posterior approaches exploit the 12th rib and its ligament. Percutaneous access is limited above by the pleura and the intercostal nerve, and that is why subcostal access is safer.

LUMBODORSAL FASCIA

  • Structure: the lumbodorsal (thoracolumbar) fascia has three layers investing the posterior wall muscles, and they merge into one laterally.
  • Common access point: the tip of the 12th rib, where all three layers have fused. There the single layer merges anterolaterally with the transversus abdominis aponeurosis.

KEY POINTS: ORGANS AND BOUNDARIES OF THE RETROPERITONEUM

  • Contents: kidneys, ureters, adrenals, pancreas, parts of the duodenum, ascending and descending colon, mesentery, vessels, lymphatics and nerves.
  • Boundaries: anterior posterior peritoneal reflection, posterior abdominal wall, cranial diaphragm, caudal extraperitoneal pelvic structures.
  • Intercostal bundle lies between the internal and innermost intercostals in the costal groove of the rib above.
  • Lumbodorsal fascia is three-layered and merges anterolaterally with transversus abdominis.

Concept — Three fascial layers fuse into one at the 12th rib tip. That single fused point is both the standard flank access site and the reason a dorsal lumbotomy can reach the kidney between muscles rather than through them.

Transversalis Fascia and Posterior Pararenal Space

  • Transversalis fascia (outer stratum) lies deep to transversus abdominis and superficial to the preperitoneal fat and peritoneum. Behind the kidney it stays anterior to the fascia of quadratus lumborum and psoas (Fig. 78.18).
  • Medial fusion with the posterior lamina of Gerota’s fascia is clinically important. This fusion must be incised to reach the renal hilum in retroperitoneal dissection.

Gerota’s Fascia (Renal Fascia) and Perirenal Space

  • Origin and name: the laminae come from the intermediate stratum, which embeds the genitourinary organs. Together they form the renal fascia, named after Dimitrie Gerota (1867–1939), a Romanian anatomist.

  • Perirenal space contents: adrenal, kidney, ureter, perirenal fat, renal vascular pedicle, gonadal vessels. Gerota’s fascia extends inferiorly to envelop the ureter and gonadal vessels (Figs. 78.19 and 78.20, from Tobin, 1944).

  • Use of the fat colour: the difference in colour guides the plane during colon mobilisation.
  • Medial extent of the perirenal space: the right and left spaces were once thought separate. In vivo cases and cadaveric injection studies suggest communication across the midline below the renal hilum.
  • Caudal extent: once thought closed inferiorly by fusion of Gerota’s fascia. It is in fact cone-shaped and open inferiorly into the extraperitoneal pelvis.
  • Clinical consequence: these boundaries contain perinephric collections but let them track caudally:
    • Urine: traumatic or iatrogenic extravasation, or calyceal rupture in obstruction.
    • Blood: traumatic or iatrogenic perinephric haematoma, or ruptured aneurysm.
    • Pus: perinephric abscess or infected urinoma.

Anterior Pararenal Space and Inner Stratum

  • Boundaries: the anterior lamina of the renal fascia behind and the posterior parietal peritoneum in front.
  • Surgical use: the space is developed medially from the white line of Toldt to reach the kidney anteriorly.
  • White line of Toldt: the lateral border of the fusion of the colonic mesentery with the posterior peritoneum. It forms when the inner stratum fuses with the primary dorsal peritoneum during gut rotation and fixation.
  • Contents: the secondarily retroperitoneal organs, namely ascending and descending colon, pancreas, and the second and third parts of the duodenum. Each was intraperitoneal until the inner stratum fused posteriorly.

KEY POINTS: RETROPERITONEAL FASCIAE AND SPACES

  • Posterior pararenal space: posterior lamina of Gerota’s fascia in front; transversalis fascia behind and laterally.
  • Perirenal space: bounded by the anterior and posterior laminae of Gerota’s fascia.
  • Perinephric collections spread caudally through the inferior opening of the perirenal space.
  • White line of Toldt: the lateral border of fusion of the colonic mesentery with the posterior peritoneum.

Concept — Each space is the territory of one embryological stratum: outer for the body wall, intermediate for the genitourinary organs, inner for the gut. The open inferior apex of the perirenal cone explains why perinephric urine, blood and pus track into the pelvis.

  • Duodenal length and parts: the duodenum is 20–25 cm in four parts. Only D2 and D3 are retroperitoneal.
  • Transperitoneal access to the kidney: the ipsilateral colon is reflected medially at the white line of Toldt, the transition from colonic visceral to posterior parietal peritoneum.

The Mesentery as an Organ

  • Current view: the mesentery is now classed as a distinct organ with intestinal, vascular, immunological and endocrine functions.
  • History: Leonardo da Vinci drew it as continuous. A fragmented model was adopted in the late 1800s, with no mesentery for the ascending or descending colon. An observational cohort of total abdominal colectomies re-established continuity from the ileocaecal to the rectosigmoid level.
  • Functions:
    • Scaffold suspending the bowel from the posterior wall.
    • First barrier between gut and body, and a sampling reservoir for systemic immunity. Mesenteric nodes regulate cell-mediated responses with the mucosa.
    • Carries postganglionic nerves from the main abdominal ganglia. Its role in the enteric nervous system is poorly understood.
  • Urological relevance:
    • Internal hernia through a surgically created mesenteric defect is potentially lethal. Close small mesenteric defects primarily after bowel isolation, because small defects carry the higher herniation risk.
    • Vascular mesenteropathy can cause loss of the bowel segment used for diversion, ureteric interposition or augmentation.

VASCULATURE

Arterial System

  • Arterial wall, three layers:
    • Intima: endothelium plus subendothelial connective tissue.
    • Media: smooth muscle and elastic tissue that control calibre, bounded by the internal and external elastic laminae.
    • Adventitia: carries the vasomotor nerves and the vasa vasorum (“vessels of the vessels”).
  • Great vessel compartment: runs from T12 to L4/5, between the perirenal spaces.
  • Aorta: enters through the aortic hiatus at T12 and runs centrally, left of the IVC.

  • What the table is for: it gives the order of origin and the anterior/lateral/posterior pattern.
  • Pattern: unpaired anterior branches supply the gut (foregut → midgut → hindgut). Paired lateral branches supply the retroperitoneal organs (adrenals, kidneys, gonads) and share their lymphatic drainage. Posterior branches supply the wall.
  • Trap: the gonadal arteries arise anterolaterally below the renals, not laterally. They occasionally arise from the renal artery, and then run with the gonadal vein.
  • Adrenal arterial supply has three sources:
    • Superior from the inferior phrenic artery. This supply is constant.
    • Middle and inferior are variable. The commonest pattern is the middle adrenal from the aorta and the inferior adrenal from the renal artery.
  • Celiac trunk: short, unpaired and anterior. It supplies the abdominal oesophagus, stomach, duodenum, spleen, liver and pancreas. The splenic vessels run along the cephalad edge of the pancreatic body and tail, so they are at risk when the inferior pancreatic edge is lifted off the anterior renal fascia in transperitoneal adrenal or renal surgery.
  • SMA: branches are the inferior pancreaticoduodenal, ileocolic, right colic and middle colic arteries. SMA injury in left-sided surgery → severe bowel ischaemia, despite the collaterals.
  • Marginal artery of Drummond: the middle colic (SMA) ↔ left colic (IMA) anastomosis. It is the SMA-to-IMA collateral that lets the IMA be sacrificed without colonic ischaemia.
  • Renal arteries: arise at L1–L2. At least a quarter of people have supernumerary renal arteries. There is no consensus on side predominance, and female renal arteries are smaller on CT angiography. Variants are covered in Chapter 84.
  • Testicular arteries: run anterior to psoas, the genitofemoral nerve and the ipsilateral ureter (and, on the right, the IVC) towards the internal inguinal ring.
  • Ovarian arteries: run anterior to the ureter and then medially through the infundibulopelvic (suspensory) ligament.
  • Lumbar arteries: a fifth pair sometimes arises from the median sacral artery.
  • IMA: branches are the left colic, sigmoid (→ two to three inferior left colic arteries) and superior haemorrhoidal (rectal) arteries. The superior rectal artery collateralises with the middle and inferior rectal arteries from the internal iliac territory, so the rectum survives IMA ligation.
  • Ureteric blood supply:
    • Proximal: renal artery.
    • Middle: aorta, with contributions from the common iliac, gonadal, uterine, middle rectal and vaginal arteries.
    • Distal: internal iliac artery, including the superior and inferior vesical arteries.
  • Direction of ureteric supply: the abdominal ureter is supplied medially and the pelvic ureter laterally.

  • IVC course: formed by the confluence of the common iliac veins below and to the right of the aortic bifurcation. It ascends anterior to the vertebral bodies, right of the aorta, running alongside it below the renal veins and becoming more anterior as it rises.
  • At the diaphragm the right crus separates the IVC from the aorta. The IVC passes through the central tendon at T8 into the right atrium.
  • Venous variability: veins vary more than arteries, although most parallel their arteries.
  • Median sacral vein: usually drains to the left common iliac vein, sometimes into the angle between the common iliacs. Avoid it when fixing mesh in sacrocolpopexy.
  • Ascending lumbar veins: run behind psoas, lateral to the spine. They link with variable lumbar veins, sometimes as a plexus in front of the vertebral bodies, and continue as the hemiazygos (left) and azygos (right) veins.

  • Sudden right varicocele → image the retroperitoneum to exclude obstructing malignancy, for example right renal cell carcinoma with venous thrombus.
  • Right-sided ureteric identification: develop the plane lateral to the gonadal vein, leaving the vein medial. A plane medial to it risks IVC injury, and lifting the kidney then risks gonadal vein avulsion.
  • Ovarian veins: drain the pampiniform plexus at the ovarian hilum via the infundibulopelvic ligament. The same left/right pattern as in males applies.

Portal and Hepatic Venous Drainage

  • Portal system: collects from bowel, spleen, pancreas and gallbladder and drains into the liver (Fig. 78.35). It does not mirror the arteries as closely as the systemic veins do.
  • Superior mesenteric vein (SMV): drains the small bowel and colon proximal to the splenic flexure. Tributaries are the right gastroepiploic, anterior and posterior inferior pancreaticoduodenal, jejunal, ileal, ileocolic, right colic and middle colic veins.
  • Portal vein formation: SMV + splenic vein → portal vein. Splenic tributaries are the inferior mesenteric, short gastric, left gastroepiploic and pancreatic veins.
  • Inferior mesenteric vein (IMV): drains the colon distal to the splenic flexure. It joins the SMV rather than the splenic vein in 40%. It lies in the mesocolon when the left colon is reflected.
  • Reoperative trap: the IMV can be mistaken for the gonadal vein when the mesentery is stuck to Gerota’s fascia.
  • Hepatic outflow: portal vein → right and left branches → hepatic sinusoids → hepatic veins (a larger upper and a smaller lower group) → anterior IVC below the diaphragm.
  • Budd–Chiari syndrome: hepatic vein occlusion causing progressive, often rapid, liver failure with jaundice, ascites, abdominal pain and hepatomegaly.

KEY POINTS: RETROPERITONEAL VASCULATURE

  • Adrenal gland arterial supply: inferior phrenic artery, aorta and renal artery.
  • Renal hila at L1; renal veins anterior to renal arteries.
  • Gonadal arteries arise anterolaterally from the aorta below the renal arteries.
  • Marginal artery of Drummond (SMA → IMA) allows IMA sacrifice without colonic ischaemia.
  • Proximal ureter supplied medially, distal ureter laterally.
  • Left gonadal vein → left renal vein at a right angle, hence more left varicoceles. Right gonadal vein → IVC.

Concept — The arterial tree follows embryological territory: unpaired anterior branches to the gut, paired lateral branches to the retroperitoneal organs. Venous anatomy is dominated by left–right asymmetry because the IVC lies on the right. Every long left vein must cross to reach it, and that single fact explains the left renal vein’s tributaries, left varicoceles, and the warning carried by a new right varicocele.

LYMPHATIC SYSTEM

  • Node structure: nodes have multiple afferent and a single efferent lymphatic.
  • Direction of flow: lymph flows cephalad and right → left, returning to the left innominate (brachiocephalic) vein.
  • Exception to the left-sided return: the head, neck, right thorax, right arm and right heart drain to the right innominate vein.
  • Pelvic and lower limb drainage: internal iliac, external iliac, common iliac, obturator and sacral nodes → lumbar nodes → lumbar trunks.
  • Why urologists care about lumbar nodes: they are the primary drainage of organs supplied by the lateral aortic branches, namely the kidneys, adrenals, ureters and gonads (Fig. 78.36).

KEY POINTS: RETROPERITONEAL LYMPHATIC SYSTEM

  • Testes are embryologically retroperitoneal, with retroperitoneal blood supply and primary lymphatic drainage.
  • Left testis → left para-aortic, with some drainage to the interaortocaval nodes.
  • Right testis → interaortocaval, with some drainage to the right paracaval nodes and a small amount to the left para-aortic nodes.
  • Testicular drainage follows the global right-to-left lymphatic flow.

Concept — The testis drains to the renal hilum because it began there. Lymph drifts right to left, so a right tumour can reach left-sided nodes but a left tumour does not reach right-sided ones.

Autonomic Nervous System

  • Two-neuron chain:
    • Preganglionic neuron, with its cell body in the central nervous system
    • → synapses in a ganglion
    • → postganglionic neuron, whose axon enters the target organ.
  • Adrenal exception: preganglionic fibres synapse directly on the adrenal medulla and trigger catecholamine release. The adrenal is effectively a specialised sympathetic ganglion.

  • Sympathetic trunks: run medial to psoas on the anterolateral spine. The lumbar arteries and veins cross them at right angles.
  • Celiac plexus is the first and largest, with paired ganglia lateral to the celiac artery. It carries much of the autonomic supply to the kidney, adrenal, renal pelvis and ureter, and some testicular fibres, which then run with the testicular artery.
  • Renal plexus: continuous with the celiac plexus and lies beside the renal arteries. It contains the aorticorenal ganglion, an inferior extension of the celiac ganglion.
  • Superior and inferior hypogastric plexuses (contiguous) carry much of the sympathetic supply to the pelvic viscera. The superior plexus runs from the caudal aorta to the front of L5.
  • Hypogastric plexus injury: extensive retroperitoneal dissection that disrupts these plexuses causes loss of seminal emission or failure of bladder neck closure → retrograde ejaculation.

Somatic Nervous System

  • Lumbosacral plexus: formed from the anterior rami of the lumbar and sacral nerves plus T12, in the retroperitoneum. It supplies the lower abdomen and lower limbs.
  • Relation to psoas: superior branches pierce psoas; inferior branches run medial to it (Fig. 78.40).
  • Subcostal (T12), iliohypogastric (L1) and ilioinguinal (L1): the subcostal runs below the 12th rib. All three cross the front of quadratus lumborum, pierce transversus and run deep to internal oblique. They supply the abdominal wall muscles and the skin of the posterolateral buttock, upper medial thigh and genitalia.
  • Genitofemoral (L1–L2): runs on the anterior surface of psoas, parallel to it, and divides near the inguinal ligament.
    • Femoral branch: passes under the inguinal ligament into the femoral sheath and supplies the upper anterior thigh.
    • Genital branch: enters the deep ring. It is motor to cremaster (the efferent limb of the cremasteric reflex) and sensory to the anterior scrotum, or to the mons and labium majus.
  • Lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh (L2–L3): sensory to the anterior and lateral thigh.
  • Obturator (L2–L4): arises behind psoas and runs to the obturator canal. It is motor to the thigh adductors.
  • Femoral (L2–L4): motor to the anterior thigh (knee extension) and to iliacus and pectineus (hip flexion). Sensory to the anteromedial lower limb.
  • Sciatic (L4–S3): most of the motor and sensory supply to the lower limb, including the posterior thigh and all leg and foot muscles.

KEY POINTS: NERVOUS STRUCTURES

  • Parasympathetic outflow is craniosacral; postganglionic fibres are often in the wall of the viscus.
  • Sympathetic preganglionic fibres leave T1–L2 and synapse in the sympathetic trunk or the autonomic plexuses.
  • The lumbosacral plexus gives somatic sensory and motor supply to the pelvis and lower limbs.

Concept — The nerves at risk are those lying on or in psoas and the pelvic side wall. Each nerve’s injury follows directly from its motor territory: adduction for obturator, knee extension for femoral, cremaster for genitofemoral, and emission and bladder neck closure for the hypogastric plexus.
