Chapter 18 — Fundamentals of Laparoscopic and Robotic Urologic Surgery

  • Osler: “Diseases that harm require treatments that harm less.” Over the past 30 years, minimally invasive urology has become predominant.
  • Multi-institutional comparisons with open surgery show equivalent efficacy and acceptable efficiency. The advantages are less pain, better cosmesis, faster recovery, shorter stay and, as techniques mature, often lower cost.
  • The objectives of even complex open surgery on the adrenal, kidney, ureter, bladder, prostate and lymph nodes can now be met with less injury. Open surgery's role is steadily shrinking.

PREOPERATIVE PREPARATION

Patient Selection and Contraindications

  • Start with a history focused on previous surgery and an examination mapping every abdominal scar. Laboratory tests, electrocardiogram and chest radiograph follow the same criteria as any major operation under general anaesthesia.
  • Severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) → arterial blood gases and pulmonary function tests, because the CO₂ pneumoperitoneum loads the lungs. Arrhythmias must be treated beforehand: hypercarbia and acidosis destabilise the myocardium.

Bowel Preparation

  • No mechanical bowel preparation for retroperitoneoscopy, extraperitoneoscopy, or transperitoneal surgery not using bowel. Propensity-matched data show no benefit for laparoscopic nephrectomy or prostatectomy, and emerging data show none even for cystectomy with conduit or neobladder.

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery Protocols

  • Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS), Europe, 2001: a multimodal, multidisciplinary programme.
  • Elements: no solids for 6 hours and carbohydrate drink 2 hours before surgery (not overnight fasting); balanced, not liberal, fluids; early removal of drains and tubes; early mobilisation; eating and drinking on the day of surgery. Later additions: counselling, thromboprophylaxis, ileus prevention, normothermia.
  • Results: stay shortened by about 30%, complications halved, fewer readmissions, lower cost. Benefit in cystectomy is seen with open or minimally invasive approaches. Surgery–anaesthesia collaboration is the key.

Preparation of Blood Products

  • Group and screen suffices for low-bleeding-risk procedures: radical nephrectomy and nephroureterectomy (transfusion 3–12%), prostatectomy (2.5% at experienced centres).
  • Crossmatched blood for extensive procedures (partial nephrectomy, cystectomy, caval thrombectomy), especially early in experience. With experience, group and screen may do for partial nephrectomy (transfusion 6–7%).

KEY POINTS: PREOPERATIVE PREPARATION

  • Patient selection starts with surgical history and a scar map.
  • Contraindications: uncorrectable coagulopathy, untreated intestinal obstruction, abdominal wall infection, massive haemoperitoneum or haemoretroperitoneum, generalised peritonitis, suspected malignant ascites.

Concept — Every relative contraindication is a problem of safe entry or tolerance of the pneumoperitoneum. The answers recur throughout the chapter: enter elsewhere (Palmer point, retroperitoneum), enter openly (Hasson), or insufflate less.

OPERATING ROOM

Setup of the Operating Room

  • Positions of equipment and staff are defined for each case. All equipment must be checked before starting (Box 18.1), with an open laparotomy tray ready.

BOX 18.1 INSTRUMENTATION CHECKLIST FOR MAKING A SKIN INCISION FOR OBTAINING THE PNEUMOPERITONEUM

  • Irrigation–aspiration working · electrosurgical unit working · CO₂ tank full, with a spare in the room · camera white-balanced, light source working · insufflator checked for flow and for its response to tubing kinking · Veress needle checked for flow and tip retraction.

Patient Positioning

  • Supine: arms tucked or on sleds. Trendelenburg or lateral: tape and belts across chest and thighs.
  • Lateral: pad all bony prominences and the strap contact points. Lower leg flexed about 45°, upper leg straight on pillows (no sciatic stretch). Pad the knee and ankle of the lower leg. Axillary roll three to four fingerbreadths below the axilla (brachial plexus). Use active warming for long cases.
  • Flank interface pressure is higher in men, with BMI ≥25, with the kidney rest (the most harmful; avoid beyond 20–30 minutes), and with full rather than half table flexion. → Men with BMI ≥25, kidney rest up and full flexion are at highest risk of rhabdomyolysis. The plain table mattress beat egg-crate or gel, and egg-crate was at least as good as the costlier gel.
  • Pelvic surgery: Trendelenburg on split-leg positioners or Allen stirrups. Never shoulder braces (brachial plexus injury).

Cord Management

  • Secure cords to the drape at two points without compressing them. Keep the camera cable separate from diathermy and suction. Place the boom or robotic cart at the foot of the table without cable tension. On the robot, wrap redundant camera cord once round the camera shaft. Leave a U-shaped loop in suction tubing when racked.

Prophylaxis and Other Preparations

  • Pneumatic compression stockings; optional 5,000 units subcutaneous heparin preoperatively; both in high-risk patients (e.g., morbid obesity), continued postoperatively.
  • Foley catheter: monitors urine output, decompresses the bladder (space, fewer pelvic injuries). Secure it with a stabiliser and put the bag at the head of the table.
  • Nasogastric or orogastric tube for upper-abdominal working space, especially on the left, where a full stomach hampers spleen mobilisation and access to the kidney.

PERFORMING THE PROCEDURE

Before the Initial Incision

  • Complete Box 18.1 just before insufflation. For the robot: console, vision cart and patient cart plugged into different circuits; cables connected; self-test and homing complete; 3D endoscope calibrated, black/white-balanced, target-aligned; patient cart draped.

Achieving Transperitoneal Access and Establishing the Pneumoperitoneum

Closed Techniques

  • Veress needle: 70- or 120-mm, 14-gauge, 2-mm outer diameter. Pre-checks: blunt tip springs back; saline passes; at 2 L/min the pressure stays ≤2 mm Hg; no leak at the hub when the tip is occluded.
  • Grasp it at midshaft and pass it perpendicularly. Two points of resistance: fascia, then peritoneum.

  • In a large series of blind Veress placement (including previous surgery and obesity), there were no major vascular or hollow-organ injuries, only minor liver lacerations managed conservatively.

Confirming placement: (1) aspiration–irrigation–aspiration: 10-mL syringe with 5 mL saline; aspirate (blood or bowel particles?) → inject freely → re-aspirate (nothing returns) → inject 2–3 mL more to clear omentum from the tip → (2) drop test: saline in the open hub falls freely → (3) advancement test: advance 1 cm without resistance (resistance = still preperitoneal) → only then insufflate, at up to 9 L/min to 15 mm Hg. Free flow is confirmed by an initial pressure <8–10 mm Hg, with flow stopping at 15 mm Hg.

  • Towel clips or Allis clamps can steady the fascia, but do not lift the fascia: it widens the fascia–peritoneum gap without enlarging the abdominal space.

Open-Access Techniques

  • Hasson: easier and, early in experience, safer. It needs a larger incision and leaks gas more. Recommended when extensive adhesions are expected; it is as efficient as closed access and possibly safer after previous surgery.

Hasson sequence: 2-cm semicircular infra-umbilical incision → open fascia and peritoneum separately, transversely, finger-sized → confirm entry by sight and touch → two 0 silk fascial stay sutures → insert the cannula with the blunt tip → seat the funnel adapter and wrap the sutures round its struts → remove the obturator → insufflate at maximum flow.

  • Hand-port access: safest for the inexperienced. Open a 6.5–7.5-cm incision, place the device, insufflate through it, and guide secondary trocars with the intra-abdominal hand (or place them under vision).

Retroperitoneal Access and the Retroperitoneal Space

Open (Hasson) retroperitoneal access (the commonest, most precise): 2–2.5-cm transverse incision in the midaxillary line just below the tip of the 12th rib → S-retractors → incise the posterior lumbodorsal fascia, split muscle → open the anterior thoracolumbar fascia snug to the index finger (limits leak) → finger confirms the psoas behind and the Gerota-covered lower pole in front → finger-develop a space → balloon inflated twice, cephalad then caudad, anterior to the psoas and posterior to Gerota's fascia → extra caudal dilatation for ureteric work; extra cephalic subdiaphragmatic dilatation for adrenalectomy.

Extraperitoneal Access and the Extraperitoneal Space

Pelvic extraperitoneal access: 1.5–2-cm infra-umbilical incision → anterior rectus sheath opened vertically 1.5 cm → recti separated → finger tunnels caudally between the rectus and posterior sheath to the pubis → puncture the transversalis fascia → digital prevesical space → balloon in the midline, then each side → prevesical fat displaced, peritoneum reflected cephalad.

  • Caveat: early retroperitoneoscopy with a standard Hasson cannula caused heavy subcutaneous emphysema and hypercarbia. This was solved by the blunt balloon port: an internal balloon plus an external foam cuff give an airtight seal.

Access Technology: Trocars, Hand Ports, and Single-Port Access

Trocars

  • A trocar is a cannula (port) plus obturator; 3–20 mm diameter, 5–20 cm long. One-way valves allow instrument exchange; multiseal valves take 5 mm and larger without leak. Remove the seal to extract tissue.
Bladed (cutting) trocarsBlunt (dilating, non-bladed) trocars

  • Robot: Xi camera fits any 8-mm port; Si camera needs 12 mm. Instruments use proprietary reusable 8-mm metal trocars with disposable valves.
  • Reducers are needed in flap-valve ports; multiseal and AirSeal valves take 5–12-mm instruments without one.
  • Retention (threaded sleeves, expandable arms, balloons) prevents dislodgement, leak and emphysema. The Blunt Tip balloon trocar sandwiches the wall between an internal balloon and an external foam ring, which is especially useful retroperitoneally.
  • AirSeal (CONMED): Intelligent Flow insufflator + valve-free port + tri-lumen filtered tubing. It holds a stable pneumoperitoneum despite suction or leak, so routine work is at 8–12 mm Hg. Smudge-free scope insertion, intact specimen extraction, continuous smoke evacuation.

Hand-Assist Devices

  • GelPort and Endopath Dextrus maintain pneumoperitoneum with the hand inside. The cost falls on the surgeon: more pain and numbness of hand, wrist and forearm, and more fatigue, than standard laparoscopy.

Placement of Initial Trocar

  • After Veress insufflation, hold the trocar in the dominant hand with the middle finger along the shaft and insert with a twisting downward motion, the other hand at the skin as a brake. Perpendicular for mid/upper abdomen; 70° caudad for the pelvis. The hiss of CO₂ from the open side arm confirms entry. With an optical trocar, monitor entry with a 0° lens.
  • Some raise the pressure to 20 mm Hg for primary trocar insertion, to protect underlying structures. Data suggest it may not increase volume or ease insertion.
  • With open access, the Hasson cannula is the first trocar.

Hand-Assist Placement

  • As a secondary port: insufflate first (skin on stretch → smaller incision). Trace the device “footprint” and plan the other ports around it. Incision length in cm = surgeon's glove size (glove 7 → 7 cm).
  • Waterproof the glove–gown seam with a drape or Ioban. Use a brown glove on the intra-abdominal hand (less glare).

Secondary Trocar Placement

  • Inspect the whole abdomen first for access injury.
  • Avoid “crossing swords” (tips meet) and “rollover”. Aim for triangulation: a four-point diamond around the target.
  • Suturing geometry: needle drivers <55° to the horizontal, 25–45° between instruments.
  • Point each port at the target, so constant redirection does not widen the tract and cause emphysema.
  • Standard approach: under vision with a 30° lens turned 180° to view the wall. Transilluminate with lights dimmed to avoid wall vessels. Make a skin incision just wide enough; twisting insertion; suture-anchor non-retaining ports with No. 2 non-absorbable sutures.
  • Never use a metal trocar with a plastic retaining ring: stray current cannot dissipate into the wall, so it injures viscera out of sight (capacitive coupling).
  • Hand-assist approach: the intra-abdominal finger guides each non-bladed trocar, which is fast and safe.

Trocar Configuration and Robotic Considerations

  • Detailed configurations are in Chapter 142 (upper tract) and Chapter 161 (pelvis). The rule: minimise intra- and extracorporeal clashing.
  • Robot: camera 8 mm (Xi) or 12 mm (Si); arm ports 8 mm; a 12-mm port with an 8-mm reducer for the robotic stapler. Ports 8–10 cm apart. Put the assistant's port caudal to the arms, not between them. Advance robotic ports until the thick black line is below the abdominal wall.

Laparoscopic Instrumentation

Instruments for Visualization

  • Four components: laparoscope, light source, camera, monitor.
  • Laparoscopes: 2.7–10 mm; 0° or 30° (range 0–45°). The 30° lens shows more and can look round structures. Deflectable tip (up to 100° in four directions; needs an adept assistant). Variable-view prism (0–120°, standard eyepiece).
  • Rod-lens with fibreoptic light versus chip-on-tip (CCD) digital scopes, which give a better image and need no separate light cord.
  • Fogging: pre-warm (warm saline or thermos), antifog solution or povidone-iodine, all-in-one defogger. Dry the eyepiece–camera junction (not an issue with digital scopes). White-balance.
  • 3D: two-lens binocular systems give depth perception, chiefly via the da Vinci InSite system (surgeon-controlled, 0° or 30°).

Instrumentation for Grasping and Blunt Dissection

  • Mostly 5 mm (range 3–12 mm). Single-action (one jaw moves) versus double-action (both jaws move).
  • Tips: blunt, pointed (dolphin), straight (duckbill), curved (Maryland), angled. Atraumatic (serrated or smooth, e.g., 3-cm bowel forceps) versus traumatic (toothed). Handles are locking or non-locking; some articulate.
  • The suction tip, the heel of the hook and Kittner (“peanut”) dissectors (5 and 10 mm) give rapid blunt dissection. Kittners do not conduct current, so they can safely lift a line of tissue for division.

Instrumentation for Incising and Hemostasis

  • Cutting mechanisms differ. Electrocautery and laser heat cells until internal gas pressure ruptures them. Ultrasonic devices use a blade vibrating at 25–55 kHz over up to 100 µm. Haemostasis is always by denatured protein: via current or light (heat), or via mechanical vibration (ultrasonic).
  • Scissors: shorter blades; hooked (sutures), micro (spatulating the ureter), curved (dissection); replaceable tips keep them sharp; some articulate to 90°.

Instrumentation for Suturing and Tissue Anastomosis

  • Needle drivers: one fixed jaw; ratchet, spring or Castroviejo lock; straight or pistol grip; articulating drivers improve angles. The Endo Stitch (10 mm) is used less as skills and robots spread.
  • Lapra-Ty clips: 3.5-mm absorbable polydioxanone clips that act as a knot, holding up to 14 days under low–mid tension. They hold best on 1-0 to 3-0 polyglactin; avoid with 4-0 and with tension on 3-0 monofilament (slippage). Uses: single or running sutures, renorrhaphy bolsters.
  • Barbed suture (V-Loc; Quill, which is bidirectional): knotless, does not slide back. Popularised for the vesicourethral anastomosis: meta-analysis shows shorter operative time with comparable complications.

Instrumentation for Stapling and Clipping

  • Endo GIA and ETS-Flex 45 need a 12-mm port and fire two triple-staggered rows, cutting between them (ETS: maximum eight loads). Loads are 30, 45 or 60 mm; articulating and roticulating versions exist.

Instrumentation for Specimen Entrapment and Morcellation

  • LapSac (1990; nylon with polyurethane lining): most tear- and leak-resistant, holds up to 2 kg, suited to morcellation, but difficult to deploy. Endocatch (10 and 15 mm): self-opening metal frame, drawstring. Spring-wire sacks make scooping easy for intact removal through a hand-port or a port enlarged to 5–7 cm.
  • Manual morcellation: finger, ring forceps or Kelly clamp. Ring forceps are preferred because they are least likely to puncture the sack.

Robotic Instrumentation

  • EndoWrist: 7 degrees of freedom, 90° articulation. Most 8-mm instruments have a 10-use limit (record the “lives” left).
  • Incising and haemostasis: round-tip and Potts scissors; monopolar HotShears, hook and spatula; bipolar Maryland, fenestrated, curved, micro, long and force; Harmonic ACE; Vessel Sealer Extend and SynchroSeal (E-100 generator).
  • Graspers: ProGrasp (most used in urology), Cadière, tenaculum, Cobra and others. An Allen bolt opens locked jaws manually after power or arm failure.
  • 5-mm line: more limited, and the 5-mm scope is 2D.
  • Needle drivers: standard or SutureCut (cuts in the crotch). Clip appliers (titanium, Hem-o-lok ML and L), suction–irrigator, probe stabiliser, retractors.
  • Robotic staplers: EndoWrist stapler and SureForm, which senses tissue compression and adjusts firing.
  • Robot failure is rare (<1%). Half is found before the patient enters, and intraoperative failures (about 0.4%) rarely force open conversion.

Exiting the Abdomen

Port Removal and Fascial Closure

  • A haphazard exit causes herniation, incarceration and haemorrhage.

Exit sequence: lower the pressure to 5 mm Hg → inspect the operative site and every port entry → perfect haemostasis → place fascial sutures under vision → remove each port under vision → let CO₂ escape passively through 5-mm sites. A hand-port is removed and closed first, then reinsufflate and close the other ports.

Instrumentation for Port Site Closure

  • Direct closure is hard with BMI >30, so use devices for en bloc fascia–muscle–peritoneum closure under vision.
  • Carter-Thomason: a cone (5, 10 or 12 mm) with two opposed angled channels; a needle-point grasper carries 0 polyglactin in through one channel and retrieves it through the other. It can also act as a “fifth port” (holding a sack, passing a vessel loop).
  • Endo Close: a spring-loaded suture carrier passed either side of the port.
  • Angiocatheter technique (cheap): a 14-G sheath passes 0 polyglactin on one side and a folded 30-inch 0 polypropylene loop on the other. The loop snares the polyglactin, which is pulled out and tied.

Closure of the Skin

  • Subcuticular 4-0 absorbable for 10-mm sites. Adhesive strips close incisions <10 mm and reinforce those ≥10 mm. Octyl cyanoacrylate glue is faster with equal cosmesis.

KEY POINTS: PERFORMING THE PROCEDURE

  • Complete the equipment checklist just before insufflation; on the robot, plug components into different circuits and check the cables.
  • Never insufflate until all signs of peritoneal entry are confirmed (negative aspiration, easy saline injection, negative saline re-aspiration, positive drop test, normal advancement test).
  • Open access when extensive adhesions are expected.
  • Non-cutting dilating trocars have superseded bladed ones: less vessel injury, less herniation.

Concept — The procedure is a sequence of verified transitions: every step, from checklist and needle tests to vision-guided trocars and inspection at 5 mm Hg, confirms the previous one before the next begins. Most catastrophic complications come from skipping a confirmation.

Choice of Pneumoperitoneum Pressure

  • 15 mm Hg is the commonest setting, but 12 mm Hg is more physiological (no change in stroke volume) and lowers postoperative pain. 10 mm Hg markedly reduces oliguria. The authors use 8 mm Hg for renal surgery with AirSeal.
  • 20 mm Hg raises filling volume by about a fifth and may reduce venous bleeding, but girth barely changes.

  • Never exceed 20 mm Hg for long; work at 10–12 mm Hg.
  • Arrhythmias: hypercapnia → tachycardia and ventricular ectopics; peritoneal stretch → vagal bradyarrhythmia. Arrhythmia is also an early warning of pneumothorax, hypoxia or gas embolism.
  • CVP is unreliable: intrathoracic pressure inflates readings, so never base decisions on it.

Respiratory Effects of the Pneumoperitoneum

  • Pressure-mediated: the diaphragm is splinted; dead space is unchanged, functional residual capacity falls, and peak airway pressure rises with intra-abdominal pressure. Use positive end-expiratory pressure in lung disease.
  • Head-down position: diaphragm rises, vital capacity falls. The endotracheal tube may slip into the right main bronchus. Pulmonary oedema occurs if left heart pressures are high. Restrict fluids in long head-down cases (limits facial swelling).

Renal Effects of the Pneumoperitoneum

  • Oliguria and anuria come from renal vein compression and direct parenchymal compression, not hormones or ureteric compression. Cortical flow falls with pressure; medullary flow rises up to 20 mm Hg, then falls.
  • Management: keep pressure ≤10 mm Hg. If needed, give furosemide, mannitol 12.5–25 g and dopamine 2 µg/kg/min to keep urine output >100 mL/hour, rather than fluid boluses, which cause overload and oedema.

Effects on Mesenteric Blood Flow and Intestinal Motility

  • Splanchnic flow falls (mesenteric vessels, liver, pancreas, stomach, spleen, bowel). Rarely, mesenteric thrombosis develops over days.
  • Laparoscopy causes less ileus than open surgery, perhaps via hypercarbia. Gut perfusion is unchanged at 15 mm Hg. Bacterial translocation rises with pressure in rats. There is no increase in reflux or regurgitation.

Acid–Base Metabolic Effects of Pneumoperitoneum

  • Respiratory acidosis comes from transperitoneal CO₂ absorption (dead space is unchanged). In normal patients, increase minute ventilation; in COPD it is dangerous.
  • COPD: arterial blood gases for any procedure with >1 hour of insufflation, continued in recovery.
  • CO₂ is absorbed transperitoneally through the peritoneum, and extraperitoneally through fat, connective tissue and disrupted lymphatics. Absorption rises over the first 30–60 minutes, then plateaus.
  • Which route absorbs more is debated. A sealed balloon-tip cannula removes any clinical difference.
  • Monitor end-tidal CO₂ and saturation; check gases in long cases and high-risk patients (airway disease, renal failure, heart failure, old age).
  • Rising end-tidal CO₂ → the anaesthetist raises rate and volume, and the surgeon lowers the pressure or desufflates.

Hormonal and Metabolic Effects and Immunologic Effects

  • Stress hormones rise as in any surgery (β-endorphin, cortisol, prolactin, catecholamines). Vasopressin rises with insufflation (significance unknown).
  • Compared with open surgery (cholecystectomy data): less hyperglycaemia, less insulin resistance, a smaller hepatic stress response and less nitrogen catabolism, which may underlie faster convalescence.
  • Less immunosuppression (C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, T-cell subsets, natural killer cells, skin tests), though no difference was found for laparoscopic versus open nephrectomy for cancer. A long-term oncological benefit is unproven.

KEY POINTS: PHYSIOLOGIC CONSIDERATIONS IN THE ADULT

  • CO₂ is standard: non-combustible and rapidly absorbed.
  • Helium suits lung disease, where hypercarbia is poorly tolerated.
  • Do not exceed 20 mm Hg for long; work at 10–12 mm Hg.

Concept — Pneumoperitoneum physiology has two drivers: pressure (caval, renal and diaphragmatic compression) and absorbed CO₂ (acidosis, sympathetic drive). Every recommendation in this section lowers one or the other, and every high-risk patient (COPD, hypovolaemia, renal impairment) is one who tolerates one of them badly.

COMPLICATIONS AND TROUBLESHOOTING IN LAPAROSCOPIC AND ROBOTIC SURGERY

  • Historically, about 4% complications and mortality 0.03–0.08%. Contemporary series show 13–22%, reflecting more complex procedures. Vascular, then adjacent-organ injuries are commonest.

Minimizing the Incidence of Complications During the Learning Curve

  • Start with low-risk patients of normal habitus.
  • Three-arena training (Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons): (1) courses (didactic, live cases, hands-on laboratory); (2) preceptorship, watching five or more cases; (3) mentoring of the first cases.
  • Add box trainers (proven benefit for cutting, clipping and suturing), 1-week mini-residencies (most graduates go on to complex laparoscopy), and virtual reality (VR), which beats no training or box training for novices.

General Procedural Complications

Malfunction of Equipment

  • Trained staff must recognise and fix faults. Keep the Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons (SAGES) troubleshooting guide and vendor contacts available, and train staff in-depth on integrated and robotic systems.
  • da Vinci malfunction about 0.4%: most found before the case (cancelled); few converted to laparoscopy or open surgery.

Complications Related to Placement of Secondary Trocars

  • Port-site bleeding: blood drips onto the viscera. Find it by cantilevering the trocar into each quadrant until it tamponades.
  • Fixes: diathermy via another port (costliest); a straight Keith needle with 0 absorbable suture passed in and out across the quadrant and tied over a gauze bolster (cheapest; the port stays usable); or a Carter-Thomason suture. Finally close the site with a closure device.
  • Prevention: transilluminate; spread subcutaneous tissue with a Kelly clamp; blunt trocars (epigastric injury about fivefold less; bleeding 3% vs 9%); place ports in the midline or ≥6 cm lateral to it.

Complications Related to Exiting the Abdomen

  • Bowel entrapment at a port: ileus and point tenderness on day 2–3. Treat by re-insufflating via another site, three ports, atraumatic clamps either side, opening the site and reducing by fingertip. Resection is rarely needed.
  • Entrapment is a technical error. Ports have a side hole near the tip that equalises pressure on withdrawal. Inspect each site on removal, and back the last port off over the scope so the scope leaves last.
  • Late-declared port bleeding, unmasked when the trocar's tamponade is removed → inspect every site at 5 mm Hg.

Early Postoperative Complications

  • Pain:
    • Port-site pain is normal early; if increasing → hernia. A port-site bulge → rectus sheath haematoma, port haematoma or hernia. Pain without swelling → broad fascial suture or a palpable knot.
    • Immediate severe diffuse pain → noxious spill (e.g., polycystic kidney fluid) or bowel injury.
    • Shoulder-tip pain, right posterior shoulder = diaphragmatic CO₂ irritation, occasionally mimicking pulmonary embolism.
    • Delayed diffuse pain with low-grade fever → occult bowel injury.
  • Incisional hernia: in adults, ports >10 mm; in children, even 5 mm. Localised pain, nausea, ileus; diagnose by CT (bowel above the fascia). Repair laparoscopically, with general surgery if strangulated.
  • Hernia prevention: blunt trocars; closure rules as tabulated above. Hand-assist midline sites herniate in 4–7%: close with interrupted non-absorbable sutures. Transverse beats vertical midline fascial incisions; in robotic prostatectomy, switching to horizontal camera and extraction incisions cut hernias from 5.4% to 0.4%.
  • DVT and pulmonary embolism (PE): no more common than after open surgery, and no randomised trials exist.
    • American Urological Association (AUA) Best Practice Statement: pneumatic compression for all, from before surgery to 48–72 hours after. High risk (morbid obesity, smoking, previous DVT): add heparin 5,000 units 2 hours before and every 12 hours after.
    • In upper retroperitoneal surgery, compression alone performed as well as heparin, and heparin may add bleeding (non-randomised data).
  • Wound infection: rare, except at hand-assist sites (about 9%). Prevent with antisepsis, port irrigation and careful closure.
  • Rhabdomyolysis (about 0.4% overall, about 1% retroperitoneal): men, long renal cases, kidney rest throughout. It presents in recovery with severe downside hip pain and brown urine, with creatine kinase >5,000 units. Padding is unproven. Prevent by limiting the kidney rest to <1 hour, avoiding hypotension, and keeping cases under 5 hours.

Late Postoperative Complications

  • After 3 weeks, complications are rare: mainly lymphatic and incisional hernia.

KEY POINTS: COMPLICATIONS AND TROUBLESHOOTING IN LAPAROSCOPIC AND ROBOTIC SURGERY

  • Start with low-risk, normal-habitus patients.
  • Gas embolism: abrupt end-tidal CO₂ rise, saturation fall, then end-tidal CO₂ collapse.
  • Four thermal mechanisms: direct activation, direct coupling, capacitive coupling, insulation failure.
  • Plan ports to avoid crossing swords, striking handles, rollover and arm collisions.

Concept — Complications follow the operation's chronology: entry (needle, first trocar), insufflation (gas in the wrong place), dissection (energy and traction), exit (entrapment, bleeding) and recovery (hernia, rhabdomyolysis, delayed bowel necrosis). Each phase has a characteristic sign, and the dangerous injuries are the ones that present days later.

TRAINING AND PRACTICING LAPAROSCOPIC AND ROBOTIC SURGERY

Equipment for Practicing Laparoscopic and Robotic Surgery

  • Models range from inanimate boxes to VR, live animals and cadavers. Use non-living models unless a live one is essential.
  • Training box: opaque top with ports, monitor, light source, camera. Commercial boxes are costly; mobile-phone and 3D-printed options (e.g., iTrainer) exist.
  • Set up as in theatre: camera between the instruments, 25–45° between instruments, <55° to the horizontal. A camera driver also trains camera skills. Practise with 3-0 suture on an SH needle.
  • Inanimate drills use paper, sponge, glove and tubing. Non-living animal tissue is realistic and cheap but messy. A chicken oesophagus and stomach works for vesicourethral anastomosis and pyeloplasty practice.

Formal Training Programs

  • Competence = technical plus non-technical skills (judgement, anticipation, economy of motion). Residency has been more “learning” than “teaching”. Safety, efficiency and cost pressures favour formal programmes.
  • Mini-residency or mini-fellowship (typically 5 days): didactics, case observation, inanimate and animate laboratory, then proctoring. About three-quarters were doing laparoscopic renal surgery at 3 years, and about 90% robotic prostatectomy after a 5-day course.

KEY POINTS: TRAINING AND PRACTICING LAPAROSCOPIC AND ROBOTIC SURGERY

  • Skills training outside the operating room prepares the learner for safe, effective surgery.

Concept — Training moves skill acquisition off the patient: from box to VR to animal or cadaver, then proctored practice. Each step adds realism at greater cost, and validated metrics decide progression.

CONCLUSION

  • Procedures unthinkable in 1990 are now routine. The robot extends complex reconstruction to more urologists and often improves precision and outcome.
  • Mastery of the basics (pneumoperitoneum physiology, access, complication management) is what makes this possible: Osler's “treatments that harm less.”
