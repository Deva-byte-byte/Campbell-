## Instalment 1 — Chapter Framing, Embryology, Normal Antenatal Bladder Sonography, Nondilated or Absent Bladder (Agenesis, Hypoplasia, Exstrophy), Megacystis (Definitions, Differential, Table 42.1, Aneuploidy, Resolution), MMIHS, Figure 42.1

### Chapter framing (opening prose)

- **ICD-10-CM (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification)** classifies birth defects as **structural or functional**, and bladder anomalies span both.
- **Structural anomalies:** exstrophy, duplication, agenesis, urachal anomalies, diverticular disease.
- **Functional anomalies:** usually **secondary**, arising from bladder outlet obstruction or neurogenic dysfunction.
- **Both structural and functional:** the bladder of **prune-belly syndrome (PBS)**.
- The chapter covers the **less common congenital anomalies and rare acquired paediatric conditions**. Embryology and prenatal imaging are included because **many congenital anomalies are now first detected prenatally**.

### CONGENITAL BLADDER ANOMALIES

#### Embryology of the Urinary Bladder

Sequence:

- **Weeks 4–6:** the **urorectal septum** divides the endodermal **cloaca** into a ventral **urogenital sinus** and a dorsal **rectum**.
- **Cranial urogenital sinus** (continuous with the allantois) → **bladder and pelvic urethra**.
- **Caudal urogenital sinus** → **phallic urethra in males, distal vagina in females**.
- **Contrast:** the **male urethra** comes from the pelvic and phallic parts, whereas the **entire female urethra** comes from the **pelvic part** of the urogenital sinus.
- **Allantois:** an extraembryonic outpouching of the yolk sac that joins the cranioventral cloaca, the future bladder.
- **Week 10:** definitive nephrons **begin producing urine**. This is the **earliest point at which lower urinary tract anomalies can show**.
- **Months 4–5:** the bladder **descends into the pelvis**. The allantoic duct does not grow with the embryo, so it **elongates and narrows** until it obliterates into a fibrous cord, the **urachus**, which becomes the **median umbilical ligament** (bladder apex to umbilicus).

*Concept —* the urachus is **one tube obliterated by descent and elongation**. Every urachal anomaly is a failure of that single process at a different level.

#### Normal and Abnormal Antenatal Sonographic Findings of the Bladder

- A **normal fetal bladder is defined by the absence of abnormality**, and the criteria for abnormality are broad. **Physiological bladder cycling** must be excluded before any finding is called abnormal.
- **Appearance:** an **elliptical, anechoic pelvic structure**, bordered **laterally by the umbilical arteries**, **anteriorly by the pubic bones** and **posteriorly by the rectosigmoid**.
- **Wall thickness ≤3 mm**, with echogenicity similar to the other pelvic structures.
- **Visualisation timeline:** the bladder is **seen in about half of fetuses at 10 weeks** (the onset of urine production), in more at 11–12 weeks, and in **almost all by 13 weeks**.
- **Transvaginal is better than transabdominal** ultrasound for image quality and detection; the bladder is seen in the large majority at 12 weeks.
- **The fetal bladder empties every 15–20 minutes.** If the bladder is not seen, a **repeat scan in the same sitting is mandatory**.
- **First-trimester bladder diameter ≤6–8 mm.**
- **Fetal sex:** unreliable **before 14 weeks**. Base it on **seeing the testes, not the phallus**.
- **Amniotic fluid:** measured on every scan. **Up to 16 WGA (weeks' gestational age)** it is mainly **placental transudate**; after that it is **mostly fetal urine**. It therefore becomes a marker of fetal urine output only **after 16 weeks**.
- **Umbilical cord:** **two arteries and one vein**, with **no fluid-filled urachus**.

*Concept —* **the fetal bladder is a cycling organ**, so one image can neither prove absence nor confirm dilation. Timing governs interpretation: emptying every 15–20 minutes, amniotic fluid becoming urine at 16 weeks, and fetal sex being reliable only from 14 weeks.

#### Nondilated or Absent Bladder

- A **non-visualised bladder** is either **absent, malformed, or present but not filling**.
- A normal bladder may not fill because of **renal dysfunction** or **ectopic drainage of urine**. **Renal development and amniotic fluid** are what distinguish these.
- **An absent bladder is diagnosed only after a repeat scan 15–20 minutes later.**

*Bladder agenesis.*

- **Mechanism:** the hindgut is usually normal, so cloacal division is regular. The defect is therefore either **atrophy of the cranial urogenital sinus** or **failure to incorporate the mesonephric ducts and ureters into the trigone**.
- **Associations:** neurological and orthopaedic anomalies, and urogenital ones: **renal dysplasia or agenesis**, and absent **prostate, seminal vesicles, penis or vagina**.
- **Incidence about 1 in 600,000.** Few cases are reported, and a minority of them were live births. There is a **significant female majority**.
- **Compatible with life only if the ureters drain ectopically**: into **müllerian structures in females** or into the **rectum in males**.
- **Confirmation:** **antegrade or retrograde contrast imaging**. **MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) or CT (computed tomography)** adds anatomical detail before surgery.
- **Renal preservation:** **ureterosigmoidostomy or an external stoma**.

*Bladder hypoplasia.*

- The bladder forms and is visible throughout pregnancy but **never reaches adequate capacity**, because it is **inadequately filled or stored** during fetal life.

| Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|
| Inadequate outlet resistance | Severe epispadias |
| Separation defect | Urogenital sinus anomalies |
| Abnormal renal development | Bilateral renal dysplasia or agenesis |
| Urine bypassing the bladder | Ureteral ectopia |

- Some hypoplastic bladders **grow once the cause is corrected**, but **later augmentation is often needed**.

*Cloacal and bladder exstrophy.*

- There is a **bladder template only**, so exstrophy is suspected when **regular bladder filling is absent**.
- **Exstrophy is distinguished from agenesis** by a **bladder template on the lower abdominal wall** and **normal amniotic fluid** throughout pregnancy.
- **Covered cloacal exstrophy:** omphalocele, pelvic diastasis and genital anomalies **with bladder filling**. The bladder may become exstrophic near birth.
- **Many cases are still missed prenatally**, although detection is improving. **Fetal MRI** adds anatomical detail for counselling.

**COMPARISON — NONDILATED OR ABSENT FETAL BLADDER**

| | Agenesis | Hypoplasia | Exstrophy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bladder on ultrasound | **Absent** (after a repeat scan) | **Present but small**, never reaches capacity | **No filling; template on the abdominal wall** |
| Amniotic fluid | Not stated; depends on renal and ectopic drainage | Not stated | **Normal throughout** |
| Core defect | Cranial urogenital sinus atrophy, or trigone not formed | Inadequate filling or storage | Open bladder plate |
| Survival or outcome | **Only with ectopic ureteric drainage** | May grow; **augmentation often needed** | Reconstructive pathway (covered elsewhere) |

*Concept —* **a non-visualised bladder is a filling problem before it is a structural one**. The discriminators are the repeat scan, the kidneys and the amniotic fluid. Exstrophy alone combines absent filling with **normal amniotic fluid and a visible abdominal-wall template**.

#### Dilated Bladder and Congenital Megacystis

*Megacystis: the concept.*

- Megacystis was originally attributed to **bladder neck obstruction** causing massive bilateral **VUR (vesicoureteral reflux)** and a thin wall. **Bladder neck surgery did not change the outcome.**
- Re-examination found **normal urethras and complete emptying on VCUG (voiding cystourethrography)**. **Reflux is the cause of the dilation, not its consequence**: urine continuously **recycles between the upper tracts and the bladder**.
- In modern usage, **megacystis means any prenatally dilated bladder**, whatever the cause.
- Like hydronephrosis, it is **a finding, not a diagnosis**. It is harder to judge because the **bladder is normally distended at times**.

*First-trimester criteria (10–14 WGA).*

- Normal controls: **sagittal bladder length <10% of CRL (crown–rump length)**, which equates to **≤6 mm**.
- Established megacystis: ratio **≥13%**, diameter **≥8 mm**.
- Therefore **≤6 mm is normal and ≥8 mm is abnormal**. Other series use **>6 mm** or **≥7 mm**.
- Megacystis is rare, at about 0.06% of fetuses in a large unselected series.

*Second and third trimesters.*

- Definitions are less precise and mostly **subjective**. Later-gestation studies focus on a disease such as **PUV (posterior urethral valves)** or PBS, where size is one finding among several.
- **Bladder cycling is the marker of normal function.** An **"enlarged bladder" is one that does not empty during 45 minutes** of observation, the commonest definition after the first trimester.
- **Formula for 15–40 WGA:** **SL (sagittal length, mm) = GA (gestational age, weeks) − 5**, with a **95% CI (confidence interval) of ±7**.
  - **SL > GA + 2 mm** (above the upper 95% CI) → **"enlarged bladder"**.
  - **SL > GA + 12 mm** (10 mm beyond the upper CI) → **"megacystis"**.
  - The two tiers allow **more specific prenatal counselling**.

**COMPARISON — DEFINITIONS OF FETAL MEGACYSTIS**

| Gestation | Method | Normal | Abnormal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **10–14 WGA** | Sagittal length, or length as a proportion of CRL | **≤6 mm; <10% of CRL** | **≥8 mm; ≥13% of CRL** (other series: >6 mm or ≥7 mm) |
| **Beyond first trimester (functional)** | Observation of cycling | Empties within 45 minutes | **No emptying within 45 minutes** |
| **15–40 WGA (formula)** | SL = GA − 5 (±7) | ≤ GA + 2 mm | **Enlarged: > GA + 2 mm. Megacystis: > GA + 12 mm** |

*Differential diagnosis.*

| Obstructive: LUTO (lower urinary tract obstruction) | Non-obstructive |
|---|---|
| **PUV** | **PBS** |
| **Urethral atresia** | **MMIHS (megacystis–microcolon–intestinal hypoperistalsis syndrome)** |
| **Urethral stenosis or anterior urethral valves** | **Cloacal anomalies** |

- Some of these conditions overlap.
- **Meta-analysis of more than 1,000 fetuses:** a diagnosis is reached in the majority. **PUV is the commonest cause**, and **no cause is found in over one-third**.
- **Male to female ratio 8:1**, because the leading cause, PUV, occurs only in males.

*Aneuploidy.*

- About **one-fifth of fetuses with megacystis are aneuploid**, mostly **trisomy 13 or 18**. This is more than maternal age and nuchal translucency predict.
- The outcome is **uniformly poor**: most pregnancies were terminated and none of the rest survived to term. The high termination rate **limits urological outcome data** in this group.

*Resolution by size (euploid fetuses, first trimester).*

- **Longitudinal diameter 7–15 mm:** the **large majority resolve spontaneously**; the rest have obstructive uropathy.
- **Longitudinal diameter >15 mm:** **none resolve**, and **all have obstructive uropathy**.

*Resolution by timing (national multicentre fetal-medicine database).*

- **Early megacystis** is detected **before 18 WGA**; **late megacystis** is detected **after 18 WGA**. Resolution before **23 WGA** counts as early resolution.
- Overall resolution is **a minority**, but **early presentation resolves about three times as often as late presentation**.
- **Early presentation with a diameter of 12 mm or less** is a sensitive predictor of resolution; most of these resolve.
- Limitation: the population was selected by a high termination rate, and genetic data were not captured.

**CONTRAST — EARLY VERSUS LATE MEGACYSTIS**

| Early (<18 WGA) | Late (>18 WGA) |
|---|---|
| Resolves more often | Resolves less often |
| **≤12 mm predicts resolution** | — |
| **Resolved before 23 WGA → no postnatal urological surgery needed** | **Resolved after 23 WGA → most still need postnatal intervention** despite prenatal resolution |

- **Clinical signal:** **persistent megacystis with oligohydramnios means LUTO**. Megacystis that resolves before delivery may have no postnatal sequelae.

*Megacystis–microcolon–intestinal hypoperistalsis syndrome.*

- About **1% of megacystis**, but it matters because of its **morbidity**.
- **Prenatal picture:** **megacystis is often the only finding**, with **normal or increased amniotic fluid** (unlike LUTO). GI (gastrointestinal) findings are rarely seen on ultrasound. **Amniotic fluid digestive enzymes are abnormal in most cases** if sampled.
- **Pathology:** **smooth-muscle dysfunction of the GI and urinary tracts**, with **ACTG2 mutations** (an actin smooth-muscle filament gene).
- **Female predominance (about 70%)**, the reverse of megacystis overall.
- **Survival:** historically **about 20%**, improving to **about 55% for cases from 2004 to 2011**.
- **Postnatal presentation:** abdominal distension, bilious vomiting, failure to pass meconium. **Bowel obstruction with an enlarged bladder should be investigated as MMIHS.**
- **Exclude Hirschsprung disease** with a **colonic biopsy showing ganglion cells**. Contrast studies show a **hypomotile small bowel and a small colon (microcolon)**.
- **Enteric management:** ileostomy, gastrostomy and **TPN (total parenteral nutrition)**. **TPN complications (bloodstream infection, liver dysfunction) are the leading causes of death.** Small-intestinal transplantation shows promise.
- **Urological management:**
  - Urological morbidity is mostly secondary to the enteric disease.
  - Short term: **CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) or vesicostomy** to prevent the complications of retention.
  - **VUR is uncommon**, so **early contrast cystography is of limited value**.
  - Once the child is enterically stable: **urodynamics**, expecting an **acontractile bladder**.
  - Most need **lifelong CIC** and lifelong urological care, as in PUV or neurogenic bladder.

**CONTRAST — LUTO VERSUS MMIHS**

| | LUTO (for example PUV) | MMIHS |
|---|---|---|
| Amniotic fluid | **Oligohydramnios** when severe | **Normal or increased** |
| Sex | **Male** (PUV) | **Female predominance** |
| Mechanism | Anatomical outlet obstruction | **Visceral smooth-muscle myopathy (ACTG2)** |
| VUR | Common | **Uncommon** |
| Bladder function | Obstructed, high pressure | **Acontractile** |
| Main cause of death | Renal and pulmonary | **TPN-related sepsis and liver failure** |

*Concept —* **megacystis is a sign whose meaning is set by size, gestation at detection and timing of resolution**, with karyotype and amniotic fluid as the overriding qualifiers. **A small, early, early-resolving bladder is benign; a large, late or persistent one signals obstruction.** Normal fluid with megacystis in a female fetus points away from obstruction and towards MMIHS.

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 1

**Key facts and cut-offs**

- **Urine production begins at week 10**, and the bladder becomes visible then (about half at 10 weeks, almost all by 13 weeks).
- **Cloacal division at weeks 4–6. Bladder descends and urachus forms at months 4–5.**
- **Fetal bladder wall ≤3 mm. First-trimester diameter ≤6–8 mm.**
- **Fetal bladder empties every 15–20 minutes**, so re-scan before calling it absent.
- **Amniotic fluid becomes fetal urine from 16 WGA.**
- **Fetal sex is reliable only after 14 weeks**, judged by the **testes, not the phallus**.
- **First-trimester megacystis:** **≤6 mm normal, ≥8 mm abnormal** (others use >6 mm or ≥7 mm). **Bladder length <10% of CRL is normal; ≥13% is abnormal.**
- **Later gestation:** **no emptying in 45 minutes**. Formula **SL = GA − 5 (±7)**: **> GA + 2 = enlarged; > GA + 12 = megacystis**.
- **Size:** **7–15 mm → most resolve; >15 mm → none resolve, all obstructive.**
- **Timing:** **early means <18 WGA; early resolution means <23 WGA**. **Early with ≤12 mm → likely to resolve.**
- **Megacystis male to female ratio 8:1.** **MMIHS is female-predominant.**
- **Aneuploidy in about one-fifth of megacystis**, mostly **trisomy 13 and 18**, with universally poor outcome.
- **Bladder agenesis:** about **1 in 600,000**, **female majority**.
- **MMIHS survival:** **about 20% historically, about 55% in recent cases.**

**Commonest**

- **Commonest cause of megacystis: PUV.** Second-largest group: **undefined (over one-third)**.
- **Commonest cause of death in MMIHS: TPN complications.**

**Gold standards and first-line**

- **To confirm bladder agenesis:** antegrade or retrograde contrast study.
- **To exclude Hirschsprung disease:** colonic biopsy showing ganglion cells.
- **Bladder management in MMIHS:** CIC.

**Genetics**

- **MMIHS: ACTG2.**

**Contrasts to hold**

- **Exstrophy vs agenesis:** exstrophy has a **template on the abdominal wall and normal amniotic fluid**.
- **LUTO vs MMIHS:** **oligohydramnios, male** vs **normal or increased fluid, female**.

**Classic traps**

- A single scan without the bladder does not mean agenesis. **Re-scan after 15–20 minutes.**
- **Megacystis is a finding, not a diagnosis.**
- **Prenatal resolution is not reassuring if it happens late**: late presentation with resolution after 23 weeks still usually needs surgery.
- **In megacystis, reflux can be the cause of the dilation, not the result of obstruction.** Bladder neck surgery does not help.
- **Female fetus with megacystis:** not PUV. Think MMIHS, a cloacal anomaly or urethral atresia.
- **MMIHS: early VCUG is low-yield**, because VUR is uncommon.
- **Amniotic fluid before 16 weeks does not reflect fetal renal output.**

## Instalment 2 — Bladder Duplication, Congenital Diverticula (Hutch Primary vs Secondary Paraureteral, Posterolateral and Syndromic), Key Points: Congenital, Urachal Anomalies (Incidental Remnant Debate, Patent Urachus, Open and Laparoscopic Excision, Urachal Sinus, Urachal Cyst, Urachal Diverticulum), Key Points: Urachal, Acquired Diverticula, Haemangioma, Fibroepithelial Polyp, Nephrogenic Adenoma, Cystitis Cystica and Glandularis, Eosinophilic Cystitis, Haemorrhagic Cystitis, Key Points: Acquired, Figures 42.2–42.8

#### Bladder Duplication

- Duplication of the bladder and urethra is **complete or incomplete**, and **sagittal or coronal** in orientation. **Complete sagittal duplication is the commonest.**

**CONTRAST — INCOMPLETE VERSUS COMPLETE DUPLICATION**

| Incomplete | Complete |
|---|---|
| The two halves **communicate** | Two **fully separate bladders**, each with normal mucosa and full-thickness muscle, **divided by a peritoneal fold** |
| Usually **one urethra** | Each usually has **its own ureter, urethra and meatus**; the two may differ in size and quality |
| **May need no surgery** if both halves drain through the common urethra | Usually reconstructed: the bladders are **combined into one** |

- **Rarely, one bladder has no urethra.** It is then **completely obstructed**, which causes **ipsilateral renal dysplasia**.
- **Continence varies.**
- **Associated anomalies:**
  - **External genital duplication in up to 90%**; **lower GI duplication in up to 42%**.
  - A duplicated vagina may connect to a separate unicornuate uterus. Each duplicated penis has its own urethra.
  - VUR, renal ectopia and dysplasia are common.
  - **Non-urological anomalies are more frequent in sagittal than in coronal duplication**: GI malformations, spinal duplication, spina bifida, and urogenital–GI fistulae.
  - **Exstrophy variant:** an exstrophic bladder and urethra **plus** a normal, closed intra-abdominal bladder.
- **Presentation:** often in the newborn because of the GI or genital anomalies. Many present later with **recurrent infection or incontinence**.
- **Work-up:** karyotype, ultrasound, videourodynamics, genitogram and cross-sectional imaging for anatomy, plus VCUG and renal nuclear scans for VUR and function. **The final plan is often deferred until endoscopy and surgical exploration.**
- **Treatment sequence:** first **preserve renal function and prevent infection** by relieving any obstruction → then, in the long term, **continence and reconstruction of the internal and external genitalia**.
  - **Both sphincter complexes competent:** **join the distal urethras**.
  - **One sphincter incompetent:** **close that bladder neck and excise its urethra**.
  - **Duplicated vaginas** are joined in the midline, with a **vulvoplasty**.
  - **Asymptomatic patients may be left uncorrected**; successful pregnancies have been reported.
- **Individualise surgery**, and perform it in **centres experienced in complex urogenital reconstruction**.

*Concept —* **sphincter competence, not bladder anatomy, decides the reconstruction**. Whatever the configuration, the order is fixed: kidneys and infection first, continence and genitalia later.

#### Congenital Diverticula

- **Mechanism:** **mucosa herniates between defects in the detrusor fibres**. The **width of the neck equals the size of the muscle defect**.
- **Incidence:** low, about 1.7% of symptomatic children undergoing imaging. The **true incidence is higher**, because asymptomatic diverticula go undiscovered.
- **Detection:** possible prenatally, but they are usually found postnatally during work-up for **infection, haematuria, incontinence or obstruction**.
- **Imaging:**
  - Ultrasound shows large diverticula.
  - **VCUG is the gold standard**, showing associated **VUR** and **post-void retention**.
  - A nuclear scan gives anatomy, function and obstruction if there is hydroureteronephrosis.
- **Diverticula are dynamic and may not appear on every study**, so repeat imaging if clinical suspicion persists.

*Paraureteral diverticula (Hutch classification).*

- **By far the commonest** congenital diverticula. **Hutch** divided them into **primary and secondary**.
- **Primary** diverticula herniate **through the ureteral hiatus, between the intravesical ureter and the roof of the hiatus**. The likely cause is a **congenitally deficient bladder wall**.
- An isolated **Waldeyer sheath** defect has been proposed, but it is **unlikely because congenital diverticula are usually unilateral**.
- **Management of primary diverticula:**
  - **Small and asymptomatic → conservative.**
  - **With VUR → many surgeons recommend excision.**
  - **Females** resolve (diverticulum together with VUR) **spontaneously more often than males**, which is attributed to **higher male voiding pressures** even without obstruction.
  - **A primary paraureteral diverticulum does not preclude endoscopic injection of dextranomer/hyaluronic acid (Deflux)** for VUR.

*Posterolateral and multiple congenital diverticula.*

- Associated with **connective tissue disorders: Ehlers–Danlos, Williams and Menkes syndromes**. They occasionally occur without a syndrome.
- They can become **massive, often larger than the bladder itself**.
- **Histology:** an **attenuated, lax muscle wall** rather than classic herniation through a defect, **without obstruction**.
- **Presentation:** **recurrent UTI (urinary tract infection)** from stasis, and **stones**.
- **Management:**
  - **Asymptomatic → close observation**, especially in connective tissue disorders.
  - **Symptomatic → resection** (open or laparoscopic). This eliminates the UTIs and often improves spontaneous voiding.
  - **Recurrence or new diverticula are common in syndromic patients**, so weigh surgery against quality of life. **Vesicostomy or suprapubic drainage** may be preferred.
  - **Postoperative urodynamics** look for obstruction or high-pressure voiding that would hasten recurrence.

**COMPARISON — BLADDER DIVERTICULA IN CHILDREN**

| | Primary paraureteral (congenital) | Secondary paraureteral (acquired) | Posterolateral or multiple (syndromic) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bladder wall | **Smooth** | **Trabeculated** | Attenuated, lax |
| Number | **Single, isolated** | **One of many** | Multiple; can be **giant** |
| On imaging | **Intermittent** | **Always present** | May be dynamic |
| Cause | **Focal congenital wall deficiency** at the ureteral hiatus | **Infravesical obstruction or neurogenic bladder** (also infection, previous surgery) | **Connective tissue disorder** (Ehlers–Danlos, Williams, Menkes) |
| Obstruction | **None** | **Present, and the cause** | None |
| Management | Observe if small; **excise if with VUR**; Deflux possible | **Treat the obstruction first**; excise and reimplant if symptomatic | Observe if asymptomatic; resect if UTI or stones; **high recurrence** |

*Concept —* **diverticular behaviour follows the wall and the pressure**. A focal defect at normal pressure gives a single, intermittent primary diverticulum. Generalised laxity gives giant, recurrent syndromic ones. Raised pressure gives multiple secondary diverticula, which is why the outlet is treated before the diverticulum.

#### KEY POINTS: CONGENITAL BLADDER ANOMALIES

- **Persistent megacystis with oligohydramnios → LUTO.** Megacystis that resolves before delivery may have no postnatal sequelae.
- **MMIHS may show only megacystis prenatally.**
- **Nondilated anomalies**, such as exstrophy, are the **most severe congenital urological malformations**. **Normal amniotic fluid is typical of exstrophy** (corrected wording; see flags).
- **Bladder agenesis is compatible with life only if the ureters drain ectopically.**
- **Connective tissue disorders cause multiple diverticula**, which may need treatment for **stasis-related infection or stones**.

*Concept (congenital anomalies as a whole) —* **congenital bladder anomalies are read through function before form**: whether the bladder fills, empties and holds its wall. The prenatal picture and the postnatal treatment both follow from those three questions.

#### Urachal Anomalies

- **Symptomatic remnant → complete excision with a bladder cuff**, open or laparoscopic. It is a simple operation with low morbidity.
- **The dilemma is the asymptomatic, incidental remnant.** Urachal **adenocarcinoma is unreported in children**, but in adults it has **high mortality and needs far more complex surgery**.

**CONTRAST — THE INCIDENTAL, ASYMPTOMATIC URACHAL REMNANT**

| Excise all | Excise only if symptomatic |
|---|---|
| Rationale: prevent progression to adult malignancy | Rationale: the risk of progression, once quantified, is low |
| Earlier position | **Now increasingly adopted** |

- **Either way, discuss the options with the family**, because of the potential for malignant change in adulthood.

*Patent urachus.*

- **Cause:** **non-descent of the bladder** or, **more commonly**, **failure of the epithelial canal to obliterate**.
- **Obstruction theory doubted:** patency is **often absent in severely obstructed fetal bladders**, and **only a minority (up to 14%)** have confirmed in-utero obstruction. Obliteration therefore seems **independent of bladder distension**.
- **Alternative theory:** **retubularisation** of an obliterated urachus, rather than primary patency. This is supported by umbilical urinary fistulae in obstruction acquired later in life.
- **Presentation (neonatal):** continuous or intermittent **umbilical drainage**, an **enlarged or oedematous umbilicus**, and **delayed healing of the cord stump**.
- **Organisms:** *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Escherichia coli*, *Enterococcus*, *Citrobacter* and, rarely, *Proteus*.
- **Diagnosis:** a **fluid-filled canal on longitudinal ultrasound**, or **contrast filling on retrograde fistulogram or VCUG**. MRI helps but depends on how full the bladder is.
- **Differentiate from a patent omphalomesenteric duct.** Both together are rare.
- **Infected or abscessed urachus:** **drain under antibiotic cover → once settled, completely excise the urachus with a bladder cuff**. Remove **all anomalous tissue** to prevent recurrence, stones and later adenocarcinoma.

*Open excision: sequence.*

Balloon catheter to distend the bladder → **transverse or midline infraumbilical incision** (in infants, a **small transverse subumbilical incision** because the dome is still high) → feeding tube or small catheter into the patent urachus for identification → circumscribe the urachus at the umbilicus, **preserving the umbilicus** for cosmesis → **longitudinal incision of the rectus fascia**, muscles separated, distended dome identified → urachus isolated with a vessel loop → **extraperitoneal dissection to the umbilicus**, umbilical portion delivered and removed → **bladder cuff including the urachal insertion** marked and excised with electrocautery → **two-layer watertight bladder closure** → catheter left overnight.

*Laparoscopic excision.*

- **Feasible even under 6 months of age** and safe at all ages. Series report no complications, no recurrences and short operating times.
- **Three-port approach:** camera in the **midline between the umbilicus and the xiphoid**, with a working port in each upper quadrant.
- **Alternative:** camera in the **left lower abdomen** with **2-mm working ports in the left lower and upper abdomen**, which gives a better view of the umbilical end.
- **Robotic-assisted and single-port** approaches have also been described.

**CONTRAST — OPEN VERSUS LAPAROSCOPIC URACHAL EXCISION**

| Open | Laparoscopic |
|---|---|
| **Extraperitoneal** | **Intraperitoneal** |
| Small incision; umbilicus preserved | **Better view of the whole urachal course and the dome** |
| No peritoneal contamination | Risk of **spilling infected or malignant material** and of **intraperitoneal urine leak** |

*Urachal sinus (umbilical–urachal sinus).*

- The urachus **obliterates at the bladder but stays open at the umbilicus**, causing a **continuously draining sinus** that looks like a patent urachus.
- **Diagnosis by sinugram:** the caudal urachus is filled with desquamated epithelium, and **there is no connection to the bladder**.
- **Mimic: a persistent omphalomesenteric duct**, which is a **Meckel diverticulum connected to the umbilicus**.
  - The sinugram cannot separate them, because neither shows a connection to the bladder or bowel.
  - **Both need complete excision.**
  - **Omphalomesenteric remnants may contain gastric or small-bowel mucosa** on histology; urachal remnants do not.

*Urachal cyst.*

- **No communication with the bladder or umbilicus**, but it can **drain intermittently** through either.
- **Usually in the distal urachus.** **More common in adults than in children.**
- The contents are desquamated epithelium, which can become infected. **The commonest organism is *S. aureus*.**
- **Presentation when infected:** umbilical abscess, bladder infection, lower abdominal pain, voiding symptoms, or a painful palpable mass.
- **Diagnosis:** ultrasound shows a **cyst between the anterior abdominal wall and the peritoneum**. **CT or MRI** if the infection is extensive or the anatomy unclear.
- **If missed**, it can **perforate into the bladder or the peritoneum**, causing **peritonitis or an enteric fistula**.
- **Treatment: drain → then completely excise.**

*Urachal (vesicourachal) diverticulum.*

- The urachus obliterates **except at the bladder apex**, leaving a diverticulum.
- **Usually asymptomatic** and found incidentally. It **drains well because of its wide mouth**, even if it enlarges with obstruction.
- **Intervention is needed only with a narrow neck** causing **stones or UTI**.

**COMPARISON — URACHAL ANOMALIES**

| | Patent urachus | Umbilical–urachal sinus | Urachal cyst | Vesicourachal diverticulum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Part left open | **Whole canal** | **Umbilical end** | **Isolated segment, usually distal** | **Bladder end (apex)** |
| Communicates with | Bladder and umbilicus | Umbilicus only | Neither (may drain intermittently either way) | Bladder only |
| Typical presentation | **Neonatal umbilical urine drainage**; delayed cord healing | Continuous umbilical discharge | **Infection**: abscess, pain, mass, UTI; **commoner in adults** | **Incidental**; stones or UTI if the neck is narrow |
| Diagnostic test | **Longitudinal ultrasound; fistulogram or VCUG** | **Sinugram** (no bladder connection) | **Ultrasound**; CT or MRI if complex | Incidental imaging |
| Main mimic | Patent omphalomesenteric duct | **Omphalomesenteric duct (Meckel diverticulum to umbilicus)** | — | — |
| Treatment | **Excise with bladder cuff** (drain first if infected) | **Complete excision** | **Drain → excise** | **Only if symptomatic** |

#### KEY POINTS: URACHAL ANOMALIES

- **Usually detected postnatally because of umbilical drainage.**
- **Infected remnant → antibiotics, then excision.**
- **Can be mistaken for a persistent omphalomesenteric duct** on preoperative work-up.
- **Discuss excision of an asymptomatic, incidental remnant with the family**, because of the potential for malignant change in adulthood.

*Concept —* **every urachal anomaly is the same tube obliterated to a different level**, so the level of residual patency predicts the communication, the symptom and the diagnostic test. Treatment converges on **complete excision with a bladder cuff**. The only live debate is the asymptomatic, incidental remnant.

### ACQUIRED BLADDER ANOMALIES IN CHILDREN

#### Noninflammatory Acquired Conditions

##### Acquired Diverticula

- **Caused by infravesical obstruction, which must be treated first.** Once outlet resistance is normal, the bladder **remodels** and diverticulectomy may become unnecessary.
- **Symptomatic → excise the diverticulum, with ipsilateral ureteric reimplantation** if the ureter lies near or within it. This can be done intravesically or extravesically, open or laparoscopically or robotically.
- **Secondary paraureteral diverticula (Hutch):** caused by **infravesical obstruction or neurogenic bladder**. Raised pressure forces mucosa out between the muscle fibres, usually as **one of many "pop-off" diverticula**. Other causes are **muscle weakened by infection** and a **muscle defect after bladder surgery**.
- **Progression (both paraureteral types):** Waldeyer sheath damaged as the diverticulum enlarges → intravesical ureter pulled out of its anchored position → **ureterovesical junction dysfunction** → eventually **ureteric obstruction** → **renal dysplasia** has been reported.
- **Vicious circle:** a large paraureteral or posterolateral diverticulum **compresses the bladder neck or posterior urethra** → outlet obstruction → the diverticulum fills and expands further → **complete urinary retention**. This is seen with the congenital diverticular syndromes and leads to surgery.

(For the comparison with congenital diverticula, see the diverticula table in Congenital Diverticula above.)

##### Bladder Hemangiomas

- **Benign vascular tumours**, mostly associated with **Klippel–Trénaunay or Sturge–Weber syndrome**.
- Usually **solitary and <3 cm**, but can be multifocal.
- Present with **gross haematuria**, with or without irritative symptoms.
- **Small → biopsy with fulguration, or Nd:YAG (neodymium:yttrium–aluminium–garnet) laser.** **Large, or at risk of rapid blood loss → partial cystectomy.**

##### Fibroepithelial Polyps

- **Rare in the bladder.** Cause haematuria and irritative symptoms; outlet or ureteric obstruction has been reported.
- **The second commonest paediatric bladder tumour, after rhabdomyosarcoma** (single-centre review over two decades).
- **Simple endoscopic resection is usually curative.**

#### Inflammatory Acquired Conditions

##### Nephrogenic Adenoma and Cystitis Cystica

*Nephrogenic adenoma.*

- A **rare benign tumour**, mostly seen in adults. In children it is a **reaction to infection, stones, trauma or surgery**.
- **Female to male ratio 5:1.** Presents with haematuria or irritative symptoms.
- **Diagnosis by cystoscopy and biopsy. Treatment by transurethral fulguration or resection.**
- **Recurrence in about 80% of children, after a latency of about 4 years**, so **routine surveillance cystoscopy is needed**. No malignant change has been reported in children.
- Long-term resolution has been reported with **ibuprofen and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole** after resection.
- **Malignant change in an adult:** in a young adult with a post-traumatic neurogenic bladder, repeatedly resected nephrogenic adenoma became adenocarcinoma, treated by radical cystectomy.
- **In biopsy series, most patients had bladder augmentation with recurrent stones and infection.** Immunohistochemistry points to an **origin from distal renal tubular cells**.

*Cystitis cystica and cystitis glandularis.*

- **Cystitis cystica:**
  - Fluid-filled cystic masses caused by **chronic inflammation**; **it can mimic a bladder tumour**.
  - **Almost exclusively female.**
  - **Treatment: long-term antibiotic prophylaxis.**
- **Cystitis glandularis:**
  - **Even rarer in children.** Caused by **metaplasia to mucus-secreting epithelium**.
  - It is suspected of being **premalignant, but few data support this**.

##### Eosinophilic Cystitis

- Sporadic case reports. **Male predominance.** The cause is unclear; **immunological disease and allergy** have been suggested.
- **Histology:** **eosinophils throughout all layers of the bladder wall.**
- **Symptoms:** dysuria, haematuria, suprapubic pain, urinary retention.
- **No typical cystoscopic appearance, so diagnosis requires transurethral biopsy.**
- **Treatment:**
  - **Transurethral resection with corticosteroids, antihistamines or antibiotics** is the most successful across all ages.
  - **In neonates and young children it may be self-limiting, so observation is justified.**
  - In a small case series, a combination of steroids, antihistamines and antibiotics was successful in every case.

##### Hemorrhagic Cystitis

- **Almost always linked to treatment of childhood cancer**, especially haematological cancer.
- **Commonest risk factor: cyclophosphamide or ifosfamide.** It is **made worse by viral infection** in the immunocompromised child: **BK virus, CMV (cytomegalovirus), adenovirus**. **Pelvic radiotherapy** can also cause it.
- **Prevention: mesna (sodium 2-mercaptoethane sulfonate)**, given together with urotoxic agents.
- **Irrigation is difficult and traumatic** because only small catheters can be used. Outcomes improved with an **early-detection protocol, avoiding catheters, and adding oral pentosan polysulfate sodium**.

**COMPARISON — PAEDIATRIC BLADDER LESIONS PRESENTING WITH HAEMATURIA OR A MASS**

| Lesion | Sex | Association or trigger | Diagnosis | Treatment | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Haemangioma** | — | **Klippel–Trénaunay, Sturge–Weber** | Cystoscopy and biopsy | **Small: fulguration or Nd:YAG. Large or bleeding: partial cystectomy** | Solitary, <3 cm, gross haematuria |
| **Fibroepithelial polyp** | — | — | Cystoscopy | **Endoscopic resection (curative)** | **Second to rhabdomyosarcoma** |
| **Nephrogenic adenoma** | **Female 5:1** | Infection, stones, trauma, surgery; **augmentation** | **Cystoscopy and biopsy** | Transurethral fulguration or resection | **80% recur (about 4 years) → surveillance** |
| **Cystitis cystica** | **Almost only female** | Chronic inflammation | Cystoscopy | **Long-term antibiotic prophylaxis** | **Mimics tumour** |
| **Cystitis glandularis** | — | Metaplasia to mucus-secreting epithelium | Biopsy | — | Premalignancy unproven |
| **Eosinophilic cystitis** | **Male** | Allergy or immunological (proposed) | **Biopsy is mandatory** (no typical appearance) | **TUR with steroids, antihistamines, antibiotics. Neonates: observe** | Eosinophils in all layers |
| **Haemorrhagic cystitis** | — | **Cyclophosphamide or ifosfamide**; BK virus, CMV, adenovirus; radiotherapy | Clinical | **Prevent with mesna**; early detection, avoid catheters, oral pentosan | Irrigation is traumatic in children |

#### KEY POINTS: ACQUIRED BLADDER ANOMALIES IN CHILDREN

- **Acquired diverticula can cause obstruction, or stasis with recurrent UTI.**
- **After resection of a nephrogenic adenoma, routine cystoscopy is required** because **80% recur in children**.
- **Cystitis cystica mimics tumour** and is treated with **long-term antibiotic prophylaxis**.
- **Eosinophilic cystitis has no typical appearance and needs biopsy.**
- **Cyclophosphamide causes haemorrhagic cystitis.** It is **prevented with mesna**, and oral pentosan polysulfate shows promise.

*Concept —* **acquired lesions divide by what drives them**:
- **Pressure** (diverticula): treat the outlet first.
- **Vascular or stromal growth** (haemangioma, polyp): ablate or resect locally.
- **Chronic irritation or toxicity** (nephrogenic adenoma, cystitis cystica, eosinophilic and haemorrhagic cystitis): remove the stimulus, biopsy because appearance misleads, and survey for recurrence.

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 2

**Key facts and cut-offs**

- **Duplication:** **external genital duplication in up to 90%**; **lower GI duplication in up to 42%**. **Complete sagittal is the commonest.**
- **Congenital diverticula** are found in about 1.7% of symptomatic children imaged.
- **Patent urachus with proven fetal obstruction: up to 14% only.**
- **Laparoscopic urachal excision is feasible under 6 months.**
- **Haemangioma: <3 cm, solitary.**
- **Nephrogenic adenoma:** **female 5:1**; **80% recur, about 4 years' latency.**

**Gold standards, commonest and first-line**

- **VCUG is the gold standard for diverticula.**
- **Paraureteral diverticula are the commonest congenital diverticula.**
- **Commonest organism in an infected urachal cyst: *S. aureus*.**
- **Fibroepithelial polyp is the second commonest paediatric bladder tumour, after rhabdomyosarcoma.**
- **Commonest cause of haemorrhagic cystitis: cyclophosphamide or ifosfamide**; prevented with **mesna**.
- **Symptomatic urachal remnant → complete excision with a bladder cuff.**
- **Infected urachus or cyst → drain with antibiotics first, then excise.**
- **Cystitis cystica → long-term antibiotic prophylaxis.**
- **Fibroepithelial polyp → endoscopic resection.**

**Eponyms**

- **Hutch:** **primary vs secondary** paraureteral diverticula; smooth, single and intermittent vs trabeculated, multiple and constant.
- **Waldeyer sheath:** the ureteric sheath disrupted as a paraureteral diverticulum enlarges, causing ureterovesical junction failure.
- **Klippel–Trénaunay and Sturge–Weber:** bladder haemangioma.
- **Ehlers–Danlos, Williams and Menkes:** multiple, giant, recurrent diverticula.
- **Hirschsprung disease:** excluded in MMIHS by biopsy showing ganglion cells.
- **Meckel diverticulum:** the omphalomesenteric remnant that mimics a urachal sinus.

**When to operate**

- **Duplication:** relieve obstruction first. If complete, join the bladders: **both sphincters competent → join the urethras; one incompetent → close that bladder neck and excise its urethra**.
- **Primary paraureteral diverticulum with VUR → excise.**
- **Syndromic diverticulum with UTI or stones → resect.**
- **Acquired diverticulum** → relieve the obstruction first; **excise with reimplantation only if still symptomatic.**
- **Vesicourachal diverticulum → only if a narrow neck causes stones or UTI.**
- **Haemangioma that is large or at risk of rapid blood loss → partial cystectomy.**

**When not to operate**

- **Incomplete duplication draining well**, or an asymptomatic duplication.
- **Small, asymptomatic primary diverticulum.**
- **Asymptomatic syndromic diverticula** (observe).
- **Acquired diverticula once the outlet is corrected** (remodelling).
- **Asymptomatic, incidental urachal remnant:** the trend is observation, after discussion with the family.
- **Eosinophilic cystitis in neonates** (may be self-limiting).

**Classic traps**

- **A primary paraureteral diverticulum does not rule out Deflux.**
- **Diverticula are dynamic**, so one normal study does not exclude them.
- **Syndromic diverticula recur after resection**, so check urodynamics after surgery.
- **Acquired diverticulum: excising it without fixing the outlet fails.**
- **A sinugram cannot separate a urachal sinus from an omphalomesenteric duct**; only histology (gastric or small-bowel mucosa) can.
- **Laparoscopic urachal excision is intraperitoneal**, with a risk of spilling infected or malignant material.
- **Urachal cysts are commoner in adults than in children.**
- **Eosinophilic cystitis has no typical look**, so biopsy is needed.
- **Nephrogenic adenoma recurs in most children**, so surveillance is mandatory.
- **Cystitis cystica mimics tumour.**
- **Irrigation in haemorrhagic cystitis is traumatic in children**; avoid catheters where possible.
