## Instalment 1 — Chapter Preamble, History, Microbiology, Epidemiology, Infection and Host Immune Response, Development of Urogenital Disease

### UROGENITAL TUBERCULOSIS

#### Chapter preamble

- **Tuberculosis (TB) can involve any organ**, including the urogenital (UG) tract. Untreated UG TB causes **irreversible tissue destruction**, with **renal failure and infertility** as the serious end-points. TB must therefore sit in the differential diagnosis of UG disorders.
- **TB is the second "great imitator"** (after syphilis). It mimics many other diseases, which delays correct diagnosis and treatment.
- **As TB becomes rarer in industrialised nations, diagnosis depends on clinical suspicion.** Clinicians see it less often, so they have to think of it deliberately.
- **Terminology:** "urogenital TB" is now preferred over "genitourinary TB" because the **urinary tract is involved far more often than the genital tract**.

#### History

- **Mycobacterium tuberculosis has co-evolved with humans.** A progenitor (*M. prototuberculosis*) may have infected early hominids more than 3 million years ago. Bony lesions consistent with TB appear in a *Homo erectus* skeleton about 500,000 years old.
- **Oldest microbiological confirmation:** Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex DNA from **9000-year-old Neolithic skeletons** in the Eastern Mediterranean. Tubercle bacilli have also been shown microscopically and molecularly in **Egyptian mummies (c. 3000 BCE)**.
- **Written descriptions of TB** come from ancient East Asia, the pre-Columbian Americas and classical Western civilisations (Greece, Rome), and continue through modern history.
- **The epidemic peak came in the 18th and 19th centuries** in Europe and North America. "Consumption" caused up to **one-quarter of deaths in the Industrial Age**.
- **Turning point, 24 March 1882:** **Robert Koch** presented the first isolation and identification of the tubercle bacillus. **24 March is World Tuberculosis Day**.

#### Microbiology

- **TB is caused by the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC):** a group of closely related **acid-fast bacteria**.

**MTBC SPECIES BY HOST RANGE**

| Host range | Species |
|---|---|
| **Humans only** | *M. tuberculosis*, *M. africanum* |
| **Humans and other mammals** | *M. bovis*, *M. canettii*, *M. microti*, *M. caprae*, *M. orygis*, *M. pinnipedii* |
| **Animals only (so far)** | *M. mungi*, *M. suricattae* |

- **Commonest human isolate worldwide: *M. tuberculosis*, then *M. bovis*.** There is regional variation: in **West Africa, *M. africanum*** causes a large share of human TB.
- **"M. tuberculosis" is often used loosely for the whole complex.** This usage is inaccurate.
- **Clinically the species are indistinguishable, but their drug susceptibilities differ.** The classic example is ***M. bovis*, which is innately resistant to pyrazinamide**, a first-line agent.
- The table sorts the species by whom they infect. The trap is to assume they all have the same drug susceptibility. **Species matters for treatment, not for the clinical picture**. This is directly relevant to bacille Calmette–Guérin (BCG, derived from *M. bovis*) infections after intravesical therapy.

*Concept —* **the complex is one disease clinically but several organisms pharmacologically**: identify the species because it changes the drug regimen, not because it changes the presentation.

#### Epidemiology

- **Global (World Health Organization, WHO):** **one-quarter of the world's population has latent MTBC infection**. In 2021 there were about **10.6 million new active cases and 1.6 million deaths**. Deaths have fallen steadily since 2000, and **TB mortality has fallen by 45% since 1990**.
- **Obstacles to control:** the **human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) epidemic in sub-Saharan Africa**, the **global rise in obesity and diabetes**, and **multidrug and extensive drug resistance**.
- **United States:** about **8300 active cases in 2022 (2.5 per 100,000)**. Incidence has declined steadily since the **1980s resurgence, which peaked in 1992**. Reported cases fell during COVID-19 in 2020 and were back to pre-pandemic levels by 2022.
  - **TB falls disproportionately on people born outside the United States.** Their incidence is **16 times** that of people born in the United States.
- **The proportion of pulmonary TB patients who also have UG disease depends on setting:**

**UROGENITAL TB BY SETTING**

| | Developing countries | Developed countries |
|---|---|---|
| **Share of global UG TB** | **> 90%** | < 10% |
| **UG TB as a proportion of pulmonary TB** | **15–20%** | **2–10%** |
| **Rank among extrapulmonary sites** | **Second** (after lymph nodes) | **Third** in the United States (after pleural and lymphatic TB); about one-quarter of extrapulmonary cases |

- **Sex and age:** **about two-thirds of patients are male**. UG TB is **mainly a disease of adults**, although it has been reported in children as young as 2 years.

*Concept —* **UG TB burden tracks the TB burden, and diagnostic delay tracks resources.** The developing world has more cases, a larger UG share and more advanced disease at presentation, and this later shows up in the rates of contracted bladder.

#### Infection, Host Immune Response and Transmission

- **Entry is by inhalation of aerosols generated by coughing** (direct soft-tissue inoculation is rare) → **alveolar macrophages phagocytose the bacilli**. From there, three outcomes are possible:
  - **Cleared:** the macrophages kill the bacilli. There is **no infection and no adaptive immune response**.
  - **Established:** the bacilli escape killing and replicate inside macrophages. **Up to 12 weeks may pass before a cellular immune response is detectable**. In that window the bacilli spread **through the lymphatics to the hilar nodes, then through the bloodstream to seed distant organs**. This is how the kidney and epididymis are seeded.
- **Granuloma formation is how the host contains the infection:**
  - **Infected macrophages secrete cytokines**: interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-12, IL-1β and tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α).
  - **Structure:** a **centre of foamy macrophages, epithelioid cells and multinucleated (Langhans) giant cells**, surrounded by a **cuff of lymphocytes**.
  - **Antigen presentation activates T cells**, which secrete IL-2, TNF-α and, **most importantly, interferon-γ (IFN-γ)**. IFN-γ maintains the granuloma and drives killing of infected macrophages and bacilli.
  - **If killing fails, the granuloma sequesters viable bacilli**, which become **dormant**.

**LATENT INFECTION VS ACTIVE DISEASE**

| | Latent TB infection | Active TB disease |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency after infection** | **90–95%** are controlled at this stage | **< 5%** progress **within the first year** (primary progression) |
| **Pathology** | **Scarring (cicatrisation) and granuloma calcification** | Renewed replication → **caseation (the characteristic lesion)** → release of bacilli |
| **Later risk** | **Lifetime reactivation risk 5–15%**, higher with comorbidity | — |
| **Infectious?** | No | **Pulmonary disease: yes** (respiratory aerosols to close contacts) |

- **Reactivation triggers (the host–pathogen balance shifts towards the pathogen):** old age, renal failure, diabetes mellitus, malnutrition, HIV infection and other immunosuppression.
- **Extrapulmonary disease on its own, including UG TB, is generally not infectious.** The exception is **transmission by direct skin inoculation or by aerosol-generating procedures on infected fluids or tissues**, which is relevant in theatre.
- **The latent/active split is a simplification.** TB is now understood as a **dynamic spectrum**: contained infection → subclinical disease → progressively infectious disease. A person can **move in either direction** as host immunity changes (comorbidity) or as the bacilli change (treatment of latent infection).

*Concept —* **TB outcome is a balance between host and pathogen, and the granuloma is where that balance is held.** Latency, reactivation and the whole spectrum are the granuloma holding, failing or re-forming as host immunity changes, and IFN-γ is the cytokine that holds it.

#### Development of Urogenital Disease

**ROUTES TO UROGENITAL TB**

| Route | Frequency | Mechanism and examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Haematogenous** | **Principal route** | Seeds the **kidneys and epididymis** first. Other UG organs are then infected by contiguous spread from these landing sites. Seeding may be confined to the UG tract or be widely disseminated |
| **2. Ascending (retrograde)** | Much less common | Classic example: **intravesical BCG for bladder cancer**. BCG is a live, attenuated *M. bovis* vaccine. UG TB follows in **about 1% (0.9%)** of patients treated with BCG, presenting as pyelonephritis, renal abscess, ureteric obstruction, cystitis, prostatitis or epididymo-orchitis |
| **3. Contiguous spread** | Rare | TB **ignores anatomical boundaries**: **spine → psoas → kidney**; gastrointestinal (GI) TB → **enterorenal and enterovesical fistulae** |
| **4. Direct inoculation** | Exceedingly rare | **Self-inoculation** of the external genitalia from infected stool or urine; **person-to-person genital inoculation** from infected genital or oral lesions |

- **Haematogenous disease may appear soon after seeding or after a long latency**, typically **decades, up to 46 years**. This is why UG TB often presents long after, or without any history of, pulmonary disease.

*Concept —* **UG TB is usually a blood-borne disease that lands in the two best-perfused UG organs, the kidney and the epididymis, and spreads from there.** The distribution of disease in the rest of the tract follows from those two starting points.

## Instalment 2 — Clinical Manifestations and Pathological Features: Presentation, Kidney, Ureter, Bladder, Epididymis/Vas/Testes/Scrotum, Prostate and Seminal Vesicles, Urethra and Penis (including Orificial TB and Papulonecrotic Tuberculid)

#### Clinical Manifestations and Pathological Features

- **The presentation is non-specific.** Patients are often **treated repeatedly for bacterial infection or investigated for malignancy** before TB is considered.
- **Symptoms depend on the site and severity of disease.** **Renal TB can be destructive yet silent until it reaches the bladder.**
- **Asymptomatic presentation** is seen in a small minority (about 8%) in developed countries, where patients seek care earlier.
- **Constitutional symptoms (fever, weight loss, night sweats, malaise) occur in fewer than 20%.** Their absence does not exclude UG TB.
- **Lower urinary tract symptoms dominate:** up to **half have dysuria alone**, about **half have storage symptoms**, and about **one-third have haematuria and flank pain**.
- **Renal colic occurs in fewer than 10%.** It is caused by passage of **necrotic papillae, clot, stones or caseous debris** in severe pyelonephritis.
- **The hallmark laboratory finding is sterile pyuria with or without haematuria.** This occurs in **more than 90%** of patients in developing countries.

**UROGENITAL TB BY SITE — FREQUENCY, ROUTE AND SIGNATURE LESION**

| Site | Frequency in UG TB | Route | Signature lesion or sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kidney** | **80% (commonest)** | Haematogenous | Caseating cavities → **autonephrectomy** (up to one-third) |
| **Ureter** | — | Descent from the kidney | **Distal stricture at the vesicoureteric junction**; **beaded corkscrew** ureter |
| **Bladder** | — | Descent from the kidney | Patchy cystitis → **contracted "thimble" bladder** (< 20 mL) |
| **Epididymis** | **10–55%** (second commonest); **bilateral in about one-third** | Haematogenous | Starts in the **globus minor (tail)**; **posterior scrotal sinus** in up to half |
| **Vas deferens** | — | Contiguous from the epididymis | **Thickened, beaded vas** |
| **Testis** | Isolated disease rare | Contiguous from the epididymis | **Hard mass that mimics a tumour**; hydrocele in about 5% |
| **Prostate** | **22–49%** | Haematogenous or urinary | **Chronic prostatitis that does not respond to antibiotics** |
| **Seminal vesicles** | — | Through the vas or the ejaculatory ducts | **Infertility, which may be the first symptom** |
| **Urethra** | **1.9–4.5% (relatively resistant)** | Usually with prostatic disease | **Urethroscrotal fistula** |
| **Penis** | Exceedingly rare | Primary, autoinoculation or haematogenous | Ulcerating papule or plaque → cavernosal nodules |

- **What the table is for:** it shows how frequency follows route. **Haematogenous landing sites (kidney, epididymis) are commonest**; organs reached by descent or contiguity come next; the **urethra and penis are rare**.
- **The trap:** the **epididymis, not the prostate, is the commonest genital site**, and the urethra is relatively spared despite constant exposure to infected urine.

##### Kidney

- **The kidney accounts for 80% of UG TB.** Renal disease is **progressive and highly destructive**, and the pathology varies with severity.
- **Kidney as collateral damage of pulmonary or systemic TB (no UG imaging changes):**
  - **Granulomatous interstitial nephritis** caused by TB: patients with pulmonary TB and renal failure, with or without pyuria, and normal UG imaging. Biopsy shows granulomas, sometimes caseating. **Treatment can reverse the renal impairment.**
  - **Immune-complex glomerulonephritis** and **secondary amyloidosis**.
- **Miliary TB (widely disseminated disease):** a heavy haematogenous load of bacilli produces **innumerable pale granulomas about 3 mm across, like millet seeds**, studding the cortex and medulla. Miliary TB **carries a high mortality**, but the renal "milia" **usually spare renal function**.
- **Localised renal TB follows a sequence:** bacilli lodge in the **periglomerular capillaries** → parenchymal granulomas form and **coalesce** → **caseation forms cavities** → abscess, chronic pyelonephritis, **parenchymal and papillary necrosis** → **flank sinus tracts** can form.
  - Examination: **costovertebral angle tenderness**.
  - As disease advances, the **calyces become inflamed and then calcify**, causing calyceal **distortion, dilatation and stenosis**.
- **Autonephrectomy (a non-functioning kidney)** occurs in **up to one-third** of patients with UG TB. There are two types:

**AUTONEPHRECTOMY — THE TWO TYPES**

| Caseo-cavernous | Fibrotic |
|---|---|
| Viable tissue is **replaced by granulomas and cavities filled with inflammatory exudate** | **Severe scarring and calcification** |
| **With or without calcification** | **Shrunken kidney** (the "putty" kidney seen on plain film) |

- **End-stage renal failure** develops in about **7%**.
- **Squamous metaplasia of the renal pelvis** can **persist after treatment**, so there is a **long-term risk of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)**.

##### Ureter

- **Ureteric TB comes from infection descending from the kidney.** Bacilli in the urine form **mural granulomas**, and **infected calculi can descend and impact**.
- **Inflammation → scarring → stricture.** Strictures are **commonest distally, at the vesicoureteric junction**. Pan-ureteric strictures give a **"beaded corkscrew"** ureter.
- **A scarred, distorted ureter can cause both obstruction and reflux.** **Stricture-related obstruction is an important cause of renal failure in UG TB.**
- **Spontaneous ureteric perforation** proximal to a vesicoureteric junction stricture has been reported (a single case). It needed stenting, medical treatment and reconstruction.

##### Bladder

- **Bladder TB is descending infection. It begins around the ureteric orifices and spreads along the lymphatics.** Bacilli implant in the urothelium, causing **patchy cystitis**, and **ulcers** form where large granulomas coalesce.
- **The dome is most affected; the trigone and bladder neck are usually spared.** Mucosal inflammation, friability and haematuria follow.
- **After about a year of chronic inflammation and scarring, the bladder contracts:**
  - Capacity **< 100 mL** → **frequency, urgency, pain and dysuria become prominent**.
  - **"Thimble" bladder**: capacity **< 20 mL**.
- **Contracture is a late complication**, and is **commoner in the developing world** (about 12% vs 4% of UG cases), because diagnosis there is made at a more advanced stage.
- **Rare presentation: acute abdomen from spontaneous bladder perforation.** It is often recognised only at operation, before TB has been considered.

##### Epididymis, Vas Deferens, Testes and Scrotum

- **The epididymis is the second commonest site of haematogenous seeding**, after the kidney. It is involved in **10–55%** of patients with UG TB and is **bilateral in about one-third**.
- **Disease begins in the more vascular globus minor.** Epithelial granulomas → fibrosis and **obliteration of the lumen** → large caseous granulomas → **nodular epididymis**, which is **swollen or hard** on examination.
- **Granulomas can adhere to the skin and ulcerate.** A **tuberculous sinus on the posterior scrotum** develops in **up to half**.
- **Vas deferens:** **thickened and beaded** because of nodular scarring.
- **Testis:** **isolated epididymal or testicular TB is rare.** More often, epididymal disease **extends into the testis**. Granulomas replace the seminiferous epithelium and connective tissue with fibrosis, giving a **hard mass that mimics a testicular tumour**. A **hydrocele** develops in about **5%**.

##### Prostate and Seminal Vesicles

- **The prostate is involved in 22–49%.** It is reached by either route, and **the route determines the picture**:

**PROSTATIC TB — HAEMATOGENOUS VS URINARY ROUTE**

| Haematogenous | Urinary |
|---|---|
| **Peripheral lesions; urethra spared** | **Urethra involved** |
| **Asymptomatic** → calcification and a hard gland | **Resembles bacterial prostatitis** |

- **Examination:** nodules or fluctuance.
- **Suspect TB in chronic prostatitis that persists despite antibiotics.** **Quinolones are active against MTBC**, but the **short courses used for bacterial prostatitis are inadequate**. Symptoms therefore fail to resolve or **recur quickly**, and this partial response is the clue.
- **Prostatic abscess is rare**; it occurs particularly in **acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS)**.
- **Seminal vesicles:** **infertility may be the first symptom of UG TB**.
  - Routes: through the **vas** (from epididymal or testicular TB), or through the **urethra and ejaculatory ducts** (from renal, bladder or prostatic TB).
  - Pathology: mural granulomas → **luminal caseation** → **calcification**.
  - Features: **low-volume ejaculate, oligospermia, azoospermia, haemospermia**; rarely, abscess.
  - Examination: **enlarged vesicles early; hard nodules late**.

##### Urethra and Penis

- **The urethra is relatively resistant to TB** and is involved in only **1.9–4.5%**. Urethral TB usually **accompanies prostatic TB** and can present with **urethroscrotal fistulae**. Isolated urethral TB is very rare.

**PENILE TB — THE THREE FORMS**

| | Primary penile TB | Orificial TB | Papulonecrotic tuberculid (PNT) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | True infection | True infection; **rapidly necrotic** | **Hypersensitivity reaction** to MTBC antigens carried to the skin from a distant focus |
| **Origin** | Skin of the penis | **Autoinoculation from the patient's own infected stool or urine**; rarely haematogenous or lymphatic | Disseminated antigen (commonest on the glans; other skin sites also possible) |
| **Lesion** | Inflamed papule or keratotic plaque (**lupus vulgaris**) → ulcer → spreads to the corpora (**pea-sized nodules**, painless and hard, or a fungating mass) | **Painful ulcers with a pseudomembrane**, eroding deeply | **Red papules → ulcers → varioliform scarring**, recurrent |
| **Pain** | Painless nodules | **Painful** | **Can be painless** |
| **Bacilli, culture, polymerase chain reaction (PCR)** | Present | Present | **Absent: culture- and PCR-negative**; histology often inconclusive (mature granulomas often absent) |
| **Host and prognosis** | Fibrosis → penile distortion, **erectile dysfunction** | **Immunocompromised or debilitated**; marks **very advanced UG or GI TB**; **poor prognosis** | Reported from Japan, South Africa and India |
| **Mimics** | **Malignancy** | — | **Syphilis, Behçet disease, recurrent herpes simplex, balanitis, SCC** |
| **Diagnosis** | Tissue | — | **Recognition + response to empirical anti-TB treatment despite negative cultures** |

- **What the table tests:** PNT is the one form in which **negative cultures and PCR do not exclude TB**. Here **the response to treatment is the diagnostic test**.

*Concept —* **In UG TB the route of spread predicts the lesion.** Haematogenous seeding gives parenchymal disease (kidney, epididymis, peripheral prostate). Descending urinary spread gives luminal and orifice disease (distal ureteric stricture, cystitis, urethral prostatic disease). Healing by fibrosis turns an infection into an obstruction (stricture, contracted bladder, obliterated epididymis, infertility).

## Instalment 3 — Diagnosis: Principles, Culture, Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests, Urine Lipoarabinomannan, Histopathology, Screening Tests (TST, IGRA), Radiography (Plain Film, IVU, CT Urography, Retrograde/Antegrade Pyelography), Ultrasonography, MRI, Cystoscopy and Ureteroscopy; Figures 31.1–31.7

#### Diagnosis

- **In developed countries, the primary goal of work-up is to isolate MTBC in culture so that drug susceptibility can be tested.** The workable order of evidence is:
  - Culture or DNA positive → **diagnosis**.
  - If negative → **caseating granulomas on tissue** in the right clinical context support TB.
  - If that is also absent → **a compatible clinical picture + probable exposure + response to empirical treatment**.
- **Up to one-fifth of UG TB coexists with pulmonary TB**, so **always look for pulmonary disease**.

##### Culture

- **Gold standard: urine acid-fast bacilli (AFB) culture.**
- **Sampling sequence:** **first-void (most concentrated) urine** → **3–5 samples on consecutive days** → **culture immediately**, because prolonged exposure to acidic urine inhibits mycobacterial growth. **Culture any biopsy or operative tissue as well.**
- **Sensitivity depends on how the test is done and on how the organism is shed:**
  - **Ideal technique:** up to **80–90%**.
  - **Real-world practice**, compounded by **intermittent shedding of bacilli**: as low as **10%**.
- **Ziehl–Neelsen stain of urine:** sensitivity **< 50%**.

**MYCOBACTERIAL CULTURE SYSTEMS**

| System | Medium | Time to detection | Setting and notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Löwenstein–Jensen (LJ)** | **Solid, egg-based** | **4–6 weeks** | **Medium of choice in developing countries**: cheapest, needs no special equipment; laborious |
| **Middlebrook 7H10** | **Solid, agar-based, transparent** | About **1 week sooner than LJ** | Developed countries; more expensive |
| **BACTEC Mycobacteria Growth Indicator Tube (MGIT)** | **Liquid**, fully automated; **fluorescence quenching** | As little as **10 days** | Developed countries; **first-line susceptibility testing is done in-house** on the MGIT |
| Semi-automated radiometric | Liquid | — | Alternative |

- **Rule:** **culture on at least one solid medium alongside the liquid system** to maximise yield.
- **Susceptibility testing:** any of the culture methods can be used. **First-line drugs are tested in-house on MGIT**; **second-line drugs and molecular resistance sequencing are done in reference laboratories**.

##### Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests

- **Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) give results in 1–2 days** and can detect MTBC when the **bacillary load is too low** for culture to succeed.
- **Urine contains natural amplification inhibitors**, which lower sensitivity in non-respiratory specimens. Sensitivity also depends on the specimen, the target sequence and the protocol.
- **GeneXpert MTB/RIF (on the market since 2010):** a self-contained, automated system (sputum processing → DNA extraction → amplification) that **detects MTBC and rifampicin resistance at the same time**.
  - **Rifampicin resistance is a surrogate for multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB)**, because **more than 90% of rifampicin-resistant strains are also isoniazid (INH)-resistant**.
  - **MDR-TB = resistance to both INH and rifampicin.**
- **Performance in urine:** systematic reviews show **good sensitivity and specificity for GeneXpert against urine AFB culture**. The underlying studies are small and heterogeneous, so the estimates are imprecise. NAAT on **epididymal biopsy tissue** performed well in a single study.
- **Role of NAAT:**
  - **United States: an adjunct only.** It **does not replace culture**, and a **negative NAAT does not exclude extrapulmonary TB**, because there is not enough high-quality sensitivity data.
  - **WHO: GeneXpert MTB/RIF is part of the initial work-up of all patients with TB.** In developing countries, **cost and equipment** are the barriers.
  - **NAATs cannot monitor treatment response.** **Dead organisms still shed nucleic acid**, so the test stays positive despite adequate treatment. **Culture is used for monitoring.**

##### Urine Lipoarabinomannan Assay

- **Lipoarabinomannan (LAM)** is a **glycolipid of the mycobacterial cell wall** found in urine, sputum and serum. Detection methods range from enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to the **point-of-care lateral-flow urine assay (LF-LAM)**, which needs **no laboratory equipment** and gives a **result in under 30 minutes**.
- **Performance in people living with HIV (PLHIV):** a systematic review showed **low sensitivity and high specificity**. Sensitivity was somewhat better when TB symptoms were present. **A positive result is useful; a negative result is not reassuring.**
- **WHO (2019) recommends adding LF-LAM for:**
  - **PLHIV with TB symptoms**
  - **People with advanced HIV disease who are unwell**
  - **PLHIV with a CD4 count < 200 cells/mm³**
- **United States:** broad access to other diagnostics limits LF-LAM mainly to academic centres.

##### Histopathology

- **Biopsy is used when urine cultures are negative.** Mycobacteria are often **not seen in tissue**, but **caseating granulomas in the right clinical context** support the diagnosis.
- **Histology is consistent with TB in only a minority of UG TB cases** (about one-third in developed countries and about one-fifth globally). **A negative biopsy does not exclude TB.**
- **Epididymal nodules:** **fine-needle aspiration cytology is diagnostic in about two-thirds**.
- **Adding a TB NAAT to tissue specimens increases diagnostic sensitivity.**

##### Screening Tests

- **The tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon-γ release assays (IGRAs) cannot distinguish latent infection from active disease.**
  - Both are approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and widely used, but they have **limited value in diagnosing active disease**. **A positive test does not rule TB disease in; a negative test does not rule it out.**
  - **Their proper use is screening for TB infection.**
- **Preferred screening test:** **IGRA** for persons **≥ 5 years born in TB-endemic countries**. **TST remains an acceptable alternative.**

###### Tuberculin Skin Test (Purified Protein Derivative, Mantoux Test)

- **Principle:** it detects an **existing cellular (delayed-type hypersensitivity) response** to MTBC antigens.
- **Technique:** tuberculin (sterile protein extract of *M. tuberculosis*) is injected **intradermally into the volar forearm** → **induration is read at 48–72 hours**.
- **Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) cut-offs are risk-stratified:**

**TST — POSITIVITY THRESHOLDS BY RISK GROUP (CDC)**

| Induration | Positive in |
|---|---|
| **≥ 5 mm** | **Recent contact** with a patient with TB; **fibrotic chest radiograph changes consistent with old TB**; **immunosuppressed** |
| **≥ 10 mm** | **Immigrants from high-prevalence countries**; **residents of or workers in high-risk institutions**; **people who inject drugs**; **comorbidities that raise the risk of active TB** |
| **≥ 15 mm** | **General population with no risk factors** |

- **The logic of the table:** **the higher the pre-test probability, the lower the cut-off**. A small reaction matters in someone likely to have been infected or unable to mount a response. The trap is to apply one cut-off to everyone.

- **TST advantages:** **cheap, no laboratory needed, easy to perform** (though staff must be trained to place and read it).
- **TST disadvantages:**
  - **Not specific for MTBC:** **BCG vaccination and non-tuberculous mycobacteria** can cause a positive reaction.
  - **Needs a second visit** for reading.
  - **False negatives in 10–25% of active TB.**
- **In UG TB the TST is usually positive.**

###### Interferon-γ Release Assays

- **Principle:** a blood test that measures **IFN-γ released by sensitised T cells in response to MTBC-specific antigens**. In effect it is an **in vitro, MTBC-specific TST**.
- **Its antigens are absent from all BCG strains and most non-tuberculous mycobacteria**, so **BCG vaccination does not cause a false positive**. This is the key advantage over TST.
- **Result in about 24 hours**, from a **single visit**.

**TST VS IGRA**

| | TST | IGRA |
|---|---|---|
| **Format** | Intradermal, in vivo | Blood, in vitro |
| **Visits** | **Two** (read at 48–72 h) | **One** (result about 24 h) |
| **Affected by BCG?** | **Yes: false positive** | **No** |
| **Affected by non-tuberculous mycobacteria?** | Yes | Mostly no |
| **Laboratory** | Not needed | Needed |
| **Latent vs active** | Cannot distinguish | Cannot distinguish |

**THE TWO IGRAs AVAILABLE IN THE UNITED STATES**

| | QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus (QFT-Plus, fourth generation, 2017) | T-SPOT.TB |
|---|---|---|
| **Sample** | **Whole blood in four tubes**: ESAT-6/CFP-10; proprietary antigens that **activate CD8 T cells**; negative control; positive control | **Separated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)** in antibody-coated wells with ESAT-6 and CFP-10 |
| **Incubation** | 16–24 h in the collection tubes | — |
| **Read-out** | **IFN-γ concentration in plasma by ELISA** | **Enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISPOT)**: **spots (IFN-γ-secreting cells) counted manually** |
| **Results** | Positive, negative or **indeterminate** | Positive, negative or **borderline** |
| **Ease** | Easier | **Harder to perform** |

*Concept —* **Culture proves disease; NAAT and LAM speed it up; TST and IGRA only prove exposure.** Every test in this section is placed by what it can and cannot do: only culture gives susceptibility and tracks treatment, and only immune-response tests are blind to the difference between latent and active TB.

##### Radiography

- **Imaging is often the first test to suggest TB** as the cause of a UG disorder. **Choose the modality by site and by the clinical picture.**

###### Plain Radiography (Kidney–Ureter–Bladder Film)

- **Calcification is frequent**, seen in about **a quarter to two-fifths** of patients (24–44%). The pattern evolves with the disease:
  - **Early:** faint punctate parenchymal calcifications.
  - **Progressive:** **globular calcification** corresponding to a tuberculous mass (Fig. 31.1).
  - **Papillary necrosis:** **triangular, ring-like calcifications** in the collecting system.
  - **Fibrotic autonephrectomy:** a small, shrunken, calcified **"cement" or "putty" kidney** with **calcific rims outlining the individual lobes**. **This lobar pattern is pathognomonic of end-stage renal TB.**
- **Infected stones** take odd shapes in a deformed, fibrosed pelvis. An **upward-pointing arrowhead stone** suggests a pelvis **"hiked up" by scar contraction**.
- **Ureteric calcification:**

| TB | Schistosomiasis |
|---|---|
| **Intraluminal** | **Mural** |

- **Bladder wall calcification is uncommon** except late, with a contracted bladder. **Prostatic and seminal vesicle calcification** is seen in about **10%**.
- **Findings beyond the urinary tract:** **vertebral body erosions** and **calcified psoas cold abscesses**.
- **Every patient being evaluated for UG TB needs a chest radiograph** to exclude coexistent **infectious pulmonary TB**.

###### Intravenous Urography

- **Intravenous urography (IVU) was formerly the gold standard for early renal TB** and produced most of the classic signs. It is still sometimes called the preferred first test in some regions, but **computed tomography (CT), CT urography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasound (US) have essentially replaced it**.
- **Early signs:** **loss of sharpness and irregular urothelial margins**; **"moth-eaten" calyces**.
- **Filling defects** from a **tuberculoma rupturing into a calyx** or from **papillary necrosis**. **Medullary cavities communicating with the collecting system**.
- **"Phantom calyx":** a stenosed calyx or infundibulum means no contrast is excreted, so the calyx is **absent where it should be**.
- **Ureter:**
  - **Pipestem ureter:** rigid, calcified, straight, tubular and **aperistaltic**.
  - **Beaded corkscrew ureter:** nodular fibrosis along the whole ureter.
  - **Both are highly suggestive of TB, especially when kidney or bladder changes are also present.**
- **Non-functioning kidney** (autonephrectomy); **fibrosed, contracted bladder** (Fig. 31.2).
- **Perinephric abscess** is suggested by **loss of respiratory movement of the kidney** or **ureteric displacement**.
- **Commonest IVU findings are obstructive changes from scarring**, not the classic erosive signs:
  - **Calyceal obliteration, infundibular narrowing, hydrocalycosis, segmental or total hydronephrosis, hydroureter** (Figs. 31.3, 31.4)
  - **Calyceal dilatation and distortion → a "cloverleaf" pattern**
  - **Vesicoureteric junction obstruction** from tuberculous cystitis or **distal-third ureteric stricture** (Fig. 31.5)
  - **Kerr's kink:** a **"hiked-up" renal pelvis with sharp angulation of the pelviureteric junction**

###### Computed Tomography With Urography

- **CT urography is the most frequently used modality in developed countries** and has replaced IVU there.
- **It shows the same features as KUB and IVU (calcification, scarring, obstruction), with greater sensitivity:**
  - **More sensitive than KUB for calcification** and for **thickening of the collecting system wall**.
  - Multidetector scanners detect **lesions of 3–4 mm**.
  - **With intravenous contrast, it assesses function** across the excretory phases.
- **Granulomas:**
  - **Small:** **hypo-attenuating, with little or no enhancement**.
  - **Large:** **mass-like with peripheral enhancement**, which can **mimic renal cell carcinoma**.
- **Particularly useful in complicated or extensive disease:** perinephric and psoas abscesses; lymph nodes, vertebrae, spleen and liver; **prostate and seminal vesicles** (enlargement, necrosis, cavitation, abscess, calcification).
- **CT compared with IVU:**
  - **Disadvantages of CT:** it gives a **higher radiation dose** and is **less sensitive for the earliest changes** (minimal urothelial thickening, subtle papillary necrosis). **For these, IVU may still be preferred in resource-limited settings.**
  - **Advantages of contrast CT:** it shows **the parenchyma, tissue oedema and hypoperfusion**.

**FIG. 31.6** CT with oral contrast in bilateral TB: the **right kidney is hydronephrotic from infundibular stenosis but retains good function**; the **left kidney is an end-stage, non-functioning, atrophic, calcified kidney**. The figure illustrates the **two end-points side by side**: **obstruction that can be salvaged vs autonephrectomy**.

###### Retrograde and Antegrade Pyelography

- **Both have been replaced by CT urography.** They remain useful **when IVU or CT is impossible because of renal insufficiency or contrast allergy**, to delineate distorted anatomy.
- **With IVU, they show whether cavities are obstructive or non-obstructive, and whether they communicate with the collecting system.**

##### Ultrasonography

- **Limited diagnostic role:** US findings are **non-specific**, and visualisation is poorer than on CT.
- **Its advantages are no radiation and low cost.** This makes it the **modality for children and pregnancy**.
- **Uses:**
  - **Scrotum:** diffusely enlarged testis or epididymis with **homogeneous or heterogeneous hypoechoic areas**, **complex collections, hydrocele**.
  - **Transrectal US:** **thickened prostate and seminal vesicles**.
  - **Kidney:** abscesses and cavities. **Septated cystic lesions suggest chronic infection.** Calcifications are **echogenic with distal acoustic shadowing**.
  - **Restricted respiratory movement of the kidney suggests a perinephric or psoas abscess.**
  - Abdomen: **ascites, lymphadenopathy, omental caking**.
- **Main use: following hydronephrosis during medical treatment.** **Fibrosis during healing can make obstruction worse**, so the kidney must be watched while the infection is being cured.

##### Magnetic Resonance Imaging

- **MRI is used less often than contrast-enhanced CT.** Like US it avoids radiation, so it is **useful in children and pregnancy**. It finds much the same abnormalities as CT, but its **better tissue resolution** can show **a single granuloma**.
- **Signal characteristics:**
  - **Small lesions:** **hypointense on both T1 and T2**.
  - **Larger lesions:** **centrally hyperintense on T2** because of central cellularity.
  - **Large lesions can mimic malignancy**, and the two cannot always be distinguished.
- **Magnetic resonance urogram (MRU):** **more sensitive than IVU for urothelial thickening and caliectasis**.
- **Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI):**
  - Distinguishes **hydronephrosis from pyonephrosis**.
  - **Monitors renal fibrosis:** the **apparent diffusion coefficient falls as fibrosis increases**, which allows staging and assessment of treatment effect.
- **Cine-MRU and dynamic MRU** assess **ureteric peristalsis**.
- **Caution with gadolinium in renal failure** because of the risk of **nephrogenic systemic fibrosis**.

| Modality | Current role | Signature signs and specific use |
|---|---|---|
| **KUB** | Frequently shows calcification | **Lobar "putty" kidney (pathognomonic of end stage)**; **intraluminal** ureteric calcification; arrowhead stone |
| **Chest radiograph** | **Mandatory in all patients** | Coexistent pulmonary TB |
| **IVU** | **Former gold standard**; still used in resource-limited settings for early disease | Moth-eaten calyx, **phantom calyx**, **pipestem** and **corkscrew** ureter, cloverleaf calyces, **Kerr's kink** |
| **CT urography** | **Most used in developed countries** | Calcification (more sensitive than KUB), granuloma that mimics renal cell carcinoma, abscesses, disease beyond the urinary tract |

| **US** | Children and pregnancy; scrotum; transrectal | **Serial monitoring of hydronephrosis during treatment** |
| **MRI / MRU with DWI** | Children and pregnancy; lesion characterisation | Single granuloma; **DWI: pyonephrosis vs hydronephrosis, grading of fibrosis** |

##### Cystoscopy and Ureteroscopy

- **Endoscopy has a limited diagnostic role.** It gives **direct vision, but the findings are non-specific**:
  - Hyperaemia, erosion, ulceration, granulomatous masses, irregular ureteric orifices.
  - **Ulcers can mimic malignancy.**
- **A "golf-hole" ureteric orifice suggests TB.** When one is found, **image or scope the upper tract**.
- **Biopsy when possible, especially if malignancy is possible.**
- **Why biopsy even though urine culture is diagnostic:** culture results **may not come back quickly enough**, and in **culture-negative patients bladder biopsy has a moderate yield** (19–52% sensitivity).

*Concept —* **The imaging signs of UG TB are the pathology of fibrosis made visible.** Destruction (moth-eaten calyx, cavities, calcification) is followed by healing that scars and shortens: phantom calyx, Kerr's kink, pipestem ureter, golf-hole orifice and thimble bladder. That is why the commonest finding is obstruction, and why hydronephrosis must be watched during treatment.

## The numbers to lock in (Instalments 1–3)

**Key facts, figures, thresholds**
- One-quarter of the world is latently infected. In 2021: about 10.6 million new cases and 1.6 million deaths.
- UG TB: > 90% of cases are in developing countries. It affects 15–20% of pulmonary TB patients in developing countries vs 2–10% in developed countries.
- UG site ranking: second extrapulmonary site worldwide (after lymph nodes); third in the United States (after pleural and lymphatic). About two-thirds of patients are male.
- The cellular immune response can take up to 12 weeks → spread through the lymphatics and blood in the meantime.
- Controlled (latent): 90–95%. Primary progression: < 5% within the first year. Lifetime reactivation: 5–15%.
- UG TB latency is up to 46 years.
- BCG instillation → UG TB in about 1%.
- Constitutional symptoms < 20%. Renal colic < 10%. Sterile pyuria ± haematuria > 90% (developing world).
- Kidney 80%. Autonephrectomy up to one-third. End-stage renal failure about 7%.
- Bladder capacity < 100 mL → symptomatic. Thimble bladder < 20 mL. Contracture after about 1 year of inflammation. Contracture commoner in the developing world (12% vs 4%).
- Epididymis 10–55%, bilateral about one-third, posterior scrotal sinus up to half. Hydrocele about 5%.
- Prostate 22–49%. Urethra 1.9–4.5%. Prostatic and seminal vesicle calcification about 10%.
- Coexistent pulmonary TB in up to one-fifth → chest radiograph for every patient.
- Urine culture: 3–5 first-void samples on consecutive days, cultured immediately. Sensitivity 80–90% when done properly vs about 10% in real-world practice. Ziehl–Neelsen < 50%.
- Culture times: LJ 4–6 weeks; 7H10 about 1 week sooner; MGIT as little as 10 days. NAAT 1–2 days. LF-LAM < 30 minutes. IGRA 24 hours. TST read at 48–72 hours.
- More than 90% of rifampicin-resistant strains are INH-resistant → rifampicin resistance is the marker for MDR-TB.
- TST cut-offs: 5 / 10 / 15 mm by risk group. TST false negatives 10–25% in active TB.
- Sensitivity order: T-SPOT.TB > TST > third-generation QFT.
- WHO LF-LAM indications: PLHIV with symptoms, advanced HIV disease, or CD4 < 200 cells/mm³.
- KUB calcification in 24–44%. CT detects lesions of 3–4 mm. Miliary granulomas about 3 mm.

**Gold standards, commonest, first-line**
- **Diagnostic gold standard:** urine AFB culture.
- **Former imaging gold standard:** IVU. **Commonest modality now (developed countries):** CT urography.
- **Commonest species:** *M. tuberculosis*, then *M. bovis*.
- **Commonest UG site:** kidney. **Commonest genital site:** epididymis.
- **Commonest IVU finding:** obstructive change.
- **Commonest site of ureteric stricture:** distal ureter / vesicoureteric junction.
- **Preferred screening test:** IGRA (persons ≥ 5 years from endemic countries).
- **Culture practice:** at least one solid medium alongside liquid culture.
- **Imaging in children and pregnancy:** US or MRI.

**Eponyms and named signs**
- **Kerr's kink:** hiked-up renal pelvis with sharp pelviureteric junction angulation.
- **Langhans giant cells:** the multinucleated giant cells at the centre of the granuloma.
- **Koch:** isolated the tubercle bacillus on 24 March 1882 (World TB Day).
- **Ziehl–Neelsen:** acid-fast stain; < 50% sensitive in urine.
- **Löwenstein–Jensen:** egg-based solid medium, 4–6 weeks.
- **Mantoux:** intradermal TST.
- **Named signs:** **putty / cement kidney** (lobar calcification, pathognomonic of end stage); **phantom calyx**; **moth-eaten calyx**; **cloverleaf calyces**; **pipestem ureter**; **beaded corkscrew ureter**; **golf-hole ureteric orifice**; **thimble bladder**; **millet-seed (miliary) granulomas**; **lupus vulgaris** (penile plaque).

**Dates**
- 24 March 1882: Koch's presentation of the tubercle bacillus (World TB Day).
- 1992: peak of the United States resurgence.
- 2010: GeneXpert MTB/RIF introduced.
- 2017: QFT-Plus, the fourth-generation assay (adds CD8-stimulating antigens).
- 2019: WHO LF-LAM recommendation.

**Classic traps**
- *M. bovis* (and therefore BCG) is **innately resistant to pyrazinamide**.
- **Sterile pyuria** → think TB. A negative routine culture does not mean no infection.
- **Quinolone-responsive prostatitis that relapses** → TB that has been partially treated.
- **TST and IGRA cannot diagnose active disease or exclude it.** They screen for infection.
- **BCG gives a false-positive TST but not a false-positive IGRA.**
- **NAAT cannot monitor treatment**, because dead bacilli still shed DNA. **A negative NAAT does not exclude extrapulmonary TB.**
- **Ureteric calcification: TB is intraluminal, schistosomiasis is mural.**
- **Bladder TB: dome affected, trigone and neck spared.**
- **The epididymis (not the prostate) is the commonest genital site.** Disease starts in the **globus minor**.
- **PNT is culture- and PCR-negative.** Diagnosis is by response to empirical treatment.
- **Miliary renal TB spares renal function**, whereas granulomatous interstitial nephritis causes renal failure with normal imaging.
- **Healing fibrosis can worsen obstruction during treatment** → monitor with US.
- **A large granuloma mimics renal cell carcinoma** (CT) or malignancy (MRI). **Epididymo-orchitis mimics a testicular tumour.**
- **Gadolinium in renal failure** → nephrogenic systemic fibrosis.
- **Pulmonary TB is infectious; isolated UG TB generally is not**, except through aerosol-generating procedures or inoculation.
