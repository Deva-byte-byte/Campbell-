
### UROGENITAL TUBERCULOSIS

#### Chapter preamble

- **Tuberculosis (TB) can involve any organ**, including the urogenital (UG) tract. Untreated UG TB causes **irreversible tissue destruction**, ending in **renal failure and infertility**, so TB belongs in the differential diagnosis of UG disorders.
- **TB is the second "great imitator"** (after syphilis); mimicking other diseases delays diagnosis and treatment.
- **As TB becomes rarer in industrialised nations, diagnosis depends on clinical suspicion**: clinicians see it less, so must think of it deliberately.
- **Terminology:** "urogenital TB" is preferred over "genitourinary TB" because the **urinary tract is involved far more often than the genital tract**.

#### History

- **Mycobacterium tuberculosis has co-evolved with humans.** A progenitor (*M. prototuberculosis*) may have infected early hominids > 3 million years ago; a *Homo erectus* skeleton about 500,000 years old has TB-consistent bony lesions.
- **Oldest microbiological confirmation:** Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex DNA in **9000-year-old Neolithic skeletons** (Eastern Mediterranean). Tubercle bacilli shown microscopically and molecularly in **Egyptian mummies (c. 3000 BCE)**.
- **Written descriptions** come from ancient East Asia, the pre-Columbian Americas and classical Greece and Rome, continuing through modern history.
- **Epidemic peak: 18th and 19th centuries** in Europe and North America; "consumption" caused up to **one-quarter of deaths in the Industrial Age**.
- **Turning point, 24 March 1882:** **Robert Koch** presented the first isolation and identification of the tubercle bacillus; **24 March is World Tuberculosis Day**.

#### Microbiology

- **TB is caused by the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC):** closely related **acid-fast bacteria**.

**MTBC SPECIES BY HOST RANGE**

| Host range | Species |
|---|---|
| **Humans only** | *M. tuberculosis*, *M. africanum* |
| **Humans and other mammals** | *M. bovis*, *M. canettii*, *M. microti*, *M. caprae*, *M. orygis*, *M. pinnipedii* |
| **Animals only (so far)** | *M. mungi*, *M. suricattae* |

- **Commonest human isolate worldwide: *M. tuberculosis*, then *M. bovis*.** Regionally, **in West Africa *M. africanum*** causes a large share.
- **"M. tuberculosis" is often used, inaccurately, for the whole complex.**
- **The species are clinically indistinguishable but differ in drug susceptibility**: classically, ***M. bovis* is innately resistant to pyrazinamide**, a first-line agent.
- The table sorts species by host. The trap is assuming uniform drug susceptibility: **species matters for treatment, not for the clinical picture** — directly relevant to bacille Calmette–Guérin (BCG, derived from *M. bovis*) infection after intravesical therapy.

*Concept —* **the complex is one disease clinically but several organisms pharmacologically**: identify the species because it changes the drug regimen, not because it changes the presentation.

#### Epidemiology

- **Global (World Health Organization, WHO):** **one-quarter of the world's population has latent MTBC infection**. 2021: about **10.6 million new active cases and 1.6 million deaths**. Deaths have fallen steadily since 2000; **TB mortality down 45% since 1990**.
- **Obstacles to control:** the **human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) epidemic in sub-Saharan Africa**, **global rise in obesity and diabetes**, and **multidrug and extensive drug resistance**.
- **United States:** about **8300 active cases in 2022 (2.5 per 100,000)**, declining steadily since the **1980s resurgence (peak 1992)**. Reported cases fell during COVID-19 in 2020 and were back to pre-pandemic levels by 2022.
  - **Disproportionate burden in people born outside the United States:** incidence **16 times** that of US-born people.
- **The proportion of pulmonary TB patients with UG disease depends on setting:**

**UROGENITAL TB BY SETTING**

| | Developing countries | Developed countries |
|---|---|---|
| **Share of global UG TB** | **> 90%** | < 10% |
| **UG TB as a proportion of pulmonary TB** | **15–20%** | **2–10%** |
| **Rank among extrapulmonary sites** | **Second** (after lymph nodes) | **Third** in the United States (after pleural and lymphatic TB); about one-quarter of extrapulmonary cases |

- **Sex and age:** **about two-thirds male**; **mainly adults**, though reported in children as young as 2 years.

*Concept —* **UG TB burden tracks the TB burden, and diagnostic delay tracks resources.** The developing world has more cases, a larger UG share and more advanced disease at presentation, and this later shows up in the rates of contracted bladder.

#### Infection, Host Immune Response and Transmission

- **Entry is by inhalation of cough-generated aerosols** (direct soft-tissue inoculation is rare) → **alveolar macrophages phagocytose the bacilli**, with three possible outcomes:
  - **Cleared:** macrophages kill the bacilli; **no infection and no adaptive immune response**.
  - **Established:** bacilli escape killing and replicate in macrophages. **Up to 12 weeks may pass before a cellular immune response is detectable**; meanwhile bacilli spread **via lymphatics to the hilar nodes, then via the bloodstream to distant organs** — this is how the kidney and epididymis are seeded.
- **Granuloma formation is how the host contains infection:**
  - **Infected macrophages secrete cytokines**: interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-12, IL-1β, tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α).
  - **Structure:** a **centre of foamy macrophages, epithelioid cells and multinucleated (Langhans) giant cells** within a **lymphocyte cuff**.
  - **Antigen presentation activates T cells**, which secrete IL-2, TNF-α and, **most importantly, interferon-γ (IFN-γ)**, which maintains the granuloma and drives killing of infected macrophages and bacilli.
  - **If killing fails, the granuloma sequesters viable bacilli**, which become **dormant**.

**LATENT INFECTION VS ACTIVE DISEASE**

| | Latent TB infection | Active TB disease |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency after infection** | **90–95%** controlled at this stage | **< 5%** progress **within the first year** (primary progression) |
| **Pathology** | **Scarring (cicatrisation), granuloma calcification** | Renewed replication → **caseation (characteristic lesion)** → bacillary release |
| **Later risk** | **Lifetime reactivation risk 5–15%**, higher with comorbidity | — |
| **Infectious?** | No | **Pulmonary disease: yes** (respiratory aerosols to close contacts) |

- **Reactivation triggers (balance shifts towards the pathogen):** old age, renal failure, diabetes mellitus, malnutrition, HIV infection, other immunosuppression.
- **Isolated extrapulmonary disease, including UG TB, is generally not infectious**, except by **direct skin inoculation or aerosol-generating procedures on infected fluids or tissues** — relevant in theatre.
- **The latent/active split is a simplification.** TB is a **dynamic spectrum** — contained infection → subclinical disease → progressively infectious disease — and a person can **move in either direction** as host immunity (comorbidity) or the bacilli (treatment of latent infection) change.

*Concept —* **TB outcome is a balance between host and pathogen, and the granuloma is where that balance is held.** Latency, reactivation and the whole spectrum are the granuloma holding, failing or re-forming as host immunity changes, and IFN-γ is the cytokine that holds it.

#### Development of Urogenital Disease

**ROUTES TO UROGENITAL TB**

| Route | Frequency | Mechanism and examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Haematogenous** | **Principal route** | Seeds **kidneys and epididymis** first; other UG organs then infected by contiguous spread from these sites. Seeding may be confined to the UG tract or widely disseminated |
| **2. Ascending (retrograde)** | Much less common | Classically **intravesical BCG for bladder cancer** (live, attenuated *M. bovis* vaccine): UG TB in **about 1% (0.9%)** of treated patients, as pyelonephritis, renal abscess, ureteric obstruction, cystitis, prostatitis or epididymo-orchitis |
| **3. Contiguous spread** | Rare | TB **ignores anatomical boundaries**: **spine → psoas → kidney**; gastrointestinal (GI) TB → **enterorenal and enterovesical fistulae** |
| **4. Direct inoculation** | Exceedingly rare | **Self-inoculation** of external genitalia from infected stool or urine; **person-to-person genital inoculation** from infected genital or oral lesions |

- **Haematogenous disease may appear soon after seeding or after long latency**, typically **decades, up to 46 years** — hence UG TB often presents long after, or without any history of, pulmonary disease.

*Concept —* **UG TB is usually a blood-borne disease that lands in the two best-perfused UG organs, the kidney and the epididymis, and spreads from there.** The distribution of disease in the rest of the tract follows from those two starting points.



#### Clinical Manifestations and Pathological Features

- **Presentation is non-specific**: patients are often **treated repeatedly for bacterial infection or investigated for malignancy** before TB is considered.
- **Symptoms depend on site and severity.** **Renal TB can be destructive yet silent until it reaches the bladder.**
- **Asymptomatic** in a small minority (about 8%) in developed countries, where patients present earlier.
- **Constitutional symptoms (fever, weight loss, night sweats, malaise) in < 20%**; their absence does not exclude UG TB.
- **Lower urinary tract symptoms dominate:** up to **half dysuria alone**, about **half storage symptoms**, about **one-third haematuria and flank pain**.
- **Renal colic in < 10%**, from passage of **necrotic papillae, clot, stones or caseous debris** in severe pyelonephritis.
- **Hallmark laboratory finding: sterile pyuria ± haematuria**, in **> 90%** in developing countries.

**UROGENITAL TB BY SITE — FREQUENCY, ROUTE AND SIGNATURE LESION**

| Site | Frequency in UG TB | Route | Signature lesion or sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kidney** | **80% (commonest)** | Haematogenous | Caseating cavities → **autonephrectomy** (up to one-third) |
| **Ureter** | — | Descent from kidney | **Distal stricture at the vesicoureteric junction**; **beaded corkscrew** ureter |
| **Bladder** | — | Descent from kidney | Patchy cystitis → **contracted "thimble" bladder** (< 20 mL) |
| **Epididymis** | **10–55%** (second commonest); **bilateral in about one-third** | Haematogenous | Starts in **globus minor (tail)**; **posterior scrotal sinus** in up to half |
| **Vas deferens** | — | Contiguous from epididymis | **Thickened, beaded vas** |
| **Testis** | Isolated disease rare | Contiguous from epididymis | **Hard mass mimicking tumour**; hydrocele in about 5% |
| **Prostate** | **22–49%** | Haematogenous or urinary | **Chronic prostatitis unresponsive to antibiotics** |
| **Seminal vesicles** | — | Via vas or ejaculatory ducts | **Infertility, possibly the first symptom** |
| **Urethra** | **1.9–4.5% (relatively resistant)** | Usually with prostatic disease | **Urethroscrotal fistula** |
| **Penis** | Exceedingly rare | Primary, autoinoculation or haematogenous | Ulcerating papule or plaque → cavernosal nodules |

- **What the table is for:** frequency follows route. **Haematogenous landing sites (kidney, epididymis) are commonest**; organs reached by descent or contiguity next; **urethra and penis rare**.
- **The trap:** the **epididymis, not the prostate, is the commonest genital site**, and the urethra is relatively spared despite constant exposure to infected urine.

##### Kidney

- **Renal disease is progressive and highly destructive**; pathology varies with severity.
- **Kidney as collateral damage of pulmonary or systemic TB (no UG imaging changes):**
  - **TB granulomatous interstitial nephritis:** pulmonary TB with renal failure, ± pyuria, normal UG imaging; biopsy shows granulomas, sometimes caseating. **Treatment can reverse the renal impairment.**
  - **Immune-complex glomerulonephritis** and **secondary amyloidosis**.
- **Miliary TB (widely disseminated):** a heavy haematogenous bacillary load gives **innumerable pale granulomas about 3 mm across, like millet seeds**, studding cortex and medulla. **High mortality**, but the renal "milia" **usually spare renal function**.
- **Localised renal TB sequence:** bacilli lodge in **periglomerular capillaries** → parenchymal granulomas **coalesce** → **caseation forms cavities** → abscess, chronic pyelonephritis, **parenchymal and papillary necrosis** → **flank sinus tracts**.
  - Examination: **costovertebral angle tenderness**.
  - With advancing disease the **calyces become inflamed, then calcify**, causing calyceal **distortion, dilatation and stenosis**.
- **Autonephrectomy (non-functioning kidney)** has two types:

**AUTONEPHRECTOMY — THE TWO TYPES**

| Caseo-cavernous | Fibrotic |
|---|---|
| Viable tissue **replaced by granulomas and exudate-filled cavities** | **Severe scarring and calcification** |
| **± calcification** | **Shrunken kidney** ("putty" kidney on plain film) |

- **End-stage renal failure** in about **7%**.
- **Squamous metaplasia of the renal pelvis** can **persist after treatment**, giving a **long-term risk of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)**.

##### Ureter

- **Ureteric TB descends from the kidney:** urinary bacilli form **mural granulomas**, and **infected calculi can descend and impact**.
- **Inflammation → scarring → stricture**, **commonest distally at the vesicoureteric junction**; pan-ureteric strictures give a **"beaded corkscrew"** ureter.
- **A scarred, distorted ureter can both obstruct and reflux.** **Stricture-related obstruction is an important cause of renal failure in UG TB.**
- **Spontaneous ureteric perforation** proximal to a vesicoureteric junction stricture (single case) needed stenting, medical treatment and reconstruction.

##### Bladder

- **Bladder TB is descending infection, beginning around the ureteric orifices and spreading along lymphatics.** Urothelial implantation causes **patchy cystitis**; **ulcers** form where large granulomas coalesce.
- **The dome is most affected; trigone and bladder neck usually spared.** Mucosal inflammation, friability and haematuria follow.
- **After about a year of chronic inflammation and scarring, the bladder contracts:**
  - Capacity **< 100 mL** → **prominent frequency, urgency, pain and dysuria**.
  - **"Thimble" bladder**: capacity **< 20 mL**.
- **Contracture is late** and **commoner in the developing world** (about 12% vs 4% of UG cases) because diagnosis there is made later.
- **Rare: acute abdomen from spontaneous bladder perforation**, often recognised only at operation, before TB is considered.

##### Epididymis, Vas Deferens, Testes and Scrotum

- **Second commonest site of haematogenous seeding, after the kidney.**
- **Disease begins in the more vascular globus minor:** epithelial granulomas → fibrosis and **luminal obliteration** → large caseous granulomas → **nodular epididymis**, **swollen or hard** on examination.
- **Granulomas can adhere to the skin and ulcerate**, forming the posterior scrotal sinus.
- **Vas deferens:** thickened and beaded from **nodular scarring**.
- **Testis:** more often than isolated disease, epididymal TB **extends into the testis**; granulomas replace seminiferous epithelium and connective tissue with fibrosis, producing the tumour-like mass.

##### Prostate and Seminal Vesicles

- **The prostate is reached by either route, and the route determines the picture:**

**PROSTATIC TB — HAEMATOGENOUS VS URINARY ROUTE**

| Haematogenous | Urinary |
|---|---|
| **Peripheral lesions; urethra spared** | **Urethra involved** |
| **Asymptomatic** → calcification, hard gland | **Resembles bacterial prostatitis** |

- **Examination:** nodules or fluctuance.
- **Suspect TB in chronic prostatitis persisting despite antibiotics.** **Quinolones are active against MTBC**, but the **short courses for bacterial prostatitis are inadequate**, so symptoms fail to resolve or **recur quickly** — the partial response is the clue.
- **Prostatic abscess is rare**, particularly in **acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS)**.
- **Seminal vesicles:**
  - Routes: via the **vas** (from epididymal or testicular TB), or via the **urethra and ejaculatory ducts** (from renal, bladder or prostatic TB).
  - Pathology: mural granulomas → **luminal caseation** → **calcification**.
  - Features: **low-volume ejaculate, oligospermia, azoospermia, haemospermia**; rarely, abscess.
  - Examination: **enlarged vesicles early; hard nodules late**.

##### Urethra and Penis

- **Urethral TB usually accompanies prostatic TB**; isolated urethral TB is very rare.

**PENILE TB — THE THREE FORMS**

| | Primary penile TB | Orificial TB | Papulonecrotic tuberculid (PNT) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | True infection | True infection; **rapidly necrotic** | **Hypersensitivity reaction** to MTBC antigens carried to skin from a distant focus |
| **Origin** | Penile skin | **Autoinoculation from own infected stool or urine**; rarely haematogenous or lymphatic | Disseminated antigen (commonest on glans; other skin sites possible) |
| **Lesion** | Inflamed papule or keratotic plaque (**lupus vulgaris**) → ulcer → spreads to corpora (**pea-sized nodules**, painless and hard, or fungating mass) | **Painful ulcers with pseudomembrane**, eroding deeply | **Red papules → ulcers → varioliform scarring**, recurrent |
| **Pain** | Painless nodules | **Painful** | **Can be painless** |
| **Bacilli, culture, polymerase chain reaction (PCR)** | Present | Present | **Absent: culture- and PCR-negative**; histology often inconclusive (mature granulomas often absent) |
| **Host and prognosis** | Fibrosis → penile distortion, **erectile dysfunction** | **Immunocompromised or debilitated**; marks **very advanced UG or GI TB**; **poor prognosis** | Reported from Japan, South Africa, India |
| **Mimics** | **Malignancy** | — | **Syphilis, Behçet disease, recurrent herpes simplex, balanitis, SCC** |
| **Diagnosis** | Tissue | — | **Recognition + response to empirical anti-TB treatment despite negative cultures** |

- **What the table tests:** in PNT alone **negative cultures and PCR do not exclude TB**; **response to treatment is the diagnostic test**.

*Concept —* **In UG TB the route of spread predicts the lesion.** Haematogenous seeding gives parenchymal disease (kidney, epididymis, peripheral prostate). Descending urinary spread gives luminal and orifice disease (distal ureteric stricture, cystitis, urethral prostatic disease). Healing by fibrosis turns an infection into an obstruction (stricture, contracted bladder, obliterated epididymis, infertility).



#### Diagnosis

- **In developed countries the primary goal is to isolate MTBC in culture for drug-susceptibility testing.** Order of evidence:
  - Culture or DNA positive → **diagnosis**.
  - Negative → **caseating granulomas on tissue** in the right clinical context support TB.
  - Also absent → **compatible clinical picture + probable exposure + response to empirical treatment**.
- **Up to one-fifth of UG TB coexists with pulmonary TB**: **always look for pulmonary disease**.

##### Culture

- **Gold standard: urine acid-fast bacilli (AFB) culture.**
- **Sampling:** **first-void (most concentrated) urine**, **3–5 samples on consecutive days**, **cultured immediately** because prolonged exposure to acidic urine inhibits mycobacterial growth. **Culture any biopsy or operative tissue too.**
- **Sensitivity depends on technique and shedding:** up to **80–90%** with **ideal technique**; as low as **10%** in **real-world practice**, compounded by **intermittent bacillary shedding**.
- **Ziehl–Neelsen stain of urine:** sensitivity **< 50%**.

**MYCOBACTERIAL CULTURE SYSTEMS**

| System | Medium | Time to detection | Setting and notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Löwenstein–Jensen (LJ)** | **Solid, egg-based** | **4–6 weeks** | **Medium of choice in developing countries**: cheapest, no special equipment; laborious |
| **Middlebrook 7H10** | **Solid, agar-based, transparent** | About **1 week sooner than LJ** | Developed countries; costlier |
| **BACTEC Mycobacteria Growth Indicator Tube (MGIT)** | **Liquid**, fully automated; **fluorescence quenching** | As little as **10 days** | Developed countries; **first-line susceptibility testing done in-house** on MGIT |
| Semi-automated radiometric | Liquid | — | Alternative |

- **Rule:** **culture on at least one solid medium alongside the liquid system** to maximise yield.
- **Susceptibility testing:** any culture method can be used; **second-line drugs and molecular resistance sequencing are done in reference laboratories**.

##### Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests

- **Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) give results in 1–2 days** and detect MTBC when **bacillary load is too low** for culture.
- **Urine contains natural amplification inhibitors**, lowering sensitivity in non-respiratory specimens; sensitivity also depends on specimen, target sequence and protocol.
- **GeneXpert MTB/RIF (marketed since 2010):** self-contained, automated (sputum processing → DNA extraction → amplification); **detects MTBC and rifampicin resistance simultaneously**.
  - **Rifampicin resistance is a surrogate for multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB)**, as **> 90% of rifampicin-resistant strains are also isoniazid (INH)-resistant**.
  - **MDR-TB = resistance to both INH and rifampicin.**
- **Performance in urine:** systematic reviews show **good sensitivity and specificity for GeneXpert against urine AFB culture**, but the underlying studies are small and heterogeneous, so estimates are imprecise. NAAT on **epididymal biopsy tissue** performed well in a single study.
- **Role of NAAT:**
  - **United States: adjunct only.** It **does not replace culture**, and a **negative NAAT does not exclude extrapulmonary TB**, for lack of high-quality sensitivity data.
  - **WHO: GeneXpert MTB/RIF is part of the initial work-up of all patients with TB**; **cost and equipment** are the barriers in developing countries.
  - **NAATs cannot monitor treatment response:** **dead organisms still shed nucleic acid**, so the test stays positive despite adequate treatment. **Culture is used for monitoring.**

##### Urine Lipoarabinomannan Assay

- **Lipoarabinomannan (LAM)** is a **mycobacterial cell-wall glycolipid** found in urine, sputum and serum. Methods range from enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to the **point-of-care lateral-flow urine assay (LF-LAM)**: **no laboratory equipment**, **result in < 30 minutes**.
- **In people living with HIV (PLHIV):** a systematic review showed **low sensitivity, high specificity**; sensitivity somewhat better with TB symptoms. **A positive result is useful; a negative result is not reassuring.**
- **WHO (2019) recommends adding LF-LAM for:**
  - **PLHIV with TB symptoms**
  - **People with advanced HIV disease who are unwell**
  - **PLHIV with a CD4 count < 200 cells/mm³**
- **United States:** broad access to other diagnostics limits LF-LAM mainly to academic centres.

##### Histopathology

- **Biopsy when urine cultures are negative.** Mycobacteria are often **not seen in tissue**, but **caseating granulomas in the right clinical context** support the diagnosis.
- **Histology is consistent with TB in only a minority** (about one-third in developed countries, one-fifth globally). **A negative biopsy does not exclude TB.**
- **Epididymal nodules:** **fine-needle aspiration cytology diagnostic in about two-thirds**.
- **Adding a TB NAAT to tissue increases sensitivity.**

##### Screening Tests

- **The tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon-γ release assays (IGRAs) cannot distinguish latent infection from active disease.**
  - Both are approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and widely used but have **limited value for active disease**: **a positive test does not rule TB disease in; a negative test does not rule it out.**
  - **Their proper use is screening for TB infection.**
- **Preferred screening test:** **IGRA** for persons **≥ 5 years born in TB-endemic countries**; **TST remains acceptable**.

###### Tuberculin Skin Test (Purified Protein Derivative, Mantoux Test)

- **Principle:** detects an **existing cellular (delayed-type hypersensitivity) response** to MTBC antigens.
- **Technique:** tuberculin (sterile protein extract of *M. tuberculosis*) injected **intradermally into the volar forearm**; **induration read at 48–72 hours**.
- **Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) cut-offs are risk-stratified:**

**TST — POSITIVITY THRESHOLDS BY RISK GROUP (CDC)**

| Induration | Positive in |
|---|---|
| **≥ 5 mm** | **Recent contact** with TB; **fibrotic chest radiograph changes consistent with old TB**; **immunosuppressed** |
| **≥ 10 mm** | **Immigrants from high-prevalence countries**; **residents of or workers in high-risk institutions**; **people who inject drugs**; **comorbidities raising active-TB risk** |
| **≥ 15 mm** | **General population with no risk factors** |

- **The logic of the table:** **higher pre-test probability, lower cut-off** — a small reaction matters in someone likely infected or unable to respond. The trap is one cut-off for everyone.

- **TST advantages:** **cheap, no laboratory, easy** (though staff must be trained to place and read it).
- **TST disadvantages:**
  - **Not MTBC-specific:** **BCG vaccination and non-tuberculous mycobacteria** can cause positives.
  - **Second visit** needed for reading.
  - **False negatives in 10–25% of active TB.**
- **In UG TB the TST is usually positive.**

###### Interferon-γ Release Assays

- **Principle:** a blood test measuring **IFN-γ released by sensitised T cells in response to MTBC-specific antigens** — in effect an **in vitro, MTBC-specific TST**.
- **Its antigens are absent from all BCG strains and most non-tuberculous mycobacteria**, so **BCG causes no false positive** — the key advantage over TST.

**TST VS IGRA**

| | TST | IGRA |
|---|---|---|
| **Format** | Intradermal, in vivo | Blood, in vitro |
| **Visits** | **Two** (read at 48–72 h) | **One** (result about 24 h) |
| **Affected by BCG?** | **Yes: false positive** | **No** |
| **Affected by non-tuberculous mycobacteria?** | Yes | Mostly no |
| **Laboratory** | Not needed | Needed |
| **Latent vs active** | Cannot distinguish | Cannot distinguish |

**THE TWO IGRAs AVAILABLE IN THE UNITED STATES**

| | QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus (QFT-Plus, fourth generation, 2017) | T-SPOT.TB |
|---|---|---|
| **Sample** | **Whole blood in four tubes**: ESAT-6/CFP-10; proprietary antigens that **activate CD8 T cells**; negative control; positive control | **Separated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)** in antibody-coated wells with ESAT-6 and CFP-10 |
| **Incubation** | 16–24 h in collection tubes | — |
| **Read-out** | **Plasma IFN-γ concentration by ELISA** | **Enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISPOT)**: **spots (IFN-γ-secreting cells) counted manually** |
| **Results** | Positive, negative or **indeterminate** | Positive, negative or **borderline** |
| **Ease** | Easier | **Harder to perform** |
| **Sensitivity** | Third-generation QFT **least sensitive** of the three tests | **Most sensitive** (T-SPOT.TB > TST > third-generation QFT in culture-confirmed TB) |

- Pooled sensitivity data for fourth-generation QFT-Plus are **not yet available**.

*Concept —* **Culture proves disease; NAAT and LAM speed it up; TST and IGRA only prove exposure.** Every test in this section is placed by what it can and cannot do: only culture gives susceptibility and tracks treatment, and only immune-response tests are blind to the difference between latent and active TB.

##### Radiography

- **Imaging is often the first test to suggest TB** in a UG disorder. **Choose modality by site and clinical picture.**

###### Plain Radiography (Kidney–Ureter–Bladder Film)

- **Calcification is frequent**, in about **a quarter to two-fifths** (24–44%), evolving with disease:
  - **Early:** faint punctate parenchymal calcifications.
  - **Progressive:** **globular calcification** of a tuberculous mass (Fig. 31.1).
  - **Papillary necrosis:** **triangular, ring-like calcifications** in the collecting system.
  - **Fibrotic autonephrectomy:** small, shrunken, calcified **"cement" or "putty" kidney** with **calcific rims outlining individual lobes** — **this lobar pattern is pathognomonic of end-stage renal TB.**
- **Infected stones** take odd shapes in a deformed, fibrosed pelvis; an **upward-pointing arrowhead stone** suggests a pelvis **"hiked up" by scar contraction**.
- **Ureteric calcification:**

**URETERIC CALCIFICATION — TB VS SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| TB | Schistosomiasis |
|---|---|
| **Intraluminal** | **Mural** |

- **Bladder wall calcification is uncommon** except late, with contracted bladder. **Prostatic and seminal vesicle calcification** in about **10%**.
- **Beyond the urinary tract:** **vertebral body erosions**, **calcified psoas cold abscesses**.
- **Every patient evaluated for UG TB needs a chest radiograph** to exclude coexistent **infectious pulmonary TB**.

###### Intravenous Urography

- **Intravenous urography (IVU) was the former gold standard for early renal TB**, source of most classic signs. Still sometimes called the preferred first test in some regions, it has been **essentially replaced by computed tomography (CT), CT urography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasound (US)**.
- **Early signs:** **loss of sharpness, irregular urothelial margins**; **"moth-eaten" calyces**.
- **Filling defects** from a **tuberculoma rupturing into a calyx** or **papillary necrosis**; **medullary cavities communicating with the collecting system**.
- **"Phantom calyx":** a stenosed calyx or infundibulum excretes no contrast, so the calyx is **absent where it should be**.
- **Ureter:**
  - **Pipestem ureter:** rigid, calcified, straight, tubular, **aperistaltic**.
  - **Beaded corkscrew ureter:** nodular fibrosis along the whole ureter.
  - **Both highly suggestive of TB, especially with kidney or bladder changes.**
- **Non-functioning kidney** (autonephrectomy); **fibrosed, contracted bladder** (Fig. 31.2).
- **Perinephric abscess** suggested by **loss of renal respiratory movement** or **ureteric displacement**.
- **Commonest IVU findings are obstructive changes from scarring**, not the classic erosive signs:
  - **Calyceal obliteration, infundibular narrowing, hydrocalycosis, segmental or total hydronephrosis, hydroureter** (Figs. 31.3, 31.4)
  - **Calyceal dilatation and distortion → "cloverleaf" pattern**
  - **Vesicoureteric junction obstruction** from tuberculous cystitis or **distal-third ureteric stricture** (Fig. 31.5)
  - **Kerr's kink:** **"hiked-up" renal pelvis with sharp pelviureteric junction angulation**

###### Computed Tomography With Urography

- **CT urography is the most used modality in developed countries**, replacing IVU there.
- **It shows the KUB and IVU features (calcification, scarring, obstruction) more sensitively:**
  - **More sensitive than KUB for calcification** and **collecting-system wall thickening**.
  - Multidetector scanners detect **3–4 mm lesions**.
  - **Intravenous contrast assesses function** across excretory phases.
- **Granulomas:**
  - **Small:** **hypo-attenuating, little or no enhancement**.
  - **Large:** **mass-like, peripherally enhancing**, can **mimic renal cell carcinoma**.
- **Particularly useful in complicated or extensive disease:** perinephric and psoas abscesses; lymph nodes, vertebrae, spleen, liver; **prostate and seminal vesicles** (enlargement, necrosis, cavitation, abscess, calcification).
- **CT vs IVU:**
  - **CT disadvantages:** **higher radiation dose**; **less sensitive for earliest changes** (minimal urothelial thickening, subtle papillary necrosis), **for which IVU may still be preferred in resource-limited settings.**
  - **Contrast CT advantages:** shows **parenchyma, tissue oedema and hypoperfusion**.

**FIG. 31.6** CT with oral contrast in bilateral TB: the **right kidney is hydronephrotic from infundibular stenosis but retains good function**; the **left kidney is an end-stage, non-functioning, atrophic, calcified kidney**. The figure illustrates the **two end-points side by side**: **obstruction that can be salvaged vs autonephrectomy**.

###### Retrograde and Antegrade Pyelography

- **Both replaced by CT urography**, but useful **when IVU or CT is impossible (renal insufficiency, contrast allergy)** to delineate distorted anatomy.
- **With IVU, they show whether cavities are obstructive and whether they communicate with the collecting system.**

##### Ultrasonography

- **Limited diagnostic role:** findings **non-specific**, visualisation poorer than CT.
- **No radiation and low cost** make it the **modality for children and pregnancy**.
- **Uses:**
  - **Scrotum:** diffusely enlarged testis or epididymis with **homogeneous or heterogeneous hypoechoic areas**, **complex collections, hydrocele**.
  - **Transrectal US:** **thickened prostate and seminal vesicles**.
  - **Kidney:** abscesses, cavities; **septated cystic lesions suggest chronic infection**; calcifications **echogenic with distal acoustic shadowing**.
  - **Restricted renal respiratory movement suggests perinephric or psoas abscess.**
  - Abdomen: **ascites, lymphadenopathy, omental caking**.
- **Main use: following hydronephrosis during medical treatment**, because **healing fibrosis can worsen obstruction** while the infection is cured.

##### Magnetic Resonance Imaging

- **MRI is used less than contrast CT.** Radiation-free, so **useful in children and pregnancy**. Findings are much as on CT, but **better tissue resolution** can show **a single granuloma**.
- **Signal characteristics:**
  - **Small lesions:** **hypointense on T1 and T2**.
  - **Larger lesions:** **centrally T2-hyperintense** from central cellularity.
  - **Large lesions can mimic malignancy**, not always distinguishable.
- **Magnetic resonance urogram (MRU):** **more sensitive than IVU for urothelial thickening and caliectasis**.
- **Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI):**
  - Distinguishes **hydronephrosis from pyonephrosis**.
  - **Monitors renal fibrosis:** **apparent diffusion coefficient falls as fibrosis increases**, allowing staging and assessment of treatment effect.
- **Cine-MRU and dynamic MRU** assess **ureteric peristalsis**.
- **Caution with gadolinium in renal failure**: risk of **nephrogenic systemic fibrosis**.

**IMAGING IN UROGENITAL TB — MODALITY, ROLE AND SIGNATURE SIGNS**

| Modality | Current role | Signature signs and specific use |
|---|---|---|
| **KUB** | Frequently shows calcification | **Lobar "putty" kidney (pathognomonic of end stage)**; **intraluminal** ureteric calcification; arrowhead stone |
| **Chest radiograph** | **Mandatory in all patients** | Coexistent pulmonary TB |
| **IVU** | **Former gold standard**; still used in resource-limited settings for early disease | Moth-eaten calyx, **phantom calyx**, **pipestem** and **corkscrew** ureter, cloverleaf calyces, **Kerr's kink** |
| **CT urography** | **Most used in developed countries** | Calcification (more sensitive than KUB), granuloma mimicking renal cell carcinoma, abscesses, disease beyond urinary tract |

| **MRI / MRU with DWI** | Children and pregnancy; lesion characterisation | Single granuloma; **DWI: pyonephrosis vs hydronephrosis, grading of fibrosis** |

##### Cystoscopy and Ureteroscopy

- **Endoscopy has a limited diagnostic role:** **direct vision, but non-specific findings**:
  - Hyperaemia, erosion, ulceration, granulomatous masses, irregular ureteric orifices.
  - **Ulcers can mimic malignancy.**
- **A "golf-hole" ureteric orifice suggests TB** → **image or scope the upper tract**.
- **Biopsy when possible, especially if malignancy is possible.**
- **Why biopsy though urine culture is diagnostic:** culture results **may come too slowly**, and in **culture-negative patients bladder biopsy has moderate yield** (19–52% sensitivity).

That is why the commonest finding is obstruction, and why hydronephrosis must be watched during treatment.




#### Treatment

- **Before antimicrobials:** sanatorium rest and nutrition; for severe UG disease, **extirpative surgery was the only hope of cure**.
- **Drug milestones:** **streptomycin 1944 → isoniazid (INH) 1952 → rifamycins 1957**, replacing both sanatoria and surgery.
- **Today most patients, including MDR-TB, are treated medically as outpatients.** **Surgery establishes the diagnosis or is adjunctive in advanced disease.**

##### Medical Therapy

- **Multidrug therapy is mandatory, for five reasons:**
  - Bacilli live in **different micro-environments** with different metabolic states and replication rates.
  - Some drugs are **bactericidal**, others only **bacteriostatic**.
  - Some act best on **rapidly replicating** bacilli, others on **metabolically quiescent** ones.
  - Drugs differ in **tissue penetration and optimal pH**.
  - **Combination therapy prevents emergence of resistance.**
- **Start with four drugs: INH, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol (RIPE)**; first-line combination gives the **best cure rate in the shortest time**.
- **Baseline tests:** **platelet count, liver and renal function**; **HIV test**; where appropriate, **hepatitis B and C and diabetes screening**.
- **Tailor the regimen to susceptibility results.** **Directly observed therapy (DOT)** secures adherence and prevents resistance.
- **Second-line agents for three situations:** **intolerance**, **resistance** or **failure** of first-line treatment; they vary in tolerability and ease of administration.
  - **Recent additions:**
    - **Fluoroquinolones and linezolid**, originally developed for other bacterial infections.
    - **Bedaquiline**, the **first new drug in 40 years developed specifically for TB**.
    - **Pretomanid** (FDA-approved 2019), **with bedaquiline and linezolid** for MDR-TB or extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB).

- **What the table is for:** pairing **each first-line drug with its signature toxicity**.
  - **INH → neuropathy** (hence pyridoxine)
  - **Rifamycins → interactions** (hence rifabutin in HIV)
  - **Pyrazinamide → gout and arthralgia**
  - **Ethambutol → the eye**
- **The pattern:** **every first-line drug except ethambutol is hepatotoxic**; ethambutol alone needs visual rather than hepatic monitoring.
- **The trap:** rifapentine is listed but **contraindicated in extrapulmonary (therefore UG) TB**.

**FIRST-LINE ANTITUBERCULOUS DRUGS — SIGNATURE TOXICITY AND MONITORING**

| Drug | Signature toxicity | Monitor or prevent |
|---|---|---|
| INH | Peripheral neuropathy; hepatitis | Pyridoxine; liver enzymes |
| Rifampicin / rifabutin | Drug interactions; hepatitis; (rifabutin) uveitis | Antiretroviral and immunosuppressant levels; liver enzymes |
| Pyrazinamide | Hyperuricaemia, arthralgia; hepatitis | Liver enzymes |
| Ethambutol | Optic neuritis (red–green colour loss) | **Visual acuity and colour vision** |

- **Standard short course for uncomplicated UG TB: 6 months:**
  - **Intensive phase, 2 months:** **daily INH + rifampicin + pyrazinamide**, plus **ethambutol until susceptibility is known**; **stop ethambutol if susceptible to the other first-line drugs.**
  - **Continuation phase, 4 months:** **INH + rifampicin**, **daily** (or **three times weekly**); **twice-weekly dosing no longer recommended.**
  - **Pyridoxine (vitamin B6)** throughout against INH neuropathy.

**TREATMENT PHASES — PURPOSE**

| Intensive phase (2 months) | Continuation phase (4 months) |
|---|---|
| Kills **rapidly multiplying** bacilli | Eradicates **slow, sporadic multipliers and persisters** |
| 3–4 drugs | 2 drugs |

- **First-line drugs suit UG TB:** **high urinary concentrations** and **good activity in acidic conditions**.
- **The new 4-month regimen** (daily rifapentine, INH, pyrazinamide, moxifloxacin) for drug-susceptible TB is **not recommended for extrapulmonary TB** and **must not be used for UG TB**.
- **Prolonged treatment:** duration depends on clinical disease and drugs used.
  - **Complicated UG TB** (abscesses, severe scarring, extensive disease) and UG TB's **higher relapse rate than pulmonary TB** lead some to treat **12–24 months**, or **≥ 6 months after surgery**.
  - **Discuss any departure from the standard short course with a TB specialist.**
- **Monitoring during therapy:**
  - **Liver enzymes monthly if pre-existing liver disease.** Drug **hepatotoxicity is reversible on stopping the drug**; avoid **alcohol and other hepatotoxic drugs**. Severe liver injury has occurred.
  - **Renal lesions can worsen during treatment:** **healing fibrosis can aggravate obstruction and bladder contraction**. Steroids may help; **surgery may be needed for new or worsening obstruction**.

*Concept —* **TB chemotherapy is designed around the biology of the bacillus.** Mixed populations and different metabolic states explain why several drugs are used and why there are two phases. Resistance explains the four-drug start and DOT. The urinary tract adds one specific hazard: **cure comes through fibrosis, and fibrosis obstructs**.

##### Corticosteroids

- **Adjunctive steroids' role is not fully established.** Rationale: **limit excessive immune-mediated destruction and scarring**.
- **Uses:**
  - **TB meningitis:** **mortality benefit; strongly recommended**.
  - **Selected cases:** **severe pulmonary TB with paradoxical worsening on treatment**; **TB pericarditis** (to prevent constriction).
  - **UG TB:** to prevent **ureteric stricture and bladder contraction**, on **anecdotal evidence only; no trials**.
- **A meta-analysis of trials in pulmonary, meningeal, pleural, pericardial and peritoneal TB** showed **reduced mortality whichever organ was involved**.

##### Surgical Therapy

- **About half (55%) of UG TB patients need surgery at some point**, rising with disease stage.
- **Indications:**
  - Relieve obstruction and drain infected material
  - Remove a **non-functioning, infected kidney** that resists cure
  - **Medically resistant hypertension from a functionally excluded kidney**
  - **Reconstruct** the urinary tract
  - Abscesses unresponsive to medical treatment → **aspiration, or percutaneous or open drainage**
- **More than half of TB operations are now reconstructive.**
- **Optimal timing: 4–6 weeks after starting medical therapy**, letting **inflammation subside, bacillary load fall and lesions stabilise**.

###### Procedures to Relieve Obstruction

- **Uraemia or sepsis → emergency decompression.** **Renal failure** usually reflects **bilateral obstruction or obstruction of a functionally solitary kidney**.
- **Early stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN)** for tuberculous ureteric stricture **limits loss of function** and **preserves the chance of later reconstruction**.
- **Escalation:**
  - **Retrograde double-J stent (preferred)** → succeeds in **under half (41%)**.
  - Fails → **antegrade stent**, internalised or externalised, via percutaneous puncture.
  - Fails → **PCN until definitive treatment**; more than one may be needed because strictures and scarring cause loculation.
- **PCN must be followed by correcting the cause**; removal without correction risks a **tuberculous cutaneous fistula** (less likely with effective concurrent chemotherapy).
- **Unsalvageable kidney → nephrectomy.**
- **Avoid high contrast-injection pressures** during stent or PCN placement, which could **disseminate infection**.

###### Nephrectomy

- **The overriding surgical goal is organ preservation.**
- **Partial nephrectomy:** **localised lesion (e.g. polar mass) unresponsive to medical therapy**.

**TOTAL NEPHRECTOMY — INDICATIONS**

| Established indications | Contested indication |
|---|---|
| **Non-functioning kidney + TB recalcitrant or recurring despite optimal therapy.** Relapse after nephrectomy with short-course therapy **< 1%** | **Non-functioning kidney without recalcitrant disease or hypertension**. Some advocate removal, but **no supporting evidence**; studies showing bacilli in removed kidneys **predate modern chemotherapy** |
| **Non-functioning kidney + medically resistant hypertension.** Hypertension improves in **about two-thirds (65%)** | |

- **Nephrectomy is performed in about one-quarter (27%) of UG TB patients**, similarly in developed and developing countries.
- **Open technique:**
  - **Oblique retroperitoneal incision**, extendable dorsally or ventrally, because of dense fibrosis.
  - **Remove granulomatous or caseous perinephric tissue with the specimen.**
  - **Ligate renal artery and vein separately** to avoid a late **arteriovenous fistula**.
  - **The ureter is usually left.**
  - Minimise lymphatic disruption; **avoid entering pleura and peritoneum**.
- **Laparoscopic nephrectomy is increasingly preferred** despite early concern about fibrosis: **less blood loss, faster recovery**, and in experienced hands only **modestly longer** (about half an hour more) than for other indications.

###### Ureteropelvic and Ureteral Surgery

- **Temporarily stent a pelviureteric junction (PUJ) or ureteric stricture** to recover renal function before definitive repair.
- **Stricture site:**
  - **Upper and mid-ureteric strictures are rare**; may suit endourology.
  - **Lower ureteric strictures are common**; **often need open surgery**.
- **Decision factors:** stricture **length and severity**, **guidewire passage**, **blood supply**, **renal function**.

**Endoscopic management**
- **TB strictures have mucosal ischaemia and dense fibrosis**, so success rates for other strictures **do not transfer**.
- **Best results:** **short stricture, residual lumen, good renal function**.
- **Strictures forming during chemotherapy and stented early (double-J) can stabilise without further treatment.**
- **Balloon dilatation** (retrograde or antegrade) has been used for ureter, PUJ, vesicoureteric junction and calyceal infundibula, usually leaving a stent. **High failure rates; repeat procedures common.**
- **Every ureteric stricture needs follow-up imaging (US or IVU)**, especially after endoscopic treatment, because **strictures can worsen as they heal**.
  - **Deterioration** → consider adding **corticosteroids**.
  - **No improvement or progression after 6 weeks of chemotherapy** → **open surgery**.

**Open surgical options**
- **Long, complex strictures need open repair**; mobilisation is hampered by **fibrosis, loss of elasticity and poor vascularity**.
- **PUJ repair in TB is harder than for congenital PUJ obstruction.**

**OPEN RECONSTRUCTION BY STRICTURE SITE**

| Site | Procedure | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **PUJ, short segment, extrarenal pelvis** | **Dismembered pyeloplasty** | — |
| **PUJ, longer stricture** | **Non-dismembered (flap) pyeloplasty** | May be impossible if pelvis heavily scarred |
| **PUJ, reconstruction impossible** | **Ureterocalicostomy** (ureter to lower-pole calyx) | **Preserve renal capsule to cover the lower pole**; **omentum** if capsule insufficient, to prevent anastomotic stenosis |
| **Upper or mid ureter** | **Excision + tension-free ureteroureterostomy**; or **Davis intubated ureterotomy** (adhesiolysis + intubation) | — |
| **Lower ureter** | **Complete excision back to healthy, well-vascularised ureter → ureteroneocystostomy** | — |
| Small gap | **Contralateral bladder mobilisation ± division of the superior vesical artery** | Gains **2–3 cm** |
| Moderate gap | **Psoas hitch** (needs good bladder capacity) | Bridges **up to 5 cm**; **avoid genitofemoral and femoral nerves** |
| Long gap | **Boari flap** ± psoas hitch | Bridges **10–15 cm**; a poorly executed flap **reduces bladder capacity**; **contracted TB bladder may be unsuitable** |
| **Multiple or recurrent strictures** | **Ileal ureteric replacement (interposition)** | When native ureter no longer a usable conduit |

- **The logic of the table:** **gap length determines the manoeuvre** (2–3 cm → 5 cm → 10–15 cm → ileum).
- **The trap:** in TB the **bladder itself may be contracted**, **ruling out bladder-based bridges** (psoas hitch, Boari flap).

###### Bladder Surgery

- **Options for a contracted TB bladder:** **augmentation cystoplasty** (first described in the 19th century for this condition) and **bladder substitution**.
- **Augmentation indication:** intolerable **frequency, nocturia, urgency, pain and haematuria**, typically with **capacity < 100 mL**.

**BOWEL SEGMENT BY DEGREE OF BLADDER CONTRACTION**

| Bladder state | Procedure / segment |
|---|---|
| **Half the bladder diseased** | Augmentation with **ileum** |
| **Severely contracted** | Augmentation with **ileocaecum or sigmoid** (stomach and caecum also used) |
| **Thimble bladder < 20 mL** | **Orthotopic bladder substitution** |

- **Usual rules for bowel in the urinary tract apply:** assess **renal function**, reconfigure bowel into a **low-pressure reservoir**, **educate the patient**, ensure **long-term follow-up**.
- **Complications:** **mucus, electrolyte disturbance, secondary bacterial infection**.
- **Laparoscopic and robot-assisted augmentation** increasingly used, with good results.

###### Urethral Procedures

- **Bladder neck contracture → transurethral incision.**
- **Urethral stricture → endoscopic treatment**, often **repeated**.
- **Tuberculous urethral fistula → chemotherapy + suprapubic drainage**, then **delayed reconstruction**.
- **A tuberculous seminal vesicle cavity** has been drained into the bladder by cold-knife incision (case report).

###### Genital Surgery

- **Extirpative genital surgery is reserved for failed medical therapy.**
- **Epididymis involved, testis spared** → **epididymectomy alone, not orchidectomy**, **preserving testicular blood supply**: **ligate the vas → begin dissection at the globus minor** → excise.
- **Testis involved** → **scrotal orchidectomy**.
- **The vas is usually diseased distal to the external ring**, so **ligation at the ring is possible and sufficient**.

##### Monitoring for Tuberculosis Relapse

- **Relapse after pulmonary TB: 2–6%**, mostly **within the first year**; needs a **second, longer or different regimen**.
- **Relapse after UG TB is higher: 6.3–22%**, even after 12 months of treatment.
  - **Reason:** an extensively diseased kidney holds **innumerable foci that are hard to sterilise**; **viable bacilli have been found after 9 months of treatment.**
- **In any recurrence, make a special effort to isolate the organism for susceptibility testing.**
- **Follow-up:** **pulmonary TB 2 years**; **UG TB, some recommend 10 years**, as **mean time to relapse is about 5 years (5.3)**.

*Concept —* **Surgery in UG TB follows chemotherapy.** It is timed at 4–6 weeks, aims first to preserve the kidney, and is mostly reconstructive. It exists because drugs sterilise but cannot reverse fibrosis. The poorly sterilised kidney is also why UG TB relapses more often and needs longer follow-up than pulmonary TB.



#### Management of Urogenital Tuberculosis in Special Situations

- **In every special situation, choose the regimen carefully** (side effects, interactions, toxicity) and **seek infectious diseases or TB specialist advice**.

##### Multidrug-Resistant, Extensively Drug-Resistant and Pre-Extensively Drug-Resistant Tuberculosis

**DRUG-RESISTANCE DEFINITIONS (UNITED STATES)**

| Category | Resistant to |
|---|---|
| **MDR-TB** | **INH + rifampicin** (the two most important first-line drugs) |
| **Pre-XDR-TB** (definition since 2022) | INH + rifampicin + **a fluoroquinolone OR an injectable aminoglycoside** |
| **XDR-TB** | INH + rifampicin + **a fluoroquinolone** + **either an injectable second-line aminoglycoside (amikacin, kanamycin, capreomycin) OR bedaquiline or linezolid** |

**OUTCOME BY RESISTANCE CATEGORY**

| | Drug-susceptible | MDR-TB | XDR-TB |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cure rate** | **94–97%** | **50–60%** | **30–50%** |
| **Conventional regimen** | 6 months | **Usually > 18 months**, sometimes with IM drugs | **5–6 drugs for ≥ 2 years** |

- **Frequency:**
  - **Worldwide:** MDR-TB in about **3.6% of new** and **20% of previously treated cases**; **one-fifth of MDR-TB has additional resistance.**
  - **United States:** **< 1.5%** at first diagnosis, stable over 20 years.
- **BPaL (bedaquiline + pretomanid + linezolid)**, approved **since 2019**: **all-oral, 6–9-month** regimen for MDR-TB, used successfully in XDR-TB **without bedaquiline or linezolid resistance**.
  - **Not yet approved for extrapulmonary TB; role in UG TB not established.**

##### Pregnancy and Lactation

- **Advise avoiding pregnancy during treatment for active TB.**
- **TB diagnosed in pregnancy → treat promptly**: **fetal risk from TB outweighs drug risk.**

**PYRAZINAMIDE IN PREGNANCY — TWO POSITIONS**

| United States | WHO |
|---|---|
| **Controversial**: fetal effects not established, perceived higher hepatotoxicity risk → usually **omitted**, so treatment lasts **9 months** | **Recommended** as part of the standard first-line regimen |

- **Breastfeeding is allowed once the mother is non-infectious**; **breast-milk drug levels are too low to be toxic.**

##### Human Immunodeficiency Virus Infection

- **HIV raises active-TB risk 15- to 20-fold**, and each infection **accelerates the other**. **Test every TB patient for HIV.**
- **TB causes > 30% of deaths among PLHIV worldwide**, reminiscent of 18th- and 19th-century Europe.
- **Extrapulmonary and UG TB are commoner in HIV**; UG TB was common at autopsy in AIDS in a small Indian study.

**UG TB — HIV-POSITIVE VS HIV-NEGATIVE**

| | PLHIV | HIV-negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Extent** | **More disseminated**; more lymphadenopathy; **bilateral renal disease** | More localised |
| **Caseation, necrosis, fibrosis** | **Less** (fibrosis needs a competent immune response) | Typical |
| **Collecting-system stenosis** | **Much less frequent** | Most patients |
| **Bladder contracture** | **Much less frequent** | Common |
| **Mortality** | **High** despite fewer obstructive lesions | Lower |

- **Treatment:**
  - **Do not delay.** Guidelines **match those without HIV**: the **6-month short course is effective**; **9 months no longer routine**.
  - **Duration depends on site, severity, tolerance, response and whether antiretroviral therapy is effective.**
  - **Interactions:** **rifamycins lower antiretroviral levels (rifampicin more than rifabutin)**, so antiretroviral doses may need increasing.

*Concept —* **In HIV, UG TB causes less fibrosis because fibrosis is an immune product.** The disease is therefore more disseminated and more lethal but less obstructive. The inverse relationship between immunity and scarring runs through the whole TB half of the chapter.

##### Renal Transplant Recipients

- **Allograft TB is rare**, usually **within 6 months** of transplantation but up to **7 years** later. Early onset reflects **immunosuppression or TB already in the donor kidney**.
- **Diagnosis is difficult:**
  - Early disease, so **imaging usually normal**.
  - Immunosuppression **blunts the usual pathology**.
  - **Fever is the usual presentation**; **urinary symptoms in only a minority (20%).**
- **Diagnostic yields:**
  - Chest radiograph: **abnormal but non-specific in about half**.
  - Sputum culture: **positive in about half**, whatever the chest radiograph shows.
  - **Urine AFB culture: positive in all patients in one study** — the key test.
  - **Many are diagnosed only on histology after graft nephrectomy.**
- **Treatment:** **rifamycin–immunosuppressant interactions** require **frequent drug-level monitoring and dose adjustment**.
- **Complications:** **graft rejection, disseminated TB, death in up to about one-third (36%)**.

**SPECIAL SITUATIONS — WHAT CHANGES**

| Situation | Regimen change | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **MDR-TB** | Second-line drugs; **BPaL** (not yet approved for extrapulmonary TB) | > 18 months conventional; 6–9 months with BPaL |
| **XDR-TB** | 5–6 drugs | ≥ 2 years |
| **Pregnancy** | **INH + rifampicin + ethambutol + pyridoxine; pyrazinamide omitted in the United States** | **9 months** |
| **HIV on effective antiretrovirals** | Standard; **daily** dosing; watch rifamycin interactions (rifabutin preferred) | 6 months |
| **HIV not on effective antiretrovirals** | As above | **9 months** |
| **Renal transplant** | Rifamycin dose adjustment with drug levels, or **rifamycin-free** regimen | **≥ 18 months if rifamycin-free** |

**KEY POINTS: TUBERCULOSIS**

- **Untreated UG TB causes irreversible damage (renal failure, infertility)**, so TB must be in the differential diagnosis of UG disorders.
- **Presentation is non-specific and indolent**, and constitutional symptoms are infrequent. **A high index of suspicion is essential.**
- **Gold standard: positive AFB culture from urine or tissue biopsy.** A diagnosis can often be made by other means, such as **NAAT** or **granulomas on histology**.
- **Optimal timing of surgery: 4–6 weeks after starting medical therapy**, so that inflammation settles, the bacillary load falls and the lesions stabilise.
- **Monitor for relapse after treatment. It occurs in up to 22%.**
