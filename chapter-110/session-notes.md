## TERMINOLOGY AND DEFINITIONS

- **Overactive bladder (OAB) is a syndrome** of one or more storage symptoms driven by an excessively frequent or exceptionally strong desire to void.
- **International Continence Society (ICS) definition** and its **four component symptoms** — daytime frequency, nocturia, urgency, urgency urinary incontinence (UUI) — are set out in Table 110.1.
- **"Urge incontinence" was renamed "urgency incontinence"** — for consistency across terms and to tie the language to an abnormal *sensation*.
- **All components reduce health-related quality of life (QoL); urgency and UUI carry the strongest negative effect.** UUI is the **most bothersome lower urinary tract symptom (LUTS) in both sexes**.
- **Daytime frequency becomes bothersome at voiding every 2 hours or more often (≈ ≥8 daytime voids)** — but bother is individual and can be significant below eight voids.
- **Nocturia becomes bothersome at ≥2 episodes, markedly so at ≥3.**
- **Isolated nocturia** (no other storage symptom) usually reflects **nocturnal polyuria or poor sleep, not lower urinary tract (LUT) dysfunction**.
- **OAB is a symptom diagnosis; detrusor overactivity (DO) is a urodynamic observation** — involuntary detrusor contractions during filling, spontaneous or provoked.
  - **Not interchangeable:** OAB patients may have no DO; conversely DO may be asymptomatic or an **artefact of instrumentation**.

<p class="tt">TABLE 110.1 Standardized Terminology and Definitions to Describe Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction Related to Overactive Bladder</p>

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Overactive bladder syndrome** | **Urgency, usually with frequency and nocturia, ± UUI, in the absence of urinary tract infection (UTI) or other obvious pathology** |
| **Increased daytime frequency** | Patient considers they void too often by day; **upper limit of normal seven daytime voids** |
| **Nocturia** | Interruption of sleep **one or more times** to micturate; **each void preceded and followed by sleep** |
| **Urgency** | Sudden, **compelling** desire to void that is **difficult to defer** |
| **Urgency (urinary) incontinence** | Involuntary loss of urine **associated with urgency** |

<p class="src">Data from Abrams et al., 2002; Haylen et al., 2010 (ICS standardisation reports).</p>

- **What the table is for:** fixing the standardised vocabulary on which every later threshold and trial endpoint depends.
- **Pattern:** every definition is a *complaint* — patient-reported, not measured.
- **Trap:** the nocturia definition counts one waking episode, yet bother only begins at ≥2; and a void that is not preceded *and* followed by sleep is not nocturia.

### Mixed Symptoms Incorporating Urinary Urgency

- **Mixed urinary incontinence (MUI):** involuntary leakage associated with **urgency *and* with effort, exertion, sneezing or coughing** — i.e. stress urinary incontinence (SUI) **plus UUI ("OAB wet")** in the same person.
- **SUI + "OAB dry" is *not* MUI** — no standard term exists; describe it (e.g. "SUI and urgency without UUI").
- **Urinary plus faecal incontinence = double (dual) incontinence**, not "mixed incontinence" — avoids confusion with MUI.
- **MUI generally means more severe leakage**; the relative contribution of each component is hard for patients to judge, especially with **insensible incontinence** (leakage the patient did not notice happening).

<p class="tt">"OAB dry" versus "OAB wet"</p>

| | **OAB dry** | **OAB wet** |
|---|---|---|
| Defining feature | Urgency **without** UUI | Urgency **with** UUI |
| Combined with SUI | "SUI and urgency" — described, not named | **MUI** |
| Psychological burden | Lower | **Higher** |
| DO on cystometry | Less likely | More likely (almost always in men) |

### Distinguishing Overactive Bladder From Bladder Pain Syndrome

- **Older definitions of urgency included fear of pain as well as fear of leakage**; pain is now **not** part of OAB but is **required** for bladder pain syndrome (BPS).
- **BPS:** **persistent or recurrent chronic pelvic pain, pressure or discomfort perceived to be related to the bladder, plus at least one other urinary symptom** (urgency or frequency).
- **Shared symptoms:** urgency, daytime frequency and nocturia; **UUI belongs to OAB, pain to BPS**.
- **Uncertain boundary:** up to about seven in ten women with bladder-centric pain also report urgency and/or UUI; both may be **variable expressions of shared afferent hypersensitivity, central sensitisation and somatisation**.

<p class="tt">OAB versus BPS — the governing contrast</p>

| Feature | **OAB** | **BPS** |
|---|---|---|
| Primary fear | **Fear of leakage** | **Fear of pain** |
| Sensation with filling | **Phasic** waves of urgency that rise and recede until severe urgency forces voiding or UUI | **Steady, accelerating rise in pain** throughout filling |
| Voided volumes | **Variable** | **More consistent** |
| Ability to defer | Limited — leakage risk | **Can defer**, at the cost of more pain |
| Site of sensation | Perineum / base of penis, or vagina / urethra | **Suprapubic** ± perineal (urethral, vaginal, penile) |
| UUI | Yes ("wet") | **No** |

<p class="kpt">KEY POINTS: OVERACTIVE BLADDER TERMINOLOGY</p>

- **OAB = urgency-defined symptom syndrome; DO = urodynamic observation.**
- **MUI = UUI + SUI**; **fear of leakage (OAB) vs fear of pain (BPS)**, possibly both central sensitisation.

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> OAB is defined by <b>a sensation (urgency), not by a measurement</b>: this is why DO, pain, SUI and polyuria each have to be separated from it explicitly, and why every "mixed" label depends on whether UUI is present.</p>

## EPIDEMIOLOGY AND ECONOMICS

### Prevalence of Overactive Bladder and Related Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms

- **Prevalence of OAB in adults is reported between about 12% and 29%, UUI between about 1.5% and 17%**; the spread reflects **differing case definitions and sampled populations**, which limit direct comparison.

<p class="tt">TABLE 110.2 Studies Examining Prevalence Data for Overactive Bladder</p>

| Study | Population | Method | Definition of OAB | Prevalence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **NOBLE** (National Overactive Bladder Evaluation) | US adults ≥18 y* | Computer-assisted telephone interview (CATI) | **≥4 urgency episodes in preceding 4 weeks and ≥8 voids/day or bladder coping mechanisms** | OAB 16.5%; with UUI 6.1% |
| **EpiLUTS** (Epidemiology of LUTS) | Multinational, ≥40 y | Internet survey | Urgency at least "sometimes" and/or UUI | **OAB 28.7% (highest)**; with UUI 17.3% |
| **OAB-POLL** (Impact of OAB on Physical and Occupational Limitations) | US, 18–70 y* | Internet survey | Urgency at least "sometimes" and/or UUI | OAB 23.3%; with UUI 12.6% |
| **NHANES** (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) | US women ≥21 y* | Interview | Any incontinence in past 12 months | UUI 7.9%; with MUI 17% |
| **BACH** (Boston Area Community Health Survey) | Boston, 30–79 y* | Interview | **American Urological Association symptom index ≥8** | LUTS 18.7% |
| **EPIC** | Multinational, ≥18 y | CATI | **ICS definitions** | **OAB 11.8% (lowest)**; with UUI 1.5% |

<p class="src">*Selective sampling to allow generalisation to the US population.</p>

- **What the table is for:** showing that **prevalence is a function of definition**.
- **Pattern:** the **loosest definition** (urgency "sometimes", internet survey, older population — EpiLUTS) gives the **highest** figure; the **strict ICS definition** (EPIC) gives the **lowest**.
- **Trap:** BACH measures **LUTS by symptom score**, not OAB; NHANES measures **incontinence**, not OAB — neither is an OAB prevalence.

- **Sex and age** (table below):
  - In **NOBLE, overall OAB prevalence was similar in the two sexes, but UUI was roughly three times commoner in women** — the sex difference lies chiefly in **wet** OAB.
  - **EpiLUTS and OAB-POLL** found OAB in women roughly **double** that in men.
  - **Beyond 75 years**, a third to two-fifths of both sexes report an OAB symptom, versus about one in ten or fewer before 55.
- **Race and ethnicity:** OAB and UUI are **slightly higher in Black men and women** than in other groups across EpiLUTS, OAB-POLL and NHANES; **Asian women** had the lowest prevalence in EpiLUTS.

<p class="tt">Sex contrast</p>

| | **Women** | **Men** |
|---|---|---|
| OAB prevalence | **1.5–2 ×** men | — |
| UUI prevalence | **2–3 ×** men | — |
| Age at which OAB rises | **40s** | **50s–60s** |
| Age at which UUI rises steeply | **After 44** | **After 64** |

### Incidence and Progression of Overactive Bladder

- **OAB is dynamic — symptoms wax and wane**; longitudinal data are limited.
- **Incidence in women: about 3–8% per year.**
- **Progression from OAB dry to UUI: about 1% per year; remission of OAB: about 6% per year** — remission outpaces progression.

### Health Burden of Overactive Bladder

- **OAB impairs daily activities, sleep, self-esteem, relationships and intimacy; UUI adds burden beyond dry OAB.** People with OAB report **less leisure-time physical activity**.
- **Work:** greater **impairment at work, more work time missed, higher unemployment**, and more absence from medical causes and disability.
- **Under-presentation:** fewer than half of people with OAB have ever discussed it with a clinician, although nearly all have seen one for other reasons — a **barrier specific to OAB, not to health care access in general**.
  - Common reasons: **not thought important, not asked by the clinician, belief that it must simply be coped with or that nothing can be done**. Fewer than half of women report ever being asked.
- **Fewer than 6% of symptomatic people take medication.**
- **Anticholinergics are only modestly superior to placebo** in aggregate, so **persistence is poor**:
  - **About 92% fail initial anticholinergic therapy within 2 years**; **most stop within 6 months**; **half never refill the first prescription**.
  - Reasons for stopping, in order: **unmet expectations > side effects > cost**.

#### Costs

- **Direct and indirect costs** fall on patients and the health system; much of the **patient's** cost is **routine containment** (pads, diapers, laundry) and scales with incontinence severity.
- **Disparities:** a **greater containment-product burden in non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic patients** and with adverse social drivers (lower education, poverty, insurance status, unmet social needs), **independent of incontinence severity**.
- **Oral pharmacotherapy can cost hundreds of dollars per month** in the United States; **no guideline-directed drug is universally covered at low cost**, even where generics exist.
- **Cost-effectiveness analysis (US payer perspective, versus conservative care):** **sacral nerve stimulation (SNS) was the costliest** option over 10 years; **intravesical onabotulinumtoxinA 100 U was the most cost-effective**.
- **Societal cost of OAB in the United States runs to tens of billions of dollars a year and is rising**; per capita costs are similar by sex, with **higher direct non-medical costs in women** (more incontinence).

#### Social Drivers of Health

- **Lower socioeconomic status is associated with UUI, more strongly as severity increases.**
- **Unmet social needs associated with OAB ± UUI:** food insecurity, financial strain, employment difficulty, access to care (transport and cost), housing instability, **lack of working toilets, plumbing and utilities**, poor social support, interpersonal stress and violence.
- **Implication:** patient-centred screening for unmet social needs belongs in comprehensive OAB care.

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> OAB is <b>common, under-presented and under-treated</b>: the burden is driven by UUI and by social context, while the modest efficacy and poor persistence of anticholinergics explain why so few symptomatic people end up on effective treatment.</p>

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND ETIOLOGY

- **Pathophysiology is multifactorial and spans every level of neural control.**
- **DO is no longer the sine qua non of OAB** — definition now rests on urgency, and DO has limited clinical utility.
  - Only **about half of patients with DO have urgency**; only **44–69% of patients with urgency show DO** on cystometry.
- **No animal model of OAB exists** — animals cannot report urgency — so research uses **surrogate markers** in three areas: **sensory activity, motor control and LUT reflexes**.
  - **Sensory (afferent):** urgency is presumed afferent-derived. Stages: **signal transduction → afferent traffic → gating → sensitisation → conscious perception**. The **urothelium and suburothelium** drive transduction via mediators, cell interactions, cytokines and growth factors; **gut inflammation can sensitise bladder afferents**; functional brain imaging is beginning to map conscious perception.
  - **Motor:** detrusor motility and its drivers (efferent nerves, interstitial cells, local mediators) summate into **whole-bladder contractility**, which determines urodynamic findings. **Localised micromotions sit beside quiescent areas**; intravesical pressure will not be understood until both are.
  - **Reflex integration:** afferents are integrated at several central nervous system (CNS) levels (table below); **the periaqueductal grey and pontine micturition centre (PMC) in midbrain and brainstem are the main regulatory level**, and **reflex sacral voiding matters in neonates and neurological disease**.
- **Functional brain imaging** implicates the **insula and prefrontal cortex**; **altered regional activity** is reported in OAB, and brain activity changes with **SNS, tibial nerve stimulation and oral anticholinergics**.

<p class="tt">Figure 110.4 legend — levels of neural control of the LUT</p>

| Level | Afferent | Integration | Efferent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Forebrain** | **Conscious awareness** (sensation) | Homeostasis, **volition**, emotion, social suitability | **Inhibition for storage; disinhibition for voiding** |
| **Midbrain / brainstem (PMC)** | Subconscious monitoring of filling | **Voiding reflex**, homeostasis (input from vegetative organs) | Bladder inhibition / outlet excitation for storage; **bladder–outlet synergy** |
| **Sacral spinal cord** | Subconscious monitoring of filling | **Immature voiding reflexes** (infants) | Bladder and outlet motor nuclei |
| **Pelvic plexus / LUT (cellular)** | Urothelium, interstitial cells, local mediators | Myovesical plexus — **micromotions** | Widespread bladder contraction |

- **Descending forebrain input to the PMC switches storage to voiding**, with synergic regulation of the sacral nuclei (detrusor contraction, outlet relaxation).

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 1

- **ICS OAB definition:** urgency ± frequency, nocturia, ± UUI; no UTI or other obvious pathology.
- **Normal daytime frequency:** ≤7 voids; **bother** from ≥8 voids (every ≤2 h).
- **Nocturia:** defined at ≥1; **bother at ≥2, marked at ≥3**.
- **Prevalence** OAB ≈12–29%, UUI ≈1.5–17%; **women 1.5–2× OAB, 2–3× UUI**.
- **Highest prevalence:** EpiLUTS (loose definition); **lowest:** EPIC (ICS definition).
- **NOBLE definition:** ≥4 urgency episodes in 4 weeks + ≥8 voids/day or coping mechanisms.
- **Incidence** 3–8%/yr (women); **progression to UUI ≈1%/yr; remission ≈6%/yr**.
- **<6% take medication; ≈92% fail anticholinergic within 2 years; half never refill.**
- **Stopping medication:** unmet expectations > side effects > cost.
- **Most cost-effective third-line:** onabotulinumtoxinA 100 U; **costliest:** SNS.
- **DO in urgency:** 44–69%; **urgency in DO:** about half.
- **Main regulatory centre:** periaqueductal grey + PMC (midbrain/brainstem).
- **Traps:** SUI + OAB dry ≠ MUI; urinary + faecal = double incontinence, not mixed; OAB ≠ DO; BPS requires pain, OAB never requires UUI; BACH and NHANES do not measure OAB.

### Afferent Mechanisms in Overactive Bladder

- **Premise:** increased sensory activity → increased sensation → OAB. Afferent endings are **densest in the suburothelial connective tissue**.

<p class="tt">Bladder afferents — the paired contrast</p>

| | **Aδ fibres** | **C fibres** |
|---|---|---|
| Calibre and myelination | **Small calibre, thinly myelinated** | **Smallest calibre, unmyelinated** |
| Normal role | **Predominant afferent**; senses **volume and detrusor contractile state** | **"Silent"** — respond only to high-intensity stimuli (extreme distension, cold, heat, chemical irritation) |
| In pathological states (animal OAB models) | — | **Become spontaneously active and hypersensitive to low-intensity input**, via spinal second-order neurons |

- **Two broad categories:** **increased afferent signalling from the bladder** or **decreased CNS capacity to modulate afferent signals**.

#### Mechanisms of Increased Afferent Activity

<p class="tt">Urothelium-based versus myogenic hypothesis</p>

| | **Urothelium-based** | **Myogenic** |
|---|---|---|
| Primary site | **Urothelium / suburothelium** | **Detrusor smooth muscle** |
| Trigger | Local **mechanical, osmotic, inflammatory, chemical** stimuli alter receptor/channel expression and sensitivity | **Increased spontaneous excitation** within muscle |
| Transmission | **Mediator release acts on adjacent afferents** — urothelium as transducer | **Enhanced propagation** of excitation across an excessive proportion of the wall |
| Supporting findings | Intrinsic homeostatic aberration, possibly to unidentified local stimuli | **Patchy denervation** (any cause of DO) → **receptor upregulation, altered membrane potential** → spontaneous contraction; **structural changes** favour spread |
| Result | Excess afferent traffic adds to fullness signal → **urgency, micturition reflex** | Exaggerated **micromotions** → urgency; **DO when coordinated** across much of the bladder |

- **Micromotions:** localised contractions, first described in pigs, present in **normal** animals and humans.
  - **Normally:** detected by afferents as **filling sensation without any change in detrusor pressure**.
  - **In OAB and DO models: exaggerated** → urgency; **coordination across a large area → urodynamic DO**.

*Other mechanisms of increased afferent signalling.* **Signals arising outside the bladder can act through shared afferent pathways** — convergence at **dorsal root ganglia, spinal cord or brain** gives **pelvic organ crosstalk**.
- **Bowel is the best-understood partner** — it shares thoracic and sacral spinal sensory innervation.
- **Animal work:** **rectal distension or inflammation** causes detrusor inhibition and/or overactivity via **convergent bladder–bowel C-fibre afferents**; colonic stimulation heightens bladder pain responses.
- **Clinical correlates:** **rectal distension changes bladder capacity, sensation and DO**; **constipation and straining impair emptying and worsen storage and voiding symptoms**.

*Abnormal handling of afferent signals.* Continuous normal afferent traffic (**"afferent noise"**) conveys pain, mechanical, chemical and motor information; **only a fraction reaches sensation**, the rest serves reflexes.
- **The CNS suppresses unnecessary sensations and facilitates homeostatic ones.**
- **If disease or dysfunction impairs this modulation, normally unconscious signals are perceived as urgency.**

#### Other Pathophysiologic Mechanisms

- **Ischaemia–reperfusion** (e.g. atherosclerosis, arterial obstruction) → **hypoxia and oxidative stress** → damage to urothelium, smooth muscle, microvasculature and nerves.
- **Chronic inflammation:** raised **cytokines, chemokines and growth factors** in bladder tissue, urine and blood.
- **Urinary microbiome:** composition **differs in UUI and OAB**; its link to bladder–brain signalling is unknown.

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> every mechanism converges on <b>one final common pathway — more afferent signal reaching awareness</b>: either the bladder (urothelium, muscle), a neighbouring organ or ischaemia–inflammation turns the signal up, or the CNS fails to turn it down.</p>

### Etiology and Attributable Risk Factors

- **Cause usually unknown and multifactorial**; risk factors initiate or amplify the mechanisms above.
- **Ageing:** clearly associated, through widespread cellular and CNS change.
- **Sex:** higher prevalence in **women, especially younger women**, and more UUI.
- **Bladder outlet obstruction (BOO):** the standard **animal model of DO**, but the **human link is not clear-cut**:
  - **OAB prevalence in women (BOO rare) is broadly similar to that in men (BOO common)**.
  - **DO often persists after prostate surgery.**
  - **Conclusion:** BOO is relevant but probably **indirect — accelerating age-related change** rather than directly causal.
- **Race and ethnicity:** associations exist; mechanisms unknown.
- **Psychological factors:** **depression and anxiety** most studied; also self-esteem, intimacy, embarrassment and shame.
  - **OAB wet carries more psychological burden than OAB dry.**
  - Possible mechanisms: **serotonergic CNS pathways; hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysregulation** — **stress acts via this route**.
  - **Women with OAB and at least mild anxiety** have worse OAB QoL and **greater temporal summation of heat pain — a marker of central sensitisation**.
- **Toileting behaviours:** women with OAB show **convenience ("just in case") voiding, delayed voiding, straining and positional preferences** — possible risk factors.
- **Coping behaviours:** **strategising** (mapping toilets, altering plans), **fluid restriction** and unhealthy toileting — associated with **stress and anxiety independent of symptoms**, self-reinforcing, and can sustain a **negative perception of the bladder even after objective improvement**.
- **Wider associations:** **bowel function, metabolic syndrome, hormonal status, psychosocial burden**.

- **Main integration in midbrain and brainstem; failed higher modulation → unconscious signals felt as urgency.**
- **Two bladder-centric theories: urothelium-based and myogenic.**
- **BOO and OAB: separate consequences of ageing, not directly causal.**

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> <b>BOO is a model, not a cause</b>: similar OAB prevalence in women and men and persistence of DO after de-obstruction show that ageing, psychology and behaviour sit upstream of, or alongside, obstruction.</p>

## CLINICAL ASSESSMENT

### Initial Evaluation

- **OAB is diagnosed by characteristic LUTS**; **empirical treatment** may start on history and non-invasive tests that characterise symptoms and exclude an acute cause.

<p class="tt">Guideline bodies — minimum initial evaluation</p>

| | **AUA/SUFU** (American Urological Association / Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine & Urogenital Reconstruction), non-neurogenic OAB | **EAU** (European Association of Urology), male and female non-neurogenic LUTS |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum | **History, physical examination, urinalysis** | **Concurs** |
| Questionnaires | Recommends **UDI / UDI-6, IIQ or OAB-q** | **Strongly encourages validated questionnaires** |
| Bladder diary | Not specified | **Consider bladder diary** |
| Urodynamics, cystoscopy, renal/bladder ultrasound | **Not in initial work-up of uncomplicated patient** | Urodynamics only when the result will change management (consensus position) |

#### History

Elements to cover:
- **Presence and bother of each OAB symptom** (urgency, UUI, daytime frequency, nocturia), **other storage symptoms (SUI)** and **voiding symptoms suggesting BOO** (straining, hesitancy, incomplete emptying); also **post-micturition symptoms, dysuria, haematuria**.
  - **Questionnaires help but are limited by poor health literacy or language barriers** (Table 110.3).
- **Fluid intake — type and volume:** caffeine and polydipsia worsen LUTS; patients often already restrict intake.
- **Occult neurological disease:** e.g. **new OAB with erectile dysfunction or tremor**, or previous **spinal surgery / disc disease** ± sensory or musculoskeletal symptoms.
- **Obstetric, gynaecological, surgical and radiotherapy history; bowel symptoms; medications.**
  - **Faecal incontinence: 7–15% of the general population, but close to 50% of women with OAB.**
- **Comorbidity** that (a) causes LUTS directly (e.g. **polyuria from poorly controlled diabetes**), (b) constrains drug choice, or (c) increases urine output (**obstructive sleep apnoea, psychogenic polydipsia, lithium-induced diabetes insipidus**).

<p class="tt">Relative contraindications identified at history</p>

| Drug class | Relative contraindications |
|---|---|
| **Antimuscarinics** | **Poorly controlled closed-angle glaucoma; cognitive impairment; history of urinary retention; impaired gastric emptying** |
| **β3-adrenergic agonists** | **Poorly controlled hypertension; cardiac arrhythmia** |

### Additional Assessment

#### Physical Examination

- **Directed examination is mandatory even when the diagnosis seems straightforward:** abdomen and pelvis, extremities, basic neurological examination, **cognition and frailty**.
- **General appearance** (gait, mobility, stature, frailty) is a surrogate for performance status and neurological disease.
- **Abdomen:** scars, masses, hernias, tenderness; **suprapubic tenderness or distension**.
- **Lower limbs:** **peripheral oedema** — fluid shifts on lying down, **relevant to nocturia**.
- **Pelvic and genital examination (± digital rectal examination, DRE, in men) is perhaps the most important component.**
  - **Women:** pelvic floor tone and pain, **pelvic organ prolapse**, **urethral prolapse or caruncle, labial adhesions**, **vaginal oestrogen status (atrophic vaginitis contributes to OAB)**, **supine stress test** for SUI when indicated.
  - **Men: prostate size and abnormality on DRE.**
  - **All: perineal sensation and anal tone.**
- **Cognition:** brief assessment in all (observation at interview); if concerned, **Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)**.
- **Frailty:** consider formal assessment in older patients — **frailty predicts poorer surgical outcomes and complications**, including in urology.
  - **Timed Up and Go Test (TUGT):** stand from sitting → walk 10 feet, timed; simple, usable in any clinic.

#### Postvoid Residual Volume

- **Not required at initial work-up, nor before lifestyle, behavioural or oral drug treatment, in uncomplicated patients.**
- **Measure postvoid residual (PVR) in:** complicated patients; **risk factors for retention**; **obstructive or voiding symptoms** (especially **men**); **previous anti-incontinence or prostate surgery**; **neurological disease**.
- **Method:** **portable ultrasound bladder scanner** (non-invasive, cheap, comfortable) → **catheterisation** if indicated or no scanner. Palpation may reveal a distended bladder.

#### Urinalysis

- **Mandatory in all** — excludes **UTI, haematuria, pyuria**.
- **Culture if infection suspected; microhaematuria → full haematuria evaluation.**

#### Other Laboratory Testing

- **Serum creatinine and urea: not routine**, but review if available.
- **Prostate-specific antigen (PSA):** consider in male LUTS **if it would alter treatment or decisions**.
- **Urine culture at discretion** in **acute-onset irritative symptoms with a negative dipstick** — **urinalysis is unreliable at low bacterial counts**.

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 2

- **Aδ = predominant, volume-sensing; C = silent, recruited in pathology.**
- **Two bladder-centric hypotheses:** urothelium-based and myogenic.
- **DO after TURP / de-obstruction often persists** → BOO indirect.
- **Minimum evaluation (AUA/SUFU and EAU):** history, examination, urinalysis.
- **Faecal incontinence:** 7–15% general population vs ≈50% of women with OAB.
- **Antimuscarinic relative contraindications:** closed-angle glaucoma (poorly controlled), cognitive impairment, retention, impaired gastric emptying.
- **β3 agonist relative contraindications:** poorly controlled hypertension, arrhythmia.
- **PVR not needed** in uncomplicated patients before first- or second-line treatment; **needed** in men with voiding symptoms, retention risk, prior outlet/anti-incontinence surgery, neurological disease.
- **TUGT:** 10 feet; **MMSE** for cognition.
- **AUA-recommended questionnaires:** UDI / UDI-6, IIQ, OAB-q.
- **Traps:** urinalysis mandatory, culture not; PSA not routine; creatinine not routine; OAB-V8 is screening only; atrophic vaginitis is a treatable contributor.

#### Bladder Diary

- **Record of fluid intake and voiding behaviour.** Most useful when patients **cannot describe or recall** their pattern, or when **polyuria** is suspected.
- **Uses:** diagnosis; tailoring **behavioural and lifestyle change**; **objective measure of treatment response**; **self-monitoring effect** — patients recognise triggers.
- **Duration: ideally 3–7 days; because this is burdensome, a minimum of 24–48 hours.**
- **Records:** time and **volume** of each void, incontinence episodes and circumstances, **degree of urgency** (Table 110.4).
- **OAB pattern: frequent, small-volume voids.**
  - **Daytime frequency counts waking voids, including the last before sleep and the first on rising.**
  - **Maximum voided volume = functional bladder capacity** — a marker of OAB severity and of DO severity.
- **Limitation:** compliance; **electronic diaries and smartphone applications** help.

<p class="tt">TABLE 110.4 Bladder Sensation Scale Used in the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire Bladder Diary</p>

| Grade | Sensation |
|---|---|
| **0** | **No desire** — voided for "social reasons" (before going out, uncertain toilet access) |
| **1** | **Normal desire, no urgency** |
| **2** | **Urgency that passed** before reaching the toilet |
| **3** | **Urgency persisting** to the toilet, **no leakage** |
| **4** | Urgency with **leakage before reaching the toilet** |

<p class="src">From Bright E, Cotterill N, Drake M, et al.: Developing a validated urinary diary: phase 1, Neurourol Urodyn 31(5):625–633, 2012.</p>

- **What the table is for:** grading each void so that **urgency, not just frequency**, is captured on the diary.
- **Pattern:** grades 0–1 are **normal or pre-emptive** voids; **grade 2 upward is urgency**; the top grade is UUI.
- **Trap:** a grade 0 ("just in case") void inflates frequency without urgency — **convenience voiding**, a behaviour, not OAB.

#### Patient-Reported Outcome Measures and Symptom Questionnaires

- **OAB is symptom-based, so patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs)** of frequency, severity and bother are central to evaluation and to **monitoring response**.
- **International Consultation on Incontinence (ICI) grades PROMs A to C, with "+" where further content-validity evidence exists.**
- **ICIQ modules** (first developed after the 1996 ICI): standardised, easy to use; **pair each symptom module with its HRQoL module**.
  - **OAB set:** **ICIQ-MLUTS / ICIQ-FLUTS** (general, sex-specific) + **ICIQ-OAB** (condition-specific) + **ICIQ-OABqol** (QoL); **ICIQ-UI** core incontinence module. Many exist in short and long forms.
- **AUA/SUFU recommend UDI, UDI-6, IIQ or OAB-q.**
- **LURN SI:** one instrument for **both sexes and the full LUTS spectrum** (OAB, pelvic floor disorders, all incontinence types); **29-item long form (with two sex-specific items) and 10-item short form**.

### Advanced Evaluation

- **Urodynamics, cystoscopy and renal/bladder ultrasound are not for the initial work-up of the uncomplicated patient.**

<p class="tt">Urodynamics — when to use, when not</p>

| **Indicated** | **Not indicated** |
|---|---|
| Result (compliance, DO, other abnormality) **will change the decision to pursue or avoid an invasive, irreversible or potentially morbid treatment** | **Initial work-up** of uncomplicated OAB |
| **Refractory to multiple OAB treatments** | **Index patient** — woman, low PVR, no significant prolapse, no neurological disease, minimal SUI — no proven outcome benefit |
| **Suspected BOO**, e.g. persistent or new OAB **after an outlet procedure** | |

- **Urodynamics may improve treatment satisfaction** but has not been shown to improve outcomes in the index patient.
- **Aim:** **reproduce the patient's symptoms** and identify factors that change management.
- **Two urodynamic diagnoses associated with OAB:** **DO** and **early and/or exaggerated filling sensation** ("increased bladder sensation" — **early and persistent desire to void**, the usual finding when DO is absent).
- **Antimuscarinics before the study — either way is defensible:**

| **Stop the drug** | **Continue the drug** |
|---|---|
| **Best chance of demonstrating DO** | Shows the **mechanism of residual refractory symptoms** on treatment |

- **DO correlates with urgency severity and with incontinence:**

<p class="tt">DO on cystometry by symptom group</p>

| Group | **Men** | **Women** |
|---|---|---|
| **OAB dry** | **60–70%** | **40–50%** |
| **OAB wet (UUI)** | **>90%** | Absent in **30–40%** — UUI is a **less reliable predictor** in women |

- **Why women's UUI predicts DO less well:** **SUI misread as urgency leakage**, and **pre-emptive voiding for SUI misread as frequency or urgency**.

<p class="tt">Figure 110.5 legend</p>

- **Overlap of symptom-based OAB (increased daytime frequency, urgency, nocturia, UUI) with urodynamic DO: correlation best for UUI, worst for nocturia.**

- **Technique points:**
  - **Provocation if DO not seen:** whatever triggers symptoms — **running water, standing**.
  - **Filling rate:** **fast filling can induce DO**, and repeated DO can obscure other parameters.
  - **Position:** **seated or standing** — OAB is experienced upright. If **severe low-volume DO masks SUI**, a **second fill supine usually reduces DO**.
- **Always assess for BOO or inefficient emptying**, for two reasons:
  1. **Storage-directed treatments may impair voiding** → intermittent self-catheterisation. **Each 10 mL rise in PVR raises the risk of catheterisation after onabotulinumtoxinA by 27%.**
  2. **Severe BOO is associated with DO**, so OAB may not improve unless BOO is treated — **yet relieving BOO does not guarantee improvement: DO persists in up to 60% of men after transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP).**

<p class="kpt">KEY POINTS: OVERACTIVE BLADDER CLINICAL ASSESSMENT</p>

- **Minimum: history, examination, urinalysis**; consider **malignancy, neurological or systemic disease, raised PVR**.
- **Culture and PVR as indicated; diaries and PROMs clarify and track.**
- **No urodynamics, cystoscopy or ultrasound initially; urodynamics when it changes an invasive decision or after multiple failures.**
</div>

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> assessment escalates <b>only when the answer would change management</b>: the uncomplicated patient is treated on history, examination and urinalysis, and every further test (PVR, diary, urodynamics) is justified by a specific decision it informs.</p>

## MANAGEMENT

- **OAB is chronic, affects QoL and is not life-threatening** — counsel in that frame.
- **No treatment is an acceptable choice** after counselling and exclusion of other conditions — best where **costs (financial, physical, opportunity) outweigh benefit**, or the patient **cannot perceive improvement** (significant cognitive impairment).
- **Consider contributors such as limited mobility** — curing the bladder will not cure immobility-driven incontinence.
- **Exception — incontinence-associated dermatitis:** painful, risks infection, pressure ulcers and wounds → **may justify more aggressive treatment**.
- **Set individual goals** (fewer pads, dryness, activities, independence, avoiding lifelong drugs or surgery); **goals may change**.
- **Realistic expectations:** most treatments **improve but do not eliminate** symptoms; improvement is **gradual and irregular**. **Critical before invasive treatment.**
- **Education** on normal bladder function empowers patients; **success depends on an engaged patient**. **The consistent placebo response in OAB trials** shows the value of non-pharmacological input, much of it education.
- **AUA/SUFU "lines" (first to fourth) rank invasiveness, adverse-event severity and reversibility against benefit — they are not a mandatory linear path; lines are often combined.**

### Conservative Management

- **Options:** bladder training, bladder control strategies, **pelvic floor muscle therapy (PFMT)**, **weight loss**, **fluid management**. Evidence is mainly on **incontinence in women**. Goal is improvement, not cure.
- **Fluid reduction:** improves symptom scores at the cost of **thirst and under-hydration**; many patients **already restrict** as coping. **A 25% reduction in intake meaningfully reduces symptoms** — encourage where appropriate.
- **Caffeine reduction:** **improves urgency and frequency but not consistently incontinence** — patient decides if the trade-off is worth it.
- **Weight loss:** reduces SUI **and UUI**. In a randomised trial of obese incontinent women, **intensive weight loss (≈8 kg vs ≈1.5 kg in controls) meaningfully reduced UUI episodes**. **Bariatric surgery: each 5% weight loss → ≈7–8% lower risk of incontinence** — discuss honestly in severe obesity.
- **PFMT and bladder training:** **50–80% reduction in incontinence episodes**, negligible risk, **endorsed by multiple societies**.
- **Combine behavioural with drug therapy** where the patient wishes — limited evidence of better outcomes.

### Pharmacologic Management

- **Two classes for OAB: antimuscarinics and β3-adrenergic agonists.**
- **Counsel on antimuscarinic side effects:** dry mouth, constipation, **cognitive effects**, visual impairment.
- **Dementia:** Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine & Urogenital Reconstruction (SUFU) white paper — **chronic use (>3 months) → 10–65% increased risk of new-onset dementia**.
  - → **Shared decision-making** with patient and/or caregivers.
  - → **Preferred: β3 agonists**, or **extended-release trospium, darifenacin or fesoterodine** (properties that limit CNS effects).
- **Response is idiosyncratic** — expect adjustment of agent and dose.
- **Antimuscarinics raise the likelihood of cure over placebo by 20–70%, but withdrawal (inefficacy or adverse effects) is high.**
- **Extended-release over immediate-release** — less dry mouth, fewer cognitive effects.
- **Inadequate response or intolerance → change dose or switch agent**; then **different agent or combination**, which may avoid invasive investigation and treatment.

<p class="tt">Drugs for OAB and male storage LUTS</p>

| Class | Role | Preferred agents / key point | Signature risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Antimuscarinics** (oral or transdermal) | OAB ± UUI; add-on in men with storage LUTS | **Extended-release; trospium, darifenacin, fesoterodine** limit CNS effects | **Dementia with >3 months' use**; dry mouth, constipation, visual blurring; **acute retention up to 3% in men** |
| **β3-adrenergic agonists** | OAB; add-on in men with storage LUTS | **Preferred class** where CNS risk matters | Relative contraindications as above |
| **α1-adrenergic antagonists** | **First-line for male voiding LUTS** | **Combination with antimuscarinic beats either alone** in benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) with OAB | Retention risk rises when antimuscarinic added |

- **Men:** combination therapy's retention risk → **caution when PVR is elevated**.

### Procedural or Surgical Management

- **"Third-line" (AUA/SUFU):** after failure of, or unsuitability for, conservative and drug therapy — or **earlier** when medical, social or personal barriers make those unworkable.
- **More invasive, more adverse events.**
- **Frail and elderly:** frail patients **do respond well**, but **frailty predicts functional decline, discharge to skilled or assisted nursing, and 1-year morbidity up to 50% even after minor urological procedures** — weigh carefully.
- **Choice (including no further treatment) is by patient preference after informed discussion.**

<p class="tt">Third-line therapies for refractory OAB</p>

| | **Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS)** | **Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA** | **Sacral nerve stimulation (SNS)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Efficacy | **Clinical response 60–80%** of drug-refractory patients | Frequency ↓29%, urgency ↓38%, **incontinence episodes ↓59%** (pooled placebo-controlled trials); **≈25% completely dry** | **5-year success ≈70–80%** (fewer UUI episodes and voids) |
| Main drawback | **Weekly office sessions for 12-week induction; response delayed to weeks 8–10** — limits compliance | **Symptomatic UTI ≈20%**; **intermittent catheterisation 0–12% in trials, but retention ≈20% in women and up to 35% in men in real-world series** | **Revision >30% at 5 years** (stimulation change, pain, inefficacy); **battery replacement eventually for all** |
| Risk profile | **Lowest** | Moderate | Highest; **costliest** |
| Cost-effectiveness | — | **Most cost-effective (100 U)** | **Costliest** |
| Recent advances | — | — | **Rechargeable, longer battery life, MRI-compatible** devices widen eligibility |

- **What this table is for:** the trade-off between **burden of attendance (PTNS), risk of retention and UTI (onabotulinumtoxinA) and revision and battery surgery (SNS)**.
- **Trap:** trial catheterisation rates for onabotulinumtoxinA **understate real-world retention**, especially in men.

### Augmentation Cystoplasty or Urinary Diversion

- **Rarely**, OAB is severe enough to justify **augmentation cystoplasty or urinary diversion**.
- **Evidence is mostly neurogenic** — QoL impact in non-neurogenic OAB is poorly known.
- **Weigh against short- and long-term risks of major surgery and against containment (pads) or indwelling catheter.**

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
<p class="kpt">KEY POINTS: CLINICAL MANAGEMENT</p>

- **Conservative → antimuscarinic (oral or transdermal) or β3 agonist → tibial or sacral neuromodulation or onabotulinumtoxinA → augmentation or diversion**, by shared decision-making.
- **Chronic antimuscarinics carry dementia risk.**
</div>

### Approach

- **Sixth ICI** provides evidence-graded pathways for **women, men and the frail elderly** (Figure 110.6); **AUA/SUFU** and **SUFU OAB Clinical Care Pathway** provide algorithms.
- **Common sequence:** careful assessment to confirm OAB and exclude alternatives → **patient education** → **conservative** (lifestyle, behavioural therapy, PFMT) → **pharmacological** → **invasive** (tibial nerve stimulation, sacral neuromodulation, chemodenervation).
- **At every step reassess symptoms, side effects and preference.**
- **Minimum trial before judging efficacy: 4–12 weeks for behavioural therapy and 4–12 weeks for drugs.**

| Stage | **Men** | **Women** | **Frail elderly** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Presentation | Urgency/frequency ± incontinence | Incontinence with mixed symptoms | **Active case finding** |
| Initial assessment | General: symptom score (incl. **frequency–volume chart**, FVC) and questionnaire, QoL and desire for treatment, **urinalysis ± culture** (treat infection, reassess). Examination: abdominal, **rectal, sacral, neurological**; pelvic floor function; **PVR** | General as for men. Examination: abdominal, **pelvic, perineal**; **cough test for SUI**; **oestrogen status**; voluntary pelvic floor contraction; PVR | Assess and treat **potentially treatable conditions and comorbidity**; QoL, goals, **patient and caregiver preference**; targeted examination incl. **cognition**, neurological, rectal; urinalysis; **FVC or wet checks, especially with nocturia** |
| Presumed diagnosis | OAB ± UUI | **Mixed incontinence — treat most bothersome symptom first** | — |
| Initial management | Lifestyle; PFMT ± biofeedback; scheduled voiding; containment products; **antimuscarinic**, **+ α-blocker if BOO** | Lifestyle; PFMT for SUI or OAB; bladder retraining for OAB; antimuscarinic | Lifestyle; behavioural therapy; **consider trial of antimuscarinic**; **treat significant PVR** |

- **Pattern:** **men — treat the outlet alongside the bladder**; **women — treat the most bothersome component of mixed incontinence first**; **frail elderly — treat comorbidity and function first, then the bladder**.

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> management is a <b>ladder ordered by invasiveness and reversibility, not a compulsory sequence</b>: each rung buys greater efficacy at greater risk, so patient goals, frailty and PVR — not the next line on the algorithm — decide how far and how fast to climb.</p>
