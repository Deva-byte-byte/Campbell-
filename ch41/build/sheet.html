Chapter 41 — Management Strategies for Vesicoureteral Reflux — Numbers, Gold Standards and Traps

If you read nothing else

  • VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) with UTI (urinary tract infection) about 30%; other indications about 17%.
  • Gold standard diagnosis: VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) or direct RNC (radionuclide cystography); both need a catheter.
  • Gold standard parenchyma: 99mTc-DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid); scar sensitivity 98%, specificity 92%.
  • Sterile VUR does not scar (Ransley–Risdon pig model).
  • Resolution: grade I–II most; III about 50%; IV–V few.
  • RIVUR: CAP (continuous antibiotic prophylaxis) halves recurrent UTI (23.6% → 12.9%); scarring unchanged.
  • Swedish Reflux Trial: girls on surveillance 57% febrile UTI vs 19% CAP.
  • BBD (bladder and bowel dysfunction) → recurrent UTI about 45% vs 15%; treat BBD first.
  • Breakthrough febrile UTI on CAP → correct VUR.
  • Scars, not VUR, drive pregnancy morbidity and hypertension.

Embryology and functional anatomy

  • Ureteric bud: weeks 4–6, from the mesonephric duct.
  • Mackie–Stephens: early bud → high lateral orifice → VUR; late bud → ectopic, obstructed.
  • Paquin: tunnel:diameter 5:1 normal vs 1.4:1 refluxing.
  • Waldeyer's sheath: outer ureteric + outer detrusor muscle → deep trigone.

Demographics

  • Infant boys predominate (up to 3:1 at 0–6 months) → 1:1 by 2 years; overall girls about 2×.
  • Uncircumcised infant boys: 10–12× UTI.
  • Circumcision NNT (number needed to treat): 111 general vs 4 with VUR; topical steroid = circumcision.
  • VUR with UTI by age: <2 y 38.6% · 2–6 y 26.6% · 7–11 y 19.7% · 12–21 y 7.6%.
  • Black children: about 3× less; no grade IV–V under 6; equal after 10.
  • Prenatal hydronephrosis 1–5%; VUR in 10–40% of screened infants.
  • Newborn VCUG only for: high-grade or bilateral hydronephrosis, cortical change, dilated ureter, ureterocele, duplex, bladder anomaly.

Inheritance and genetics

  • Siblings about 30% (to 50%); higher <3 years; identical twins near-complete concordance.
  • AUA (American Urological Association): sibling VCUG if cortical abnormality, size asymmetry, UTI, or hydronephrosis grade 3–4.
  • No mass sibling screening.

Grading

  • International classification (1981): I ureter · II pelvis, not dilated · III mild dilatation · IV tortuosity · V gross, papillae lost.
  • RNC grades: 1 = I; 2 = II–III; 3 = IV–V.
  • UDR (ureteral diameter ratio) = max ureter in false pelvis ÷ L1–L3 distance; predicts resolution and BUTI (breakthrough UTI).
  • Interobserver agreement poor in grades II–IV.

Aetiology

  • Spina bifida: VUR up to 30% → early CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) + anticholinergic.
  • Normal voiding about 100 cm H₂O without VUR → structural UVJ (ureterovesical junction) failure is key.
  • Detrusor overactivity = commonest urodynamic finding with VUR.
  • Reflux during filling = poor prognosis for resolution.

Diagnosis and lower tract

  • VCUG filling height ≤70 cm H₂O.
  • RNC dose: about 1% of continuous-fluoroscopy VCUG; misses grade I; best for follow-up.
  • Indirect RNC: no catheter; needs toilet training.
  • Cyclic VCUG → 12–20% more VUR detected.
  • VCUG timing: once urine sterile and child improved (classically ≥1 week).
  • Contrast-enhanced urosonography: no radiation; ≈ VCUG.
  • Routine cystoscopy: no role.
  • PIC (positional instillation of contrast): recurrent febrile UTI + negative VCUG; contrast from 1 m under anaesthesia; not first line.
  • Invasive urodynamics: refractory cases only; uroflow + EMG (electromyography) non-invasive.
  • VUR + UVJ obstruction signs → relative contraindication to endoscopic injection.

Upper tract and cortical defects

  • Ultrasound cannot diagnose VUR; normal in about 25% of refluxing infants.
  • VUR about 3× cortical defects after infection; VUR present in 30% of pyelonephritis.
  • Congenital damage: grades IV–V, male infants, no UTI; grades I–III normal.
  • Greatest scarring risk: first year; <4 years after a single UTI.
  • Big bang (Ransley–Risdon): most scarring after the first pyelonephritis.
  • Compound papillae at the poles → intrarenal reflux; neonate at 2 mm Hg.
  • Hypertension in 34–38% with scars; reflux nephropathy = commonest secondary cause in children.
  • VUR correction does not cure hypertension; segmental resection or nephrectomy may.
  • USRDS (US Renal Data System): reflux nephropathy RRT (renal replacement therapy) 1.9 → 0.6 per million (1996–2014).

Guidelines

  • AAP (American Academy of Pediatrics) 2011, febrile UTI 2–24 months: ultrasound first; VCUG after a second febrile UTI or abnormal ultrasound.
  • AAP culture threshold: 50,000 CFU/mL (colony-forming units) + abnormal urinalysis; oral = parenteral.
  • NICE (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence): >3 years → no routine VCUG; 6 months–3 years response within 48 h → no VCUG.
  • TDA (top-down approach): DMSA first; VCUG only if abnormal.
  • Normal DMSA → <1% high-grade VUR; abnormal in about 70% of febrile UTIs.

Associated anomalies

  • UPJO (ureteropelvic junction obstruction) in VUR 0.75–3.6%; high grade 5× more.
  • VUR + UPJO → fix obstruction first.
  • Duplex: VUR = commonest anomaly, lower pole (Weigert–Meyer).
  • Paraureteral diverticulum → resolves like primary; ureter into diverticulum → won't resolve.
  • MCDK (multicystic dysplastic kidney): contralateral VUR about 25%.
  • Renal agenesis: contralateral VUR 24–28%.
  • Megacystis-megaureter: non-obstructive; normal urethra; reimplant; never operate on the outlet.
  • Obstructive refluxing megaureter: dilute ureteric contrast; injection may obstruct.

Follow-up, adulthood and pregnancy

  • Dx/HA (dextranomer/hyaluronic acid) mound calcifies in >50% → mimics stone.
  • VUR in pregnancy: pyelonephritis about 33% vs <5%.
  • Scars → hypertension about 3×, pre-eclampsia about 8×.
  • Offspring of affected mothers: VUR about 43%.

Natural history

  • Grade II 63–85%; grade III about 50%, about 90% of these within 4 years.
  • Observation period: 5 years; age at presentation > grade as a predictor.

Landmark studies

  • IRSC (International Reflux Study in Children): high grade, CAP vs surgery → UTI 38% both; scars equal.
  • IRSC 10 years: equal growth and renal function.
  • Birmingham: scars equal, all within 2 years.
  • Swedish: grade III–IV, 1–2 y: girls 19% CAP, 23% endoscopic, 57% surveillance; boys no difference.
  • RIVUR: 607, grade I–IV, TMP-SMX (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) vs placebo; NNT high risk 5 vs low risk 18.
  • PRIVENT: absolute UTI reduction 6% (19% → 13%).

Current medical management

  • Open correction >95% success.
  • CAP: little benefit grade ≤II without BBD; benefit grade ≥III in girls.
  • 15% have febrile UTI within 2 years; 15% of these scar.
  • Off CAP: 8–10% develop febrile UTI.
  • <2 months: trimethoprim or amoxicillin; no sulfonamide (jaundice).
  • Nitrofurantoin: interstitial lung disease (rare, full dose).
  • Dose at night.
  • Smellie BUTI: sensitive organism → non-adherence or low dose; resistant → high residual.
  • Hidas 2-year BUTI on CAP: low 8.6%, intermediate 26%, high 62.5%.
  • High risk: female, <1 y, white, bilateral, grade III–V, BBD, scars, multiple febrile UTIs.

Classic traps

  • Trap: VUR causes UTI — Actually: it accelerates pyelonephritis only.
  • Trap: every renal defect with VUR is a scar — Actually: high-grade male infants have congenital dysplasia.
  • Trap: normal ultrasound excludes VUR — Actually: ultrasound cannot diagnose VUR.
  • Trap: CAP prevents scarring (RIVUR) — Actually: prevents UTI only.
  • Trap: correcting VUR cures hypertension — Actually: parenchymal; correction does not.
  • Trap: RNC detects all grades — Actually: misses grade I.
  • Trap: fix VUR first with coexisting UPJO — Actually: relieve obstruction first.
  • Trap: megacystis-megaureter = valves — Actually: non-obstructive; normal urethra on VCUG.
  • Trap: TMP-SMX in a 1-month-old — Actually: sulfonamide jaundice; use trimethoprim or amoxicillin.
  • Trap: one negative fill–void cycle excludes VUR — Actually: cyclic study finds 12–20% more.
