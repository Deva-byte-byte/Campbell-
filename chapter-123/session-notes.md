@@INST Instalment 1 — Chapter Preamble (Epidemiology, Barriers, Gold Standard), Classification, Pathophysiology and Aetiology, Mechanism of Continence With Surgical Devices (Compression Devices, Non-adjustable and Adjustable Slings) · pp. 2584–2585

- **Male stress urinary incontinence (SUI)**, the involuntary leakage of urine, **substantially impairs quality of life (QoL)**. Causes: congenital anomalies, injury, genitourinary surgery, other conditions.
- **Radical prostatectomy (RP) for prostate cancer (CaP) is the leading cause.** Mechanism: **failure to store because external sphincter resistance is insufficient**.
- **The US burden of post-RP urinary incontinence (UI) remains high** because of RP volume, but is **expected to fall**. Reasons: better technique; fewer RPs after the **2012 US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommendation against prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening**; more **active surveillance**.
- **QoL impact drives treatment-seeking.** Perineal slings and the artificial urinary sphincter (AUS) work by **increasing outlet resistance**.
- **Barriers to care are physician and patient perceptions.** **Decisional regret is highest after conservative treatment**, which argues for outreach.
- **The AUS is the gold standard, effective for all degrees of SUI.** **Bulbar urethral slings**, now several generations old with long-term data, suit **mild-to-moderate UI**.

## CLASSIFICATION, PATHOPHYSIOLOGY, AND ETIOLOGY

- **Continence needs the bladder, the internal sphincter (bladder neck and prostatic urethra) and the external sphincter (rhabdosphincter).** Male SUI (leakage with raised abdominal pressure) occurs **only when both sphincters are impaired**.

<p class="tcap">Internal versus external sphincter incompetence — causes</p>

| Internal sphincter | External sphincter |
|---|---|
| Pelvic surgery or radiation | **Radical prostatectomy (commonest)** |
| Bladder neck injury | Pelvic radiation; pelvic fracture urethral injury |
| Specific sympathetic neuropathic dysfunction | Traumatic and acquired myelopathy |
| Embryological disruption | Spinal dysraphism, sacral agenesis, exstrophy–epispadias complex |

- **Reduced compliance, detrusor overactivity (DO) and urgency urinary incontinence (UUI) complicate diagnosis** and demand careful evaluation.
- **Prevalence: about 11% of men aged 60–64 and 31% over 85.** Incidence tracks the causative procedures.
- **Historical post-RP UI ranges from 2.5% to 87%.** The spread reflects patient factors, surgeon factors and method. **Self-report gives higher rates than physician-recorded outcomes.**
- **Continence improves progressively for up to 2 years after RP.**
- **Nerve sparing reduces post-RP SUI**, probably by preserving sensory and motor pudendal innervation of the rhabdosphincter. It gives **faster return, not better final continence** (meta-analyses).
- **Autonomic afferent denervation and impaired membranous urethral sensitivity** are associated with SUI.
- **Other mechanisms**: damage to the rhabdosphincter, pelvic floor or bladder neck. Incontinent men differ in **urethral pressure profile, length and sphincter morphology**.
- **Posterior reconstruction speeds recovery but does not improve long-term continence.**
- **Bladder neck stenosis lowers continence**, mainly because **secondary procedures damage the external sphincter**.
- **Vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis (VUAS)** impairs coaptation. Its treatment can cause SUI because **the contracture extends into the membranous urethra**, which shows endoscopically as **loss of coaptation and radiating folds**.
- **Climacturia** (leakage with arousal or orgasm) is little studied; neurogenic mechanisms have been proposed.

**Concept —** *Male continence runs on **two sphincters in series**. RP removes the internal one, which leaves the **rhabdosphincter carrying continence alone**. Every post-RP risk factor damages that sphincter, its innervation or its coaptation.*

## MECHANISM OF CONTINENCE WITH SURGICAL DEVICES

### Compression Devices

- **AUS (AMS 800)**: a fluid-filled cuff around the bladder neck or bulbar urethra gives **circumferential compression over about 2 cm**. **The compliance of the pressure-regulating balloon (PRB) sets the compression**, and the PRB is chosen for tissue quality and cuff site.
- **Standard bulbar PRB is 61–70 cm H₂O, the balance between occlusion and erosion.** A lower pressure gives less continence and is used when erosion risk is excessive.
- **An open cuff removes the resistance**, so men **without detrusor contractility can still void by straining or Credé manoeuvre**.
- **ProACT**: **paired silicone balloons beside the bladder neck give lateral compression**. They are **adjusted through two scrotal ports**.

### Male Slings

#### Nonadjustable Slings

- **Transobturator slings reposition and lengthen the membranous urethra without significant compression.**
  - Urodynamics: **leak point pressure rises without obstruction**.
  - Fluoroscopy and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI): **longer membranous urethra; elevated bladder neck, posterior bladder wall and rhabdosphincter**.
- **Virtue (quadratic fixation, four arms)**: **compression against the urogenital diaphragm plus separate elevation** through prepubic and transobturator arms.

#### Adjustable Slings

- **ATOMS**: a **cushion under the bulbar urethra compresses it**, in men with a **functional sphincter**. It is **topped up with saline through a scrotal port**.

<p class="tcap">Continence devices — mechanism and adjustability</p>

| Device | Mechanism | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| AUS | **Circumferential compression**; patient cycles cuff to void | None after implant (PRB fixed) |
| ProACT | Non-circumferential, lateral compression at bladder neck | Two scrotal ports |
| Advance / Advance XP | **Repositioning, not compression** | None |
| Virtue | Compression **plus** elevation | None |
| ATOMS | Cushion compression of bulbar urethra | Scrotal port |

- **What the table is for**: mechanism predicts candidacy. **Only the AUS does not depend on residual sphincter function.**
- **The trap**: the transobturator sling is **not compressive**. It works only if there is a functioning sphincter to reposition.

**Concept —** *Devices either **replace** the sphincter (AUS) or **assist a residual sphincter** (repositioning slings), and partial compressors sit between the two. This distinction drives every later selection decision.*

@@INST Instalment 2 — Evaluation and Diagnosis I: Initial Work-up, History, Physical Examination, Patient Reported Outcome Measures, Voiding Diaries, Pad Weight Testing, AUA/SUFU Evaluation Algorithm (Fig. 123.3) · pp. 2585–2587

## EVALUATION AND DIAGNOSIS

- **Initial evaluation: history, examination, post-void residual (PVR), urinalysis.** **Cystoscopy and pressure–flow urodynamics (UDS)** are added as indicated for VUAS, residual sphincter function and storage.

### History

- Record **type, degree and severity**; prior surgery and **pelvic radiotherapy (RT)**; **radiation cystitis**; neurological symptoms.
- **Key task: classify as SUI, UUI, mixed or overflow.** **Leakage during sleep and nocturia help separate severe SUI from urgency.**
- After CaP treatment: **disease status, planned adjuvant therapy, life expectancy**.

### Physical Examination

- Examine the **abdomen, back, genitalia, perineum, rectum and nervous system**.
- **Assess manual dexterity before an AUS.** **Treat skin breakdown or fungal/bacterial infection before surgery.**
- **Previous incisions** guide PRB placement. **Inguinal hernia or prior repair** matters for an external-ring route. **Scrotal pathology** (hydrocele, hernia, mass) affects pump placement.

### Patient Reported Outcome Measures

- **Patient reported outcome measures (PROMs)** quantify symptoms and bother and inform the choice of operation.
- **UCLA Prostate Cancer Index and Expanded Prostate Cancer Index Composite (EPIC)** capture post-RP SUI and UUI. They are research tools, and their **clinical performance and responsiveness have not been assessed**.
- **Incontinence Symptom Index and Incontinence Impact Questionnaire-7** have been used for AUS outcomes but were **not developed for male SUI**.

### Voiding Diaries

- **A 2–7-day diary** counts episodes, estimates **capacity**, **identifies urgency and UUI** and helps separate SUI from UUI.

### Pad Weight Testing

- **Pad counts are unreliable** because the threshold for changing a pad varies. **The 24-h pad weight test is objective**: pre-weighed pads → worn 24 h → reweighed → **weight gain = leakage = severity**.
- **SUFU Pad Test** (Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine & Urogenital Reconstruction): a questionnaire on **number, size and wetness of pads over 24 h**. It **correlates with actual loss after RP** but is **not validated for predicting surgical outcome**.

<p class="tcap">Fig. 123.3 AUA/SUFU guideline evaluation algorithm for incontinence after prostate treatment (Sandhu et al., 2019)</p>

| Presentation | Pathway |
|---|---|
| **Urgency or urgency-predominant mixed UI** | American Urological Association (AUA)/SUFU overactive bladder guideline |
| **SUI or stress-predominant mixed UI** | Pelvic floor muscle exercises/therapy (PFME/PFMT) + goals → interval follow-up → **improved, not bothered: no treatment**; **not improved or bothered: discuss surgical vs non-surgical** → any choice may later lead to the surgical algorithm |
| **Other pathology suspected** (microhaematuria, low flow, high PVR) | Cystoscopy ± UDS → **anastomotic stricture: treat, interval cystoscopy**; other pathology: treat → back to PFMT pathway |

- **The logic**: exclude urgency-predominant disease and obstruction first. **Surgery follows only failed or unsatisfactory PFMT.**

**Concept —** *Evaluation proves the leak is **sphincteric** and measures **how much**. Type decides whether SUI surgery is relevant, and pad weight decides which device.*

### Standing Cough Test

- **A standing forceful cough (or Valsalva) in clinic demonstrates SUI** and **correlates with 24-h pad weight**.

<p class="tcap">Standing cough test — grade and mean 24-h pad weight (Yi et al., 2020)</p>

| Grade | Leakage | Pad weight |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Reported, **not demonstrable** | ~57 g |
| 1 | **Delayed drops** | ~117 g |
| 2 | **Early drops**, no stream | ~223 g |
| 3 | **Delayed stream** | ~385 g |
| 4 | **Early, persistent stream** | ~513 g |

- **What it is for**: a **clinic proxy for pad weight**. It is graded on **timing (delayed vs early)** and **form (drops vs stream)**. **Grades 0–2 fall below 400 g; grades 3–4 lie in AUS territory.**
- **The trap**: Grade 0 is not dry. The patient still loses about 57 g a day.

### Laboratory

- **Urinalysis and culture are mandatory. Investigate every abnormality and eradicate urinary tract infection (UTI).**
- **Creatinine (renal function) and PSA (cancer status).** **Evaluate renal insufficiency carefully before surgery.**

### Cystoscopy

- **Mandatory when VUAS, bladder neck contracture or stricture is suspected**, because **unrecognised obstruction compromises the outcome**.
- **Assess residual sphincter function: poor coaptation → AUS, not a transobturator sling.**
- **Recurrent SUI after AUS**: mechanical failure, fluid leak, atrophy.
- **After explant for erosion, before any new AUS**: exclude stricture and diverticulum.

### Urodynamics

- **UDS measure capacity, compliance and contractility.** **Intrinsic sphincter deficiency is present in nearly all.**
- **Not routine. Indicated if the diagnosis is in doubt**: mixed UI, SUI not demonstrable, overflow, suspected poor compliance.
- **DO with mixed UI**: counsel that **UUI may persist after surgery**.
- **Poor compliance is the graver finding**, because **high-pressure storage damages the kidneys**. It **does not reliably worsen AUS continence**, so **upper-tract deterioration can proceed silently**.
- **Detrusor hypocontractility favours the AUS**: the cuff opens for voiding (straining or Credé), whereas a sling adds **fixed** resistance that a weak detrusor may not overcome.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS**

- **History and examination** define type, severity and capacity in most men.
- **Uroflowmetry and PVR** screen for obstruction.
- **Obstruction, significant overactivity or poor contractility → UDS.**
- **Urethral, vesicourethral or bladder neck pathology → cystoscopy.**
- **Urinalysis (± culture) before surgery.**
</div>

- **Fig. 123.4**: post-RP sphincter with a **quadrant defect at 9–12 o'clock and incomplete coaptation**. **Not an ideal transobturator sling candidate**, because the sling relies on repositioning a functional sphincter.

**Concept —** *Cystoscopy and UDS each answer a **device-selection question**. Cystoscopy asks whether there is a sphincter worth repositioning. UDS asks whether the bladder can empty against fixed resistance and store safely.*

@@INST Instalment 4 — Indications for Surgery: Conservative Therapy and Timing, Shared Decision Making, Contraindications, AUS, ProACT and Sling Outcomes, Severity Thresholds, MASTER Trial, AUA/SUFU Surgical Algorithm (Fig. 123.5), Key Points · pp. 2587–2589

## INDICATIONS FOR SURGERY

- **Indication: irreversible sphincter incompetence with UI affecting QoL.**
- **Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT)** ± biofeedback or electrical stimulation **works for post-RP SUI**. It shortens time to continence (better at 1 and 3 months with stimulation), with **no benefit beyond 12 months**.
- **Do not delay surgery for severe or gravitational SUI with no improvement after 6 months**, especially with a **cystoscopic sphincter defect**.
- **Use shared decision making**, weighing QoL gain against **performance status and life expectancy**.
- **AUS and perineal slings are first-line.** **Bulking agents are not in the post-RP or post-transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) algorithm.** Reserve them for **small-volume leakage in men unfit for more**.
- **Selection factors**: severity, bother, **cognition, manual dexterity, body mass index (BMI), prior RT, prior surgery including urethroplasty, bladder function (e.g. neurogenic)**.

<p class="tcap">Contraindications to male SUI surgery</p>

| Require an alternative to SUI surgery (absolute) | Relative |
|---|---|
| **Low-compliance bladder threatening the kidneys** (reflux at low pressure) | **Frequent future transurethral management**: bladder cancer, refractory VUAS, radiation cystitis |
| **Low-capacity bladder** (severe frequency or UUI) | Repeated instrumentation → **erosion or infection** |
| **Tissue too poor for a sling or AUS** → clamps, catheters, bladder neck closure, diversion | |

- **Radiation cystitis** often needs **large-bore catheters for clot irrigation**. Give **hyperbaric oxygen and require 6 months free of refractory bleeding before an AUS**. **Repeated clot retention → cystectomy and diversion.**

### Compression Devices

#### Artificial Urinary Sphincter

- **Standard for moderate-to-severe SUI with adequate cognition and dexterity.** It works **without detrusor contraction and after RT**, and has the **longest follow-up**.
- **Success predictors: surgeon experience, high volume, intact compliance.** **Multiple reoperations are common.**

#### ProACT

- **Fewer pads and better QoL.** In a randomised trial it matched slings for continence, **with more urethral erosion**. **It does worse than slings after RT.**

### Slings

- **For mild-to-moderate SUI; non-adjustable or adjustable.** The commonest are the **Advance and Advance XP (transobturator)**. **Advance XP is durable**, whereas the **I-STP TOMS deteriorates progressively over 5 years**.
- **Adjustable slings have limited data.** **ATOMS beats ProACT** on dry rate, improvement, satisfaction and pad use.

<p class="tcap">Device outcomes — pooled efficacy and principal caveat</p>

| Device | Pooled efficacy | Principal caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **AUS (AMS 800)** | Cure ~56%; social continence (0–1 pad/day) 55–77%; **satisfaction ~90%** | **Revision 10–29% at 3 years**; needs dexterity and cognition |
| **ProACT** | Dry ~60% | **More erosion than slings**; worse after RT |
| **Transobturator sling** | **Cure ~60%** | Fails with RT, severe UI, prior surgery |
| **ATOMS** | Dry ~67%; improved ~90% | **Complications ~16%**; limited data |

- **The pattern**: pooled dry rates cluster at 55–67% for every device. **What differs is durability, erosion and performance after RT.** Only the AUS has robust long-term data across all severities.
- **The trap**: ProACT's parity with slings hides **more erosion and poor post-RT results**.

- **Predictors of sling failure: prior RT, greater severity, previous surgery.**
- **Severity by 24-h pad weight: mild <150 g, moderate <400 g.** Transobturator slings are mainly for mild-to-moderate UI.
- **MASTER (landmark randomised controlled trial (RCT), AUS vs transobturator sling)**: **the sling was non-inferior for continence at 12 months.** The chapter stresses **AUS superiority with pad weight >250 g or prior RT**.
- **Slings remain an option in severe SUI** for men who **refuse an AUS** (fear of infection, erosion, failure) or have **limited physical or cognitive capacity**.

@@INST Instalment 5 — Surgical Decision Making in the High-Risk Patient: Fragile Urethra Definition, Prior Pelvic Radiotherapy, Previous Erosion/Infection (CAD, Age, Diabetes, Smoking), Previous Urethroplasty, Low Testosterone, Erosion-Prevention Strategies, Key Points · pp. 2589–2590

## SURGICAL DECISION MAKING IN THE HIGH-RISK PATIENT

- **"Fragile urethra" = raised risk of AUS cuff erosion.** Risk factors: **prior pelvic RT, previous failed AUS (erosion or infection), previous urethroplasty, hypogonadism**.

### Prior Pelvic Radiotherapy

- **RT causes microvascular injury to the corpus spongiosum**, and the risk persists despite modern delivery.
- **Erosion occurs in 3.4–33% after RT vs 1–13% without it**, and **irradiated men erode sooner** (median ~1 vs ~3 years).
- **RT predicts the first explant but not later implant failures.** Those are driven by prior erosion and urethroplasty.

### Previous Erosion/Infection

- **Coronary artery disease (CAD) and age >80 years increase erosion risk.**
- **Diabetes and smoking have not been shown to be independent risks**, but the series are small and may underestimate risk, so **optimise both**.
- **After one erosion, 5-year re-erosion approaches 50%.**

### Previous Urethroplasty

- **An established, independent risk, and greater than RT** in a multi-institutional study.
- **Transection divides the bulbourethral arteries**, so the urethra loses its dual blood supply. **Non-transecting urethroplasty preserves them in theory; any benefit for erosion is unproven.**

### Low Testosterone

- **An independent erosion risk.** **Urethral androgens probably support urethral health**, and most men who eroded in one series were hypogonadal.
- **Neither ADT (androgen deprivation therapy) as a risk nor testosterone supplementation as protection has been shown.**

*Strategies to reduce erosion in high-risk men.*

- **Optimise medically** for wound healing and urethral health.
- **Commonest erosion site is disputed**: a **single-institution series found mainly ventral or lateral** erosions, a **multi-institutional series roughly equal dorsal, ventral and lateral**.
- **Transcorporal (TC) AUS (Webster, 2002)** puts **tunica albuginea between the dorsal urethra and the cuff**. In theory it preserves vascularity and **adds bulk where the spongiosum is thinnest**. **Evidence is mixed.**
- **Reduced-pressure PRB (51–60 cm H₂O) or a cuff 0.5 cm larger than measured**: supporting data are scarce.
- **Many men still prefer repeated AUS attempts** to pads, a catheter or diversion.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS**

- **Erosion risks**: RT, failed AUS, urethroplasty, hypogonadism.
- **Optimise medically; consider TC cuff and reduced-pressure PRB.**
</div>

**Concept —** *All high-risk factors converge on **one pathway: an ischaemic spongiosum under a compressive cuff**. Each countermeasure **adds tissue** (TC cuff, larger cuff), **lowers pressure** (51–60 PRB) or **preserves supply** (optimisation, non-transecting urethroplasty).*

## TECHNIQUE OF IMPLANTATION OF CURRENTLY APPROVED AND AVAILABLE DEVICES

### Operative Preparation for All Implant Surgeries

- **Sequence**: **general or spinal anaesthesia, low lithotomy** → shave → **chlorhexidine–alcohol prep** of lower abdomen, genitals and perineum → **intravenous antibiotics within 60 min: aminoglycoside 5 mg/kg + first- or second-generation cephalosporin or vancomycin** (AUA Best Practice Statement) → drape → ± urethral catheter.
- **A 10-min chlorhexidine scrub then iodine povacrylex–alcohol (DuraPrep)** gives fewer positive skin cultures than povidone–iodine, **but no fewer implant infections**.

### Artificial Urinary Sphincter

#### Bulbar Cuff Artificial Urinary Sphincter

- **Perineal incision; cuff as proximal as possible on the bulbar urethra, proximal to corporal fusion.** This allows **safe circumferential dissection**, **protects the cuff from opening when sitting** and gives the **widest spongiosum**.
- **Sequence**: skin → **Colles fascia** → **bulbospongiosus** → **incise Buck fascia where it reflects onto the diverging corpora** → **2-cm tunnel (cuff width) dorsal to Buck fascia over the urethral roof**, sharp and under vision → right-angle clamp → **measure circumference (usually 4.0–4.5 cm)** → place cuff → tubing through bulbospongiosus into the **deep perineal space beneath Colles**.
- **Sizing**: **consistency of method matters more than catheter in or out.** The **cuff fits slightly tighter than the tape**; if obviously wrong, move to the next size. **Looser cuffs give better continence.** **Best test: visual and endoscopic appearance.**
- **Sizes run 3.5–11 cm** (0.5-cm steps to 8.0, then 9, 10, 11). **The large sizes are for the bladder neck.**
- **3.5-cm cuff**: for **atrophy after a previous AUS** or **very distal placement after revisions**. It **erodes more after RT**, so **prefer TC and/or a 51–60 cm H₂O PRB**.

#### Pressure-Regulating Balloon

- **Scrotal, perineal or abdominal route**, chosen by previous incisions, habitus and preference.
- **Abdominal**: lower-quadrant incision **ipsilateral to the pump** → incise rectus or external oblique fascia → split muscle → **preperitoneal (or intraperitoneal) space** → digital pocket → close fascia around the tubing.
- **External inguinal ring (penile-implant technique)**, from a subinguinal or perineal incision: through the **floor of the inguinal canal into the retropubic space**. **Ectopic submuscular**: **above transversalis fascia, behind the posterior rectus sheath, no sutures** (transversalis fascia prevents extrusion).
- **Avoid the ring and ectopic routes after mesh hernia repair, radical cystectomy or extensive abdominal surgery. Use the abdominal approach**, which lowers bladder and bowel injury.

<p class="tcap">PRB pressure by indication</p>

| PRB (cm H₂O) | Use |
|---|---|
| **51–60** | Fragile urethra (RT, atrophy, prior erosion) |
| **61–70** | **Standard bulbar cuff** |
| **71–80** | **Bladder neck cuff** |

- **Pressure rises with the robustness of the tissue under the cuff.** **Trap**: raising PRB pressure to treat atrophy increases erosion.
- **Fill with 23 mL**; **bladder neck cuffs need more** (per manufacturer). **Solutions**: **0.9% saline**; **Cysto-Conray 60 mL + 15 mL sterile water**; **Hypaque 25% 50 mL + 60 mL sterile water**. **Always dilute contrast with sterile water (isotonic).** **No contrast with iodine or contrast allergy.**

#### Control Pump Placement

- **Anterior scrotum**, through the inguinal, scrotal or perineal incision.
- **With an abdominal PRB**: dissect down **above the abdominal fascia, deep to Scarpa** → a finger **invaginates the dependent scrotal skin into the incision** → strip fascia off dartos → **subdartos pocket**.
- **Single-incision**: subdartos pouch directly, pump oriented **towards the foot (scrotal)** or **towards the head (perineal)**.

#### Making the Connections

- **Connect in the abdominal incision when there is one**, which protects the connections and eases later exploration. **Quick connectors** are secure and watertight.
- **Cycle the device several times, then urethroscopy**: **closed cuff = slight blanching** (adequate coaptation, filling, connection); **deactivated = minimal coaptation**; **excessive coaptation → larger cuff**.

**Concept —** *First-time technique follows **one rule: go proximal**. That site gives the thickest spongiosum and a cuff protected when sitting, and it **saves the distal urethra for the revisions most men will need**. Sizing and PRB choice then **tune compression against ischaemia**.*

@@INST Instalment 7 — Technique of Implantation II: Tandem Cuff AUS, Transcorporal Cuff (Technique, Erectile Function, Advantages, Gullwing, Bulbospongiosus Wrap), Transscrotal AUS, Bladder Neck AUS (Open and Robotic), Cuff-Approach Comparison, Key Points · pp. 2592–2595

#### Tandem Cuff Artificial Urinary Sphincter

- **Indication: persistent significant UI after a single cuff, from incomplete occlusion** (a minority of men) → **second bulbar cuff**.
- **Sequence**: via the perineal incision → circumferential dissection **≥1.5–2 cm distal to the primary cuff** → new cuff → **divide the existing tubing and join with a metal Y-connector** → **add 3 mL** → **device deactivated for 6 weeks**.

#### Transcorporal Cuff

- **Indications: difficult primary or revision surgery; urethra <3.5 cm.** After erosion or urethroplasty, **use a different site** along the bulbar urethra.
- **Sequence**: **parallel longitudinal incisions in the corporal tunica albuginea** near the spongiosum → dissect **through the corporal septum** from side to side → **ventral tunica stays on the dorsal spongiosum** → **imbricate the lateral tunical edges behind the urethra** (less haematoma) → measure.
- **Erectile function**: the risk is theoretical; function is **preserved in most sexually active men** in small series.
- **High-risk series: most cured or improved, no erosions.** Overall evidence is mixed because most series combine techniques. **Especially useful when proximal atrophy leaves only a small distal urethra.**
- **Variants**: **"Gullwing"**, a complete tunical wrap through wider incisions, gives **circumferential rather than dorsal-only protection** in theory but has no long-term data. **A cuff around the bulbospongiosus** protects the ventral urethra: **high 5-year revision but erosion very rare**.

#### Transscrotal Artificial Urinary Sphincter

- **Wilson (2003)**: all components through **one transverse scrotal incision**. It was designed for **reoperation after atrophy**, allowing a distal tandem or TC cuff.
- **Continence is inferior**, because the approach **cannot reach the proximal, wider bulbar urethra**: **perineal cuffs are mostly 4.5 cm, transscrotal mostly 4.0 cm**. The **modified enhanced-exposure version (2010)** allows larger proximal cuffs.
- **The authors prefer the perineal route, which keeps distal sites for revision.**

#### Bladder Neck Artificial Urinary Sphincter

- **Optional, invasive and harder**, for sphincteric UI with an **intact prostate** (e.g. **myelomeningocele, neuropathic disorders**). **Contraindicated after RP.**
- **Open**: lower abdominal incision → expose bladder base → circumferential dissection of the **vesicoprostatic junction**. **Robotic**: dissects the **bladder–seminal vesicle plane under vision** and avoids that incision.
- **Adults: cuff usually ≥8 cm, PRB 71–80 cm H₂O, more fluid.** **Children may need very different specifications.**

| Approach | Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| **Bulbar perineal (standard)** | Proximal, thickest spongiosum; saves distal sites | — |
| **Tandem** | More occlusion | **More distal-cuff erosion**; **uses revision sites**; not first-line |
| **Transcorporal** | **Less urethral injury at reoperation** (prior erosion, injury, urethroplasty, rectourethral fistula repair); **better fit (bulk)**; possible erosion reduction (unproven) | **More transient retention needing suprapubic cystostomy** |
| **Transscrotal** | Single incision | **Inferior continence** (smaller, more distal cuff) |
| **Bladder neck** | **Less erosion and atrophy** | Invasive; **contraindicated after RP** |

- **The pattern**: every alternative trades the proximal, well-vascularised site for **bulk or extra occlusion**. **Trap**: the bladder neck cuff erodes least but is barred after prostatectomy, the commonest indication.

## MALE SLINGS

### Transobturator Bulbourethral Sling

- **First described by Rehder and Gozzi (2007)**, to **reposition and lengthen the membranous urethra**. **Indications: SUI after CaP treatment, TURP or laser enucleation.** **Advance XP** (improved anchoring) has replaced the Advance.
- **Sequence**:
  1. Perineal incision → **open bulbospongiosus in the midline** and dissect it off the spongiosum to the **perineal body** → **mark this level** → **release the proximal bulb by 1–2 cm** so it can be repositioned.
  2. Develop the space between spongiosum and diverging corpora. **Feel the inferior pubic ramus behind each crus** so the trocar lands on the fingertip.
  3. **Stab incision ~1 fingerbreadth below adductor longus, in the groin crease** → spinal needle locates the **obturator foramen just lateral to the pubic ramus**.
  4. **Helical passer, outside-in**: thigh → foramen → perineal wound medial to the corpus. **Two "pops"**, then turn. **Exit high in the angle between spongiosum (medial) and inferior pubic ramus (lateral).**
  5. Mesh positioned loosely and pulled to the thigh **without tension**. **Proximal edge at the marked distal perineal body.**
  6. **Fix to the spongiosum with four absorbable sutures.**
  7. **Pull both arms equally under cystoscopy until the bulbar mucosa coapts.** The **perineal body and bulb move 3–4 cm proximally and cephalad**.
  8. Remove sleeves → trim at skin, bury or tunnel → **close bulbospongiosus, Colles fascia, skin**.

### Quadratic Sling

- **Virtue**: **two transobturator strips elevate, two prepubic arms compress.** **Prepubic fixation is important.** A small **mild-UI** cohort was **durable at 3 years**. Its role is undefined, but because it **compresses** it **may suit wider severities and aetiologies**.

### Adjustable Continence Devices

- **ProACT sequence**: catheter; **contrast fills the bladder** to show the neck on fluoroscopy → **sharp trocar** through a perineal incision → **exchange for a blunt trocar beyond the pelvic diaphragm** → advance to the bladder neck → **U-channel sheath deploys the balloon**, filled with saline → repeat on the other side → **titanium port in a scrotal subcutaneous tunnel** → close; remove catheter.
- **ATOMS sequence** (dissection as for Advance XP, but **bulbospongiosus exposed, not opened**): palpate crura, find the foramen → **needles through the superomedial corner of the foramen**, around the ischiopubic ramus, **out through the triangle of bulbospongiosus (medial), ischiopubic ramus (lateral) and transverse perineal muscle (posterior)** → suture through the needle eye, withdraw; repeat on the other side → tie the arms to the sutures and pull through → **cushion centred under the bulbar urethra** → fix mesh arms to the cushion (supplied polypropylene sutures), trim → **subdartos scrotal port filled with 9 mL of saline** → close.

- **Slings: mild-to-moderate SUI, intact contractility, healthy non-irradiated urethra.**
- **Transobturator slings need an intact external sphincter.**
- **Quadratic and adjustable slings: limited efficacy and durability data.**

@@INST Instalment 10 — Artificial Urinary Sphincter Complications: Urinary Retention, AUS Infection (InhibiZone, Immediate Salvage), Urethral Erosion (Rates, Risk-Factor Reconciliation, Explant, Stricture Management, Reimplantation), Urethral Atrophy, Mechanical Failure (Device Life), Key Points · pp. 2597–2599

## ARTIFICIAL URINARY SPHINCTER COMPLICATIONS

### Urinary Retention

- **Rare; commoner with TC and 3.5-cm cuffs.**
- **Sequence**: **confirm deactivation** → **small catheter for 24–48 h** → **if the second voiding trial at 48 h fails → suprapubic cystostomy** (less erosion risk), **placed under ultrasound or fluoroscopy** so the PRB is not punctured.
- **Retention persisting for weeks = undersized cuff** → replace it, correlating with preoperative UDS. **Late-onset retention → endoscopy and UDS** for proximal obstruction, erosion or detrusor failure.

### Artificial Urinary Sphincter Infection

- **1–3% after a first AUS; up to 10% in high-risk men.** **Organisms: skin flora, *Staphylococcus epidermidis* and *S. aureus*.**
- **InhibiZone (rifampicin + minocycline) has not reduced AUS infection** as it has for penile implants.
- **Late infection (>4 months)**: indolent organisms from surgery or **haematogenous spread** → **antibiotic prophylaxis before urinary tract manipulation** (AUA).
- **Scrotal pain is the key early sign** (± erythema, oedema, pus). **Implant infection does not respond to antibiotics, so explant.**

<p class="tcap">AUS infection — delayed versus immediate salvage</p>

| Traditional | Immediate salvage (infected, **non-eroded**) |
|---|---|
| **Remove device → wait several months → reimplant** | **Remove all → seven-solution antiseptic irrigation (penile prosthesis protocol) → immediate new device**; **most salvaged** |
| Default | **Contraindicated with sepsis, ketoacidosis, necrotising infection, erosion, immunosuppression, gross pus** |

### Urethral Erosion

- **2–15% of implants; up to 35% with multiple risk factors.** **Delayed activation has lowered the rate**, especially after reimplantation. **Catheterisation (± deactivation) is itself a risk.**

|---|---|
| **Catheter only** → urethrogram before removal | Traditional; **high stricture rate when urethral loss is extensive** |
| **Pseudocapsule closure** | High in one series; **only prior RT (not erosion size) predicted stricture** |
| **In situ repair without mobilisation** | **Fewer strictures than catheter alone** |
| **Mobilisation + spatulated anastomotic urethroplasty** | Fewer strictures than catheter alone **with >50% loss** |

- **A multi-institutional counterpoint**: **complete erosion strictures more often**, and **catheter, urethrorrhaphy (capsule repair) and anastomotic urethroplasty did not differ significantly**.
- **Rule: <50% urethral loss → catheter; >50% → unresolved.** Balance stricture risk against contamination and **the harm of mobilising in an infected field**.
- **Reimplantation**:
  - Wait **3–6 months** and **confirm patency (cystoscopy or retrograde urethrogram)**.
  - **New cuff proximal or distal to the erosion site, never at it** (scar, poor blood supply).
  - **Early re-erosion reaches 35%**, mostly within about 7 months.
  - **Nightly deactivation after reimplantation for prior erosion** gives high success with low recurrence.
  - **TC and/or a low-pressure PRB** reduce injury at reoperation and may protect against re-erosion.

### Urethral Atrophy

- **Chronic compression of the spongy tissue under the cuff.** Onset is **insidious and slow** (unlike device failure). It is **one of the commonest reasons for revision**.
- **Options: downsize; move proximal or distal to thicker spongiosum; TC; tandem.** **A higher-pressure PRB alone increases erosion.**
- **Authors: downsize at the same site if possible. If the cuff is already 4.0 cm, move it proximally, or distally with TC,** and measure afresh. In a large revision series **most cuffs were downsized** and **distal relocation was the commonest move**. Downsizing improves pad use markedly.
- **Subcuff atrophy is contested.** One high-volume centre **treats every failure as mechanical and replaces the cuff at the same size**, with acceptable results. **Pressure profilometry confirms true atrophy in only about one-fifth of failures.**

### Mechanical Failure

<p class="tcap">AUS device life — body text versus guideline figure</p>

| Source | Device life | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Chapter text | **7–10 years** | Authors' synthesis of device-survival data |
| AUA/SUFU (Fig. 123.5) | **8–10 years** | Guideline counselling figure |

- **No infection or erosion, and within 3 years of implant → replace the failed component alone.** Early failures are **mostly pump or cuff**, at a **median of ≤1 year**.
- **A slow PRB leak is hard to confirm in theatre, so if in doubt replace everything.** **After 3 years most failures are the PRB, so replace the whole device.** An **ohmmeter or burette** test of the PRB may let experienced implanters avoid this.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS**

- **Failed second voiding trial at 48 h → suprapubic cystostomy.**
- **Match erosion management to severity and contamination.**
- **After erosion, TC and/or low-pressure PRB.**
- **Replace the entire AUS if >3 years old** (PRB failure).
</div>

**Concept —** *Nearly every AUS complication arises at the **cuff–spongiosum interface**. Too tight or too long compressed gives **atrophy, then erosion**; a breach gives **infection**. Management therefore **protects the urethra before the device**: remove everything, wait, move the site, lower the pressure.*

@@NUMBERS
- **Retention**: catheter 24–48 h → fail second trial at 48 h → suprapubic cystostomy (image-guided). Persisting weeks → cuff undersized.
- **Infection**: 1–3% primary; up to 10% high risk; late >4 months; organisms *S. epidermidis*, *S. aureus*.
- **Salvage contraindications (absolute)**: sepsis, ketoacidosis, necrotising infection, erosion, immunosuppression, gross pus.
- **Erosion**: 2–15%; up to 35% with multiple risk factors. Stricture after erosion 8–35%.
- **Erosion <50% urethral loss** → catheter; >50% → unresolved.
- **Reimplant** 3–6 months, after confirmed patency; new site proximal or distal; early re-erosion up to 35%.
- **Device life**: 7–10 years (text), 8–10 years (AUA/SUFU).
- **Mechanical failure**: <3 years → replace component (pump/cuff, median ≤1 year); >3 years → replace whole device (PRB).
- **When to operate**: infection → explant; erosion → explant all components; retention for weeks → replace undersized cuff.
- **Traps**: InhibiZone does not cut AUS infection. Remove the whole device at erosion even if the PRB looks clean. Higher-pressure PRB for atrophy → erosion. Catheterising a man with an AUS is itself an erosion risk. Salvage is contraindicated when the device has eroded.

@@INST Instalment 11 — Special Circumstances: Spongiosal Vascular Insufficiency, Urethral and Vesicourethral Anastomotic Stricture (ROBUST III, Synchronous VUAS Treatment), UI After Cystectomy and Neobladder, Dual AUS and Inflatable Penile Prosthesis, Climacturia and Mini-Jupette Sling, Key Points · pp. 2599–2600

## SPECIAL CIRCUMSTANCES

### Managing Device Implantation With Spongiosal Vascular Insufficiency

- **A fragile urethra erodes more readily**, so **protect its blood supply**. **Stricture needing repair before an AUS → non-transecting urethroplasty** (preserves the bulbourethral arteries). **Use TC AUS or a lower-pressure PRB.** **Testosterone replacement in hypogonadal men** is investigational.

### Urethral and Vesicourethral Anastomotic Stricture

- **A stricture at the cuff site** reflects **compression or ischaemia** and **may herald erosion**. Periodic dilation has managed one such stricture.
- **Stricture proximal to an AUS**: **balloon dilation under vision** or **holmium laser through a flexible cystoscope or ureteroscope**.
- **ROBUST III (landmark RCT)**: the **US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved paclitaxel-coated balloon** vs **direct vision internal urethrotomy (DVIU)/dilation**. **Freedom from intervention at 1 year: 83.2% vs 21.7%.** **Functional success 67% at 3 years** (recurrent bulbar strictures). **No VUAS data.**
- **Post-erosion stricture repaired by urethroplasty**: nearly all men got another AUS, **mostly TC**, and **about a quarter re-eroded by 2 years**.
- **VUAS after AUS**: **balloon or laser urethrotomy via ureteroscope**. **A 22–24 Fr scope proximal to the cuff → uncouple the cuff surgically first.** **Refractory → robotic VUAS reconstruction**, which spares the cuff (AUA guideline).

<p class="tcap">VUAS and AUS — sequential versus synchronous treatment</p>

| Traditional | Newer |
|---|---|
| **Correct VUAS before AUS**, avoiding treatment of recurrence with a cuff in place | **Non-obliterated VUAS (<12 Fr) dilated at the same sitting as AUS** |
| — | **No difference** in later VUAS treatments or in erosion |

### Urinary Incontinence After Cystectomy and Neobladder Reconstruction

- **SUI after orthotopic neobladder: 33–95%**, and **QoL depends on regaining continence**.
- **An AUS improves most men, but most need revision** (over a third of revisions for erosion or infection). Contributors: **prior RT, bacterial colonisation, clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC)**.
- **PRB ectopic via a lateral counter-incision, behind the rectus**, to avoid neobladder and bowel.

### Dual Implantation of Artificial Urinary Sphincter and Inflatable Penile Prosthesis

- **An AUS and an inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) have similar device survival and revision whether placed synchronously or metachronously.** On meta-analysis, **reoperation is ~25% either way**, with high satisfaction. **Counsel that dual implants need more revisions than a single IPP or AUS.**

<p class="tcap">Dual AUS + IPP — synchronous versus metachronous</p>

| Synchronous | Metachronous |
|---|---|
| **Penoscrotal (IPP) + perineal (proximal bulbar AUS) incisions** | **AUS first; IPP after activation** |
| Safe and effective | Patients **prefer continence before ED treatment** |

- **IPP already present**: standard bulbar AUS. **If TC is needed, dissect between the tunica and the prosthesis pseudocapsule**, and **close the corporotomy with allograft (e.g. Tutoplast)** for haemostasis.
- **Dual implants after neobladder**: **no difference from age-matched controls** in infection, mechanical failure or revision.

### Climacturia

- **Orgasm-associated UI in 20–68% after RP**, including **leakage on arousal**.
- **Condoms and precoital voiding help little.** The authors use a **sling or AUS according to the degree of SUI and climacturia**.
- **Mini-jupette sling (at IPP placement for ED)**: **3-cm corporotomies** → **graft (e.g. Tutoplast) sutured to the medial edges** → **inflation tightens the graft across the urethra**, compressing it during sex. **With mild SUI, leave the IPP partly inflated.** Single-institution 5-year data show **durable improvement in most, and no erosion or infection**.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS**

- **VUAS after AUS: balloon or ureteroscopic laser.**
- **Synchronous AUS + IPP is safe.**
- **Mini-jupette at IPP**: ED with climacturia and mild SUI.
</div>

**Concept —** *One rule unites these cases: **protect the spongiosal blood supply and keep the cuff out of the path of instrumentation**. Procedures are sequenced (continence first, strictures treated first or the cuff uncoupled) so the cuff **never meets an unplanned large-bore scope or catheter**.*

@@NUMBERS

@@INST Instalment 12 — Sling Complications (Non-adjustable, Adjustable, Non-circumferential Compression Devices), Gaps and Future Directions (Innovations, Registries, Surgical Training), Summary, References · p. 2600

## SLING COMPLICATIONS

### Nonadjustable Slings

- **Pain and wound problems are the commonest complications** (meta-analysis). **Inguinal pain from trocar passage is self-limiting: median visual analogue scale (VAS) score 0 at 3 months.**

### Adjustable Slings

- **ATOMS complications are commoner**, which may limit adoption.

### Noncircumferential Compression Devices

- **ProACT: bladder or urethral perforation is the commonest complication.**

<p class="tcap">Complications of slings and non-circumferential compression devices</p>

| | Non-adjustable sling | ATOMS | ProACT |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chronic pain | 1.3% | 1.5% | — |
| Erosion | **0–0.4%** | **10%** (with infection) | 3.8% |
| Explant / revision | 0–0.4% | **10–15.8%** | **22%** |
| Other | Urgency or retention 4.3–10.3% | — | **Perforation 5.3%; leak 4.1%; migration 6.5%** |

- **The pattern**: **complications rise with adjustable hardware**, and the non-adjustable mesh sling is safest. **Trap**: "adjustable" is not gentler; **ATOMS and ProACT erode and need revision far more often**.

## GAPS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

### Innovations and Emerging Concepts in Devices

- **The AUS works well but often needs multiple revisions, with significant morbidity.** **Slings and compression devices often succeed but have frequent complications and often miss patients' goals.**
- **Future**: **electronic control, patient-specific dynamic pressure control, new devices**, to improve durability and widen eligibility.

### Registries

- **Most long-term data are small, retrospective and single-institution.** **Registries and multi-institutional prospective studies are needed.** Trials are recruiting: AUS vs sling, ATOMS vs AUS, Virtue.

### Surgical Training

- **More than 150,000 men have had an AUS or sling**, yet **many urologists never perform SUI surgery**. **Fellowships and simulation** should widen access.

## SUMMARY

- **SUI surgery stays important** because localised CaP is still often treated surgically.
- **Assessment: history, examination, pad weight, uroflowmetry, PVR; cystoscopy and UDS selectively.**
- **The AUS is the most established treatment for moderate-to-severe SUI**, especially in complex cases.
- **Slings suit mild-to-moderate UI without prior urethral surgery, RT or detrusor acontractility.** Results after RT are poorer, and **neurogenic or post-traumatic use needs evidence**.
- **Comparison is confounded** by baseline severity, endpoints, follow-up and selection bias. **MASTER is the one RCT comparing them directly.**
- **Almost 90% of men report better urinary QoL after surgery, whatever the severity.**
- **Erosion and mechanical failure drive the search for alternatives.**

@@NUMBERS
- **Non-adjustable sling**: chronic pain 1.3%; urgency/retention 4.3–10.3%; erosion/explant 0–0.4%; inguinal pain VAS 0 at 3 months.
- **ATOMS**: chronic pain 1.5%; erosion + infection 10%; explant 10–15.8%.
- **ProACT**: perforation 5.3% (commonest); erosion 3.8%; leak 4.1%; migration 6.5%; revision 22%.
