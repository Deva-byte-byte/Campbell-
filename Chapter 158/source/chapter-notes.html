Chapter 158 — Active Management Strategies for Localised Prostate Cancer

  • Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) era uniformity: PSA testing and community screening from the early 1990s → radical treatment for most patients regardless of risk, apparently vindicated by serially falling US prostate cancer mortality.
  • Evidence against treating everyone:
    • Observation of low-risk disease → low rates of metastasis and prostate cancer death.
    • Randomised treatment-versus-observation trials → mortality reduction too small to justify the quality-of-life cost, largely because of high early death from other causes.
    • Large randomised screening trials → no robust mortality decline for all patients despite prolonged follow-up.
  • 15-year randomised data in favourable-risk disease: surveillance → more metastatic progression but no difference in prostate cancer mortality; explained by high crossover to treatment within 5–10 years, competing mortality and much-improved treatment of metastatic disease.
  • The shift: from choosing a treatment → also choosing its timing relative to disease risk and expected longevity; for favourable risk, early radical therapy may cost more quality of life than the cancer costs life.
  • Many early-stage patients still carry significant lifetime mortality risk → contemporary care is individualised: longevity, disease aggressiveness and extent, patient goals.
  • Management spectrum:
    • More likely to die of competing risks → observation.
    • Good longevity, benefit of treatment unclear → active surveillance, deferring therapy until need is clear.
    • Needing therapy → radical prostatectomy (RP), external radiation, interstitial radiation, or thermal ablation (whole or partial gland).
  • Modality selection requires counselling on risks and benefits, recognition of host factors, comparative data and patient preference.

Concept — Management of localised prostate cancer is a question of timing as well as modality: cancer mortality risk is always weighed against competing mortality, and every later decision follows from that balance.

Host Evaluation

Longevity Assessment/Competing Risks for Mortality

  • Competing mortality dilutes treatment benefit in large efficacy trials; several randomised trials show other-cause death risk exceeding prostate cancer death risk.
  • National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN): "life expectancy estimation is critical to informed decision making" in early detection and treatment.
  • Prostate Cancer Outcomes Study (PCOS) (US prospective cohort): other-cause mortality modelled on comorbidity and age; 10-year prostate cancer mortality 3–18% by risk category versus ≥33% other-cause mortality with any comorbidity.
  • The "10-year life expectancy" rule: applied bluntly, it has produced lower treatment rates in older patients and possibly overtreatment of younger ones; best practice is an individualised assessment of age, comorbidity and cancer mortality risk.
  • Tools: NCCN recommends Social Security Administration tables or World Health Organization (WHO) Life Tables; prostate cancer–specific models also exist, in which the age–comorbidity relationship is central.
  • Longevity is an indirect measure of comorbidity and shapes both whether and which treatment: significant cardiac, pulmonary or haematological comorbidity may leave longevity and benefit intact but contraindicate surgery (anaesthetic or bleeding risk).
  • Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI): most widely used scale; designed to predict 1-year inpatient mortality; later shown prognostic across many cancers.
  • Prostate cancer–specific CCI (PCCI): reweights comorbidities to predict 10-year mortality in prostate cancer; in the Veterans Health Administration (VHA), age-adjusted 10-year other-cause mortality rises steeply across score bands, approaching certainty at the highest scores.
  • Care Assessment Needs (CAN) score: automated electronic health record score (VHA) built for short-term hospitalisation and mortality; stronger predictor of 5-year overall survival than CCI or PCCI in both localised and metastatic disease.
  • Age-based bias: clinicians judge longevity by age rather than comorbidity → comorbid younger men treated more often than healthy older men despite far higher other-cause mortality; treatment of patients with limited life expectancy falls with age; older high-risk patients undertreated despite reasonable 10-year longevity. Remedy: age-adjusted longevity from validated comorbidity indices and life tables.
  • Longevity must be weighed against aggressiveness:
    • Aggressive cancers may benefit from treatment despite shortened longevity, especially at advanced stage.
    • VHA practice: RP use falling in low-risk and rising in high-risk disease, including in men with ≤10-year longevity.
    • High-risk, poorly differentiated disease: absolute benefit of aggressive treatment shrinks and number needed to treat rises steeply with increasing Charlson score.
    • Lead time to symptoms and death is governed by Gleason score and stage.
  • High risk of metastasis or death within 10 years → consider aggressive therapy even with competing risks in the same period.

Baseline Urinary/Sexual/Bowel Function

  • Baseline function sets the size of the quality-of-life benefit and risk; pre-existing dysfunction → more intense or protracted morbidity. Assess with validated instruments for shared decision-making.

Prostate size

  • American Brachytherapy Society: >60 cm³ technically difficult to implant — pubic arch interference and large seed numbers.
  • Cytoreductive androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT) shrinks the gland by ~25–40%.
  • >80 g → neoadjuvant ADT may be required before radiotherapy, to reduce rectal and bladder dose.
  • Unusually large gland with early-stage disease → surgery may be preferred, avoiding months of ADT and its effects.

Chronic retention

  • Obstructive retention → consider surgery. Bladder atony → surgery still possible; urodynamic compliance predicts recovery of continence.
  • Radiation planned → convert indwelling catheter to intermittent catheterisation → bladder cycling, preventing radiation fibrosis.

Transurethral resection and radiation

  • Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) before, not after, EBRT for those with or at risk of retention; post-treatment transurethral resection (TUR) → higher incontinence, especially after brachytherapy.
  • TURP before brachytherapy: historically ↑ post-implant incontinence; contemporary experience challenges this; limited TUR (obstructing median lobe/bladder neck, minimal lateral lobe and apical resection) is safe before brachytherapy.
  • Previous aggressive TURP → ↑ urinary toxicity with radiotherapy, though long-term severe symptoms may be fewer.
  • Delay radiation 6–8 weeks after TURP → ↓ incontinence and bladder neck contracture.
  • TURP, microwave or laser defects: brachytherapy dosimetry challenge (dose is placed peripherally, sparing urethra and bladder neck); cryoablation needle placement risks intraurethral position. Small glands (<30 cm³) with large defects → alternative strategies.

Neurogenic dysfunction

  • Parkinson disease: careful urological history; urodynamics quantify risk of post-treatment incontinence or worse LUTS.
  • Severe compliance abnormalities → surgery preferred, given progressive bladder fibrosis after radiation.

Concept — Surgery removes the obstructing gland; radiation leaves it to shrink and scars the bladder — so obstruction, a large gland and a poorly compliant bladder favour surgery, while irritative symptoms and prior resection defects are worsened or complicated by radiation.

Sexual Function

  • Recovery depends on treatment quality and host factors: baseline function, use of phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) inhibitors, frequency of sexual activity, and vascular risk factors (diabetes, atherosclerosis, hypercholesterolaemia).
  • Age and preoperative erectile function predict recovery at 2 years after bilateral nerve sparing.
  • Poor baseline function → more non-nerve-sparing surgery, independent of cancer severity — baseline shapes the plan.
  • Increasing age → lower potency recovery after surgery and greater decline after radiation.
  • More frequent preoperative sexual activity → more postoperative sexual bother, independent of functional recovery.

Bowel Function/Other Conditions Affecting Treatment Choice

  • Radiotherapy → more bowel dysfunction than surgery in comparative data.
  • Careful gastroenterological history for radiation candidates: pre-existing faecal frequency/urgency, irritable bowel, tenesmus or extensive rectal surgery → warn of exacerbation; gastroenterological evaluation before therapy.
  • Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn disease, ulcerative colitis) → suggested more severe acute and late toxicity with pelvic radiotherapy.
  • Neurogenic bladder → risk of functional deterioration or incontinence after any treatment; neurogenic or inflammatory bowel dysfunction → worse bowel function after EBRT.

Patient Preference/Biases

  • Preference carries unusual weight because no compelling data favour one treatment in individual cases.
  • Preconceptions from family, friends, the internet and lay media; many patients consider only surgery.
  • Surgery choosers → concerned with cancer mortality; non-surgical choosers → concerned with quality of life; obstructive LUTS → more likely to elect surgery.
  • Longevity assessment may be the most important component of counselling, given competing risks and the observed safety of deferral in most favourable- and intermediate-risk cancers.
  • Physicians are often ineffective at conveying patient-specific risks; patients prefer specific, quantitative estimates to generalisations.
  • Shared decision-making: collaborative decision by patient and clinician, advocated for PSA testing and treatment by the American Urological Association (AUA), American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) and Society of Urologic Oncology (SUO); consensus exists on implementation strategies.
  • Patients who perceive their involvement as active (whatever their initial preference) → better informed, less decisional regret.
  • Decision aids (written, in-person, web-based): meta-analysis of randomised trials → negligible association with health outcomes; newer trials → improved decision-making. Aids should account for physician recommendation and family wishes.
  • The urologist's recommendation remains the most influential factor.

Concept — Without a clearly superior treatment, the decision turns on which risk domain the patient will accept — so the quality of information on longevity and side effects, not the length of the menu, determines a good choice.

Assessment of Prostate Cancer Risk

Tools for Risk Stratification

  • Purpose: estimate cancer mortality over time → decide whether to treat and which options are reasonable.
  • Most acknowledged criteria: clinical stage, biopsy Gleason score, pretreatment PSA.
  • No single criterion identifies all high-risk patients; no standardised system exists — an obstacle to counselling, outcome evaluation, trial design and guidelines.

Definitions of Low, Intermediate, and High Risk

  • The core challenge: apparently indolent cancers with unrecognised aggressive biology.
  • AUA/ASTRO/SUO stratify into very low, low, intermediate and high; NCCN additionally substratifies intermediate and high risk.
  • NCCN low- and intermediate-risk groups span a broad range of aggressiveness; optimal treatment remains under investigation.
  • Validation of the groups: very low risk → less adverse surgical pathology than low risk; favourable-risk groups harbour adverse features beyond low risk, affecting surveillance selection; very high risk → worse pathological and oncological outcomes after RP and more additional therapy than high risk.

Gleason Scoring and Grade Groups

  • Gleason system (1966): glandular architecture; score = dominant pattern + largest minority pattern; patterns 1–5, 5 least differentiated.
  • International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) 2005: patterns 1 and 2 abandoned → effective scale 6–10; several variants formerly pattern 3, and all cribriform patterns, reclassified as pattern 4.
  • Grade migration: more 3+4 and 4+3 than in historical cohorts → better prognostic accuracy, but historical and contemporary outcomes are not directly comparable; upward migration of former low-risk cancers has opened surveillance to some Gleason 3+4 disease.
  • Grade groups: proposed 2013, advocated by ISUP 2014 to communicate aggressiveness on a 1–5 scale; correlation with prostate cancer–specific mortality validated.

Epstein Criteria

  • Epstein criteria (1994) predict insignificant cancer — organ-confined (<pT3a), volume <0.5 mL, no high-grade component:
    • PSA density <0.15 ng/mL/g
    • Gleason ≤6, no adverse biopsy pathology
    • <3 positive cores (of a six-core biopsy)
    • ≤50% cancer in any core
  • Predictive accuracy 79%; revised 2004 with higher accuracy (up to ~84%). Among the most used tools for selecting surveillance candidates.
  • Caveat: validated on systematic biopsy before surgery; accuracy with saturation or MRI-targeted biopsy is unclear; modified global grading has been proposed for MRI-targeted biopsy.
  • Other biopsy correlates of aggressiveness and organ confinement: Gleason score; number of positive cores; distribution and volume of cancer; perineural invasion; lymphovascular invasion; ductal, signet-ring or neuroendocrine differentiation.

Staging

  • Guidelines vary. Most support radionuclide bone scan for high-risk disease; those addressing soft tissue endorse pelvic CT or MRI.
  • NCCN: technetium-99m bone scan + CT or MRI for high-risk and unfavourable intermediate-risk patients at high risk of occult metastasis (clinical parameters or nomograms). Equivocal bone scan → positron emission tomography (PET)/CT or MRI.
  • "Choosing Wisely": American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) 2012 and AUA 2013 — reduce staging imaging in low-risk disease. Compliance poor: low-risk over-imaged, high-risk paradoxically under-imaged; guidelines lower inappropriate imaging but also appropriate imaging.

MRI for EPE and SVI

  • T2-weighted imaging (T2WI): best spatial resolution. Gross capsular extension or disruption is highly predictive; subtle irregularity or bulging has low predictive value.
  • Length of capsular contact improves prediction; adding diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) or dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) imaging improves subtle EPE detection by localising the dominant tumour — though at lower spatial resolution.
  • High PPV matters in high-risk surgery: knowing the EPE site helps reduce positive margins.
  • SVI: T2-hypointense mass in the seminal vesicle, contiguous with tumour at the base; T2WI + DWI improves accuracy; high sensitivity and very high specificity.
  • EPE and SVI predict worse oncological outcome and early biochemical recurrence → counselling, treatment selection and planning.

PSMA PET in Staging

  • PET uses radiolabelled biomolecules to detect metabolic activity or cancer-associated surface molecules; fused with CT or MRI for localisation. Potential roles: surveillance selection and pre-treatment staging.
  • Focus on prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) PET for high-risk staging and detection of recurrence.
  • PSMA PET → greater sensitivity for metastasis than bone scan and CT, while retaining high specificity.

Nomograms for Prediction of Stage and Disease Outcome

  • Early PSA era: prognosis rested on PSA alone; recognition that Gleason and stage often diverged from PSA → Partin tables (1993): PSA + Gleason + clinical stage → predicted pathological stage at RP.
  • Partin tables: multi-institutional validation, accurate 72.4% of the time; generalisable across populations; derived from Johns Hopkins RP experience and periodically updated.
  • Later organ-confinement nomograms add age, digital rectal examination (DRE), PSA density or percentage core involvement.

Risk Stratification Nomograms

  • Predict clinical outcomes after treatment — relapse and survival.
  • Other candidate variables (extent of cancer on biopsy, pre-diagnosis PSA velocity, PSA doubling time, tertiary Gleason pattern) remain of uncertain value — not routinely collected.
  • Further nomograms inform active surveillance, RP, neurovascular bundle preservation, omission of pelvic lymph node dissection, brachytherapy and EBRT; postoperative nomograms (age, PSA, pathological grade and stage) reassess biochemical progression-free survival and the likely success of adjuvant or salvage radiation.

Prostate MRI in Risk Assessment

  • Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) version 2: minimum technical standards and structured reporting of suspicion for clinically significant cancer.
  • PI-RADS score and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) on DWI correlate with outcome → usable for risk stratification.
  • Surveillance: tumour ADC may mark progression and identify those who will benefit from radical treatment.
  • Before treatment: ADC and PI-RADS predict biochemical recurrence after RP and radiation; MRI improves post-EBRT nomograms; pretreatment MRI predicts recurrence after combined brachytherapy + EBRT in intermediate- and high-risk disease.
  • MRI EPE or SVI before RP predicts biochemical recurrence; substituting MRI for pathological EPE/SVI in UCSF-CAPRA gives comparable performance — outcome prediction before surgery.
  • With clinical parameters and biomarkers, MRI guides the need for repeat biopsy when biopsy and MRI are discordant.

Concept — MRI is moving from a detection tool to a prognostic variable: radiological EPE, SVI and ADC can stand in for pathology before any treatment.

Selection of Treatment: Comparative Studies

Oncologic Outcomes

  • High-quality comparative data are scarce; the Institute of Medicine placed localised prostate cancer among the top 25 comparative-effectiveness priorities.
  • Biochemical-free survival is a flawed end point:
    1. Definitions vary widely.
    2. PSA kinetics differ — radiation's cytotoxic effect lasts months to years and leaves prostatic PSA sources.
    3. ADT (common with high-risk radiation) delays biochemical progression.
  • Cancer-specific and overall mortality are the most valid end points but need protracted follow-up; enrolling low-risk or comorbid patients → non-oncological deaths obscure treatment effect.

Comparative Population-Based Studies

  • Swedish population data, the Cancer of the Prostate Strategic Urologic Research Endeavor (CaPSURE) registry and several Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) analyses → higher prostate cancer–specific mortality after radiation than after RP, adjusted for age and risk.
  • Bias: historical preference for radiation in older, more comorbid, higher-risk men is incompletely captured; selection, detection and confounding bias can yield results contradicting randomised trials.

Scandinavian Prostate Cancer Group Study Number 4 (SPCG-4)

  • RP versus watchful waiting; first randomised intention-to-treat comparison with overall and cancer-specific survival end points; long follow-up; blinded pathology review.
  • Limitation: pre-PSA era — nearly 90% of 695 men had palpable disease; does not reflect current practice.
  • 23.2 years: deaths 200/347 (RP) vs 247/348; prostate cancer deaths 63 vs 99 → relative risk (RR) 0.56; absolute difference 11.0 percentage points; number needed to treat 8.
  • Greatest benefit: age <65 (RR 0.45) and intermediate risk (RR 0.38); older men → fewer metastases (RR 0.68).

Prostate Cancer Intervention Versus Observation Trial (PIVOT)

  • RP versus observation, PSA era; planned 2000 men, recruited only 731 (addressed by longer follow-up).
  • 10 years: no difference in overall or prostate cancer mortality; no variation by age, race, performance status or comorbidity; no benefit in low risk; RP reduced all-cause mortality with PSA >10 ng/mL and approached significance in intermediate/high risk; bone metastases 4.7% vs 10.6%, more marked with PSA >10 and intermediate/high risk.
  • Limitations: underpowered (91% power for a 25% relative reduction only after reformulation; ~1200 needed); ~20% non-adherence; only 10% younger than 60; inclusion required life expectancy >10 years yet ~40% dead at 10 years (≈5% from prostate cancer) and up to 60% at 15 years (~10% from prostate cancer) → poor baseline selection.
  • 19.5 years: absolute risk reduction (ARR) all-cause mortality 5.5% (61.3% vs 66.8%; P = 0.06, NS); prostate cancer mortality ARR 4.0% (7.4% vs 11.4%; P = 0.06, NS); intermediate risk: all-cause ARR 14.5% (significant); no benefit in low or high risk. RP reduced treatment for progression but increased incontinence and erectile and sexual dysfunction.

Prostate Cancer Outcomes Study (PCOS)

  • Population-based cohort, age 55–74, diagnosed 1994–1995; RP (1164) vs EBRT (491) within 1 year; propensity-scored multivariable analysis.
  • 15 years: 568 deaths, 104 from prostate cancer; RP → overall mortality HR 0.60, disease-specific HR 0.35 (P < 0.0001).
  • Benefit greatest in younger, lower-risk men but also seen in older and high-risk men.
  • Signs of selection bias: differences appeared as early as 3 years — inconsistent with the natural history of early-stage disease; in low risk, overall but not cancer-specific mortality favoured RP → unrecorded differences (volume, true stage, non-oncological mortality predictors).

Prostate Testing for Cancer and Treatment (ProtecT)

  • 1999–2009: 82,429 men aged 50–69 PSA-tested; 2664 diagnosed; 1643 randomised — active monitoring 545, surgery 553, radiotherapy + 3–6 months ADT 545. 62% of eligible men accepted randomisation; mostly low–intermediate risk.
  • Adherence: 88% monitoring, 71% prostatectomy, 74% radiation.
  • 10 years (primary end point): no difference in prostate cancer mortality; metastases higher with monitoring. Mortality ~1% against 15% predicted at design → reduced power.
  • 15 years: prostate cancer mortality 2.7% (surveillance), 2.2% (surgery), 2.9% (radiation); P = 0.53; other-cause death 21.7%; metastatic progression 6.3% overall.
  • Surgery vs radiation: no difference in mortality, metastasis-free survival or ADT use at 15 years; recurrence and secondary therapy not reported. Treated at age <65 → lower prostate cancer mortality with surveillance or surgery than radiation; >65 → no difference surgery vs radiation.
  • Population: 77% low risk; only 6% GG ≥3; 9.6% high risk (D'Amico); 2.5% high risk (UCSF-CAPRA) → explains the low mortality.
  • Half of all metastatic or locoregional progression occurred in the surveillance arm; surveillance patients reaching prostatectomy showed high upgrading and adverse pathology → poor pre-MRI baseline risk assessment.
  • Verdict: initial surveillance in favourable-risk disease, including much favourable intermediate risk, does not greatly affect prostate cancer mortality; extension to higher risk is unclear.

Functional Outcomes

  • With oncological differences controversial, quality of life drives the choice and must be discussed in detail.
  • Older comparisons came from single-institution centres of excellence (poor generalisability, selection bias) or population datasets with inadequate fields, instruments and operator variability; large comparative studies now exist (Table 158.2).

PCOS

  • RP → more incontinence and erectile dysfunction at 2 and 5 years; less bowel urgency at 2 and 5 years; no significant difference in any domain at 15 years.
  • Convergence reflects decline in both groups: age, declining sexual interest or acceptance of dysfunction, and additional cancer treatment.

ProtecT

  • Prostatectomy: greatest harm to sexual function and continence; partial recovery but worse than other arms throughout.
  • Radiotherapy: sexual function worst at 6 months, then partial recovery, stable below baseline; little effect on continence; bowel function worse at 6 months then recovered, except increasing bloody stools; voiding and nocturia worse at 6 months, equal by 12 months.
  • Active monitoring: gradual decline in sexual and urinary function.
  • No differences in anxiety, depression, or general or cancer-related quality of life.

Comparative Effectiveness Analysis of Surgery and Radiation (CEASAR)

  • Prospective population-based study, enrolled 2011–2012.
  • Low risk: 3-year sexual function score 12 points better after EBRT than RP (52 vs 39); high risk: difference not clinically significant; with a binary definition (erections firm enough for intercourse), no major difference in any risk stratum.
  • With AS added: RP → greater decline in sexual function and continence than EBRT or AS, but fewer irritative symptoms than AS; no meaningful bowel or hormonal differences beyond 12 months.
  • Erectile disadvantage of RP seen only in the highest quartile of baseline function; none in the remaining three quartiles.

Open Versus Robotic Prostatectomy

  • One randomised phase III trial: robotic-assisted laparoscopic and open RP → similar functional outcomes at 12 weeks.
  • Robotic surgery improves functional and oncological outcomes along a single surgeon's learning curve.
  • Community data: similar recovery pattern over 3 years → patients should not expect different oncological or quality-of-life outcomes by approach.
  • Robotic surgery may ease identification of nerve-preservation planes and wider dissection in pT3 disease — possibly surgeon bias.

Concept — Surgery front-loads urinary and sexual harm that partly recovers; radiation spares early function but adds bowel toxicity and a slow cumulative decline — the curves converge by 15 years, so the choice is about when and where the patient accepts the harm.

Patient Counselling: A Stepwise Decision Approach

  • Many options and conflicting advice → confusion and anxiety; a menu of options lacks context because the goals of the strategies diverge.
  • A methodical, stepwise approach reduces confusion; the patient decides in which domain to accept risk — oncological control and cancer mortality versus side effects and quality of life.

Method of surveillance

  • Serial annual biopsy (traditional) detects occult high grade and progression but cannot distinguish them.
  • Confirmatory biopsy within 6–12 months of diagnosis in most protocols; later frequency varies; modelling shows less frequent biopsy only slightly delays detection while greatly reducing biopsies.
  • PRIAS (increasingly used, especially in Europe): periodic PSA with less frequent biopsy; PSA doubling time (PSADT) triggers biopsy or intervention.
  • MRI-led monitoring, biopsy only on clinical or radiological progression → still investigational.

Decision Two: Partial-Gland Versus Whole-Gland Treatment

  • Offered where focal therapy is available — typically thermal ablation. Selection is controversial but usually requires unifocal or limited-focality cancer with spatial concordance between imaging and biopsy.
  • Counselling: investigational; no long-term follow-up; possible retreatment; worse outcomes if conventional salvage therapy is later needed. Ideally within a clinical trial.
  • Selection by transperineal template mapping biopsy or by imaging. High risk: consensus against (no long-term locoregional control data). Low risk (usually suited to surveillance): no consensus. Most favour intermediate-risk, limited-focality disease. A joint FDA, SUO and AUA workshop addressed trial design, end points and selection.
  • Unilateral disease on systematic biopsy is usually not unilateral at prostatectomy; low-risk clinical features do not improve prediction. Template mapping better shows multifocality (optimal template undecided); MRI-targeted biopsy improves selection, accepting untreated indolent foci.
  • Index lesion hypothesis: the dominant tumour — generally the largest focus with the highest Gleason score, most likely MRI-visible — drives metastasis and lethality. Untested long term, yet used to justify leaving low-grade secondary and tertiary foci untreated → high likelihood of residual cancer; long-term efficacy and survival impact unknown.

Concept — Focal therapy rests on the untested index-lesion hypothesis, which is why it is confined to limited-focality intermediate-risk disease and, ideally, to trials.

Decision Three: Surgery Versus Radiation Versus Whole-Gland Ablation

  • Whole-gland ablation shares focal therapy's problems: little long-term data, frequent retreatment, and salvage surgery complicated by periprostatic fibrosis. Its attraction is preservation of sexual and urinary function.

  • Radiation forms share broadly comparable side-effect profiles; oncological outcomes are relatively equivalent at 10 years; the choice mostly rests on which anticipated side effects the patient finds most tolerable.

Decision Four: Type of Treatment Delivery

  • Best discussed by a specialist experienced in that modality.
  • Surgery: open versus robotic-assisted laparoscopic approach, chosen by the surgeon's experience and comfort.
  • Radiation options: EBRT (intensity-modulated, image-guided and/or three-dimensional conformal); interstitial (permanent seed implants or high-dose-rate temporary insertion); stereotactic body radiotherapy; proton therapy; combinations; conventional or hypofractionated schedules.
  • Concern that counselling is biased by financial incentives and physician bias.

Conclusion

  • Active management depends on evaluation of both the cancer and the host: relative risk of progression, metastasis and cancer death framed against competing mortality and expected longevity.
  • Cancer risk: biopsy pathology, image-based staging, MRI, genomic markers — combined in validated nomograms or risk scores. Longevity: age, comorbidity and standardised scales.
  • Because treatments are many and their goals divergent, a stepwise decision-tree approach simplifies counselling, informs decisions and reduces decisional regret.
Key Points
  • Individualised assessment of prostate cancer mortality risk against competing risks of death over time.
  • Modality selection by shared decision-making: risks and benefits, host factors and patient preference.
  • Baseline urinary, bowel and sexual function determine the quality-of-life benefit and risk of each treatment.
  • Longevity assessment may be the most important part of counselling, given competing risks and the safety of deferral in most favourable- and intermediate-risk cancers.
  • Mortality risk is assessed with biopsy pathology, genomic testing, imaging and nomograms.
  • A methodical stepwise approach to the patient and the options reduces confusion and anxiety.
