Chapter 61 — Urologic Considerations in Pediatric Renal Transplantation

  • Children who need kidney replacement therapy (KRT) are more likely than adults to have a congenital anomaly of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), including obstructive uropathy.
  • The urologist's task is to make sure the condition that destroyed the native kidneys does not destroy the graft. Urology should be part of the multidisciplinary team before end-stage kidney disease (ESKD).
  • Five therapeutic goals: (1) unimpeded drainage from kidney to lower tract · (2) a capacious, low-pressure reservoir giving socially acceptable continence · (3) volitional emptying · (4) no infection · (5) the fewest operations.
  • Avoid a reactive approach (referral only once the graft is in jeopardy); optimise the lower tract early.
  • Chronic rejection is unavoidable, but every added insult accelerates it.

Concept — The graft is only as safe as the bladder it drains into. Paediatric transplant urology is the work of correcting, before transplantation, the lower-tract hostility that caused the original renal failure.

PRETRANSPLANT ASSESSMENT

  • Screen every child for urological problems, although the yield is low when there is a clear nephrological cause.
  • Basic screen: detailed history + renal ultrasound + uroflow with postvoid residual (PVR) if toilet-trained. This rules out most abnormalities.
  • Routine voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) is not necessary. Consider it with a history of neuropathic bladder, obstructive uropathy, VUR or previous lower-tract surgery, and with recurrent febrile urinary tract infection (UTI), unexplained worsening hydroureteronephrosis or refractory voiding problems.

Focused Assessment

  • A known urological abnormality → targeted assessment directed by the condition and by the state of the bladder and kidneys.
  • VCUG shows changes with filling and emptying, the urethra, and the presence and grade of reflux. Abnormal bladder dynamics on any test → urodynamics.
  • Indications for urodynamics: neuropathic bladder; outlet obstruction from PUVs or a large ureterocele; and selected refractory voiding dysfunction, high-grade hydronephrosis or recurrent UTI.
  • Urodynamics measure capacity, compliance, detrusor activity, sensation, emptying and sphincter function, to define graft-threatening dynamics and guide therapy.
  • Therapies that follow: drugs (see table below), clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC), intradetrusor botulinum toxin, reconstruction.
  • Treatment interacts with emptying: an antimuscarinic for poor compliance can impair emptying and so create the need for CIC. A sensate or difficult urethra → continent catheterisable stoma.
  • Urodynamics are serial, not one-off: repeat them to track response and change over time.

  • Incomplete emptying → behavioural training.
  • Urethral abnormality → surgical repair.

The outcomes then branch:

  • Inadequate compliance or capacity → bladder cycling, CIC and anticholinergics, ± antireflux surgery → reassess → still inadequate → bladder augmentation.
  • Inadequate emptying → CIC ± catheterisable stoma.
  • Adequate compliance, capacity and emptying → clear for transplant.

History negative → RUS + PVR:

  • Normal → clear for transplant.
  • Hydronephrosis or incomplete emptying → join the history-positive pathway.

Footnote: an anuric child with no urological history needs no further testing. With neurogenic bladder, PUVs or complex history, consider bladder cycling and anticholinergics.

KEY POINTS: UROLOGIC EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT

  • The five goals (drainage, low-pressure storage, volitional emptying, no infection, fewest procedures).
  • Most children needing KRT have uropathy; assessment is directed by the condition.
  • Urodynamics (capacity, compliance, emptying, sphincter) for neuropathic bladder, PUVs, refractory dysfunction, high-grade hydronephrosis or recurrent UTI.

Concept — Assessment is history-driven and stepwise: a negative history needs only ultrasound and residual, and every positive finding funnels towards one question, whether the bladder can store at low pressure and empty.

PRETRANSPLANT PREPARATION

Detrusor Overactivity, Low Capacity, and Poor Compliance

  • The commonest early bladder abnormalities in ESKD are low capacity, detrusor overactivity and poor compliance. This is the typical initial picture with PUVs.
  • Bladder dynamics evolve. The valve bladder moves from overactivity and low capacity → better capacity and less contractility → insufficient contractility and poor emptying (“myogenic failure”).
  • Overactivity and poor compliance are the most dangerous patterns, because high pressure reaches the kidney even without reflux. Leaving them untreated puts the graft at risk.
  • Target: storage up to expected capacity for age at detrusor pressure <40 cm H₂O (during filling or at leak), plus social continence.
  • Medical treatment first (antimuscarinics); augmentation is second line. Medical treatment often brings in CIC. Some children delay CIC with double voiding or Valsalva emptying, and α-blockers can aid emptying.
  • Never assume volitional emptying is effective: prove it with repeated low PVRs and uroflowmetry.
  • Oxybutynin is titrated upwards because of its side effects, watching catheterised volumes, incontinence and hydronephrosis.
  • Adherence to pretransplant CIC is a test of adherence to the transplant regimen. A family that cannot manage CIC should be assessed for their ability to manage post-transplant care.

Capacity

  • The trend away from augmentation may have gone too far. Augment a small, non-compliant, high-pressure bladder when maximal medical treatment and CIC still give pressures that are safe only briefly or regularly exceed 40 cm H₂O.
  • There is no compelling evidence that augmentation increases complications during or after transplant.
  • Choice of segment depends on anatomy, previous surgery and whether a continent catheterisable stoma is wanted.

Infections

  • Graft pyelonephritis in an immunocompromised host causes major morbidity and can lead to graft loss.
  • The usual cause is urinary stasis: inadequate emptying, persistent obstruction or VUR. UTIs will not improve after transplant, so they are dealt with beforehand: double or triple voiding, or CIC.
  • Distinguish true UTI from colonisation, which is almost universal with CIC.
  • Persistent UTI despite good emptying and CIC technique → an anatomical abnormality or chronic colonisation.
  • Colonisation may respond to long-term antibiotics, intravesical antimicrobials and regular irrigation.
  • Anatomical causes such as diverticula or ureteric stumps → targeted imaging and surgery.

Bladder cycling: catheterise and instil saline in progressively increasing volumes with a set dwell time, then drain → start volumes empirically from what is tolerated and increase at regular intervals → add medication for capacity and compliance → target = expected capacity for age (age-based formulas) → steady gains without pain or leak predict further expansion → confirm safe pressure, capacity and compliance by urodynamics → clear for transplant only when acceptable, otherwise augment.

  • A renal allograft must never be used to test whether a bladder works.
  • Cycling also tests the family's ability to perform CIC.

Decision to augment the bladder.

  • Early proactive management reduces or delays the need to augment. A trial of regular CIC, overnight drainage and maximal medical therapy identifies the few who need it. Enterocystoplasty remains one of the most effective ways to provide storage.
  • Broad indication: inability to protect the upper tracts and to reach >75% of expected capacity at <40 cm H₂O while catheterising no more often than every 3–4 hours. These thresholds are somewhat arbitrary.

KEY POINTS: PRETRANSPLANT PREPARATION

  • Commonest abnormality: low-capacity, high-pressure, poorly compliant bladder.
  • Augmentation does not clearly raise transplant risk; recurrent pyelonephritis does (graft loss).
  • Pretransplant CIC and bladder cycling both treat the bladder and test the family.

Concept — Preparation follows one hierarchy: drugs → CIC → cycling → augmentation. Each step is tested before the next, because the bladder changes over time and augmentation is irreversible. The PUV and neuropathic bladders sit on opposite trajectories, so the same urodynamic numbers argue for delay in one and for early surgery in the other.

Cutaneous Stomas

  • A defunctionalised bladder may sit behind an ileal or colon conduit, vesicostomy, pyelostomy or ureterostomy. The donor ureter can drain into these.
  • Contemporary preference is definitive bladder optimisation before transplant. A long-term stoma or transplant ureterostomy is reserved for severe neurodevelopmental impairment or inability to tolerate CIC.
  • A functioning ileal loop can become the augmentation patch during refunctionalisation, even in an anuric child. If augmentation proves unnecessary and the child still makes urine, reimplant the native kidney(s) into the bladder and discard the loop.

Reconstruction Strategies

  • Factors to weigh: dialysis type, likely graft site, timing relative to KRT, need for bowel surgery, and the native kidneys.

Dialysis Issues

  • On peritoneal dialysis (PD): intraperitoneal surgery usually forces a temporary or permanent switch to haemodialysis. Surgery can sometimes be done at PD catheter insertion, but bowel surgery raises the risk of catheter infection and removal. Protect the peritoneum, because PD is often the best temporary KRT in children.
  • Approaching KRT: dialysis before reconstruction improves metabolic state (healing, tolerance of bowel reabsorption), but extensive surgery may lose the peritoneum for PD.
  • On haemodialysis: risks are line infection with postoperative sepsis and arteriovenous fistula injury from low flow or pressure.

Graft Placement

  • Very small child, possibly intraperitoneal graft → move mesenteric pedicles away from the midline. Such children rarely have earlier reconstruction, and most grafts fit extraperitoneally.
  • A previous psoas hitch or extensive ipsilateral reimplant dissection makes ipsilateral iliac graft placement difficult.
  • In difficult cases, a detailed operative report with a diagram is of great value to later surgeons.

Timing

  • Major reconstruction is best done before transplant. Allow 6–12 weeks to heal after enterocystoplasty or continent diversion.
  • Unilateral native nephrectomy at transplant is reasonable, especially on the graft side.
  • Avoid concurrent procedures in infants and small children because of prolonged anaesthesia and haemodynamic instability. Prophylactic anticoagulation against graft thrombosis also raises bleeding risk.

Enterocystoplasty

  • It is feasible on dialysis or near ESKD, much as in any child. The segment and strategy are chosen on functional anatomy: stoma need, native bladder available, bladder neck and continence, and bowel function.
  • An unsalvageable bladder needing continent diversion should not wait for transplant. Exception: a child transplanted in the first 2 years of life, before continence is expected.
  • Metabolic problems are not an absolute contraindication. Urine reabsorption may bring dialysis forward in advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD). In less marginal cases, better bladder dynamics and fewer infections may delay ESKD.

Limiting the Risk of Infection

  • Prevent infection proactively, not only after systemic illness. Targets: high-grade reflux, persistent hydronephrosis with or without reflux, and CIC-dependent children (colonised and UTI-prone).
  • A non-dilated, non-refluxing native kidney is unlikely to be the source of infection.
  • Circumcision in boys at risk of ESKD, especially with valves, should be discussed early. It may slow progression to CKD and reduce post-transplant UTI.

Combining Nephrectomy and Transplantation

  • Generally avoided to limit morbidity and anaesthetic time. It is mostly done to make room for an adult graft in a small child or beside very large kidneys. The best case is a single ipsilateral nephrectomy through the transplant incision.

KEY POINTS: PRETRANSPLANT RECONSTRUCTION

  • Spontaneous emptying decides the need for a catheterisable stoma.
  • Augment for capacity <75%, pressure >40 cm H₂O, CIC every ≤3 hours, despite maximal medical therapy.
  • PD → intraperitoneal surgery needs temporary haemodialysis; reconstruct well before transplant.
  • Native nephrectomy indications as in the table above.

Concept — Every reconstructive decision is judged by what it costs the future transplant: the peritoneum for PD, the iliac fossa for the graft, the ureter for salvage, the native kidney for fluid and hormones. Preserve by default, and remove only for a named indication.

TRANSPLANTATION

  • The vascular technique is covered in Chapter 92, with the same principles in children. For the paediatric urologist, lower-tract reconstruction is the intraoperative focus and the main source of urological complications.

Ureteral Anastomosis

Surgical Techniques and Options

  • As in VUR surgery, there are intravesical and extravesical options. The authors favour a non-refluxing anastomosis into the bladder: it is simple, effective and low-morbidity, although an antireflux mechanism is not always essential.

Extravesical ureteroneocystostomy (preferred): after reperfusion and haemostasis, partially fill the bladder (saline or dilute antibiotic) → clear the anterolateral wall; traction sutures lift the lateral bladder and tension the wall → check the ureter will reach → detrusorotomy 3–4 cm down to mucosa, in an anatomical line that avoids kinking the donor ureter → raise detrusor flaps → excise a small mucosal disc at the distal trough → trim the ureter and spatulate 4–5 mm → interrupted mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis with fine absorbable sutures → anchor stitches through detrusor and ureteric adventitia against eversion → close the flaps over the ureter with interrupted absorbable sutures, not too tight at the entry point.

Ureteral Stenting

  • Routine stenting is debated, and there is no conclusive evidence for it in children.
  • Stent when: the reimplant is difficult (especially into an abnormal bladder), or the graft ureter looks traumatised or devascularised.
  • In adult series with deceased-donor grafts, stenting reduced ureteric complications at the cost of more infection.
  • Standard: a short double-J removed at 4–6 weeks by cystoscopy, timed with removal of a tunnelled line or PD catheter.
  • Early removal at 5–7 days (stent tied to the urethral catheter tip, or an externalised stent or splint) may reduce stent complications and infection.

KEY POINTS: TRANSPLANT URETERONEOCYSTOSTOMY

  • Extravesical non-refluxing reimplant preferred; ureteroureterostomy effective.
  • No data for routine stenting; early removal is safe and may cut infection.

COMPLICATIONS

  • Vascular and rejection complications are covered in Chapter 92. Urological complications are uncommon and rarely cost the graft.
  • Thrombosis is one of the commonest causes of early graft loss in children, so some centres anticoagulate early, which demands careful haemostasis.
  • Pre-existing obstructive or reflux uropathy increases all urological complications. The link between PUVs and graft dysfunction is inconsistent across series. Apply special vigilance and a lower threshold to intervene.

Urine Leaks

  • Rare and early: wound drainage, rising drain output or a perinephric collection. Fluid creatinine distinguishes urine from lymph or peritoneal fluid.

Sequence: check every drain, above all the bladder catheter (irrigate it; replace it if removed) → transplant ultrasound: hydronephrosis means distal obstruction, so consider percutaneous nephrostomy → confirm stent position → localise the leak with a mercaptoacetyltriglycine (MAG3) scan (if function is adequate) or a cystogram (anastomotic leak, or pelvicalyceal leak if a stent allows reflux) → small leak: observe → small child with high output: a larger catheter may resolve it → persistent or large leak: percutaneous drainage and/or exploration.

  • Prevention: at transplant, place the largest catheter the urethra will take.
  • Repair by cause:
    • Bladder-side leak → revise the anastomosis and drain.
    • Short distal ureteric necrosis → mobilise the bladder and reimplant.
    • Long necrosis → ipsilateral or even contralateral native ureter; otherwise psoas hitch or bladder flap. Stent and drain carefully.

Infection

  • Early infections relate to the catheter, stent and instrumentation. Late infection is a relatively frequent, delayed complication that reflects bladder function and the original uropathy in an immunosuppressed host.
  • High-grade or worsening hydronephrosis → pyelonephritis and functional loss, so evaluate promptly. Routine assessment covers emptying, hydronephrosis and selective VCUG.
  • On CIC with no correctable cause → prophylactic antibiotics and bladder irrigation, plus proactive bladder management.
  • Graft VUR with recurrent febrile UTI → surgical correction. Reflux without infection and with normal bladder function → observe.

Concept — Transplant reflux is managed by consequence, not by grade. Infection plus a solitary immunosuppressed kidney justifies surgery, its absence justifies observation, and the bladder must be optimised before the ureter is blamed.

Hydronephrosis and Obstruction

  • Hydronephrosis is frequent after transplant and signals obstruction in up to 8%. The graft is unusually sensitive to obstruction, and the grade of hydronephrosis does not track the degree of impairment.
  • Rising creatinine and cystatin C often herald obstruction. More than half of obstructions occur within 100 days, so long-term ultrasound surveillance is needed.
  • Normal pre-transplant bladder → this pattern is ureteric obstruction until proven otherwise. A known or suspected bladder problem → evaluate and treat both. Obstruction is commoner with bladder abnormality, especially PUVs.
  • Isolated VUR is unlikely to cause progressive hydronephrosis and falling function.

  • Obstruction and rejection can coexist. Diagnostic tests for obstruction in a graft are unreliable, so keep a low threshold for diagnostic stenting. Occasionally, an acutely failing graft recovers after stenting even without rejection.
  • Site and cause: usually distal stenosis at the reimplant, often with ischaemia of the distal donor ureter. The implant technique and stenting are not conclusively linked to obstruction. PUV bladders are a risk factor.
  • Extrinsic causes: lymphocele and post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease (PTLD).
  • Treatment:
    • Focal narrowing → balloon dilatation and stenting for 4–6 weeks.
    • Long-term stenting (adult practice) is of uncertain value in children.
    • Open repair should not be delayed excessively: native ureter, bladder flaps, or pyeloureteral anastomosis to a healthy retained native ureter.
    • Avoid non-urothelial substitutes such as the appendix where possible, and follow reoperative principles (well-vascularised tissue).

Bladder Dysfunction

  • It raises infection risk and can impair drainage, which harms the graft. It is hard to separate from ureteric obstruction, so it needs sequential diagnostic drainage:
    • Catheter for 1–2 weeks → creatinine falls and hydronephrosis improves → bladder is the cause.
    • If not → stent plus catheter and recheck.
  • Treatment: behavioural and medical measures and a new or intensified CIC programme. Augmentation rarely, and only after maximal medical treatment. A continent stoma if urethral CIC is difficult.

Stones

  • Uncommon (rates in the table above); risk is higher with a history of stones.
  • With proper management, stones do not increase graft loss but are linked to more UTIs.
  • Bladder stones reported in the past were often on retained suture material.
  • Donor-derived stones: bench flexible ureteroscopy can clear small stones during cold preparation. Adult data suggest “donor-gifted” lithiasis is rarely troublesome and can be observed; there are no paediatric data.
  • New asymptomatic stone → monitor closely and look for a cause. Treat promptly if >1 cm and growing, or causing obstruction, infection or graft dysfunction.
  • Any standard modality may be used, but choose the one most likely to succeed in a single intervention, even if it is more complex. Account for graft position, stone size and site, and retrograde access to the ureter.

KEY POINTS: COMPLICATIONS

  • Stenosis 6%, leak 3%, stones 2%, significant reflux 2%.
  • Leaks early; infection late, reflecting the bladder; transplant VUR costs more than native VUR.
  • Creatinine rise plus hydronephrosis: obstruction and rejection may coexist; obstruction is usually distal.
  • Bladder dysfunction causes infection and can itself obstruct.

Concept — Post-transplant urological complications sort by time: leaks early, obstruction within about 100 days, infection late. Nearly all are amplified by the original bladder. That is why every diagnostic pathway begins with a catheter, which separates bladder from ureter, before anything more invasive.

SUMMARY

  • Success rests on a clear pretransplant bladder strategy, urologist involvement in every case, diligent surveillance before damage is irreversible, and multidisciplinary collaboration.
