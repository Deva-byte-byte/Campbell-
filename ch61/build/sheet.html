Chapter 61 — Urologic Considerations in Pediatric Renal Transplantation — Numbers, Gold Standards and Traps

If you read nothing else

  • Safe storage: detrusor pressure <40 cm H₂O at age-expected capacity.
  • Augment: capacity <75% expected, pressure >40, CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) every ≤3 h, despite maximal therapy.
  • Never use the allograft to test the bladder.
  • Preferred anastomosis: extravesical non-refluxing ureteroneocystostomy.
  • Salvage for graft-ureter stenosis: ureteroureterostomy to the native ureter.
  • Commonest urological complication: ureteric stenosis (6%).
  • Obstruction: >half within 100 days; usually distal at the reimplant.
  • Creatinine rise + hydronephrosis: catheter first; obstruction and rejection may coexist.
  • Transplant VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) + recurrent febrile UTI (urinary tract infection) → reimplant; asymptomatic → observe.
  • Default: keep native kidneys; remove only for a named indication.

Introduction and pretransplant assessment

  • Five goals: unimpeded drainage · low-pressure capacious storage · volitional emptying · no infection · fewest operations.
  • Commonest age at transplant: 13–17 years.
  • Commonest congenital cause: hypoplasia, aplasia or dysplasia, then obstructive uropathy; commonest acquired: FSGS (focal segmental glomerulosclerosis).
  • Young boys → PUVs (posterior urethral valves); adults → hypertension, diabetes.
  • Screen all: history + renal ultrasound + uroflow with PVR (postvoid residual).
  • Routine VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) not needed; do it if neuropathic bladder, obstruction, VUR, previous surgery, febrile UTI.
  • Urodynamics: neuropathic bladder, PUVs or large ureterocele, refractory dysfunction, high-grade hydronephrosis, recurrent UTI.
  • Anuric, no urological history → no further testing.
  • Algorithm: small capacity <30% → anticholinergic and reassess.

Pretransplant preparation

Bladder:

  • Commonest early ESKD (end-stage kidney disease) bladder: low capacity, overactivity, poor compliance.
  • Most dangerous: overactivity and poor compliance (pressure reaches the kidney without reflux).
  • Valve bladder trajectory → myogenic failure.
  • First line: antimuscarinic (oxybutynin, titrate up); augmentation second line.
  • Better tolerated: solifenacin, darifenacin, mirabegron (β3-agonist), botulinum toxin.
  • α-blocker → aids emptying.
  • Volitional emptying never assumed; prove it with repeated PVR and uroflow.
  • CIC adherence = test of transplant adherence.
  • Cycling target: expected capacity for age; confirm by urodynamics.

Augmentation:

  • No evidence augmentation raises transplant complications.
  • Ileum or sigmoid = commonest segments; malignancy risk → lifelong surveillance.
  • Gastric: haematuria–dysuria syndrome, worst in anuric patients; rarely used.
  • Ureterocystoplasty: always explore first; no mucus, stones or metabolic disturbance.
  • PUV bladder → delay augmentation; neuropathic, borderline → augment earlier.
  • Stomas sited medial to the transplant incision.
  • Gibson incision, retroperitoneal = usual graft site; <15 kg → aorta and IVC (inferior vena cava), possibly transperitoneal.

Timing:

  • Reconstruct before transplant; 6–12 weeks to heal after enterocystoplasty.
  • Exception: transplant in the first 2 years → defer continent diversion.
  • No concurrent procedures in infants; anticoagulation raises bleeding.
  • Metabolic problems = not an absolute contraindication to diversion.

Dialysis:

  • On PD (peritoneal dialysis): intraperitoneal surgery → temporary haemodialysis.
  • PD + small kidneys → retroperitoneal (prone bilateral); PD restarts immediately.
  • PD + large kidneys → posterior open or laparoscopic morcellation.
  • High risk → embolisation.

Native nephrectomy indications:

  • Refractory hypertension · nephrotic syndrome with malnutrition · massive polyuria · recurrent upper-tract UTI · large stone burden · malignancy risk · very large kidneys · massive VUR (relative).
  • Keep otherwise: water excretion, erythropoietin, vitamin D.
  • Combined with transplant: avoid; best = single ipsilateral nephrectomy through the transplant incision.

Ureter:

  • Normal ureter → keep; transect high (salvage ureteroureterostomy, stoma).
  • Dilated, refluxing or obstructed → remove totally.
  • Circumcision in boys with valves → fewer UTIs.
  • Ileal loop → augmentation patch, or discard after native reimplant.

Transplantation

  • Extravesical: detrusorotomy 3–4 cm; spatulate 4–5 mm; mucosa-to-mucosa.
  • Barry technique: 4-cm tunnel between parallel incisions.
  • Ureteroureterostomy: normal native ureter, small bladder, short donor ureter, hostile pelvis.
  • Routine stent: no evidence; stent if reimplant difficult or ureter devascularised.
  • Double-J out at 4–6 weeks; early removal at 5–7 days → fewer infections.

Complications

  • Thrombosis = a leading cause of early graft loss in children.
  • Pre-existing uropathy → more urological complications.
  • Rates: stenosis 6% (obstruction up to 8%), leak 3% (<5%), stones 2%, significant reflux 2%.
  • Leak: early; fluid creatinine confirms; check the catheter first.
  • Localise the leak with MAG3 (mercaptoacetyltriglycine scan) or cystogram.
  • Largest catheter the urethra takes = prevention.
  • Long ureteric necrosis → native ureter (ipsilateral or contralateral), psoas hitch, bladder flap.
  • Endoscopic injection for graft VUR: about one-third resolve; higher obstruction risk.
  • Reimplant for graft VUR: intravesical preferred; stent.
  • ≥2 UTIs → lower graft GFR (glomerular filtration rate) at 4 years.
  • Mild hydronephrosis + signs of rejection → biopsy first.
  • Bladder dysfunction test: catheter 1–2 weeks → creatinine falls → bladder is the cause.
  • PUVs → lower obstruction-free survival.
  • Focal stenosis → balloon dilatation + stent 4–6 weeks.
  • Lymphocele, PTLD (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease) = extrinsic causes; graft mass = PTLD until proven otherwise.
  • Stones: treat if >1 cm and growing, obstructing or infected; choose single-intervention success.
  • Donor stones: bench flexible ureteroscopy; donor-gifted stones observed (adult data).

Classic traps

  • Trap: test the bladder after transplant — Actually: never use the graft to test the bladder.
  • Trap: no reflux means a safe bladder — Actually: high pressure harms the kidney without reflux.
  • Trap: augmentation raises transplant risk — Actually: no compelling evidence.
  • Trap: gastric augment for an acidotic child — Actually: haematuria–dysuria syndrome; abandoned.
  • Trap: augment the PUV bladder early — Actually: postpone; it heads towards myogenic failure.
  • Trap: remove native kidneys routinely — Actually: keep unless indicated.
  • Trap: routine stenting is evidence-based — Actually: no data; stent selectively.
  • Trap: Deflux for graft VUR — Actually: poor resolution, higher obstruction risk.
  • Trap: hydronephrosis grade reflects graft impairment — Actually: it does not correlate.
  • Trap: isolated VUR explains a rising creatinine with hydronephrosis — Actually: suspect obstruction.
