Chapter 54 — Management of Paediatric Kidney Stone Disease

Chapter Notes · Campbell-Walsh-Wein Urology, Part V Paediatric Urology, pp. 1091–1106

Epidemiology of Early-Onset Kidney Stone Disease

  • Nephrolithiasis is a disorder of mineral metabolism punctuated by acute symptomatic events; about 50% recur within 5 years of the index presentation.
  • United States (US) prevalence has doubled: 5% (1988–1994) → 11% (2015–2018).
  • Incidence in youth is rising 5–10% per year, disproportionately to adults; the greatest rise is in adolescents, particularly females — in females aged 15–19 years, 75 → 120 per 100,000 (1997 → 2012), 52% higher than in males of the same age.
  • Sex ratio reverses with age of onset: adult-onset disease was historically male-predominant, 3 : 1 (gap now narrowing); early-onset disease is female-predominant. The reason is unknown.
  • Ethnicity: commonest in non-Hispanic white children → Hispanic white → African American.
  • Early-onset disease is defined as onset at <24 years.
  • Burden: rising hospitalisations, emergency department (ED) visits, operations and costs — at least US$375 million per year (2010) in hospital and ED care alone, before outpatient care, drugs, imaging and caregivers' lost earnings. Recurrence turns a single event into a chronic condition.

  • Children's urine is more alkaline, which favours CaP and disfavours uric acid (which precipitates in acid urine).
  • The view that childhood stones are mostly genetic, inborn-error or infective is no longer true; most are idiopathic CaOx, as in adults.
  • Randall's plaques — hydroxyapatite (calcium phosphate) deposits on the renal papillae — are the platform on which most idiopathic CaOx stones grow; children with CaOx stones show the same papillary changes as adults (white papillary deposits to which stones attach).

Determinants

  • Genetic susceptibility × exposome (non-genetic environmental exposures) × comorbid disease — rare monogenic disease excepted.
  • Heritability is about half, higher in males (twin studies); it is not explained by rare monogenic disease (primary hyperoxaluria, cystinuria). Polygenic determinants remain undefined, though polymorphisms raise risk both in healthy people and in those with diabetes, obesity and gout.
  • Incomplete genetic determination implies gene–exposome interaction — diet, medications, ambient environment. Low fluid and high sodium intake are the two key Western-diet risks.
  • Medications act by two routes:
    • Direct — the drug crystallises through poor urinary solubility: 1–2% of stones.
    • Indirect — antibiotics: oral cephalosporins, broad-spectrum penicillins, nitrofurantoin, fluoroquinolones and sulfonamides raise the odds of stones (population-based case–control study); risk wanes but persists up to 5 years and is greatest in children; the same association is seen in adult women.
  • Proposed mechanism — microbiome disruption: children with CaOx stones have fewer oxalate-degrading and butyrate-producing taxa. Butyrate is anti-inflammatory, protects against renal ischaemic injury, maintains the gut mucosal barrier and regulates intestinal SLC26 oxalate transporters; losing both functions synergistically increases oxalate absorption and excretion.

A Disorder of Mineral Metabolism

  • About 50% recur within 3–5 years of the index stone — equal to or higher than in adults; adolescents have more symptomatic events than younger children.

Key points: epidemiology of kidney stone disease

  • Dramatic rise in paediatric incidence, particularly in adolescent females; female predominance in early-onset disease.
  • About 80% of paediatric stones are predominantly CaOx; CaP slightly commoner, pure uric acid less common than in adults.
  • Genetic susceptibility, the exposome and comorbid disease are the primary determinants.
  • A disorder of mineral metabolism, associated with cardiovascular disease, CKD and decreased BMD.

Concept — Early-onset stone disease is the adult disease started early: the same idiopathic CaOx-on-Randall's-plaque pathway, driven by gene–exposome interaction, with a longer runway for recurrence, CKD, hypertension and bone loss.

Evaluation

Child with Suspected Nephrolithiasis

  • Older children present as adults do: flank pain, nausea and vomiting, lower urinary tract symptoms.
  • History: onset, frequency and duration of pain; personal and family stone history; relevant urological surgery; factors raising pre-test probability. Always ascertain fever.
  • Young or non-verbal children cannot localise pain → rely heavily on imaging and laboratory tests.

Imaging

  • European Association of Urology (EAU) and an American Urological Association (AUA) technical paper agree: ultrasound (US) first; non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) only if US is non-diagnostic and suspicion remains high.
  • Image Gently Alliance (2007) — campaign to lower paediatric radiation; US-first pathways reduce initial CT use.
  • Diagnostic algorithm: suspected stone → US (grey-scale + colour Doppler) → positive → treat as indicated; negative + high suspicion ± secondary signs (e.g. hydronephrosis) → low-dose NCCT; negative + low suspicion → stop.
  • US stone criteria (both): hyperechoic focus in papilla, calyx or pelvis → confirmatory twinkle artefact on colour Doppler.
  • Maximise Doppler pulse repetition frequency (PRF) to suppress vascular-flow noise.
  • Posterior acoustic shadow greatly raises specificity when present, but modern harmonic and compound imaging rarely shadows stones <4 mm.
  • Stones missed on US are usually clinically insignificant (small or non-obstructing). Renal resistive indices have been studied in adults as an adjunct.
  • US resolution exceeds many CT scans: twinkling foci of about 2 mm invisible on CT are often Randall's plaques ± small attached stones (CT shows only hyperdense papillae) — consistent with US overestimating stone size by 1–2 mm. Plaques share the US characteristics of stones but are typically <4 mm.

  • Children carry greater CT risk — longer life expectancy, radiosensitive tissue; a substantial minority have ≥2 CTs per episode.
  • When CT: high suspicion + non-diagnostic US (e.g. hydronephrosis without visible stone) → low-dose NCCT of abdomen and pelvis → size and location of a ureteric stone decide observation versus surgery → prevents silent obstruction and loss of renal function.
  • Dose reduction: 40 mA (half standard) for children ≤50 kg keeps diagnostic accuracy; much lower amperage still detects 2–8 mm stones at about one-third of the dose.

Concept — Paediatric imaging is governed by radiation economy: US is accepted despite imperfect sensitivity because what it misses is usually insignificant, and CT is reserved for the one situation — a suspected obstructing ureteric stone not seen on US — where a miss would cost renal function.

Child with Confirmed Nephrolithiasis

Medical History and Physical Examination

  • Family history of stones, haematuria or CKD → inherited disease: cystinuria, primary hyperoxaluria, CYP24A1 mutations, Dent disease.
  • Dietary history in every child: fluid, salt, vitamins C and D, mineral supplements, special diets (ketogenic).
  • Medication history: corticosteroids; furosemide, acetazolamide; indinavir; antibiotics; topiramate, zonisamide.
  • At-risk history: prematurity, urinary tract anomalies, urinary tract infection (UTI), malabsorption, prolonged immobility.
  • Examination clues: dysmorphism → Williams syndrome; rickets → Dent disease, hereditary hypophosphataemic rickets with hypercalciuria (HHRH); tetany → familial hypomagnesaemia with hypercalciuria and nephrocalcinosis (FHHNC), autosomal dominant hypocalcaemic hypercalciuria (ADHH); gout → hypoxanthine–guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT) deficiency, phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate synthetase (PRPS) superactivity.

Metabolic Evaluation

  • An identifiable metabolic abnormality raises recurrence risk up to fivefold → every child has a comprehensive metabolic evaluation.
  • Rationale in early onset: more autosomal recessive (monogenic) causes; a possibly more severe phenotype; a longer time for recurrence and end-organ effects (e.g. BMD).
  • Start with stone analysis — infrared spectroscopy or X-ray diffraction; cystine, struvite, 2,8-dihydroxyadenine (2,8-DHA) and xanthine are diagnostic in themselves.

Urinary Metabolic Abnormalities

Hypercalciuria

  • Commonest abnormality: 30–50% of stone-forming children. Commonest cause at all ages: idiopathic hypercalciuria — hypercalciuria without hypercalcaemia and no other cause.
  • Familial idiopathic hypercalciuria: gene(s) unknown; autosomal dominant, incomplete penetrance. Present in about 4% of healthy children, 40–50% of whom have a family history of stones.
  • Definition: >4 mg/kg/day in children >2 years, or total >200–250 mg/day. Not toilet-trained → random calcium : creatinine; school-age normal ≤0.21 mg/mg; physiological excretion is higher in younger children.
  • Sequence: confirm hypercalciuria → check serum calcium → exclude secondary causes → only then diagnose idiopathic hypercalciuria.

Hyperoxaluria

  • Oxalate is an end product of glyoxylate and ascorbic acid metabolism, renally excreted; 80–85% endogenous, 10–15% dietary. Normal <40 mg/day/1.73 m²; young children → random oxalate : creatinine.
  • Primary = inherited hepatic overproduction (rare) versus secondary = increased absorption or excess precursor intake (commoner).

Primary Hyperoxaluria (PH)

  • PH1–3: rare autosomal recessive disorders of hepatic oxalate overproduction → hyperoxaluria → nephrocalcinosis and nephrolithiasis → progressive CKD.
  • Phenotype spans neonatal ESRD to occasional stone passage in adulthood → diagnosis often delayed, sometimes until loss of a transplanted kidney.

Secondary Hyperoxaluria

  • Excess intake of oxalate or precursors (high-dose vitamin C) versus enteric hyperabsorption.
  • Gut oxalate absorption varies inversely with dietary calcium → calcium-deficient diets raise urinary oxalate.
  • Enteric causes (fat malabsorption, chronic diarrhoea): inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (cystic fibrosis), biliary disease, small-bowel resection / short bowel.
  • Mechanism: free fatty acids bind luminal calcium → more unbound oxalate absorbed.

Hypocitraturia

  • Citrate is reabsorbed and metabolised in the proximal tubule. Defined (24 h) as citrate : creatinine <128 mg/g (males), <300 mg/g (females).
  • Mechanism: proximal tubular intracellular acidosis (metabolic acidosis or hypokalaemia) → ↑ citrate reabsorption.
  • Causes: ketogenic diet; topiramate, acetazolamide; dRTA; chronic diarrhoea. Incomplete dRTA has no overt acidosis or hypokalaemia → missed unless acid-load testing is done.
  • Most cases are idiopathic; diets high in animal protein, low in fibre and potassium lower citrate.

Cystinuria

  • Autosomal recessive; SLC3A1 or SLC7A9 → failed proximal reabsorption of cystine, lysine, ornithine, arginine — only cystine crystallises.
  • Normal <60 mg/day/1.73 m²; homozygotes often >400.
  • Usually presents in the second or third decade, but can present in infancy with staghorn calculi, occasionally bladder stones.
  • Poor solubility → recurrent stones → long-term renal failure risk; UTI; mixed cystine/struvite stones.

Hyperuricosuria

  • Children excrete more uric acid: fractional excretion 30–50% (neonate) → 8–12% (adult level) by adolescence. Paradox: uric acid stones are <5% of paediatric calculi.
  • Epitaxy: one crystal lattice (uric acid) seeds growth of another (CaOx) in the same orientation → hyperuricosuric CaOx stones.
  • Definition >815 mg/day/1.73 m²; per-GFR excretion is constant after age 2. Spot urine (>2 years): >0.56 mg/dL GFR = urine uric acid × plasma creatinine ÷ urine creatinine (all mg/dL).
  • Greatest risk for uric acid stones: hyperuricosuria + low urine pH.

Key points: evaluation of children with kidney stone disease

  • An identifiable metabolic abnormality increases recurrence risk.
  • Every child needs a comprehensive evaluation, including 24-hour urine for calcium, oxalate, uric acid, sodium, citrate, creatinine, volume, pH and cystine.
  • Idiopathic hypercalciuria in about 30–50% of stone-forming children.
  • Hypocitraturia, hyperoxaluria and cystinuria remain important and must be identified.

Concept — Every urinary risk factor is either an excess of a promoter (calcium, oxalate, urate, cystine) or a deficit of an inhibitor (citrate), each with a rare monogenic cause at one end and a common idiopathic or dietary cause at the other — hence the work-up runs stone analysis → serum → indexed urine → specific markers or genetics.

Management of Children and Adolescents with Kidney and Ureteral Stones

Medical Expulsive Therapy

  • Options for a ureteric stone: observation with analgesia → observation plus medical expulsive therapy (MET) → surgery.
  • MET = α-blockers (less often calcium-channel blockers): α1A/α1D antagonism in the distal-third ureter and vesicoureteric junction → ureteric dilatation.
  • Spontaneous passage is higher in adults, for stones <5 mm and for distal stones. α-Blockers help most in adults with distal stones >5 mm; the paediatric size of greatest benefit is unknown.
  • AUA / Endourological Society (EUS) 2016: uncomplicated ureteric stones ≤10 mm → observation ± α-blocker MET (moderate; grade B). A later meta-analysis of randomised trials confirmed α-blockers increase passage in children.
  • Counsel: off-label; maximum duration unknown but ≤6 weeks; tamsulosin appears effective and safe.

Surgical Management

  • Up to 60% of children with renal or ureteric stones need surgery, yet nephrolithiasis has the fewest randomised trials of ten major kidney diseases.
  • Options as in adults — ureteroscopy (URS), shockwave lithotripsy (SWL), percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) — all need anaesthesia and, traditionally, radiation.
  • Ideal modality: (1) high monotherapeutic success; (2) low complication risk; (3) return to baseline activity; (4) minimal radiation; (5) minimal anaesthesia; (6) no ancillary procedures.
  • Patient-centred choice: patient and stone factors, surgeon experience, resources — and, most importantly, patient-driven goals of care.
  • Only 8 AUA/EUS 2016 recommendations address children; half are expert opinion. The 2017 National Urology Research Agenda prioritised paediatric stones; the Pediatric KIDney Stone (PKIDS) Care Improvement Network is comparing clinical and patient-reported outcomes of URS, SWL and PCNL.

Surgical Antimicrobial Prophylaxis

  • All URS and PCNL; SWL only if infection risk is raised. No paediatric-specific recommendations.
  • Urine culture before every upper tract procedure — guides antibiotics, especially for percutaneous surgery, high-grade obstruction and indwelling stents.
  • Authors' regimen: negative culture, uncomplicated URS → perioperative cefazolin; percutaneous surgery or existing stent/nephrostomy → fluoroquinolone or ampicillin + gentamicin. Stop within 24 hours unless infection is suspected.

Goals of Therapy

  • As in adults: stone clearance with the least harm to physical, emotional and social health.
  • Primary determinants: stone size and location, anatomy, patient (and provider) preference; secondary: comorbidity, composition, equipment. In practice the treating site strongly shapes the choice.

Stone Clearance

  • The most important surgical outcome; residual fragments → growth and symptomatic events.
  • No consensus on significant residual fragment size in children; assessment (visual at URS, US, CT) varies, hampering comparison.
  • Why 4 mm on US: US overestimates by about 2 mm + 2 mm = conventional CT threshold for insignificance.

Radiation

  • Children face more future imaging and interventions, and higher cumulative risk (longer life, radiosensitive tissue). Exposure spans diagnosis, operation and surveillance.
  • URS and PCNL use fluoroscopy; SWL does unless US-targeted. PCNL delivers several times the dose of URS; bilateral URS more than unilateral.
  • Limits: ≤50 mSv in any 12 months; average <20 mSv/year over 5 years.
  • Reduction levers: maximise source-to-skin distance, improve skin-to-intensifier distance, collimate; lower kVp, pulsed dosing, paediatric dose settings, no routine magnification; intermittent low-dose rather than continuous fluoroscopy; radiation checklists; US guidance for URS, PCNL access and SWL targeting.

Ancillary Procedures / Anaesthesia

  • Several procedures and anaesthetics are often needed: a small ureter may force a pre-stent for passive dilatation (an extra anaesthetic); SWL often needs retreatment.
  • Concern: anaesthetic neurotoxicity in the developing brain, especially pre-school children.

Concept — Paediatric stone surgery is judged on a ledger with more columns than in adults: clearance must be bought with the fewest anaesthetics and the least cumulative radiation, because the child carries both costs across a longer lifetime.

Ureteroscopic Management of Upper Urinary Tract Calculi

  • URS is a mainstay: pooled paediatric series show clearance approaching 90% with mostly low-grade complications. Thulium fibre laser cleared better than low-power holmium, without more complications.
  • Efficacy falls with lower-pole location and small patient size: small kidneys and tight infundibulopelvic angles limit manoeuvring; children <6 years have lower success and more complications; <20 kg carries a high complication rate.
  • Pre-stenting. Forcing a scope or active dilatation risks perforation and stricture, so some ureters need a stent first; but AUA/EUS advise against routine pre-stenting, which is linked to more ED visits after URS. α-Blockers may aid primary access (preliminary).

Ureteroscopic Technique in Children

Sequence: general anaesthesia → confirm sterile urine culture → lithotomy (sized stirrups; infants: gel rolls under the knees; pad pressure points; leg weight on heels, not calves or knees — peroneal nerve) → broad-spectrum prophylaxis → rigid cystoscopy (7.5, 10 or 14.5 Fr) → retrograde pyelogram (RPG) + wire under fluoroscopy → calibrate orifice; safety wire via 8/10 Fr dual-lumen catheter/coaxial dilator → if 8/10 Fr will not pass: 4.6–4.8 Fr stent for ≥14 days, then return → URS.

  • No balloon dilatation of the orifice (stricture); no active distal dilatation — injury risk, and the ureter proximal to the orifice also limits safe URS.
  • Semirigid for ureteric, flexible for proximal ureteric and renal stones — both over a safety wire. UAS (10/12 or 12/14 Fr) routine for flexible URS, especially large proximal ureteric stones and renal burdens needing basketing.
  • URS is less position-dependent than SWL, but the small paediatric lower pole is still hard to reach: 150–200 µm fibres for the lower pole (better deflection) versus 365 µm for ureteric, mid- and upper-pole stones.
  • Irrigant isotonic, at body temperature → no hyponatraemia or hypothermia. Basket when feasible; otherwise holmium:YAG fragmentation.
  • Postoperative stent decision: duration, number of scope passes, visible ureteric trauma or oedema. Removal: string 5–7 days (home or nurse) versus cystoscopy under anaesthesia after 7 days.
  • Stent symptoms are common → paracetamol, ibuprofen, α-blockers, anticholinergics; no routine opioids.

Limitations and Complications

  • Ureteric injury, UTI, bleeding; >Clavien III uncommon.
  • Ureteric injury → abort and stent (limits shear, ischaemia, extravasation). Injury arises at scope introduction, during instrument passage — especially at an impacted stone — and when withdrawing a basketed stone too large for the ureter.
  • Stricture: RPG defines degree, length and location → short mid-ureteric: prolonged stenting (uncertain long-term) · endoscopic: laser incision, not balloon · longer, denser or recurrent: formal (often robotic) reconstruction · distal: ureteroneocystostomy · proximal: pyeloplasty · mid: excision and primary anastomosis.

Key points: ureteroscopic management of upper tract calculi

  • Miniaturised instruments and adapted adult techniques treat calculi <15 mm effectively and safely.
  • No routine pre-stenting.
  • Postoperative stenting depends on duration, number of passes and visible trauma or oedema.

Concept — Every paediatric ureteroscopy decision is a negotiation with calibre: the ureter's size decides pre-stenting, wire, sheath and scope, and the kidney's size decides fibre choice — so passive dilatation is preferred to force, and the smallest instrument that works is chosen.

Shockwave Lithotripsy

  • Introduced in the early 1980s; first successful paediatric SWL in 1986. Guideline role: URS or SWL for ureteric stones unlikely to pass; SWL or URS first-line for renal burden ≤20 mm.
  • Efficacy varies with lithotripter generation, technique, habitus and clearance definition; habitus matters less in children — shorter skin-to-stone distance.

Shockwave Lithotripsy Technique in Children

  • General anaesthesia usually (sedation in some older children); no bowel preparation; retrograde opacification occasionally for radiolucent stones.
  • Energy matched to size: low power (<20 kV) → less migration, better fragmentation; ≤2500–3000 shocks per session (<2000 in very young children).
  • Shielding and positioning prevent haematoma and lung contusion; foam tape shields the lungs without blocking energy to the stone.
  • Ungated shocks are safe in children — adult arrhythmias are unlikely.
  • Rate: in adults, higher rate and energy lower clearance; in children 60 and 90/min were similar, but 80/min cleared better than 120/min → slower rates favoured.

Key points: conservative management and shockwave lithotripsy

  • Smaller and more distal stones pass more often.
  • α-Blockers may increase passage of ureteric stones in children.
  • SWL and URS clear similarly for stones <15 mm, varying with location, density and anatomy.

Concept — SWL's paediatric advantages (short skin-to-stone distance, fewer anaesthetics, fewer ED returns) are real, but its effectiveness is dictated by stone physics — size, density and lower-pole drainage — so it matches URS only within an envelope of small, soft, favourably placed stones.

Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy

  • Early reluctance — large instruments, parenchymal damage, radiation, sepsis, bleeding — gave way to experience: PCNL is first-line for renal stones >20 mm, clearing about 90%.
  • Bleeding rises with tract size; a 24–30 Fr tract in a child ≈ a 72 Fr tract in an adult. Miniaturisation → less bleeding with stone-free rates ≥75%.

Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy Planning for Pediatric Patients

  • Experience is paramount. CT first → amenability, optimal calyx, size, location, adjacent organs, spinal deformity (scoliosis, lordosis, kyphosis).
  • Consent: transfusion; delayed haemorrhage needing angioembolisation; sepsis; pneumothorax, haemothorax, urinothorax; incomplete clearance; adjacent-organ injury.
  • Treat UTI first: culture with sensitivities 2–3 weeks before; positive → full course. Bladder urine may not reflect pelvic urine or stone culture. In adults, 1 week of empirical ciprofloxacin may reduce infection. Intravenous ampicillin + gentamicin at operation.

Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy Technique for Pediatric Patients

Sequence: general anaesthesia with hypothermia prevention (warm theatre, warmed isotonic irrigant, short operation, draping, temperature monitoring) → cystoscopy + 4–5 Fr open-ended ureteric catheter to just below the pelviureteric junction (PUJ) → prone on gel bolsters (respect contractures; latex precautions in myelomeningocele; pad) → US-guided access (or fluoroscopy) → 16- or 18-gauge needle → No. 11 blade → 8 and 10 Fr coaxial dilators → safety wire, preferably down the ureter → 18 Fr sheath (24 Fr for staghorn) → RPG → 15/18 Fr rigid nephroscope with dual ultrasonic–mechanical lithotripter or laser → flexible scope to confirm clearance and reach mid-pole calyces → nephrostomy (7 Fr pigtail in infants → 16 Fr in adolescents) → removal after urine clears and a capping trial → discharge usually day 1; nephrostogram if the child cannot report pain.

  • Lower-pole access: the posterior calyx is the one immediately above the most inferior calyx on anteroposterior fluoroscopy (>90%). Upper-pole access: below the 11th rib, medial calyx → less pneumothorax.
  • US target: the needle appears as a linear echogenic focus; aim for the papillary tip adjacent to the stone. Mid-pole mini-PCNL access is feasible for a pelvic stone even in an infant.
  • Paediatric kidneys (especially infants') are mobile and push away → watch the balloon tip and bony landmarks; trim the sheath in small children for manoeuvrability and less torque.
  • Tubeless PCNL: insufficient evidence in children. "Sandwich therapy" (PCNL + SWL for residuals) is historical — limited by PCNL's clearance, miniaturisation and SWL hypertension concerns.

Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy Complications

  • About 10% intraoperative, 20% postoperative; mostly minor, but up to 16% ≥Clavien III; transfusion <10%.
  • Intraoperative: failure to dilate, acute haemorrhage, loss of access, pelvic perforation. Postoperative: UTI/sepsis, fragment obstruction, delayed haemorrhage from pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous malformation.
  • Determinants: operative time, sheath size, mid-calyceal puncture, partial staghorn.
  • Bleeding → abort; Foley or re-entry catheter through the tract. Pelvic injury → stop; antegrade stent. Hydrothorax, colonic injury and late bleeding are managed as in adults.
  • PCNL only where paediatric interventional radiology can embolise.

Key points: percutaneous nephrolithotomy

  • Indicated for large upper tract burdens (>20 mm).
  • US and/or fluoroscopy for access.
  • Clearance about 90%; risks of bleeding, sepsis, pneumothorax, adjacent-organ injury.

Concept — PCNL's place in children rests on one trade: the highest single-session clearance for large burdens, paid for in tract-related bleeding — so every refinement (miniaturisation, US access, trimmed sheaths, interventional radiology back-up) exists to shrink the tract's relative size and its haemorrhagic cost.

Laparoscopic and Robotic-Assisted Pyelolithotomy

  • Not indicated for upper tract stones except with an anatomic abnormality — e.g. pyelolithotomy during pyeloplasty for PUJ obstruction.

Secondary Prevention

Fluid

  • The cornerstone: dilutes calcium, oxalate, uric acid and cystine. Most children drink too little; adolescents' main barriers are unawareness of volume drunk and poor response to the need to drink.
  • Targets: ≥ maintenance (children); >2–2.5 L/day (adolescents, adults); 1.5–2 L/m² in cystinuria or PH; more with insensible losses.
  • Prevention of Urinary Stones with Hydration (PUSH) trial: randomised test of financial incentives and coaching to sustain intake.
  • Other fluids: orange juice, lemonade, blackcurrant juice (↑ pH and citrate) and coffee (↑ volume) protect; grapefruit juice harms; soft drinks controversial. Counsel water (sugar, calories) — amount matters more than type.

Concept — Almost every preventive measure pulls one of three levers — urine volume, promoter excretion, or inhibitor and pH — which is why calcium is not restricted (it removes oxalate in the gut), why sodium is (it drives calciuria), and why alkali must stop short of the calcium phosphate threshold.
