## Instalment 1 — Epidemiology of Early-Onset Stone Disease, Stone Composition and Randall's Plaques, Determinants (Genetics, Exposome, Antibiotics and Microbiome, High-Risk Groups), Stone Disease as a Disorder of Mineral Metabolism, Evaluation of Suspected Nephrolithiasis, Imaging (Figs 54.1–54.3)

### EPIDEMIOLOGY OF EARLY-ONSET KIDNEY STONE DISEASE

- **Nephrolithiasis is a disorder of mineral metabolism** punctuated by acute symptomatic events; **about 50% recur within 5 years** of the index presentation.
- **United States (US) prevalence has doubled**: 5% (1988–1994) → **11% (2015–2018)**.
- **Incidence in youth is rising 5–10% per year**, disproportionately to adults; **the greatest rise is in adolescents, particularly females**.
  - Females aged 15–19 years: annual incidence **75 → 120 per 100,000** (1997 → 2012), **52% higher than in males** of the same age.
- **Ethnicity**: commonest in non-Hispanic white children → Hispanic white → African American.
- **Definition used in the chapter**: early-onset stone disease = onset at **<24 years** of age.
- **Health-care burden**: rising hospitalisations, emergency department (ED) visits, operations and expenditure — **at least US$375 million per year** (2010) in hospital and ED care alone, before outpatient visits, drugs, imaging and caregivers' lost earnings. **Recurrence turns a single event into a chronic condition** of pain and morbidity.

**Sex ratio reverses with age of onset**

| | Adult-onset | Early-onset |
|---|---|---|
| Sex ratio | **Historically male 3 : 1**; gap now narrowing | **Female predominance**, greatest in adolescence |
| Explanation | — | **Unknown** |

**Stone composition — adults versus children**

| Predominant composition | Adults | Children |
|---|---|---|
| Calcium oxalate (CaOx) | 75–80% | **Similar (about 80%)** |
| Calcium phosphate (CaP) | about 5% | **Slightly more common** |
| Struvite | 10–20% | Not separately stated |
| Pure uric acid | about 5% | **Less common** |

- **Why the shift: children's urine is more alkaline** — alkaline urine favours CaP and disfavours uric acid, which precipitates in acid urine.
- **The belief that childhood stones are mostly genetic, inborn-error or infective is no longer true**; most are idiopathic CaOx, as in adults.
- **Randall's plaques** are **hydroxyapatite (calcium phosphate) deposits on the renal papillae** and are **the platform on which most idiopathic CaOx stones grow**. Children with CaOx stones have **the same papillary changes as adults**.

**Figure legend — Fig. 54.1** Randall's plaques in a 16-year-old with CaOx stones: (A) white deposits on the renal papillae; (B) the sites to which idiopathic CaOx stones attach.

#### Determinants

- **Three-way intersection**: **genetic susceptibility × exposome** (non-genetic environmental exposures) **× comorbid disease** — rare monogenic disease excepted.
- **Heritability is about half, and higher in males than females** (twin studies). It is **not explained by rare monogenic disease** (primary hyperoxaluria, cystinuria); **the polygenic determinants are still undefined**, although polymorphisms raise risk both in healthy people and in those with diabetes, obesity and gout.
- **Incomplete genetic determination implies gene–exposome interaction.** The exposome comprises diet, medications and ambient environment.
  - **Diet: low fluid intake and high sodium intake** are the two key Western-diet risk factors.
- **Medications act by two routes** (pair):
  - **Direct** — the drug itself crystallises because of poor urinary solubility: **1–2% of stones**.
  - **Indirect — antibiotics**: **oral cephalosporins, broad-spectrum penicillins, nitrofurantoin, fluoroquinolones and sulfonamides** raise the odds of nephrolithiasis (population-based case–control study, United Kingdom). **Risk wanes but persists for up to 5 years and is greatest in children**; the same association is seen in adult women.
- **Proposed mechanism — microbiome disruption**: children with CaOx stones have **fewer oxalate-degrading and butyrate-producing bacterial taxa** (case–control study).
  - Butyrate is anti-inflammatory, protects against renal ischaemic injury, maintains the gut mucosal barrier and **regulates intestinal SLC26 oxalate transporters**.
  - **Loss of both bacterial functions acts synergistically**: ↑ intestinal oxalate absorption → ↑ urinary oxalate → stones.

**High-risk groups**

| Group | Mechanism of stone formation |
|---|---|
| **Bowel-segment reconstruction** (augmentation cystoplasty) — spina bifida: **about 1% per year after augmentation, ≥10 × the general population** | **Metabolic acidosis; bowel mucus as nidus; bacterial colonisation; hyperoxaluria** from malabsorption after bowel resection |
| Malabsorption disorders | Enteric hyperoxaluria |
| Urinary stasis (urological anomalies, immobility) | Stasis |
| **Enteral tube feeds** | Feeds **high in sodium and oxalate, low in calcium** → ↑ renal calcium excretion and ↑ gut oxalate absorption; **hyperoxaluria commoner than in ambulatory stone formers** |
| **Non-ambulatory children** | ↑ bone resorption, ↓ osteoblastic activity → **hypercalciuria**; **more often need surgery**; **more infection stones (calcium carbonate, struvite)** |

- **Complex children carry several interlocking risk factors at once.**

#### A Disorder of Mineral Metabolism

- **Recurrence is high**: about **50% within 3–5 years** of the index stone — **equal to or higher than in adults**; **adolescents have more symptomatic events than younger children**.
- **Extrarenal associations** (mostly adult data; see table).

| Outcome | Association with nephrolithiasis | Proposed mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronic kidney disease (CKD)** | ↑ risk; **end-stage renal disease (ESRD) roughly doubled** after ≥1 episode; **risk rises with number of episodes**; also seen in United Kingdom population data including children | — |
| **Hypertension** | ↑ risk, of similar size to adult estimates from the **Health Professionals Follow-Up Study** and **Nurses' Health Study I and II** | **Altered renal calcium and sodium handling** |
| **Coronary heart disease** | ↑ risk in women, **relatively greater in younger women** | — |
| **Fracture / low bone mineral density (BMD)** | ↑ fracture risk, **greatest at 10–19 years** | **Hypercalciuria and negative calcium balance** |

- **Childhood is the critical window for bone-mass accrual** → identify children at risk of low BMD early; **citrate and thiazides may reduce adult osteoporosis risk**.
- **Children may be at particular risk**: early onset may mark **a more severe phenotype**, and children have **a longer lifetime** over which comorbidity can develop.

> **KEY POINTS: EPIDEMIOLOGY OF KIDNEY STONE DISEASE**
> - **Dramatic rise in paediatric incidence**, particularly in adolescent females.
> - **Female predominance** in early-onset disease.
> - **About 80% of paediatric stones are predominantly CaOx**, as in adults; **CaP slightly commoner and pure uric acid less common** in children.
> - **Genetic susceptibility, the exposome and comorbid disease** are the primary determinants.
> - Stone disease is **a disorder of mineral metabolism**, associated with **cardiovascular disease, CKD and decreased BMD**.

*__Concept —__ Early-onset stone disease is not a paediatric curiosity but the adult disease started early: the same idiopathic CaOx-on-Randall's-plaque pathway, driven by gene–exposome interaction, with a longer runway for recurrence, CKD, hypertension and bone loss.*

### EVALUATION

#### Child with Suspected Nephrolithiasis

- **Older children present as adults do**: flank pain, nausea and vomiting, lower urinary tract symptoms.
- History: onset, frequency and duration of pain; personal and family stone history; urological surgery affecting risk or management; factors raising pre-test probability. **Always ascertain fever.**
- **Young or non-verbal children cannot localise pain** → rely heavily on imaging and laboratory tests.

#### Imaging

| Guideline body | Initial study | Second-line |
|---|---|---|
| European Association of Urology (EAU) | **Ultrasound (US)** | **Non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT)** if US non-diagnostic and suspicion remains high |
| American Urological Association (AUA) technical paper | **US** | Same |

- **Image Gently Alliance (2007)** — campaign to lower paediatric radiation exposure; **US-first clinical pathways reduce initial CT use**.

**Figure legend — Fig. 54.2 Diagnostic imaging algorithm for suspected kidney or ureteric stones**
Child with suspected stone → **US (grey-scale + colour Doppler)** →
- Positive → treat as clinically indicated.
- Negative + **high clinical suspicion ± secondary signs on US (e.g. hydronephrosis)** → **low-dose NCCT**.
- Negative + low clinical suspicion → stop.

**US criteria for a stone** (both required): (1) **hyperechoic focus** in a papilla, calyx or renal pelvis → (2) **confirmatory twinkle artefact** on colour Doppler.
- **Maximise the Doppler pulse repetition frequency (PRF)** → suppresses noise from normal vascular flow → more accurate stone detection.
- **Posterior acoustic shadow**: often present with larger stones and **greatly increases specificity**, but **modern harmonic and spatial-compounding imaging rarely produces a shadow for stones <4 mm**.
- **Missed stones are usually clinically insignificant** (small or non-obstructing). Renal resistive indices have been studied in adults as an adjunct.
- **US resolution exceeds that of many CT scans** → twinkling foci of **about 2 mm** too small for CT are often **Randall's plaques with or without small attached stones** (CT shows only hyperdense papillae). This fits the finding that **US overestimates stone size by 1–2 mm**.

**US versus NCCT**

| | Ultrasound | Non-contrast CT |
|---|---|---|
| Sensitivity | **>70%** | **About 100%** (gold standard) |
| Specificity | **>95%** | **About 100%** |
| Radiation | None | Ionising |
| Ureteric stones | **Poorly visualised** | Confirms or excludes |
| Foci of about 2 mm | Detected (often plaques) | Not seen as distinct calculi |
| Size estimate | **Overestimates by 1–2 mm** | Reference |
| Role | **First-line** | **Second-line**: US non-diagnostic + high suspicion |

- **CT radiation**: attributable cancer risk from one scan is small but **cumulative with repeat scans**; a substantial minority of children have ≥2 CTs per stone episode. Children carry **greater risk: longer life expectancy and more radiosensitive developing tissue**.
- **When to do CT**: high suspicion + non-diagnostic US (e.g. no stone seen but hydronephrosis) → **low-dose NCCT of abdomen and pelvis** → confirms or excludes a ureteric stone and gives size and location → decides observation versus surgery → **prevents silent obstruction and loss of renal function**.
- **Dose reduction**: **halving tube current to 40 mA in children ≤50 kg** does not impair diagnosis; much lower amperage still detects **2–8 mm** calculi at about one-third of the standard dose.

**Figure legend — Fig. 54.3** Ureteric stone on US: echogenic focus on grey-scale with confirmatory twinkle artefact on colour Doppler; **maximise PRF**; **stones <4 mm typically lack a posterior acoustic shadow** on modern equipment.

*__Concept —__ Paediatric stone imaging is governed by radiation economy: **US is accepted despite imperfect sensitivity because what it misses is usually clinically insignificant**, and CT is reserved for the one situation — a suspected obstructing ureteric stone not seen on US — where a miss would cost renal function.*

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 1

- **Key figures**: recurrence **about 50% within 5 years** (3–5 years in children); US prevalence **5% → 11%**; youth incidence **+5–10% per year**; females 15–19 years **75 → 120 per 100,000**, **52% above males**; adult male : female **3 : 1** historically; early-onset = **<24 years**; cost **≥US$375 million per year** (2010).
- **Composition**: adults CaOx **75–80%**, CaP **5%**, struvite **10–20%**, uric acid **5%**; children **about 80% CaOx**, **more CaP, less uric acid**.
- **Randall's plaque = hydroxyapatite** (calcium phosphate), not CaOx.
- **Drug-crystal stones 1–2%**; antibiotic risk persists **up to 5 years**, **greatest in children**; five classes — cephalosporins, broad-spectrum penicillins, nitrofurantoin, fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides.
- **Spina bifida after augmentation: about 1% per year, ≥10 × baseline.**
- **Fracture risk greatest at 10–19 years.**
- **Gold standard imaging: NCCT** (about 100% sensitivity and specificity). **First-line: US** (**>70% sensitive, >95% specific**).
- **No shadow for stones <4 mm** on modern US; **US overestimates size by 1–2 mm**; twinkling foci **about 2 mm** may be plaques.
- **Low-dose CT: 40 mA for ≤50 kg.**
- **Dates**: **Image Gently Alliance 2007** — paediatric radiation-reduction campaign.
- **Named cohorts**: Health Professionals Follow-Up Study; Nurses' Health Study I and II — adult stone–hypertension association.
- **Classic traps**:
  - Sex ratio: adults male-predominant, **early-onset female-predominant**.
  - "Childhood stones are mostly genetic or infective" — **false; mostly idiopathic CaOx**.
  - **Uric acid stones are less, not more, common in children** despite higher urate excretion (alkaline urine).
  - **Absence of an acoustic shadow does not exclude a stone <4 mm** — rely on twinkle.
  - **PRF is maximised, not reduced**, to avoid vascular twinkle mimicking stones.
  - A 2 mm twinkling focus on US that is invisible on CT is often **a Randall's plaque**, not a missed stone.

## Instalment 2 — Confirmed Nephrolithiasis: History, Examination and Metabolic Evaluation; Urinary Metabolic Abnormalities — Hypercalciuria, Hyperoxaluria (Primary and Secondary), Hypocitraturia, Cystinuria, Hyperuricosuria; Tables 54.1 and 54.2

### EVALUATION (continued)

#### Child with Confirmed Nephrolithiasis

##### Medical History and Physical Examination

- **Family history of stones, haematuria and CKD** → flags inherited disease: **cystinuria, primary hyperoxaluria, CYP24A1 mutations, Dent disease**.
- **Focused dietary history in every patient**: fluid and salt intake; vitamins C and D; mineral supplements; special diets (e.g. **ketogenic diet**).
- **Medication history**: corticosteroids; diuretics (**furosemide, acetazolamide**); protease inhibitors (**indinavir**); **antibiotics**; antiepileptics (**topiramate, zonisamide**).
- **At-risk history**: prematurity, urinary tract anomalies, urinary tract infection (UTI), intestinal malabsorption, prolonged immobility.

**Examination sign → condition to suspect**

| Sign | Conditions |
|---|---|
| Dysmorphic features | **Williams syndrome** |
| Rickets | **Dent disease**; hereditary hypophosphataemic rickets with hypercalciuria (HHRH) |
| Tetany | Familial hypomagnesaemia with hypercalciuria and nephrocalcinosis (FHHNC); autosomal dominant hypocalcaemic hypercalciuria (ADHH) |
| Gout | Hypoxanthine–guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT) deficiency; phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate synthetase (PRPS) superactivity |

##### Metabolic Evaluation

- **An identifiable metabolic abnormality raises recurrence risk up to fivefold** → **every child should have a comprehensive metabolic evaluation.**
- **Start with stone analysis** — **infrared spectroscopy or X-ray diffraction** of a passed stone. **Diagnostic compositions: cystine, struvite, 2,8-dihydroxyadenine (2,8-DHA), xanthine.**
- **Rationale for evaluating early-onset disease**: **higher probability of autosomal recessive (monogenic) causes**; a possibly **more severe phenotype**; and **a longer time for recurrence and end-organ effects** (e.g. reduced BMD). Serum and urine studies are often done together.

**Serum and screening studies**

| Test | Purpose / when |
|---|---|
| **Creatinine** | **Essential** — acute kidney injury or CKD |
| Calcium, phosphorus, bicarbonate, magnesium, uric acid | Hyper/hypocalcaemia-associated stones; hyper/hypouricaemia; **normocalcaemic hypercalciuria disorders** — HHRH, Bartter syndrome, distal renal tubular acidosis (dRTA), FHHNC |
| **Intact parathyroid hormone (PTH)** | **Not routine — primary hyperparathyroidism is rare in children**; only if **hypercalcaemia + hypophosphataemia** |
| 25-hydroxyvitamin D | Hypercalcaemic and/or hyperphosphataemic children with a relevant exposure |
| **25-hydroxyvitamin D : 24,25-dihydroxyvitamin D ratio** (normal <25–30) | **CYP24A1 screen** — hypercalcaemia + hypercalciuria once other causes excluded |
| Spot urine **β2-microglobulin or retinol-binding protein** (low-molecular-weight protein) | **Dent disease screen** — males with recurrent calcium stones plus proteinuria, or family history of renal failure, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis or recurrent stones |

- **CYP24A1 biallelic loss of function** → loss of peripheral vitamin D catabolism → **persistently high 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D** → **mimics vitamin D intoxication**.

**24-hour urine** — sequence: collect → analyse **calcium, oxalate, uric acid, sodium, citrate, creatinine, volume, pH and cystine** (cyanide–nitroprusside screen) → **index to weight, body surface area (BSA) or creatinine** → check adequacy by **creatinine excretion** (adult normal 15–20 mg/kg/day) → compute supersaturations (CaOx, CaP, uric acid).
- **One versus two collections: debated** — one may suffice; two yield more specific diagnoses.
- **Not toilet-trained → spot urine solute : creatinine ratios** (calcium, uric acid, citrate, oxalate; Table 54.1) — less precise.
- **Repeat testing weeks to months after a dietary change or a new drug.**
- **Microscopy for crystalluria is generally non-diagnostic, except three pathognomonic crystals**:

| Crystal | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| **Hexagonal** | **Cystine** |
| **Coffin-lid** (triple phosphate) | **Struvite** |
| **Brown, spiculated** | **2,8-DHA** |

### URINARY METABOLIC ABNORMALITIES

#### Hypercalciuria

- **The commonest abnormality: 30–50% of stone-forming children.**
- **Commonest cause in children and adults: idiopathic hypercalciuria** — hypercalciuria **without hypercalcaemia** and with no other identifiable cause.
- Familial idiopathic hypercalciuria: gene(s) unidentified; **autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance**.
- **About 4% of healthy children** have idiopathic hypercalciuria; **40–50% of them have a family history of stones**.
- **Definition**: **>4 mg/kg/day in children >2 years**, or total **>200–250 mg/day**.
- **Not toilet-trained → random calcium : creatinine ratio**; **school-age normal ≤0.21 mg/mg**; physiological excretion is higher in younger children.
- **Diagnostic sequence**: confirm hypercalciuria → **check serum calcium** → classify and exclude secondary causes → only then diagnose idiopathic hypercalciuria.

**Hypercalciuria classified by serum calcium**

| Serum calcium | Causes to exclude |
|---|---|
| **Hypercalcaemic** | **Hyperparathyroidism; biallelic CYP24A1 mutations; hypervitaminosis D**; when indicated — prolonged immobilisation, sarcoidosis, malignancy, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, corticosteroid excess, adrenal insufficiency, Williams syndrome |
| **Hypocalcaemic** | **Hypoparathyroidism; ADHH** (gain-of-function calcium-sensing receptor mutation) |
| **Normocalcaemic** (most → idiopathic) | Prematurity; diuretics (furosemide, acetazolamide); topiramate; ketogenic diet; Dent disease; Bartter syndrome; FHHNC; dRTA; HHRH; possibly medullary sponge kidney |

*__Concept —__ **Hypercalciuria is a sign, not a diagnosis**: serum calcium sorts it into three bins, and idiopathic hypercalciuria is named only once the normocalcaemic bin has been cleared of its secondary causes.*

#### Hyperoxaluria

- Oxalate is **an end product of glyoxylate and ascorbic acid metabolism**, excreted mainly by the kidney.
- **80–85% of urinary oxalate is endogenous; 10–15% dietary.**
- Normal excretion generally **<40 mg/day/1.73 m²**; young children → random oxalate : creatinine ratio (Table 54.1).
- **Primary versus secondary** (pair): **primary = inherited hepatic overproduction** (rare) versus **secondary = increased absorption or excess precursor intake** (commoner).

##### Primary Hyperoxaluria (PH)

- **PH types 1, 2 and 3: rare autosomal recessive disorders of endogenous (hepatic) oxalate overproduction** → hyperoxaluria → **nephrocalcinosis and nephrolithiasis** → CaOx deposition → **progressive CKD**.
- **Phenotype ranges from neonatal ESRD to occasional stone passage into adulthood** → **diagnosis often delayed, sometimes made only after loss of a transplanted kidney**.

**Primary hyperoxaluria types compared**

| | PH1 | PH2 | PH3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gene | **AGXT** | **GRHPR** | **HOGA1** |
| Defective enzyme | **Alanine–glyoxylate aminotransferase (AGT)** — hepatic, **peroxisomal**; **pyridoxine (vitamin B6) is a cofactor** | Glyoxylate reductase–hydroxypyruvate reductase | **Mitochondrial** 4-hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate aldolase (4th step of hydroxyproline pathway) |
| Urinary marker (with ↑ oxalate) | **↑ Glycolate** (and glyoxylate) | **↑ L-glycerate** | **↑ 4-hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate** |
| Severity | **Most severe** | **Less severe than PH1, but not benign** — can reach ESRD | Not stated |
| Specific note | **Profound B6 deficiency can mimic PH1** | — | — |

- **Diagnostic sequence**: raised 24-h urine oxalate → **urinary glycolate, glycerate and 4-hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate** → **genetic testing** (increasingly available).
- **Raised oxalate with all three markers normal → highly suggestive of secondary hyperoxaluria.**

##### Secondary Hyperoxaluria

- Two routes: **excess intake of oxalate or its precursors** (including **high-dose vitamin C** — oxalate is a by-product of ascorbic acid metabolism) versus **enteric hyperabsorption**.
- **Gut oxalate absorption varies inversely with dietary calcium** → **calcium-deficient diets increase hyperoxaluria**.
- **Enteric causes (fat malabsorption / chronic diarrhoea)**: inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (cystic fibrosis), biliary tract disease, small-bowel resection / short-bowel syndrome.
- **Mechanism**: free fatty acids → **bind luminal calcium** → more **unbound oxalate** → absorbed → hyperoxaluria.

#### Hypocitraturia

- Citrate is **reabsorbed and metabolised in the proximal tubule**.
- **Definition (24 h)**: citrate : creatinine **<128 mg/g in males, <300 mg/g in females** (Table 54.1 gives male normal >180 mg/g — see comparison above).
- **Mechanism**: **proximal tubular intracellular acidosis** (from **metabolic acidosis or hypokalaemia**) → ↑ citrate reabsorption → hypocitraturia.
- **Associated causes**: ketogenic diet; **topiramate, acetazolamide**; **dRTA**; chronic diarrhoea.
- **Incomplete dRTA occurs without overt acidosis or hypokalaemia** → easily missed unless provocative **acid-load testing** is available.
- **Most hypocitraturia is idiopathic**; a diet **high in animal protein and low in vegetable fibre and potassium** lowers citrate excretion.

#### Cystinuria

- **Autosomal recessive; SLC3A1 or SLC7A9** → defective proximal tubular dibasic amino-acid transport.
- Failure to reabsorb **cystine, lysine, ornithine and arginine — only cystine crystallises** under physiological conditions.
- Excretion: **normal <60 mg/day/1.73 m²; homozygotes often >400 mg/day/1.73 m²**.
- **Presentation**: typically renal colic in the **second or third decade**, but **can present in infancy with staghorn calculi** and occasionally bladder stones.
- **Poor cystine solubility** → recurrent stones → **long-term risk of renal failure** if untreated; associated UTI; **mixed cystine/struvite** stones occur.

#### Hyperuricosuria

- **Children excrete more uric acid than adults**: fractional excretion (FE) **30–50% in neonates → adult 8–12% by adolescence**.
- **Paradox: despite this, uric acid stones are <5% of paediatric calculi.**
- **Epitaxy**: the crystal lattice of one mineral (uric acid) provides a base on which a second (CaOx) grows in the same orientation → **hyperuricosuric CaOx stones**.
- **Definition: >815 mg/day/1.73 m² BSA.** Relative excretion per GFR is **constant after 2 years**.
- **Not toilet-trained, >2 years**: hyperuricosuria = **>0.56 mg/dL GFR** on spot urine, where **urine uric acid (mg/dL) × plasma creatinine (mg/dL) ÷ urine creatinine (mg/dL)**.
- **Greatest risk factor for uric acid stones: hyperuricosuria + low urine pH.**

**Hyperuricosuria by serum urate**

| Serum urate | Causes |
|---|---|
| **High (hyperuricaemia)** | **Inherited purine disorders — HPRT deficiency (Lesch–Nyhan syndrome), PRPS superactivity**; lymphoproliferative disorders; polycythaemia |
| **Low (hypouricaemia)** | **Hereditary renal hypouricaemia** — **SLC22A12 or SLC2A9** (proximal urate transporters): hyperuricosuria, stones, **exercise-induced acute renal failure** |
| Not specified | Excess purine intake (animal protein, anchovies, mussels); haemolysis; **uricosuric drugs (probenecid, salicylates, losartan)**; cyanotic congenital heart disease; melamine toxicity; idiopathic (familial) |

| | **Distal RTA** | AD, AR | SLC4A1; ATP6V1B1; ATP6V0A4 | **Hypocitraturia, metabolic acidosis**, hypokalaemia, nephrocalcinosis |

*AD, autosomal dominant; AR, autosomal recessive.*

- **What the table is for**: recognising which child's stones are monogenic — the rationale for evaluating every early-onset stone former.
- **Pattern**: **autosomal recessive dominates**; the **X-linked trio (Dent, HPRT, PRPS) presents in boys**; **every normocalcaemic-hypercalciuria disorder causes nephrocalcinosis**.
- **Traps**: **Bartter = metabolic alkalosis; dRTA = metabolic acidosis** — both hypokalaemic and hypercalciuric; **Dent disease is detected by low-molecular-weight proteinuria**, not by serum calcium.

> **KEY POINTS: EVALUATION OF CHILDREN WITH KIDNEY STONE DISEASE**
> - **An identifiable metabolic abnormality increases recurrence risk.**
> - **Every child needs a comprehensive initial evaluation**, including a 24-hour urine for calcium, oxalate, uric acid, sodium, citrate, creatinine, volume, pH and cystine.
> - **Idiopathic hypercalciuria is found in about 30–50%** of stone-forming children.
> - **Hypocitraturia, hyperoxaluria and cystinuria** remain important causes and must be identified.

*__Concept —__ Every urinary risk factor is either **an excess of a promoter** (calcium, oxalate, urate, cystine) or **a deficit of an inhibitor** (citrate), and each has the same two-tier structure: a rare monogenic disorder at one end and a common idiopathic or dietary cause at the other — which is why the work-up always runs stone analysis → serum → indexed urine → specific markers or genetics.*

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 2

- **Metabolic abnormality → up to fivefold recurrence** → evaluate every child.
- **Stone analysis first**: infrared spectroscopy or X-ray diffraction; **diagnostic: cystine, struvite, 2,8-DHA, xanthine**.
- **24-h urine panel**: Ca, oxalate, urate, Na, citrate, creatinine, volume, pH, cystine; adequacy = creatinine **15–20 mg/kg/day** (adult).
- **Hypercalciuria**: **>4 mg/kg/day (>2 years)** or **>200–250 mg/day**; spot Ca : Cr **≤0.21 mg/mg** normal (school age); infants **<0.8** (0–6 mo), **<0.6** (7–12 mo).
- **Hypercalciuria in 30–50% of stone formers**; idiopathic hypercalciuria in **about 4% of healthy children**, **40–50% with family history**; **autosomal dominant, incomplete penetrance**.
- **Oxalate**: **80–85% endogenous**; normal **<40 mg/day/1.73 m²** (table <50).
- **PH markers**: PH1 **glycolate**; PH2 **glycerate**; PH3 **4-hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate**; all normal → **secondary**.
- **Hypocitraturia**: **<128 mg/g male, <300 mg/g female** (table: male normal >180).
- **Cystine**: normal **<60 mg/day/1.73 m²**; homozygote **>400**.
- **Uric acid**: FE **30–50% neonate → 8–12% adolescent**; stones **<5%**; hyperuricosuria **>815 mg/day/1.73 m²** or **>0.56 mg/dL GFR** (spot, >2 years).
- **Commonest**: hypercalciuria; commonest cause **idiopathic hypercalciuria**.
- **Gold standard stone analysis**: infrared spectroscopy / X-ray diffraction.
- **Eponyms**:
  - **Randall's plaque** — papillary hydroxyapatite platform.
  - **Dent disease** — X-linked CLCN5, **low-molecular-weight proteinuria**.
  - **Bartter syndrome** — salt wasting with **metabolic alkalosis**.
  - **Lesch–Nyhan syndrome** — **HPRT deficiency**, X-linked, developmental delay + hyperuricaemia.
  - **Williams syndrome** — **dysmorphism with hypercalcaemic hypercalciuria**.
- **Pathognomonic crystals**: hexagonal = cystine; coffin-lid = struvite; brown spiculated = 2,8-DHA.
- **Classic traps**:
  - **PTH is not routine in children** — only with hypercalcaemia + hypophosphataemia.
  - **CYP24A1 mimics vitamin D intoxication** (high 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D).
  - **Incomplete dRTA has no systemic acidosis** — suspect it in unexplained hypocitraturia.
  - **Low dietary calcium raises urinary oxalate.**
  - **B6 deficiency mimics PH1.**
  - **Cystinuria can present in infancy** (staghorn), not only in the second or third decade.
  - **Hereditary renal hypouricaemia**: **low** serum urate with **high** urinary urate and exercise-induced renal failure.

## Instalment 3 — Medical Expulsive Therapy, Surgical Management and the 2016 AUA/Endourological Society Paediatric Guidelines (Table 54.3), Surgical Antimicrobial Prophylaxis, Goals of Therapy (Stone Clearance, Radiation, Ancillary Procedures and Anaesthesia), Ureteroscopy: Outcomes, Pre-Stenting, Dusting versus Fragmentation, Access Sheaths, Equipment, Technique, Complications (Figs 54.4–54.5)

### MANAGEMENT OF CHILDREN AND ADOLESCENTS WITH KIDNEY AND URETERAL STONES

#### Medical Expulsive Therapy

- **Options for a ureteric stone**: observation with analgesia → observation plus medical expulsive therapy (MET) → surgery.
- **MET = α-blockers** (less often calcium-channel blockers) to aid ureteric stone passage.
- **Mechanism**: antagonism of **α1A and α1D receptors**, concentrated in **distal-third ureteric smooth muscle and at the vesicoureteric junction** → ureteric dilatation.
- **Spontaneous passage without MET is higher in adults, for smaller stones (especially <5 mm) and for distal stones.**

| | Adults | Children |
|---|---|---|
| Stone size with greatest α-blocker benefit | **Distal stones >5 mm** | **Unknown** |

- **AUA / Endourological Society (EUS) 2016**: children with **uncomplicated ureteric stones ≤10 mm → offer observation ± MET with α-blockers** (moderate recommendation; grade B evidence).
- **Since 2016**: a meta-analysis of non-blinded randomised controlled trials (RCTs) showed that **α-blockers increase stone passage in children**.
- **Counselling**: **off-label** use; **maximum duration unknown but should not exceed 6 weeks**; **tamsulosin appears effective and safe** in children.

#### Surgical Management

- **Up to 60% of children with renal or ureteric stones need surgery.**
- **Evidence gap**: of ten major kidney diseases, **nephrolithiasis has the fewest RCTs**.
- **Options, as for adults: ureteroscopy (URS), shockwave lithotripsy (SWL), percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL)** — **all need anaesthesia and, traditionally, radiation**.
- **Six characteristics of the ideal paediatric modality** (proposed framework):
  1. High monotherapeutic success.
  2. Low complication risk.
  3. Rapid return to baseline activity.
  4. Minimal radiation.
  5. Minimal anaesthesia.
  6. No ancillary procedures.
- **Patient-centred selection**: patient and stone factors; surgeon experience and resources; and — **most importantly — patient-driven goals of care**.
- **AUA/EUS 2016 guideline**: **only 8 recommendations address children, and half rest on expert opinion alone.**
- **2017 National Urology Research Agenda** prioritised paediatric stone research. The **Pediatric KIDney Stone (PKIDS) Care Improvement Network** is running an observational trial comparing clinical and patient-reported outcomes of URS, SWL and PCNL.

**TABLE 54.3 American Urological Association and Endourological Society 2016 Guidelines for the Surgical Management of Stones in Pediatric Patients** (rebuilt, grouped by clinical scenario; Assimos et al., 2016)

| Scenario | Recommendation | Strength | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ureteric stone, uncomplicated, ≤10 mm** | **Observation ± α-blocker MET** | Moderate | B |
| Ureteric stone unlikely to pass, or failed observation/MET | **URS or SWL**, by anatomy and body habitus | **Strong** | B |
| Ureteric stone before URS | **No routine pre-stenting** | Expert opinion | — |

- **What the table is for**: the whole decision tree for paediatric stone surgery in eight lines.
- **Pattern**: **two size cut-offs — 10 mm (ureteric, observe) and 20 mm (renal, SWL/URS versus PCNL/SWL)**; **SWL appears in every active renal row**; only two recommendations are "strong".
- **Traps**: **strength ≠ evidence** — "CT before PCNL" is **strong** despite **low (C)** evidence; mixing up the **10 mm ureteric** and **20 mm renal** thresholds; the 10 mm threshold is **≤**, not <.

#### Surgical Antimicrobial Prophylaxis

- **Indicated for all URS and PCNL; for SWL only when infection risk is increased.** No paediatric-specific recommendations exist.
- **Urine culture before every upper tract procedure** → confirms sterility and guides preoperative antibiotics, particularly for **percutaneous procedures, high-grade obstruction and indwelling stents**.

| Scenario (authors' practice) | Prophylaxis |
|---|---|
| Negative culture, uncomplicated URS | **Perioperative cefazolin** |
| Any percutaneous procedure, or pre-existing ureteric stent or nephrostomy | **Fluoroquinolone or ampicillin + gentamicin** |

- **Stop within 24 hours postoperatively** unless infection is clinically suspected.

### GOALS OF THERAPY

- **Same as adults: stone clearance with the least harm to physical, emotional and social health.**
- **Primary determinants**: stone size and location, patient anatomy, patient (and provider) preference. **Secondary**: comorbidity, stone composition (if known), equipment.
- **In practice, the choice is heavily influenced by where the child is treated.**

#### Stone Clearance

- **The most important surgical outcome**; residual fragments → stone growth and future symptomatic events.
- **No consensus on the clinically significant residual fragment size in children**; assessment varies (visual at URS, postoperative US or CT), making cross-study comparison difficult.
- **Why 4 mm is used as a cut-point**: **US overestimates stone size by about 2 mm**, and **2 mm is the conventional CT threshold** for clinically insignificant fragments → 2 + 2 = 4 mm on US.

#### Radiation

- **Children are more likely than adults to need future imaging and interventions**, and their cumulative risk is higher (longer life expectancy, radiosensitive tissue).
- Exposure occurs at **diagnosis, operation and post-operative surveillance**. **URS and PCNL use fluoroscopy; SWL does too unless US is used for targeting.**
- **PCNL delivers several times more fluoroscopy and effective dose than URS**; bilateral URS more than unilateral (single-institution data).
- **Occupational-style dose limits**: **maximum 50 mSv in any 12 months; average <20 mSv per year over 5 years.**

**Levers to reduce radiation**

| Lever | Measure |
|---|---|
| Geometry | **Maximise source-to-skin distance**; improve skin-to-image-intensifier distance; **collimate** (narrow the window) |
| Settings | **Lower kVp; pulsed dosing**; appropriate paediatric dose settings; **avoid routine magnification**; pulse settings for small children |
| Mode | **Intermittent low-dose rather than continuous fluoroscopy** |
| Process | **Radiation-reduction checklists** (↓ fluoroscopy time); technician-operated C-arm |
| Substitute imaging | **US guidance for URS, PCNL access and SWL targeting** (authors: US for PCNL access and SWL targeting) |

#### Ancillary Procedures / Anaesthesia

- **Paediatric stone treatment often needs several procedures and anaesthetics.**
- **URS**: a small ureter may not admit the ureteroscope → **"pre-stent" for passive dilatation → an extra anaesthetic**.
- **SWL**: **frequent retreatment**.
- **Concern: anaesthetic neurotoxicity in the developing brain, especially in pre-school children.**

*__Concept —__ Paediatric stone surgery is judged on a ledger with more columns than in adults: **clearance must be bought with the fewest anaesthetics and the least cumulative radiation**, because the child carries both costs across a longer lifetime — which is why the ideal-modality criteria read as they do.*

### URETEROSCOPIC MANAGEMENT OF UPPER URINARY TRACT CALCULI

- **URS is a mainstay of surgical treatment**: pooled paediatric series (systematic review) show **clearance approaching 90%** with **mostly low-grade (≤Clavien III) complications**.
- **Laser advances**: **thulium fibre laser gave better clearance than low-power holmium laser, with no increase in complications** (single-centre series).
- **Efficacy is modified by stone location and patient age/size**:
  - **Lower-pole stones clear less well**; **small kidneys and tight infundibulopelvic angles** restrict manoeuvring.
  - **Children <6 years: lower success and more complications** than older children.
  - **Children <20 kg: high complication rate, rising in the youngest and smallest.**
- **Pre-stenting** (pair):
  - **Why it happens**: forcing a scope or **active dilatation risks perforation and stricture** → passive dilatation with a stent.
  - **Why not routinely**: **AUA/EUS recommend against routine pre-stenting**; **pre-stenting is associated with more ED visits after URS**. **α-blockers may facilitate primary ureteric access** (preliminary data).
- **Optimal technique undefined.**

**Laser dusting versus fragmentation–extraction**

| | Dusting | Fragmentation and extraction |
|---|---|---|
| Settings | **High frequency (>50 Hz), low energy (<0.4 J)** | **Low frequency, high energy** |
| Fragment size | **<1 mm — passes spontaneously** | **1–4 mm — basket-extracted** |
| Adult outcomes | **Lower clearance, less stent use**, similar unplanned encounters | — |
| Drawback | — | **Multiple ureteric passes** → access sheath |

- **Ureteral access sheath (UAS)** (pair):
  - **Benefits**: repeated upper tract access; **↓ intrarenal pressure**; **↓ operative time**; eases flexible URS in altered anatomy or tortuous ureters. Sizes: inner 9–12 Fr, outer 11–14 Fr.
  - **Risk**: **some ureters cannot accommodate a sheath → ureteric injury**. Benefit for paediatric clearance is **unproven**.

#### Equipment

**Semirigid versus flexible ureteroscopes**

| | Semirigid | Flexible |
|---|---|---|
| Outer size | **4.5–6 Fr** | **7.5–9 Fr** |
| Working channel | 2.4–3.5 Fr | 1.8–3.6 Fr |
| Optics | Fibreoptic or digital | Fibreoptic or digital |
| Tip deflection | — | **Up to 270°** → most lower-pole stones |
| Use | **Ureteric stones** | **Proximal ureteric and renal stones** |

- Wires 0.035, 0.025 and 0.018 inch; stents 3.7–8 Fr.

#### Ureteroscopic Technique in Children

**Sequence**: general anaesthesia → **confirm sterile urine culture** → lithotomy (appropriately sized stirrups; **infants: gel rolls under the knees** for modified lithotomy; pad all pressure points; **leg weight on the heels, not calves or knees — peroneal nerve**) → broad-spectrum antibiotic prophylaxis → rigid cystoscopy (7.5, 10 or 14.5 Fr) → **retrograde pyelogram (RPG) + wire under fluoroscopy** → calibrate ureteric orifice; **safety wire via 8/10 Fr dual-lumen catheter** in unstented ureters or when the scope will not pass easily → **if the 8/10 Fr dilator will not pass: place a 4.6–4.8 Fr stent for ≥14 days of passive dilatation** and return → URS.

- **No balloon dilatation of the orifice (stricture risk); no active distal ureteric dilatation** — injury risk, and **the ureter proximal to the orifice also limits safe URS**, especially with a sheath.
- **Scope choice by stone location and anatomy**: **semirigid for ureteric; flexible for proximal ureteric and renal** — **both with a safety wire**.
- **UAS (10/12 or 12/14 Fr) routine for flexible URS**, especially large proximal ureteric stones and larger renal burdens needing basketing (adult data also suggest better clearance).
- **URS is less position-dependent than SWL**, but the small paediatric lower pole is still hard to reach.

**Laser fibre choice**

| Fibre | Use | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **150–200 µm** | **Lower-pole stones** | **Better scope deflection** |
| **365 µm** | Ureteric, mid- and upper-pole stones | Deflection not limiting |

- **Irrigant isotonic and at body temperature** → avoids **hyponatraemia and hypothermia**.
- **Basket-extract when feasible**; otherwise **holmium:YAG** fragmentation.
- **Postoperative stent decision: procedure duration, number of ureteroscope passes, visible ureteric trauma or oedema.**
- **Stent removal** (pair): **string left 5–7 days** → removed at home or at a nursing visit, versus **no string → cystoscopy under anaesthesia after 7 days**.
- **Stent symptoms are common** → paracetamol (acetaminophen), ibuprofen, α-blockers, anticholinergics; **no routine opioids**.

#### Limitations and Complications

- **Ureteric injury, UTI, bleeding**; **serious complications (>Clavien III) uncommon**.
- **Ureteric injury → abort the procedure and place a stent** — limits shear injury, ischaemia and extravasation of irrigant or urine.
- **Where injury happens**: scope introduction; antegrade or retrograde instrument passage (wires, baskets, dilators), **especially at an impacted stone**; **withdrawing a basketed stone too large for the ureter**.
- **Stricture**: **RPG defines degree, length and location.**

| Stricture | Management |
|---|---|
| Short mid-ureteric | Prolonged stenting (long-term success uncertain) |
| Endoscopic option (authors) | **Laser incision preferred; balloon dilatation not used** |
| **Longer, denser or recurrent** | **Formal reconstruction, often robotic** |
| Distal | **Ureteroneocystostomy** |
| Proximal | **Pyeloplasty** |
| Mid-ureteric | **Excision and primary anastomosis** |

> **KEY POINTS: URETEROSCOPIC MANAGEMENT OF UPPER TRACT CALCULI**
> - **Miniaturised instruments and adapted adult techniques allow calculi <15 mm to be treated effectively and safely.**
> - **Routine pre-stenting before URS is not recommended.**
> - **Postoperative stenting depends on procedure duration, number of passes, and visible ureteric trauma or oedema.**

*__Concept —__ Every paediatric ureteroscopy decision is a negotiation with calibre: **the ureter's size decides pre-stenting, wire, sheath and scope, and the kidney's size decides fibre choice** — so passive dilatation is preferred to force, and the smallest instrument that works is chosen.*

## Instalment 4 — Shockwave Lithotripsy (Selection, Complications, Contraindications, Technique), Comparative Effectiveness of URS and SWL, Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy (Tract Size, Mini/Micro-PCNL, Planning, Technique, Sandwich Therapy, Complications; Figs 54.6–54.7), Laparoscopic and Robotic Pyelolithotomy, Secondary Prevention (Fluid, Sodium, Calcium, Animal Protein, Oxalate, Potassium/Citrate, Other Dietary Factors, Medications)

### SHOCKWAVE LITHOTRIPSY

- **Introduced early 1980s; first successful paediatric SWL in 1986.**
- **AUA/EUS recommendations relevant to SWL**: (1) **URS or SWL** for ureteric stones unlikely to pass or after failed observation/MET, by anatomy and habitus; (2) **renal burden ≤20 mm → SWL or URS first-line**.
- **Efficacy varies with** lithotripter generation, technique, body habitus and the definition of clearance.
- **Body habitus matters less in children — shorter skin-to-stone distance.**

**Predictors of SWL success versus failure**

| Factor | Favourable | Unfavourable |
|---|---|---|
| **Size** | **<10 mm** | **>10 mm** — clearance falls |
| **Location** | Non-lower-pole | **Lower pole** (adult meta-analysis, lower-pole stones <2 cm: **PCNL > SWL and URS > SWL**, especially 10–20 mm) |
| **Density** (measure on preoperative CT) | **<1000 Hounsfield units (HU)** | High attenuation — **cystine, brushite** |
| **Collecting system** | Short infundibulum | **Long infundibulum; infundibulopelvic angle >45°** |
| Nomograms | — | Prior stone history, location, burden, sex, age |

**Complications**
- **Haematuria expected**; rarely subcapsular or perirenal haematoma.
- **Fragments must pass** → intermittent renal colic.
- **Steinstrasse** (ureteric obstruction by a column of fragments) — uncommon but real, **especially after large stones**; may need intervention.
- **Mis-focusing → injury to colon, vessels, lung, spleen, pancreas.**
- **Adult data: SWL for renal (not ureteric) stones associated with incident hypertension** — probably a legacy of older, more powerful, wide-focal-zone lithotripters; **relevance to children unknown**.

**Contraindications to SWL**

| Absolute | Relative |
|---|---|
| **Uncontrolled bleeding diathesis** | Solitary kidney |
| **Untreated UTI** | Urinary tract obstruction |
| **Pregnancy** | Abnormal anatomy |

#### Shockwave Lithotripsy Technique in Children

- **General anaesthesia usually** (intravenous sedation in some older children); **no bowel preparation**.
- Ureteric catheter with retrograde opacification occasionally used to **localise radiolucent stones**.
- **Energy commensurate with child size**: **low power (<20 kV)** → less stone migration, better fragmentation; **maximum 2500–3000 shocks per session (<2000 in very young children)**.
- **Shielding and positioning** + dose matched to size → fewer **haematomas and lung contusions**; **foam tape shields the lung fields** but must not block transmission to the stone.
- **Ungated shocks are safe in children** — the arrhythmias seen in adults are unlikely.
- **Shock rate**: in adults, higher rate and energy → lower clearance; paediatric optimum unknown — **60 and 90 shocks/min gave similar results; 80/min cleared better than 120/min** → slower rates favoured.
- **Stents**: AUA/EUS 2016 → **stent or nephrostomy for SWL of stones >20 mm**.

**Guideline versus authors' practice — large renal stones**

| | AUA/EUS 2016 | Authors |
|---|---|---|
| Renal burden >20 mm | **PCNL or SWL** (SWL with stent/nephrostomy) | **PCNL** |
| Upper size limit for SWL | None stated | **No SWL for stones >15 mm** |
| Reason | Expert opinion | **SWL poorly effective for large burdens; PCNL safe and highly effective** |

### COMPARATIVE EFFECTIVENESS OF URS AND SWL

**Studies comparing URS with SWL in children**

| Study | Design / population | Findings | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multi-institutional comparison | Retrospective, paediatric | **Similar clearance, residual fragments <4 mm and retreatment**; **SWL: fewer ED visits (infection, flank pain) and fewer anaesthetics per treatment** | **Equal efficacy; SWL less morbid** |
| Prospective randomised trial | **Pre-school children** | **Similar clearance, complications and operative time** | Equal |
| Unpublished AUA/EUS meta-analysis | Paediatric | **Similar clearance** | Equal |

- **Together these underpin the guideline**: **SWL or URS first-line** for ureteric stones after failed observation/MET, or for renal burden **≤20 mm**.

> **KEY POINTS: CONSERVATIVE MANAGEMENT AND SHOCKWAVE LITHOTRIPSY**
> - **Smaller and more distal stones are more likely to pass spontaneously.**
> - **α-Blockers may increase spontaneous passage** of ureteric stones in children.
> - **Overall clearance is similar for SWL and URS for stones <15 mm**, but efficacy varies with stone location and density and with patient anatomy.

*__Concept —__ SWL's paediatric advantages (short skin-to-stone distance, fewer anaesthetics, fewer ED returns) are real, but **its effectiveness is dictated by stone physics — size, density and lower-pole drainage** — so it matches URS only within an envelope of small, soft, favourably placed stones.*

### PERCUTANEOUS NEPHROLITHOTOMY

- **Early reluctance**: large instruments in small kidneys, parenchymal damage and renal function, radiation, and major complications (sepsis, bleeding).
- **Now first-line for renal stones >20 mm in children**, with **clearance of about 90%**.
- **Bleeding risk rises with tract size**; **a 24–30 Fr tract in a child is relatively equivalent to a 72 Fr tract in an adult.**
- **Miniaturisation** of sheaths and nephroscopes and better lithotripters → smaller tracts → **less bleeding with stone-free rates ≥75%**.

**PCNL by tract size**

| | Standard | Mini-PCNL | Micro-PCNL |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tract | 24–30 Fr | **<20–24 Fr** | **<10 Fr** |
| History / status | — | **First described 1998, for children**; since adopted in adults | **"All-seeing needle" optical puncture**; **not FDA approved in the US**; promising abroad (low complications, high stone-free rates) |
| Paediatric role | — | Under investigation | Under investigation |

- **Guideline versus authors** (see table above): AUA allows SWL or PCNL for >20 mm; **the authors recommend PCNL**. **PCNL should be done only by experienced urologists with the training and equipment.**

#### Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy Planning for Pediatric Patients

- **Surgeon experience is paramount.**
- **CT before PCNL** (AUA/EUS) → amenability to PCNL; **optimal calyx**; stone size and location; **adjacent organs**; **spinal deformity (scoliosis, lordosis, kyphosis)** altering renal anatomy.
- **Consent risks**: bleeding needing transfusion; **delayed haemorrhage needing angioembolisation**; sepsis; **pneumothorax, haemothorax, urinothorax**; incomplete treatment; adjacent-organ injury.
- **Treat UTI first**: urine culture with sensitivities **2–3 weeks before**; positive → **full course of antibiotics**.
  - **Bladder urine may not reflect renal pelvic urine or stone culture.**
  - Adults: **empirical ciprofloxacin for 1 week pre-operatively may reduce infective complications**.
  - **Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics (e.g. ampicillin + gentamicin) at the procedure.**

#### Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy Technique for Pediatric Patients

**Sequence**: general anaesthesia with **hypothermia prevention** (warm theatre, warmed isotonic irrigant, shorter operating time, draping, temperature monitoring) → **cystoscopy + 4 or 5 Fr open-ended ureteric catheter to just below the pelviureteric junction (PUJ)** (for RPG or saline distension) → **prone over gel bolsters** (accommodate contractures without forcing joints; **latex precautions in myelomeningocele**; pad pressure points) → **US-guided access** (fluoroscopy an alternative) → select calyx → **16- or 18-gauge spinal needle** → **No. 11 blade skin incision** → **8 and 10 Fr coaxial dilators** over the guidewire → **safety wire** via safety-wire introducer or dual-lumen catheter (**preferably down the ureter**) → **18 Fr sheath (24 Fr for partial or complete staghorn)** → RPG through the ureteric catheter to redefine anatomy → **15/18 Fr rigid nephroscope**; **dual ultrasonic–mechanical lithotripter or laser** → **flexible cystoscope/ureteroscope** to confirm clearance and reach mid-pole calyces → **nephrostomy tube** (**7 Fr pigtail in infants → 16 Fr in adolescents**) → remove once urine clears + **capping trial** → discharge usually **postoperative day 1**; **nephrostogram** to confirm antegrade drainage in children who cannot communicate pain.

**Calyceal access rules**

| Pole | Rule | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Lower** | **Posterior calyx = the one immediately superior to the most inferior calyx on anteroposterior fluoroscopy (in >90%)** | Identifies posterior calyx |
| **Upper** | **Below the 11th rib, medial calyx** | **↓ pneumothorax** |

- **Paediatric specifics**: **children's (especially infants') kidneys are more mobile and push away** during needle and sheath placement → watch the **balloon tip position and bony landmarks**; **trim the sheath's extracorporeal length** in small children → better manoeuvrability, less torque on the kidney.
- **Tubeless PCNL: insufficient evidence in children.**
- **"Sandwich therapy"** (PCNL + adjunctive SWL for residual fragments) — **historical; indications now limited** because PCNL clears about 90% of large burdens, nephroscopes are smaller, and SWL raises hypertension concerns.

#### Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy Complications

- **Not rare: about 10% intraoperative and 20% postoperative**; mostly minor, but **up to 16% ≥Clavien III**; **transfusion <10%**.

| Intraoperative | Postoperative |
|---|---|
| Failure to dilate tract | **UTI / sepsis** |
| **Acute haemorrhage** | Ureteric obstruction by fragment |
| **Loss of access** | **Delayed haemorrhage — pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous malformation** |
| Renal pelvic perforation | — |

- **Determinants of complications** (large multicentre study): **operative time, sheath size, mid-calyceal puncture, partial staghorn calculi**.
- **Management**:
  - **Significant bleeding → abort; Foley or re-entry catheter through the tract** (tamponade); treat as in adults.
  - **Renal pelvic injury → stop; antegrade ureteric stent** if feasible.
  - Hydrothorax, colonic injury and postoperative bleeding are managed as in adults.
- **PCNL only in hospitals with a paediatric interventional radiology team able to embolise.**

> **KEY POINTS: PERCUTANEOUS NEPHROLITHOTOMY**
> - **PCNL is indicated for large upper tract burdens (>20 mm) in children.**
> - **US and/or fluoroscopy** can be used for access.
> - **Clearance about 90%**, with risks of **bleeding, sepsis, pneumothorax and adjacent-organ injury**.

*__Concept —__ PCNL's place in children rests on one trade: **the highest single-session clearance for large burdens, paid for in tract-related bleeding** — so every refinement (miniaturisation, US access, trimmed sheaths, interventional radiology back-up) exists to shrink the tract's relative size and its haemorrhagic cost.*

### LAPAROSCOPIC AND ROBOTIC-ASSISTED PYELOLITHOTOMY

- **Not indicated for upper tract stones except with an anatomic abnormality** — e.g. **pyelolithotomy during concomitant pyeloplasty for PUJ obstruction**.

### Secondary Prevention

**Dietary measures**

| Factor | Mechanism | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| **Fluid** | **Dilutes calcium, oxalate, uric acid and cystine** | **≥ calculated maintenance (children); >2–2.5 L/day (adolescents, adults)**; **1.5–2 L/m² in cystinuria or PH**; more with ↑ insensible losses |
| **Sodium** | **Competes with calcium for tubular reabsorption → calciuria**; ↓ salt also ↓ cystine excretion | **<2–3 mEq/kg/day (children); <2.4 g/day (adolescents, adults)** in hypercalciuria or calcium stones; also in cystinuria |
| **Calcium** | **Binds dietary oxalate in the gut → ↓ oxalate absorption and excretion** | **Do not restrict** (also protects against osteoporosis); excess vitamin D without calcium supplements not linked to stones (adults) |
| **Animal protein** | **Purine load → uricosuria, acid urine; sulfur amino acids → sulfuric acid → ↑ urinary calcium, ↓ citrate, ↓ pH**; vegetable and dairy protein not lithogenic | **Aim for 100% of recommended daily allowance** for age (growth) — avoid excess; cystinuria: little evidence for restricting cystine/methionine, but ↓ animal protein may raise pH |
| **Oxalate** | Dietary load; **vitamin C → oxalate** | **Limit in CaOx formers**; **stop vitamin C supplements in CaOx formers with hyperoxaluria** |
| **Potassium / citrate** | **High potassium protective**; K restriction ↑ urinary calcium; **hypokalaemia ↓ citrate**; chronic low K intake lowers urinary K and citrate even without hypokalaemia | **Potassium-rich fruit and vegetables** (citrate directly, and indirectly via potassium) |
| **Magnesium** | **Complexes oxalate** in gut and urine → ↓ absorption and CaOx supersaturation; lower risk in males | **Supplementation may help secondary hyperoxaluria** |
| Carbohydrate / sucrose | Carbohydrate → hypercalciuria; sucrose → stones | — |
| **Phytate** (cereals, legumes, vegetables, nuts) | **Binds calcium; proposed CaOx inhibitor** | — |

- **Fluid is the cornerstone. Most children and adolescents drink too little.** In adolescents with stones, the commonest barriers are **unawareness of the volume actually drunk and poor response to the perceived need to drink more**.
- **Prevention of Urinary Stones with Hydration (PUSH) RCT**: testing **financial incentives and coaching** to sustain high fluid intake against symptomatic recurrence.
- **Other fluids**:
  - **Protective**: **orange juice, lemonade, blackcurrant juice** (↑ urinary pH and citrate); **coffee** (↑ volume).
  - **Harmful: grapefruit juice** (↑ calcium stone risk).
  - Soft drinks: **controversial**.
  - **Authors counsel water** because of the sugar and calories in juices and sodas; **amount matters more than type**.
- **The calcium paradox**: **higher dietary calcium lowers stone risk** — the classic trap.
- **High-oxalate foods**: nuts (almonds, peanuts, cashews, walnuts, pecans), **spinach**, soybeans, tofu, **rhubarb**, beets, sweet potatoes, wheat bran, okra, parsley, chives, black raspberries, star fruit, green tea, **chocolate**.

#### Medications

- **Pharmacotherapy when fluid and diet fail to control stone formation.**
- **Only thiol-containing agents and lumasiran are FDA approved in children for secondary prevention**; all others are **off-label**.

**Drugs for secondary prevention**

| Agent | Indication | Dose: child (adult) | Mechanism | Key cautions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hydrochlorothiazide** | **Hypercalciuria not responding to sodium restriction** | **1–2 mg/kg/day** (25–100 mg/day) | Thiazide diuretic → ↓ urinary calcium | **Potassium depletion → add potassium citrate** |
| **Potassium citrate** | **CaOx stones with low or normal citrate**; **uric acid stones (target pH >6.5)**; **cystinuria (target pH >7)** | **2–4 mEq/kg/day** (30–90 mEq/day) | Alkali; citrate supply | GI side-effects common; **over-alkalinisation (pH >6.5) → CaP stones** |
| Potassium–magnesium citrate | ↓ CaOx recurrence | — | Alkali + magnesium | — |
| Sodium citrate | Alkali alternative | — | Alkali | **Less ideal — sodium load ↑ calciuria** |
| **Tiopronin (α-mercaptopropionylglycine)** | **Cystinuria failing fluid, diet and alkali** | — | **Thiol reduces the cystine disulfide bond → soluble cysteine–drug complex** | Adverse effects similar to D-penicillamine, perhaps slightly fewer; **commonest thiol** |
| **D-penicillamine** | As tiopronin | — | As tiopronin | **Many**: fever, GI upset, liver dysfunction, taste loss, **marrow suppression**, trace-metal deficiency, **membranous glomerulopathy**, **myasthenia gravis**, **elastosis perforans serpiginosa** |
| **Allopurinol** | **Hyperuricaemia + hyperuricosuria (PRPS superactivity, HPRT deficiency)**; **adenine phosphoribosyltransferase (APRT) deficiency**; hyperuricosuric CaOx stones **without** hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria or hypocitraturia | **4–10 mg/kg/day** (max 300 mg/day) | **Xanthine dehydrogenase inhibition** | **Xanthinuria → xanthine stones** (rare) |
| **Pyridoxine (B6)** | **PH1** | **Start 2–5 → titrate to 8–10 mg/kg/day** | **AGT cofactor** | **Large doses → sensory neuropathy** |
| **Lumasiran** | **PH1** | — | **RNA interference (RNAi) → ↓ glycolate oxidase** (substrate reduction) | — |

- **Thiol monitoring**: liver enzymes, full blood count, urinalysis, **copper and zinc**. **Special assays (solid-phase assay or high-performance liquid chromatography) distinguish free cystine from cysteine–drug complexes** → guide long-term therapy.
- **Uric acid stones — mainstay is high urine flow + alkalinisation**, not allopurinol.
- **Pyridoxine responsiveness**:
  - **10–30% of PH1 children are pyridoxine-sensitive** (**>30% ↓ urinary oxalate**).
  - **Gly170Arg or Phe152Ile homozygotes** are more likely to respond and keep renal function, but **sub-genotype does not predict response** → **trial pyridoxine in every PH1 patient** while diagnosis and response are assessed.
  - **No benefit in other hyperoxalurias** unless there is true B6 deficiency.
- **Lumasiran — ILLUMINATE-A (landmark RCT)**: **urinary oxalate ↓ 65%**; **84% reached ≤1.5 × upper limit of normal**. FDA approved for PH1.

**Urine pH targets and ceilings**

| Situation | pH |
|---|---|
| Uric acid stones — target | **>6.5** |
| Cystinuria — target | **>7** |
| Calcium stone former on alkali — avoid | **>6.5** (CaP risk) |

*__Concept —__ Almost every preventive measure pulls one of three levers — **urine volume, promoter excretion, or inhibitor and pH** — which explains why calcium is not restricted (it removes oxalate in the gut), why sodium is (it drives calciuria), and why alkali must stop short of the calcium phosphate threshold.*

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 4

- **SWL dates**: introduced early **1980s**; first paediatric **1986**. **Mini-PCNL first described 1998** (for children).
- **SWL cut-offs**: size **<10 mm** best; **<1000 HU** favourable; **infundibulopelvic angle >45°** unfavourable; **<20 kV**; **2500–3000 shocks max (<2000 very young)**; **80/min better than 120/min**.
- **SWL absolute contraindications**: uncontrolled bleeding diathesis; untreated UTI; pregnancy. **Relative**: solitary kidney; obstruction; abnormal anatomy.
- **Hard stones for SWL: cystine, brushite.**
- **Ungated shocks safe in children.**
- **AUA: >20 mm → PCNL or SWL (+ stent/nephrostomy)**; **authors: PCNL, and no SWL >15 mm**.
- **URS = SWL** for clearance in children; **SWL fewer anaesthetics and ED visits**.
- **PCNL**: first-line **>20 mm**; clearance **about 90%**; child **24–30 Fr ≈ adult 72 Fr**; mini **<20–24 Fr**; micro **<10 Fr**; stone-free **≥75%** with small tracts.
- **PCNL complications**: intraoperative **about 10%**, postoperative **about 20%**, **≥Clavien III up to 16%**, **transfusion <10%**.
- **PCNL technique**: culture **2–3 weeks** pre-op; **4–5 Fr** ureteric catheter; **16–18 G** needle; **8 + 10 Fr** dilators; **18 Fr sheath (24 Fr staghorn)**; **15/18 Fr** nephroscope; nephrostomy **7 Fr (infant) → 16 Fr (adolescent)**; discharge **day 1**.
- **Lower-pole posterior calyx = one above the most inferior on AP (>90%)**; **upper pole below the 11th rib, medial calyx**.
- **Complication determinants**: operative time, sheath size, mid-calyceal puncture, partial staghorn.
- **When to operate (open/laparoscopic/robotic)**: **only with anatomic abnormality** (e.g. PUJ obstruction + pyeloplasty). **Do not** use routinely for upper tract stones.
- **Fluid**: ≥ maintenance (child); **>2–2.5 L** (adolescent/adult); **1.5–2 L/m²** (cystinuria, PH).
- **Sodium**: **<2–3 mEq/kg/day** (child); **<2.4 g/day** (adolescent/adult).
- **Drug doses**: hydrochlorothiazide **1–2 mg/kg/day**; potassium citrate **2–4 mEq/kg/day**; allopurinol **4–10 mg/kg/day (max 300)**; pyridoxine **2–5 → 8–10 mg/kg/day**.
- **pH**: uric acid **>6.5**; cystine **>7**; CaP risk **>6.5**.
- **Pyridoxine-responsive PH1: 10–30%** (response = **>30%** oxalate reduction).
- **FDA-approved in children for prevention**: **thiols and lumasiran only**.
- **Commonest thiol: tiopronin.**
- **Landmark trials**: **ILLUMINATE-A** (lumasiran, PH1) — **oxalate ↓ 65%; 84% ≤1.5 × ULN**. **PUSH** — hydration incentives and coaching RCT (ongoing).
- **Eponyms**: **Steinstrasse** — ureteric column of post-SWL fragments. **Elastosis perforans serpiginosa** — skin reaction specific to D-penicillamine.
- **Classic traps**:
  - **Do not restrict dietary calcium** — higher calcium intake lowers stone risk.
  - **Sodium citrate is not equivalent to potassium citrate** (sodium load → calciuria).
  - **Over-alkalinisation → CaP stones.**
  - **Allopurinol is not first-line for uric acid stones** (hydration + alkali are).
  - **Allopurinol → xanthine stones.**
  - **Pyridoxine genotype does not predict response** — trial it in all PH1.
  - **Tubeless PCNL is not established in children.**
  - **Bladder culture ≠ pelvic or stone culture.**
  - **Grapefruit juice harms; orange juice and lemonade help.**
  - **Vitamin C supplements → oxalate.**
