Chapter 112 — Nocturia

  • Nocturia is voiding during the hours of intended sleep. It carries substantial morbidity: a risk factor for falls and fractures, a major disruptor of normal sleep architecture and a potential harbinger of serious systemic disease.
  • Aetiology is multifactorial — "Sleep CALM": Sleep disorders, Comorbidities, Actions (modifiable behaviours and lifestyle factors), Lower urinary tract dysfunction, Medications.
  • One common mechanism: every Sleep CALM factor disturbs the balance between nocturnal urine production, storage capacity and normal sleep architecture — and that balance is best characterised by the bladder diary.
  • Evaluation: systematic yet individualised — history and examination → tailored ancillary testing.
  • Management: optimise the Sleep CALM factors first; pharmacotherapy targeting excess nocturnal urine production is reserved for appropriately selected patients refractory to conservative management.

Nocturia—What, When, and Why?

  • ICS definition (2019): the number of times urine is passed during the main sleep period — from the time a person intends to sleep until the time they intend to rise.
  • The main sleep period is defined by the individual's own sleep schedule, not by societal norms or the solar cycle (sunset to sunrise). "Night-time" is used for convenience but technically means the individual's sleep period.
  • Shift workers: nocturia is pervasive across all work and sleep schedules, and similar mechanisms operate independent of the solar cycle.
  • Current terminology does not exclude patients who void only once per night and does not incorporate bother.

Rationale for Evaluation and Management

Nocturia Is Common

  • Older age is among the most robust correlates, but nocturia is common across ages, races and sexes worldwide.
  • 1 in 5–6 people aged 20–40 years void ≥2 times per night; up to 3 in 5 people >70 years void ≥2 times nightly.
  • Boston Area Community Health (BACH) survey (landmark): higher prevalence in Black and Hispanic than White respondents; partly mediated by socio-economic status — a disparity in urological practice requiring investigation.

Nocturia Is Bothersome

  • Among the most bothersome urinary complaints in the general population; bother is linked to reduced quality of life (QoL) and more depressive symptoms.
  • High bother → more difficulty initiating sleep, returning to sleep after a void, and more morning fatigue.
  • Most people with ≥2 voids per night report significant bother; but frequency and bother correlate only at population level — one void per night can be highly bothersome.
  • Bother drives help-seeking; many with nocturia never seek help. Nocturia may dominate the interview and mask other LUTS.
  • Any nocturia that brings a patient to urological care is, by definition, clinically significant, irrespective of the number of voids.

Impact of Nocturia

Sleep

  • Harm is mediated largely through sleep architecture; urge-driven awakening is the commonest cause of adult sleep disruption.
  • Affects sleep latency (time to fall asleep after lights out), sleep maintenance and sleep efficiency (percentage of time in bed spent asleep).
  • An independent predictor of insomnia and reduced sleep quality in older adults; worsens wakefulness in baseline insomnia. Polysomnography confirms an independent association with poor sleep across the whole night.
  • Harm far exceeds the minutes lost to toileting — because of timing.

  • Structure: non-REM (NREM) ≈75% of sleep, stages N1 (transition), N2 (deeper), N3 ("slow-wave", deepest); cycles alternate NREM and REM, 4–6 cycles per night; slow-wave sleep is only 10–25% of total sleep.
  • Consequence: daytime fatigue and poor sleep quality even when total sleep time is preserved.
  • Home electroencephalography: less slow-wave sleep when the first void falls in the first two sleep cycles. A shorter interval to first void → worse whole-night sleep quality and more daytime dysfunction.

Falls and fractures

  • Nocturia is among the LUTS most strongly associated with falls and hip fractures; meta-analysis shows increased risk of both.
  • Older adults are doubly exposed: most affected, and ageing erodes coordination, strength, vision and cognition — hazardous when hurrying to the toilet in the dark. Weigh fall risk in them and other high-risk groups.

Beyond Bother—Nocturia, Morbidity, and Mortality

  • Nocturia may be a hallmark of cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, hepatic, endocrine, neurological, psychiatric or immunological disease.
  • Evidence is strongest for cardiometabolic and vascular disease: hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidaemia, obesity, metabolic syndrome, coronary artery disease, arrhythmias, cerebrovascular disease, stroke and post-stroke sequelae.
  • Mortality: in a nationally representative US sample, ≥2 voids per night was associated with worse survival than 0–1, particularly in those <65 years; meta-analysis shows increased mortality in both sexes.
  • Causality is not established; the link is likely mediated by falls, poor sleep and underlying comorbidity (falls history further raises mortality risk).

  • Common (highest in older adults), bothersome, the commonest cause of adult sleep disruption; hits deep sleep.
  • Linked to falls, fractures and systemic disease; management matters beyond bother.

Concept — Nocturia matters because of when it strikes and what it signals. The first void falls in the early, restorative slow-wave sleep, and the symptom may be the visible edge of systemic disease. Its harm is therefore out of proportion to the number of voids.

Causes of Nocturia: "Sleep CALM" and the Bladder Diary

  • Extensive differential. The relationship with poor sleep is bidirectional: sleep studies of patients with disordered sleep (no urinary complaint) find bathroom trips at the time of awakening.
  • Nocturia may share a pathophysiological pathway with systemic disease; lifestyle, lower urinary tract dysfunction (LUTD) and medications may cause or aggravate it.
  • Idiopathic nocturia is small but real. Most real-world cases are caused by one or more of the five Sleep CALM factors.

Bladder Diaries: Connecting Causal Factors to Clinical Symptoms

  • The Sleep CALM factors look heterogeneous, but all disrupt the balance between nocturnal urine production, functional bladder storage capacity and normal sleep architecture. The bladder diary — a guideline cornerstone — reveals this relationship objectively.
  • Urine production is continuous. Even after emptying at bedtime, volume climbs towards maximum capacity; when it is reached there are only two endpoints: wake to void, or enuresis.
  • So sleeping through requires that nocturnal production not exceed maximum functional capacity. A production–storage mismatch arises from excess volume (polyuria) and/or insufficient capacity.
  • Sleep architecture is the third arm: primary sleep disorders produce voids independent of bladder volume ("convenience" voids while already awake). Disordered sleep may also increase nocturnal production and/or lower the arousal "set-point" at which bladder volume overcomes sleep.

Diary technique

Disposable collection container → record time and volume of every void → record time, volume and type of every drink → mark time going to bed to sleep and time rising (separates day from night) → record bladder sensation (urgency) and incontinence episodes for each void.

Nocturia Severity and Sleep

  • "Going to bed intending to sleep" and "rising intending to rise" bookend the main sleep period; voids between them = ANV.
  • Intent governs: the last void of the night does not end the sleep period if the patient stays in bed intending to sleep, even if sleep never returns. A void at or just after the end of the period is the first morning void (daytime frequency).
  • Diary-based ANV is a gold standard for nocturia severity. Symptom questionnaires correlate only modestly with diaries: respondents more often overestimate, especially with severe symptoms, while those with low frequency may underplay it.
  • Interview-reported severity correlates with the diary in some populations, but inconsistently, and less well as ANV rises; overestimation at standardised interview persists however the question is phrased.
  • FUSP is the most important sleep measure in the diary. It indexes how long the patient stays in early slow-wave sleep uninterrupted.
    • FUSP is inversely related to the number of night-time voids.
    • Prolonged FUSP marks successful treatment, with better whole-night sleep and daytime function; improved blood glucose has been reported after FUSP prolongation.
    • Hence FUSP is an important outcome measure and treatment target.
  • Total sleep duration cuts both ways: nocturia can truncate sleep (patient gets up for the day after a late void), and treatment that lengthens FUSP lengthens total sleep; conversely, impaired efficiency may keep patients in bed longer — longer total sleep (>8 h) is more common in those with ≥2 voids. Total sleep time is needed for calculations but has limited clinical utility on its own.

Nocturnal Urine Production

  • NUV = all nocturnal voids + first morning void. Rationale: the untested assumption that people void before bed and shortly after waking, so first-morning urine was made during sleep.
  • NPi = NUV/TUV (often ×100 as a percentage); NUP = NUV/hours of main sleep period.

Functional Bladder Storage Capacity

  • Small functional capacity may be night-time-specific or span 24 h (mirroring NP versus global polyuria).
  • True capacity = voided volume + post-void residual (PVR); in practice NMVV (nocturnal) and 24-h MVV are reasonable proxies.
  • No consensus on diminished capacity: normal usually >300–400 mL; 150–300 mL recognised as abnormal in recent literature.

Quantifying the Relationship Between Nocturnal Urine Production and Storage

  • Urge-driven waking depends on the relationship between production and capacity, not either alone.
  • Ni = NUV/24-h MVV — production expressed as a function of the patient's own capacity. Ni >1 = NUV exceeds capacity; higher = greater mismatch. Ni correlates more strongly with nocturia frequency than production or capacity alone.
  • NBCi quantifies the gap between the patient's own maximum capacity and the volumes at which night voids actually occur.
    • PNV = Ni − 1: the voids expected if every void occurred exactly at maximum capacity. One is subtracted because the last nocturnal volume leaves as the first morning void, which is not a nocturia episode.
    • NBCi = ANV − PNV.

Worked example: NUV 800 mL, MVV 200 mL → Ni = 4 → PNV = 3 (three night voids of 200 mL, plus 200 mL at first morning void) → diary shows 6 night voids → NBCi = 6 − 3 = 3.

  • NBCi = 0: every night void at full capacity. NBCi >0: voids occur below MVV; higher values = greater discordance.
  • Investigate diminished nocturnal capacity when NBCi >1.3; NBCi >2 is strongly associated with severe nocturia.

  • Convenience voids (little or no urgency on the diary — woken by something other than urge) partly explain night-specific small capacity; they are significantly smaller than urge-driven voids.
  • But convenience voiding is uncommon in the general population and remains infrequent even in clinic patients with depression, anxiety or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Night-specific diminished capacity is a small but important entity.
  • NP, global polyuria and small capacity are not mutually exclusive.
    • Classical NP: high night production offset by low day production, so 24-h volume is normal.
    • Current ICS NP criteria (NPi >33% or NUP >90 mL/h) do not preclude concurrent global polyuria (>40 mL/kg/24 h).
    • A small nocturnal capacity may coexist with daytime capacity that is normal, increased, or small but further reduced at night.

  • Multiple diary abnormalities are common, and more so as nocturia frequency rises.

Bladder Diaries: Practical Considerations

  • Standard of care in all guidelines, yet under-used: completed by fewer than half of patients seen by urologists and about a third seen by general practitioners.
  • Adherence is the pervasive obstacle: interference with lifestyle and routine ("too busy", "took too long", "at work that day"). In a urogynaecology sample, unemployment and fewer children independently predicted completion — professional, family and social commitments make diaries cumbersome.

Evaluation and Management

  • Diaries define the precise production–capacity–sleep relationship. Sleep CALM organises the initial evaluation: history, comprehensive examination and basic testing in all → individually tailored adjunctive testing and multidisciplinary care as indicated (Table 112.2).
  • Guidelines support a validated disease-specific questionnaire at baseline and for monitoring; none is preferred. TANGO ("Targeting the individual's Aetiology of Nocturia to Guide Outcomes") covers the Sleep CALM factors and aids multidisciplinary assessment.
  • Because Sleep CALM factors cause nocturia by the same mechanisms, first-line management is patient-specific optimisation of every Sleep CALM abnormality found.

Sleep CALM: Sleep Disorders

  • Relationship is bidirectional: urge-driven waking damages early deep sleep; conversely obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), insomnia, restless legs syndrome and parasomnias may themselves underlie nocturnal toileting.
  • OSA (sudden cessation of breathing from airway obstruction) is the best-described link and a common cause of nocturia. OSA incidence rises with nocturia frequency; severe sleep-disordered breathing → more nocturia; high nocturia frequency predicts severe OSA in some studies — hence calls to add nocturia to OSA screening questionnaires.

Melatonin and behavioural treatment of insomnia

  • Melatonin (pineal hormone regulating the sleep cycle) may be a primary treatment for some subpopulations, particularly older adults. Age correlates with both disordered sleep and nocturia, and older adults are most vulnerable to sedative-hypnotics, anticholinergics and antidiuretic therapy.
  • Evidence for melatonin is limited, with modest results in some subpopulations; defining ideal candidates is a pressing research need.
  • Behavioural treatment of insomnia: individualised sleep-hygiene education improved sleep in older adults with chronic insomnia (small randomised trial); participants with baseline nocturia also had fewer nocturnal voids. But nocturia dampened the sleep-quality benefit.
  • So an integrated cognitive-behavioural intervention for insomnia with nocturia has been proposed by a multidisciplinary sleep panel; efficacy is under investigation.
  • Know when to involve sleep experts in a multimodal plan.

From the guidelines

  • EAU Female LUTS (strong, LoE 1a): women with nocturia and a history suggestive of OSA → refer to a sleep clinic for CPAP assessment.
  • EAU Male LUTS panel on nocturia (grade C, LoE 2): sleep-promoting agents may be used to aid return to sleep.

Concept — OSA is the model for the whole chapter. One disorder raises production (ANP), lowers capacity (detrusor instability) and fragments sleep, which is why treating the sleep disorder yields one of the largest nocturia responses of any intervention.

Sleep CALM: Comorbidities

  • Nocturia is intertwined with cardiovascular, endocrine, renal, hepatic, neurological and immunological disease; nocturia management is being studied within cardiology, endocrinology, neurology and chronic kidney disease care.
  • These conditions cause nocturia chiefly through excess urine production. There is growing evidence that atherosclerosis-mediated bladder vascular damage and ischaemia reduce functional capacity. Sleep disorders are highly prevalent in medical disease — comorbid insomnia may be commoner than primary insomnia.

Volume Overload

  • By day, intravascular fluid "third-spaces" into the interstitium and collects in dependent legs; it is not excreted by day but mobilised when lying down, adding to NUV — even in healthy adults.
  • Exaggerated in hypervolaemic states such as congestive heart failure, of which third-spacing is cardinal.
  • Acute decompensated heart failure: more nocturia before admission ↔ more leg oedema and greater NUV; diuretic treatment reduced nocturia alongside oedema and NUV. Similar findings in cardiology outpatients with overt overload treated to best practice.

Hypertension

  • Normal circadian dip of 10–20% in blood pressure during sleep. Non-dipping hypertension occurs in up to 40–50% of hypertensives and independently predicts target-organ damage and adverse cardiovascular outcomes.
  • Non-dipping probably mediates the nocturia–hypertension link: nocturia is independently associated with higher night-time pressure and blunted dipping.
  • Mechanism: renin–angiotensin–aldosterone dysregulation → excess aldosterone → nocturnal natriuresis; aberrant sympathetic activity and natriuretic peptides contribute.
  • Optimising hypertension may help nocturia. Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ) is a potent antihypertensive with a more pronounced antihypertensive but more modest diuretic effect than loop diuretics such as furosemide.
    • Pilot study: HCTZ 25 mg improved nocturia, NUV and NPi in men with terazosin-refractory LUTS.
    • Adjunctive HCTZ 12.5 mg in elderly losartan-refractory hypertension did not worsen nocturia.
    • Bedtime antihypertensive chronotherapy trials support better circadian control and fewer major cardiovascular events, but did not measure nocturia.

Disorders of Water Homeostasis

Arginine vasopressin (AVP) physiology: made in the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei → transported to the posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis) → released to osmotic and volume stimuli (rising serum osmolality is the main stimulus) → binds V2 receptors on collecting-duct principal cells → ↑ water permeability → water reabsorbed down osmotic gradients until isotonic.

Sleep CALM: Actions

  • Assess excess fluid, caffeine and alcohol, particularly in the 3–4 h before bed (Figure 112.7).
  • Epidemiology is inconsistent — large cohorts show no clear association, and some find nocturics less likely to take evening caffeine. This probably reflects cross-sectional design: patients self-regulate intake as self-treatment.
  • Interventional evidence is thin:
    • Crossover trial in OAB: 25% fluid reduction reduced nocturia, urgency and daytime frequency, but the nocturia gain was of modest clinical significance.
    • Men with 24-h output >30 mL/kg guided to <30 mL/kg: about one fewer night void, correlating with the fall in nocturnal volume; no adverse events.
    • Bottom line: benefit varies; patients with higher baseline intake gain most.
  • Physiology is the stronger evidence: urine output tracks total intake.

Sleep CALM: Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction

  • Nocturia is often isolated, but is also a component of bladder outlet obstruction (BOO), OAB, detrusor underactivity, neurogenic bladder, dysfunctional voiding and the genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM). It is commoner in men with clinically significant BPH and is present in up to 85% of OAB trial participants.
  • Historically nocturia was just one LUTS attributed to BPH (and a core OAB feature) long before it was studied in its own right.
  • Isolated nocturia is not a LUTS and would not be expected to respond to lower urinary tract drugs. Those drugs — historically the backbone of treatment — seldom give clinically meaningful benefit, but remain reasonable when LUTD is justifiably suspected.

Benign Prostatic Obstruction

  • Relieving BOO should help nocturia by lowering PVR, increasing functional capacity, reducing global frequency and reducing afferent input from the bladder neck and prostatic urethra.
  • α-blockers: inhibit noradrenaline at prostatic smooth muscle → ↓ prostatic tone and outlet resistance. α1-receptors also lie in bladder, urethra and CNS, and α-blockers reduce CNS afferent activity. Available: alfuzosin, doxazosin, tamsulosin, terazosin, silodosin.
  • 5α-reductase inhibitors (5ARIs), dutasteride and finasteride, block formation of serum and intraprostatic dihydrotestosterone (DHT) → prostatic involution and ↓ volume.
  • Nocturia data come almost entirely from secondary or post hoc endpoints in BPH/LUTS trials, in which nocturia did not determine eligibility.

Overactive Bladder Syndrome

  • Antimuscarinics (historical cornerstone): M3 receptors mediate detrusor contraction; antagonism of post-junctional excitatory receptors → relaxation. Muscarinic receptors in the urothelium and afferent pathways may also contribute to OAB.
  • Proposed nocturia mechanisms: ↓ afferent activity (less nocturnal urgency), ↑ nocturnal capacity, longer inter-void interval. Agents: darifenacin, fesoterodine, oxybutynin, propiverine, solifenacin, tolterodine, trospium.
  • Evidence mostly secondary/post hoc; significance inconsistent. Nocturnal urgency and nocturnal voids are among the most refractory OAB symptoms — good daytime response does not predict night-time benefit.

Nocturia and the Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause

  • GSM: LUTS plus genital and sexual symptoms from reduced oestrogenic stimulation. Nocturia is common within it. Mechanism is probably multifactorial: LUTD + excess nocturnal production + sleep impairment.
  • Hormone replacement therapy may help postmenopausal women with bothersome urinary symptoms: oral oestrogen, vaginal ring or pessary, and several formulations in a recent pilot. The selective oestrogen receptor modulator ospemifene is potentially effective.

Multicomponent Therapy

  • Most nocturia has more than one cause, and the Sleep CALM factors interact:
    • Poor health and comorbidity burden are the strongest predictors of sleep disturbance; many chronic-disease drugs cause insomnia.
    • LUTS severity is linked to cardiovascular risk, inflammation and systemic disease.
    • Older age brings more disease, adverse sleep changes and polypharmacy.
  • A multifactorial cause needs a multimodal plan — the basis of the EAU Male LUTS "Shared Care Pathway", which addresses sleep, medical and lower urinary tract factors concurrently.
  • Small trials show benefit from multicomponent, aetiology-directed treatment; an individualised plan addressing several Sleep CALM factors in older male US veterans improved frequency, bother and sleep outcomes.
Key points: Evaluation and Management
  • Optimise every Sleep CALM factor; sleep is bidirectional — CPAP, melatonin, insomnia therapy may help.
  • Head-to-toe work-up: nocturia may be the sole sign of disease, and treating disease helps nocturia.
  • Evening fluid, alcohol, caffeine, sodium restriction: variable evidence, low risk.
  • LUTD drugs: modest benefit; β3 vs antimuscarinic unknown; oestrogen in GSM.
  • Reconcile medications; multicomponent treatment is likely additive.

Concept — Sleep CALM is a diagnostic checklist and a treatment plan at once. Each factor causes nocturia through production, capacity or sleep, so identifying it is already the start of treating it. Because the factors coexist, they are treated together, not in turn.

Refractory Nocturia: A Stepwise Approach

Figure 112.8 — Sleep CALM diagnostic algorithm (Monaghan et al., 2023).

History + physical examination + individualised ancillary testing → identify modifiable Sleep CALM factors → optimise Sleep CALM → conservative management → pharmacotherapy in carefully selected patients — with the voiding diary running alongside every step.

  • Even refractory nocturia starts conservatively: lifestyle measures have variable evidence but minimal risk. Drugs are for nocturia refractory to Sleep CALM optimisation and conservative management.
  • In the USA, bedtime antidiuretic replacement is the only drug specifically indicated for nocturia — for ≥2 voids per night due to NP. Guidelines also discuss timed diuretics (e.g. early afternoon), which shift urine output into the day and reduce NUV.
  • Why target nocturnal volume even when capacity is small:
    • Production and capacity both determine the mismatch, but excess nocturnal production is the stronger correlate of frequency and of treatment response.
    • NP patients benefit most; but even patients without NP respond to volume-reducing treatment.
    • In patients with impaired nocturnal capacity, production-lowering therapy helps them void closer to capacity. In diary-confirmed small capacity, improvement came with falling NUV and unchanged capacity.
    • Production-lowering therapy improves nocturia in BPH and OAB, as monotherapy or add-on.
  • EAU Male LUTS panel on nocturia (grade A, LoE 3): discuss lifestyle changes to reduce nocturnal volume and episodes and improve sleep.

Antidiuretic Replacement Therapy

  • Endogenous AVP normally rises during sleep, conserving water, lowering NUV and counteracting bladder filling. A blunted nocturnal AVP rise occurs in some NP samples — leading some to regard NP as partial central DI.
  • AVP is technically difficult to measure, and its role may differ between populations; but synthetic antidiuretics predictably ↑ water permeability and reabsorption, dilute extracellular fluid and concentrate urine.
  • Bedtime antidiuretic → ↓ NUV: a rational option for refractory nocturia without other identifiable contributory factors.
  • Desmopressin (1-deamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin, DDAVP): selective V2 agonist — antidiuretic action of AVP without pressor activity. Used since the 1980s; approved for nocturia in >80 countries.
  • US-approved low-dose formulations for NP with ≥2 voids per night: desmopressin acetate nasal spray (DANS) and orally disintegrating tablets (ODT). They offer greater bioavailability, faster onset and a better efficacy–side-effect balance, notably for hyponatraemia — the most clinically significant side effect.
  • Antidiuretic therapy is supported by level 1b evidence from multiple phase III placebo-controlled randomised controlled trials (RCTs) (Table 112.7).

Sodium Monitoring

  • Hyponatraemia risk is most strongly associated with older age, particularly with low baseline sodium, and is dose-dependent. It was seen mainly with earlier high-dose oral tablets in older patients (sodium <130 mmol/L in up to about 5%). Risk with DANS or ODT is lower.
  • Low-dose rationale: the median effective dose (therapeutic response in 50%) is far lower than the dose for maximal antidiuresis. Low doses still achieve 33–50% improvement in many patients. Supra-threshold plasma levels prolong antidiuresis, so low doses also avoid effects on fluid handling beyond the sleep period.
  • Regular sodium monitoring is required.

Diuretics

  • Population studies link diuretic use and nocturia. Furosemide, the commonest US diuretic, increases urine output for 6–8 h (without chronic kidney disease).
  • Taken in the hours before sleep, furosemide causes NP; taken >6 h before bed, it does not raise NUV. So the association is probably mostly confounding by volume-overload disease (diuretic use marks the comorbidity) rather than a direct drug effect.
  • Appropriately timed (morning or early-afternoon) diuretics are a primary treatment for NP in otherwise healthy adults — level 1b evidence. They work by restoring the circadian rhythm of natriuresis, shifting output into the day.

Key points: Additional Treatment Considerations
  • Desmopressin: the only US-approved nocturia drug — DANS/ODT for ≥2 voids with NP, lower hyponatraemia risk than old tablets.
  • Women more sensitive (sex-specific ODT); hyponatraemia is age- and dose-dependent, mostly within the first 2–3 weeks; strict sodium monitoring prevents most.
  • Afternoon diuretics: large effect in small studies.

Concept — Refractory nocturia is treated by moving urine production out of the night. Desmopressin suppresses night-time water diuresis; timed diuretics finish the sodium diuresis before bed. Either way the price is sodium balance, so dose, age and monitoring define safe use.

Antidiuretic and Diuretic Therapy: Key Trials and Physiologic Considerations

Desmopressin Acetate Nasal Spray

  • Approval rested on two phase III placebo RCTs (pooled): adults ≥50 years reporting ≥2 episodes and confirmed ≥2.16 nightly voids over 2-week screening; 1.66 or 0.83 mcg/0.1 mL (1.11 mcg in one study only) vs placebo, 12 weeks.
  • Co-primary endpoints: change in frequency; ≥50% responder rate. Both doses beat placebo on both (see Table 112.7). Sodium checked every 2 weeks.
  • Hyponatraemia (≤125 mmol/L, or <130 with symptoms): 0% at 0.83 mcg, 1.1% at 1.66 mcg, 0.2% placebo.

Desmopressin Orally Disintegrating Tablets

  • Women are more sensitive to DDAVP — greater effect on NUV and longer action.
    • Animal studies: greater renal V2 expression and response in females.
    • Human physiology: men show greater osmotic sensitivity of AVP release but lower renal sensitivity to AVP.
    • Possible basis: the V2 receptor gene is on the X chromosome (differential expression with X-inactivation); sex hormones may modulate.
  • Consequence: women do as well on a lower dose and are at risk of hyponatraemia at higher doses.

Development path: 4-week dose-ranging trial (10–100 mcg, both sexes) → post hoc: lower doses effective only in women → two 12-week sex-specific trials (25 mcg women; 50 or 75 mcg men) → Japanese sex-specific trials in NP refractory to behavioural counselling.

  • In the dose-ranging trial, most hyponatraemia occurred in adults ≥65 years on 100 mcg — above the US maximum dose.
  • In men, 75 mcg added little efficacy over 50 mcg but markedly more hyponatraemia — hence approval of 50 mcg.
  • Japanese trials: men improved at 25 and 50 mcg; women on 25 mcg did not reach significance on the primary endpoint. However, improvement continued in both sexes in a long-term extension, with the largest effect in women on open-label 25 mcg after 1 year.

Future Directions

  • Patient-centred diagnosis: user-friendly electronic bladder diaries, remote visits, comorbidity-driven questionnaires for multidisciplinary care.
  • Single-void nocturnal urine samples (volume, osmolality) collected at the first night void and analysed on standard laboratory equipment — a possible alternative way to diagnose NP.
  • Point-of-care biomarkers: urinary AVP in early-morning urine as a potential NP marker, especially with impaired circadian AVP rhythm; plasma copeptin predicts DDAVP response in children with NP-related enuresis — possibly relevant to adults.
  • Nocturnal urine composition is heterogeneous: patients differ in the balance of water and sodium diuresis — the basis for individualised treatment.
