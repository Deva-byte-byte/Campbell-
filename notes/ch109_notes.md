<h1>Chapter 109 — Neuromuscular Dysfunction of the Lower Urinary Tract</h1>

## Objectives

- **Neuropathic lower urinary tract (LUT) dysfunction is largely predictable** from the site and nature of the lesion plus the physiology of storage and emptying — usually, but not always.
- **Secondary factors** (below) explain why storage and voiding abnormalities **persist after the precipitating cause has resolved**.
- Each disorder should be described by five **urodynamic parameters** (International Continence Society terms):
    - **Sensation** — normal, absent, impaired.
    - **Detrusor activity** — normal, overactive, areflexic, impaired contractility.
    - **Compliance** — normal, decreased, increased.
    - **Smooth sphincter** — synergic, dyssynergic.
    - **Striated sphincter** — synergic, dyssynergic, bradykinetic, impaired voluntary control, fixed tone.

## Patterns of Neuropathic Voiding Dysfunction

- Discrete lesions affect filling/storage and emptying/voiding in a **consistent manner**, determined by: **(1) the area affected; (2) its function and contents; (3) whether the process is destructive, inflammatory or irritative.**
- **Acute dysfunction may differ from chronic.**

### Neuroplasticity

- **Plasticity** — inherent capacity for structural and functional modification (morphologic, neurochemical, electrical, organisational); **reversible up to a point, then fixed** — explains **persistent symptoms after the initial stimulus**.
- **Most obvious examples:** reorganisation of the micturition reflex **after complete cord transection above S2**, and peripheral reorganisation after **damage to the parasympathetic innervation**.
- **Bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) as a plastic process:**
    - Stretch from overdistension or **ischaemia** → molecular events → **↑ contractile protein synthesis and hypertrophy**; excitation–contraction proteins altered; **emptying initially preserved**.
    - Extracellular matrix: **↑ type III : type I collagen ratio**, type III relocates within bundles → **↓ compliance**; ischaemia (obstruction or atherosclerosis) promotes fibrosis.
    - **Partial denervation** of intrinsic innervation (pressure + ischaemia).
    - **Storage failure** = matrix change (↓ compliance) + **phasic DO** — myogenic (partial denervation) or neurogenic (**afferent plasticity mediated by nerve growth factor [NGF]**; abolished experimentally by anti-NGF immunisation).
    - **Emptying failure** = reversal of compensation or breakdown of calcium handling (uptake, storage, release).
    - Fetal-sheep obstruction model: shift in muscarinic, purinergic and nitrergic mechanisms, hypocontractility (possible **atropine resistance**), **loss of the urothelial nitric-oxide–mediated negative inotropic effect**, reduced compliance.
- **Persistence after relief of obstruction:** ice-water test (**C-fibre–mediated**) more often positive in BOO → **primitive reflex circuitry**; persistent **afferent neuroplasticity explains symptomatic failures after urodynamically successful de-obstruction**. Rat data: DO that develops in BOO may be adaptive and normalises (with gene expression) after de-obstruction.
- **Retrograde neurotrophic signalling** alters spinal cord protein expression after SCI and irritant cystitis — may explain persistence after the stimulus is removed (e.g. **interstitial cystitis**).

**Concept —** the lesion starts the dysfunction but plasticity maintains it: **afferent sensitisation and matrix remodelling outlive their cause**, so removing the trigger (obstruction, irritant) does not guarantee resolution.

## Diseases at or Above the Brainstem

### Cerebrovascular Disease

#### Cerebrovascular Accident (Stroke)

- **Epidemiology:** a leading cause of death and disability; **~795,000 per year in the USA, 15 million worldwide**; prevalence **~60/1000 at ≥65 years, 95/1000 at ≥75 years**; ~1 in 200 will have a CVA; **fifth leading cause of US death**, but **~75% survive**, often with urgency, frequency and incontinence.
- **Causes:** thrombosis, occlusion, haemorrhage → infarction, usually **around the internal capsule**. Elderly patients bring **pre-existing LUT pathology**; cognitive impairment, dysphasia, immobility and low motivation complicate management.
- **Urinary incontinence (UI):** **32–79% on admission → 25–28% at discharge → 12–19% months later**.
    - **UI persisting 7 days after stroke predicts poor survival and dependence**; UI on admission independently predicts 3-month death (confounded by infection and malnutrition).
    - Associated with **age ≥75, dysphagia, visual field defect, motor weakness**.
    - Lenticulocapsular strokes → **emotional incontinence**.
    - UI + faecal incontinence is the commonest pattern entering rehabilitation (~⅓).
    - **Commonest UI type = impaired-awareness incontinence** (insensate/delayed awareness) > pure urgency UI (UUI); **impaired awareness carries the worse prognosis**.
- **Acute phase:** **retention from detrusor areflexia ("cerebral shock")**, mechanism unclear → fixed deficit emerges over weeks–months.
- **Chronic pattern:** **phasic DO** with **intact sensation** (urgency, frequency) and **synergic smooth and striated sphincters**; if the patient can voluntarily contract the striated sphincter → urgency/frequency only; if not → **UUI**.
    - Lesions **confined to basal ganglia or thalamus → normal voluntary sphincter control**; lesions of **cortex and/or internal capsule → cannot forcefully contract the sphincter**. "**Uninhibited sphincter relaxation**" is a **misnomer** — the defect is in corticospinal voluntary control.
    - Hemispheric side (dominant, non-dominant, bilateral) does **not** change urodynamic findings.
    - Two UI mechanisms: **impaired striated sphincter control** and **lack of appreciation of filling/impending contraction**.
- **True detrusor–striated sphincter dyssynergia (DSD) does not occur**; reported "dyssynergia" is **pseudodyssynergia** — electromyographic (EMG) flare from voluntary attempts to inhibit an involuntary contraction. **Guarding reflex intact.**
- **Persistent areflexia is rare** (estimates up to 20%); post-stroke retention predicts poor function and recovery.
- **Men with pre-stroke LUTS:** poor flow/high post-void residual (PVR) usually = prostatic obstruction, but **full urodynamics before outlet surgery** to exclude **DO with impaired contractility**.
- **Drug trap:** antimuscarinics crossing the blood–brain barrier (**M1 blockade**) worsen confusion.
- **Symptom burden 1 month post-stroke:** ≥1 LUTS in most; **nocturia commonest**, then urgency, frequency; **urgency most bothersome**; bother tracks lower-limb paresis.
- **Mechanism of DO:** loss of cortical control of the **pontine micturition centre (PMC)** ± facilitated excitation; glutamatergic, dopaminergic and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic changes; ↑ cytokines and neuronal nitric oxide synthase in cord and bladder (middle cerebral artery occlusion models).

#### Brainstem Stroke

- Brainstem = midbrain, pons, medulla; **PMC coordinates micturition**.
- **Midbrain lesions — no LUTS; medullary and especially pontine lesions — LUTS** (nocturnal frequency, voiding difficulty, retention, UI).
- **Pontine infarct → low capacity (and, in one series, detrusor underactivity [DUA]); medullary infarct → emptying disorder.** Patterns inconsistent → **urodynamics prudent**.

### Dementia

- Atrophy of grey and white matter, **especially frontal lobes**; causes include vascular, **Alzheimer (principal cause in the elderly)**, Pick, Creutzfeldt–Jakob, syphilis, traumatic brain injury (TBI), encephalitis. Prevalence **~6% at ≥65**.
- LUTS not constant, but when present = **UI** — from DO or a **normal but inappropriately timed reflex**: patient may retain sphincter control yet void anywhere because **impaired mentation removes the reason not to**.
- UI **3× more common with dementia**; prevalence estimates **30–100%**, confounded by ageing and under-reporting; higher in Lewy body, Pick and normal-pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) than Alzheimer.
- **Anticholinergics** worsen cognition, delirium and falls; Alzheimer involves **cortical cholinergic loss**. Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction (**SUFU**) white paper: anticholinergic use for overactive bladder (OAB) carries **10–65% increased risk of new-onset dementia**, possibly dose-dependent → **β3 agonist strongly preferred**.

### Traumatic Brain Injury

- **Most common severe neurologic impairment from trauma**; 27–69 million/year globally.
- **Initial areflexia** possible. **Above the PMC → DO with coordinated sphincters; isolated brainstem injury below the PMC → DSD may be added.**
- Early UI common; retention (PVR ≥100 mL) less so. **UI/storage symptoms → bilateral lesions, poor function; retention → diabetes or faecal impaction.** Many remain incontinent after rehabilitation; **concomitant spinal injury** complicates management.

### Brain Tumour

- Dysfunction depends on **location, not histology**; **superior frontal lobe** most implicated.
- **DO + UI**; patients may be **unaware of all LUT events** → cannot attempt suppression. Sphincters synergic; pseudodyssynergia possible.
- Improvement after resection suggests tumour-related overactivity may be a **positive (activating)** rather than a release phenomenon. **Frontal space-occupying lesions can cause retention** without other deficits. **Posterior fossa tumours: voiding dysfunction in 32–70%.**

### Cerebellar Ataxia

- Cerebellar degeneration ± brainstem, cord, dorsal roots: incoordination, ↓ deep tendon reflexes (DTRs), dysarthria, dysmetria, choreiform movements.
- **UI with DO and synergic sphincters**; retention/high PVR from **areflexia** or **DSD (cord involvement)**.
- **Stress UI (SUI) without DO** in some spinocerebellar degeneration → attributed to **Onuf nucleus → pudendal** involvement.

### Normal-Pressure Hydrocephalus

- **Classic triad: dementia, gait ataxia, UI**; normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure, dilated ventricles; idiopathic or after CVA.
- **UI in 98–100%**, from **DO with synergic sphincters**.
- **Ventriculoperitoneal shunt** is the mainstay and improves urgency and UUI.

### Cerebral Palsy

- **Non-progressive brain injury** in the first year (up to 3 years); infection or hypoxia; delayed motor development, altered tone, exaggerated reflexes.
- **Most have urinary control and normal storage/emptying.** Systematic review: **~55% report ≥1 LUTS, ~46% UI**; risk ↑ with **spastic subtype, moderate–severe functional impairment, severe cognitive impairment**.
- Pattern = **suprapontine: DO with coordinated sphincters**; **DSD** in a minority (spinal damage). Voiding difficulty may reflect **failure of pelvic floor relaxation rather than true DSD**; **retention appears in adults** — difficulty may progress with age.
- **Adults with CP and LUTS: poor compliance and detrusor leak point pressure (DLPP) ≥40 cm H₂O in about half → full evaluation** (renal risk, worse with pelvic floor spasticity).
- Management: **conservative (education, pads, perineal hygiene) succeeds in ~¾**; some need intermittent catheterisation or reconstruction. **Cognitive impairment is not a barrier** to continence, but limits options needing cooperation.

### Parkinson Disease

- **Pathology:** loss of dopaminergic neurons of the **substantia nigra pars compacta** (nigrostriatal tract to caudate and putamen) → **tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia**. Other contributors: striatum, thalamus, periaqueductal grey (PAG), PMC, ventral tegmental area.
- **Diagnosis:** **gold standard = neuropathology** — **Lewy neurites** and **Lewy bodies** (intracytoplasmic eosinophilic hyaline inclusions; not PD-specific). **Asymmetry + resting tremor + levodopa response** best separate PD from other parkinsonism (none individually specific).
- **Epidemiology:** both sexes equally; **0.3% of population, 3% at ≥65**; **second commonest neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer**.
- **Other parkinsonism:** MSA, progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal degeneration, vascular parkinsonism, Lewy body dementia; **~5% of "PD" = Parkinson-plus** (early dementia/falls, symmetry, wide-based gait, autonomic failure; worse prognosis).
- **LUT dysfunction in 35–70%**; average **~5 years after onset**, but LUTS **at onset in up to half** — associated with **worse progression**, depression and poorer quality of life (QoL); **frequency and nocturia** most damaging.
- **Mechanism:** basal ganglia normally **inhibit** the micturition reflex; nigral cell loss removes it. Proposed: **loss of inhibitory D1 receptors allows facilitatory D2 action**; central D2 stimulation ↓ capacity and worsens DO. Early **delayed perception of filling** (visceral sensory deficit).
- **Symptoms:** **storage (urgency, frequency, nocturia, UUI) in 50–75%**; remainder obstructive or mixed.
- **Urodynamics:** **DO commonest**; smooth sphincter synergic; sporadic striated activity during involuntary contraction (in many) is **not obstructive and not true DSD**. **Pseudodyssynergia** and **sphincter bradykinesia** (delayed relaxation) mimic DSD. Impaired contractility possible; **areflexia uncommon — suggests MSA**. Positron emission tomography (PET): DO activates cerebellum most, with no pontine change.
- **Outlet surgery:** older "PD" cases with post-TURP incontinence were **MSA**. **TURP is not contraindicated in PD** (striated acontractility extremely rare) — but **confirm PD, exclude MSA, and do (video)urodynamics**; poorly sustained contractions ± slow sphincter relaxation lower expectations.
- **PD drugs:** levodopa, anticholinergics and dopamine agonists show no consistent LUTS effect; **bromocriptine worsens urgency and DO but improves emptying**.
- **Treatment:**
    - **Oral OAB drugs effective** (meta-analysis of randomised trials: ↓ nocturia, frequency, incontinence, ↑ QoL, no excess serious events). **Preferred (limited central nervous system [CNS] effect): mirabegron, extended-release fesoterodine, trospium, darifenacin** (SUFU).
    - **Botulinum toxin** effective (up to 9 months); **posterior tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS)** and **sacral neuromodulation (SNM)** effective (most proceed to implant; many stop oral drugs).
    - **Subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (DBS)** improves bladder sensation and capacity by **modulating afferent processing** (anterior cingulate and frontal cortex); benefit on **nocturia inconsistent** between studies.
    - **Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation** — capacity and first sensation improve for **~2 weeks**.
    - Limits: mobility (toilet access), **hand function for clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC)**.

### Multiple System Atrophy

- **Glial α-synucleinopathy**, progressive, unknown cause: parkinsonism, cerebellar, **autonomic (urinary and erectile)** and pyramidal features. **MSA-P** (parkinsonian) and **MSA-C** (cerebellar) replace **striatonigral degeneration, olivopontocerebellar atrophy and Shy–Drager syndrome** (orthostatic hypotension, anhidrosis; possibly late-stage MSA).
- **Diffuse cell loss** (cerebellum, substantia nigra, globus pallidus, caudate, putamen, inferior olives, **intermediolateral columns**, **Onuf nucleus**) explains **earlier, more severe LUTS and erectile dysfunction (ED)** than PD. Sexes equal; **middle-age onset; poor prognosis**.
- **Early:** urgency, frequency, UUI **up to 4 years before diagnosis**; DO, **↓ compliance** (distal cord autonomic neurons). **Late:** difficulty initiating/maintaining voiding (pontine and sacral lesions) — poor prognosis.
- **Outlet:** **open bladder neck** on cystourethrography and **striated sphincter denervation** on motor-unit EMG → sphincteric UI in women; **prostatectomy hazardous** (most MSA men worsen after TURP; women incontinent after pelvic floor repair).
- **Treatment difficult:** treating DO worsens emptying; treating sphincteric UI worsens emptying; **outlet reduction rarely indicated**. Goal = **facilitate storage + CIC** (often impossible late). **Desmopressin helps predominant nocturia**; antimuscarinics usually disappoint.

### Progressive Supranuclear Palsy

- Parkinsonism + **vertical gaze palsy**, falls, cognitive impairment. LUTS: frequency, urgency, UI.
- **Impaired detrusor contraction very common, DO common**; sphincter abnormalities reflect **Onuf nucleus neuronal loss**.

**Concept —** above the pons the **reflex is intact but disinhibited**, so the expected pattern is **DO with synergic sphincters**; true DSD needs a lesion **below** the PMC, and any open bladder neck or sphincter denervation points to a **multisystem (autonomic) process such as MSA**.

## Diseases Primarily Involving the Spinal Cord

### Multiple Sclerosis

- **Epidemiology:** adults **20–50** (onset commonly 30–38), **female predominance (≥2:1; ~75% female)**; **2.9 million worldwide**; a leading cause of neurogenic LUT dysfunction (NLUTD). Optic, pyramidal, ataxic, bladder, bowel and sexual symptoms; **progressive forms are predominantly spinal**.
- **Pathology:** immune-mediated **demyelination with axonal sparing** → impaired saltatory conduction; **plaques** in white matter; commonest in **lateral corticospinal and reticulospinal columns of the cervical cord** (cervical almost always; lumbar ~40%, sacral ~18%). Cortical/midbrain lesions → intellectual change/euphoria.
- **Functional imaging:** neurogenic DO (NDO) activates frontal executive areas (inhibitory signal); DSD adds emotional, motor and brainstem activation. Mild-obstruction/preserved-contractility patients have **denser calcitonin gene–related peptide (CGRP) and substance P innervation**.
- **Prevalence:** LUTS in **50–90%**, **UI 37–72%**, retention 2–52%; LUT dysfunction **correlates with disability**; **isolated presenting complaint in up to 15%** (acute retention or acute urgency/frequency); up to 80% affected at some point.
- **Urodynamics:** **DO commonest (34–99%)**; **DSD with DO in 30–65%**; **impaired contractility/areflexia 12–38%** (complicates treatment); **~10% normal**; smooth sphincter synergic; sphincteric flaccidity <15%; pelvic floor weak; **pelvic floor spasticity only with DSD**. Multiple lesions limit diagnosis from urodynamics alone.

- **Sensation usually intact → distinguish pseudodyssynergia from true DSD.** Brief DSD in a woman may be harmless; **sustained DSD with prolonged high pressure causes complications**; contraction amplitude relates to upper tract deterioration.
- **Risk factors for complications:** **(1) DSD in men; (2) high detrusor filling pressure; (3) indwelling catheter.**
- **Upper tracts:** international committee — progressive MS **rarely damages the upper tracts**, unlike SCI; others find disability predicts poor compliance and vesicoureteral reflux (VUR); **VUR rare (~5%), linked to progressive MS**. **No factor predicts upper tract change**; symptoms do not track disease → **baseline urodynamics essential**.
- **Management:**
    - **Aggressive, anticipatory medical management** — surgery needed in <10%, none developed hydronephrosis (regimens: antimuscarinic + CIC commonest; drugs alone; CIC alone; behavioural).
    - **Avoid irreversible therapy** — urodynamic pattern and compliance change over time; surgery declining.
    - **Individualised bladder rehabilitation** (re-education, pelvic floor, emptying techniques, bowel programme) improves QoL in a randomised trial.
    - **Early MS: antimuscarinics + CIC are the cornerstone.** Advanced MS (Expanded Disability Status Scale [EDSS] ≥7): no specific guidelines.
    - **Credé/Valsalva contraindicated, especially with DSD.**
    - **Indwelling catheter only after all else fails; if used, suprapubic** (~30% use indwelling).
    - **OnabotulinumtoxinA:** durable, safe; majority achieve ≥50% UI reduction (MS and SCI).
    - **SNM** effective in small series; **MRI-compatible devices** have widened eligibility.

**Concept —** MS is **multifocal and relapsing**, so the pattern (DO, DSD, areflexia) is unpredictable and changes; hence **baseline urodynamics, reversible therapy and repeated reassessment**, and a lower upper-tract risk than SCI.

### Spinal Cord Injury

#### Epidemiology, Morbidity, General Concepts

- Causes: violence, fracture/dislocation (motor vehicle, diving, falls), vascular injury or surgery, infection, disc prolapse, hyperextension.
- Risks: **UTI, sepsis, upper and lower tract deterioration and calculi, autonomic dysreflexia**, skin breakdown, psychological morbidity.
- **Anatomy:** column (vertebral) and cord levels diverge — **always state which**. **Sacral cord begins at column T12–L1; cord ends (conus) → cauda equina at ~L2.** Injury may extend cephalad/caudad; multiple levels occur. Complete anatomic transection is rare.
- **US data:** **~54 per million (~17,000 new cases)/year**; ~302,000 prevalent. Mechanisms: **motor vehicle > falls > violence > sport**; **80% male**; mean age at injury risen from **29 (1970s) to 42**. **Incomplete tetraplegia largest group**; **<1% full recovery by discharge**. Most injuries at/above T12 column; cervical → tetraplegia, thoracic/lumbar/sacral → paraplegia.
- **Long-term:** UI persists in many; **paraplegics leak more often than tetraplegics** (catheter dependence); CIC associated with more UI than other methods.
- **Mortality shift:** renal failure was the leading cause (pre-World War II and earlier data); now **pneumonia and septicaemia** — reflects better bladder care.
- **Stones:** ~7% within 10 years, **greatest risk in first 3 months**; **98% apatite or struvite**; two peaks — **acute (immobilisation hypercalciuria)** and **chronic (catheter-related, mainly lower tract)**.
- **Experimental:** contusion level alters retention (T1 lesions spare a descending pathway); crush worse than contusion; **KATP channels regulate spontaneous activity in neurogenic muscle, BKCa in normal**.
- Stem-cell repair, reinnervation and sexual/reproductive function are outside this chapter.

#### Spinal Shock

- **Decreased excitability of cord segments at and below the lesion**: absent somatic reflexes, flaccid paralysis; autonomic activity also suppressed → **acontractile, areflexic bladder**, smooth contour, no trabeculation.
- **Sacral reflexes (anal, bulbocavernosus) may never disappear, or return within minutes–hours**; function above the lesion may also be depressed. Pathophysiology poorly understood (grey matter trauma, protein accumulation, reperfusion).
- **Outlet:** **bladder neck closed and competent** (unless prior surgery or thoracolumbar sympathetic injury); some striated EMG activity; **maximum urethral closure pressure (MUCP) reduced but maintained**; **guarding reflex absent, no voluntary control** → **UI only with overflow**.
- **Management:** **retention is the rule — catheterise; keep storage pressure low. CIC preferred**, though small-bore urethral or suprapubic catheters give similar outcomes at this stage.
- **Recovery:** if the distal cord is intact, **reflex contractions return** — weak and poorly sustained at first, then stronger → **involuntary voiding between catheterisations, alongside return of lower-limb DTRs**.
- **Duration: usually 6–12 weeks in complete suprasacral lesions; up to 1–2 years; shorter if incomplete.**

#### Suprasacral Spinal Cord Injury

- **Mechanisms of reflex recovery (C-fibre–evoked):** loss of bulbospinal inhibition; synaptic strengthening/sprouting; transmitter changes; **afferent sprouting**. Animal findings: **C-fibre sensitisation (NGF)**, dorsal root ganglion enlargement, **sodium-channel shift from high-threshold tetrodotoxin-resistant to low-threshold tetrodotoxin-sensitive**; ↑ glutamate/glycine/taurine; urothelial barrier disruption; **prejunctional M1 → M3 shift**; ↑ urothelial ATP release (nicotinic/purinergic, atropine-resistant); ↑ spinal NGF; myosin heavy-chain changes; **TRPA1** modulation attenuates overactivity.
- **Characteristic pattern: DO + smooth sphincter synergia (if below sympathetic outflow) + striated DSD → incomplete emptying.** Examination: spasticity, hyperreflexia, abnormal plantars, impaired sensation. **Guarding reflex absent/weak if complete, preserved but variable if incomplete.**
- **DSD timing:** external sphincter contraction **starts before the detrusor contraction** in most (Delay A ~2 s; Delay B ~7.6 s to +10 cm H₂O) — associated with complete injury and continuous DSD.
- **DSD → functional obstruction, poor emptying, high pressures.** Poorly sustained contraction may occur near the **conus** (occult second lesion, pelvic-floor inhibitory reflex, loss of higher facilitation). **Reflex voiding can be triggered** (suprapubic tapping).
- **Severity of DSD consequences:** **complete > incomplete; continuous > intermittent; male > female.**
- **DSD type vs lesion:** **incomplete → type 1; complete → types 2 and 3**; no relation to **level**; type evolves in about a quarter, stable in ~⅔.
- **Management logic:** it is both storage and emptying failure. **If pressures are (or can be made) low → treat as emptying failure with CIC.**
    - **Antimuscarinics, β3 agonists or both** (preponderance of evidence); **intradetrusor botulinum toxin** (randomised trials) if refractory or lifelong drugs unsuitable.
    - **Sphincterotomy or intrasphincteric onabotulinumtoxinA** (men) lowers DLPP and converts to **storage failure** managed with a condom collector — **only for those unwilling/unable to do CIC**; counsel on failure, repeat procedures, monitoring, skin breakdown.
    - **Anterior sacral root stimulation with deafferentation** established; ventral root rerouting and stem cells investigational.

**Key Points: Management of Patients With Suprasacral Spinal Cord Injury**

- **Pattern: DO, smooth sphincter synergia (below sympathetic outflow), striated sphincter dyssynergia.**
- **Initial and periodic evaluation** to find and correct: **high-pressure storage, high DLPP, VUR, stones (upper and lower), infection — especially with reflux.**
- **CIC for emptying failure; oral drugs (anticholinergic, β3 agonist) or chemodenervation for storage failure.**

#### Sacral Spinal Cord Injury

- After shock: ↓ DTRs, flaccid paralysis, absent sensation. **Detrusor areflexia with normal or high compliance initially**; **↓ compliance may develop** (decentralisation-induced plasticity).
- **Outlet:** **competent but non-relaxing smooth sphincter**; **striated sphincter with fixed tone, no voluntary control**; closure pressures ↓. **Bladder neck may later open.**
- **Straining/Credé → "obstruction"** at a closed bladder neck or fixed distal sphincter. Fig. 109.4: **low compliance, incompetent bladder neck, distal sphincter fails to open on straining** — typical of sacral cord or efferent root injury.
- **Management: CIC for emptying + keep storage pressure acceptable (oral drugs or botulinum toxin).**

**Key Points: Management of Patients With Sacral Spinal Cord Injury**

- Same risk factors, with emphasis on **storage pressure — silent upper tract deterioration can occur without VUR.**
- **Low-pressure storage + CIC**; pharmacologic or electrical stimulation may aid emptying.

<p class="tcap">Comparison — Suprasacral vs Sacral SCI (after spinal shock)</p>

| | Suprasacral (complete) | Sacral (complete) |
|---|---|---|
| Detrusor | **Overactive** | **Areflexic** |
| Compliance | Normal initially | Normal/high → **may fall** |
| Smooth sphincter | Synergic (dyssynergic if above T7–T8) | Competent non-relaxing → may open |
| Striated sphincter | **Dyssynergic** | **Fixed tone**, no voluntary control |
| Somatic signs | Spasticity, hyperreflexia | Flaccidity, ↓ DTRs |

#### Neurologic and Urodynamic Correlation

- **Correlation is general, not exact:** somatic completeness ≠ autonomic completeness; multiple occult levels.
- Incomplete SCI: bladders "not at risk" deteriorate and vice versa; **management changes are often needed without neurologic change**.
- **Classic correlation (550 patients):** suprasacral lesions → normal voiding, DSD, or — paradoxically — **areflexia** (occult distal lesion or disordered sacral integration). **DSD occurred only with suprasacral cord lesions, never with supraspinal lesions** → **(1) coordination is supraspinal; (2) DSD implies a lesion between the pontine–mesencephalic reticular formation and the sacral cord.** Suprapontine patients voided synergically; many with DO kept voluntary sphincter control (**separate pathways for bladder and sphincter**); voluntary sphincter contraction did **not** abolish detrusor contraction (complex, not a simple sacral reflex). Mixed patterns reflect **separate parasympathetic and pudendal nuclei**.
- **Thoracolumbar (column T10–L2) lesions least predictable** — roughly half areflexic, half DO.
- **With precise imaging (CT/MRI):** suprasacral → **DO and/or DSD in 95%**, low compliance (<12.5 mL/cm H₂O) and DLPP ≥40 cm H₂O in ~40%; sacral → **areflexia, low compliance and high leak point pressure in most**; combined lesions intermediate.
- **Upper tract risk:** detrusor **fibrosis** severity; **leak point pressure ≥75 cm H₂O**; **DO for more than one-third of filling** → renal damage → **treat NDO aggressively**.
- **Repeat urodynamics** vary in capacity/pressures but reliably detect DO — one study may not suffice.
- **Bottom line: manage by urodynamics, not neurology; do not infer neurology from urodynamics** (complete-lesion data largely valid). Regular urodynamic follow-up.

#### Autonomic Dysreflexia

- **First described by Guttmann and Whitteridge, 1947.** **Potentially fatal emergency unique to SCI above cord T6–T8 (sympathetic outflow)**; **cervical (60%) > thoracic (20%)**; onset usually after spinal shock, possibly years later; **requires viable distal cord**.
- **Features:** exaggerated sympathetic response to stimuli **below** the lesion — **pounding headache, hypertension, flushing and sweating above the lesion; bradycardia typical** (tachycardia/arrhythmia possible). Range: mild pre-void headache → **cerebral haemorrhage, seizure**.
- **Triggers:** **bladder or rectal distension** (instrumentation, tube change, blocked catheter, clot); calculi, gut pathology, long-bone fracture, sex, electrocoagulation, pressure sores, sport. **Resolves quickly when the stimulus is removed.**
- **Urodynamic risk factors:** complete injury, cervical level, DSD, poor compliance, ≥2 years since injury. **DSD invariable; smooth sphincter dyssynergia usual in men.**
- **Mechanism:** afferent nociceptive input → spinal reflex sympathetic outflow (arteriolar, pilomotor, visceral spasm, sweating) **without descending medullary inhibition**; loss of bulbospinal control of preganglionic neurons; large vascular bed (probably **splanchnic**) involved; sympathetic plasticity.
- **During urodynamics** (defined as systolic rise ≥20 mm Hg): common at ≥T6, **but also occurs below T6**; associated with continuous DSD and poor compliance; **bradycardia in a minority** → **monitor all SCI patients**.
- **Prevention:** endoscopy under **spinal or carefully monitored general anaesthesia**; **urodynamics with continuous haemodynamic monitoring**.
- **Treatment sequence: remove stimulus (drain bladder) → drug therapy if persistent → neuroablation if intractable.**

#### Vesicoureteral Reflux

- **17–25% of SCI**, more in suprasacral; drivers = **high intravesical pressure (filling and voiding) and infection**. Persistent VUR → chronic renal damage (common in SCI deaths from renal disease); **high pressures damage kidneys even without VUR**.
- **Chronic urethral catheterisation is worst for the upper tracts** (more hydronephrosis than CIC or spontaneous voiding).
- **First treat the bladder, not the reflux: lower storage pressure and outlet resistance** — drugs, urethral dilatation (myelomeningocele), neuromodulation, deafferentation, augmentation, sphincterotomy.
- Failing that, anti-reflux surgery in a thick bladder is hard; **transureteroureterostomy** complicated by stones, recurrent VUR, obstruction; **submucosal bulking** adds an option.
- **Destroyed urethra** (long-term catheter): **bladder neck closure + suprapubic catheter**; diversion (including detubularised segments) remains common.
- **Urodynamic artefact: VUR makes capacity look larger, pressures lower and DO less significant.**

#### Spinal Cord Injury in Females

- ↓ protein and bone mass, ↑ fat → skin breakdown, fractures, osteoporosis; **menopausal flushes mimic autonomic dysreflexia**; UI and UTI worsen with age.
- **Paraplegic women usually master CIC; lack of an external collecting device is the core problem.** Most **tetraplegic women need indwelling catheters** (leakage around catheter, upper tract change).
- **Complications: CIC ≪ reflex voiding with pads < indwelling catheter.** Poor-recovery cervical SCI in women with permanent catheters = frequent **bladder stones, bypass leakage, recurrent infection** → viewed as **management failures**; good recovery → most void by triggering.
- **SUI surgery in selected women:** mid-urethral or **autologous slings** reasonable; bulking less invasive but weak data.

### Cervical Myelopathy

- Compression from **spondylosis, ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, disc herniation**.
- Storage and obstructive symptoms; **DO common, DSD in some — yet underactive detrusor in others**, unexpected for cervical disease → **urodynamics guide therapy**.

### Acute Transverse Myelitis

- **Rapid motor, sensory and sphincter loss with a defined upper sensory level, no compression**; parainfectious, autoimmune, vascular or demyelinating; **stabilises in 2–4 weeks**, non-progressive, variable recovery.
- **Retention at presentation almost universal**; acute phase often areflexic; after shock **DO predominates, DSD common**; persistent retention or storage symptoms common at 6 months; **severity does not predict urodynamics**.
- Long-term: many need antimuscarinics and **intradetrusor toxin**, a few augmentation; some develop hydronephrosis → **urodynamics before irreversible therapy; manage as SCI.**

### Neurospinal Dysraphism

- **Definitions:** **spina bifida occulta** = bony arch defect only; **spina bifida cystica (aperta)** = bony + neural tube defect: **myelomeningocele** (neural elements herniated; **~90% of cystica**, most devastating) vs **meningocele** (meningeal sac only); prefix **lipo-** if fat present.
- **Level:** cervical 2%, thoracic 5%, lumbar 26%, **lumbosacral 47%**, sacral 20% — **level correlates poorly with urodynamics**.
- **Incidence ~1/1000 live births; LUT dysfunction ~90%**; falling with **folate** and prenatal diagnosis; **up to 85% reach adulthood** → transitional care.
- **"Typical" adult myelodysplastic bladder: areflexic with an open bladder neck**; fills until **fixed external sphincter pressure** is reached, then leaks; SUI with abdominal pressure. **DSD in 10–15%** — these have a **functional bladder neck** and can be continent if the reflex is controlled. Continence often **improves after puberty**, but tolerance of leakage falls.
- **Neurologic examination fails most obviously here:** mix of DO/areflexia, **low compliance common**, DSD, low urethral pressure, **striated denervation in the majority**.
- **Main goal: avoid high storage pressure and upper tract deterioration.** Poor compliance, high voiding pressures and DO predict renal decline; childhood DO predicts later bilateral renal dysfunction.
- **SUI:** raise outlet efficiency **without raising closure pressure enough to reduce compliance** — **periurethral bulking safer than pubovaginal sling or artificial urinary sphincter**; men: bulking ± "prostatic sling"; continent patients stay on CIC.
- **Management:** medical + **intravesical onabotulinumtoxinA**, then **augmentation or diversion** on failure; surgery **not necessarily superior** (higher UI in surgical cohort — confounded by severity). Augmentation patients younger and more often male; conduit patients older, female, costlier.
- **Xiao procedure** (L5 → S3 ventral root anastomosis): with detethering ↑ capacity and ↓ overactivity, **no continence gain** (small randomised trial).
- **Tethered cord syndrome (TCS):** **stretch-induced dysfunction of a caudally anchored cord** (scar, thick filum, bony septum, tumour); **3–15% of myelomeningocele**. Adults: prior dysraphism with late progression, or new subtle symptoms; **children present after growth spurts, adults after spinal stretch (sport, collision)**. Symptoms: back pain, leg weakness, foot deformity, scoliosis, sensory loss, bowel/bladder dysfunction. **No typical pattern — urodynamics**; DO most frequent. **Release improves urodynamics in a minority; urinary recovery lags (~4 months) behind pain (~1) and motor (~2)**; most improvement within 6–12 months.
- **Transition to adult care:** multidisciplinary; goals = **low detrusor pressure, preserved compliance, social continence**; **lifelong follow-up mandatory**.

### Tabes Dorsalis, Pernicious Anaemia

- **Tabes (syphilitic myelopathy):** dorsal columns and posterior sacral roots → **loss of bladder sensation, large PVR = classic "sensory neurogenic bladder"**; some show low compliance or DO.
- **Pernicious anaemia:** vitamin B12 malabsorption (no intrinsic factor) → **subacute combined degeneration** → same sensory pattern.

### Subacute Combined Degeneration

- **Demyelination of posterior and lateral columns**; **B12 deficiency** (diet, malabsorption, gastrectomy, fish tapeworm, autoimmune) or **copper deficiency**.
- Mixed storage and voiding LUTS; areflexia, NDO or normal; **B12 replacement reverses dysfunction** in many.

### Poliomyelitis

- When present: **"motor neurogenic bladder" — retention, areflexia, intact sensation**; LUT dysfunction reported in 4–42%; survivors have more hesitancy, weak stream, incomplete emptying, nocturia, UUI.

<p class="tcap">Comparison — Sensory vs Motor Neurogenic Bladder</p>

| | Sensory | Motor |
|---|---|---|
| Lesion | Dorsal columns / posterior roots | Anterior horn / motor efferents |
| Examples | **Tabes, pernicious anaemia/SACD, diabetes** | **Poliomyelitis** |
| Sensation | **Lost** | **Intact** |
| Detrusor | Decompensates from overdistension | Areflexic |
| Capacity | Large | Retention |

**Concept —** in the cord, **level decides pattern** — suprasacral lesions give **high-pressure DO with DSD**, sacral lesions **areflexia with fixed outlet** — but **completeness, multiple levels and plasticity** break the rule so often that **urodynamics, not the neurologic level, directs management**, and the enemy throughout is **sustained high storage pressure**.

## Disease Distal to the Spinal Cord

### Disc Disease

- **Anatomy:** sacral cord segments lie at **L1–L2 vertebral bodies (conus)**; sacral roots descend behind L2–L5 as the **cauda equina** to exit at sacral foramina — so **any lumbar prolapse can compress sacral roots**.
- **Most prolapse is posterolateral (spares the cauda equina); central prolapse in 1–15% → compression.** Commonest levels **L4–L5, L5–S1**.
- **Signs:** low back pain radiating in a girdle; **perineal/perianal sensory loss (S2–S4)**, **lateral foot sensory loss (S1–S2)**.
- **Incidence 27–68%** (biased series). **Commonest finding: normally compliant areflexic bladder** with normal or partially denervated pelvic floor; patients **strain to void** or present in retention; **DO occasionally** from root irritation.
- **Compliance usually preserved (unlike myelomeningocele)** — because disc injury is **more incomplete for preganglionic parasympathetic fibres** and **more sensory than motor**; low compliance in myelomeningocele is attributed to **preganglionic parasympathetic motor injury**.
- **Laminectomy may not restore function**; some develop new DO or areflexia after surgery → **pre-operative urodynamics** (clinical and medicolegal).
- **Cauda equina syndrome:** **perineal sensory loss + loss of voluntary anal and urethral sphincter control + loss of sexual response**; **1–5% of prolapsed lumbar discs**; initially **acontractile detrusor, no sensation**, sphincter EMG often silent. **Somatic (sphincter EMG) recovers before autonomic (detrusor).**
- **Urgent decompression — within 48 hours** gives significantly better urinary and rectal recovery than later surgery (meta-analysis); most regain continence, but recovery is variable.

### Spinal Stenosis

- Narrowing of canal, root canals or foramina (congenital, developmental, acquired), **may occur without disc prolapse**; compression → damage, **ischaemia**, oedema.
- **Lumbar stenosis:** exercise-related back/leg pain, cramping, paraesthesia **relieved by rest** (sacral root ischaemia). Spectrum from cervical cord compression to cauda equina syndrome.
- **Cervical spondylotic stenosis:** **DO if inhibitory reticulospinal tracts compressed; underactivity if posterior funiculus (proprioception) involved**.
- No consistent pattern → **urodynamics are the cornerstone**; **decompression improves bladder symptoms in about half** and improves PVR, capacity and flow after successful surgery.

### Radical Pelvic Surgery

- **Pelvic (inferior hypogastric) plexus** lies beside the rectum (and vagina); injury commonest after **abdominoperineal resection (APR) and radical hysterectomy**.

| Procedure | Historical incidence of LUT dysfunction |
|---|---|
| **APR** | **20–68%** |
| **Radical hysterectomy** | **16–80%** |
| Anterior resection | 20–25% |
| Proctocolectomy | 10–20% |

- **Current rates lower with nerve-sparing**; **permanent in 15–20%** of those affected. Rectal cancer treatment: ~⅓ have LUT dysfunction; **APR has greatest impact**; chemoradiotherapy compounds it; sexual dysfunction common.
- **Mechanisms:** denervation/decentralisation, nerve tethering or scar, direct trauma, devascularisation.
- **Decentralisation plasticity:** parasympathetic decentralisation → **adrenergic hyperinnervation**, converting sympathetic **β (relaxant) bladder response to α (contractile)**; not seen with pelvic plexus neurectomy alone or with added hypogastric neurectomy; new cholinergic inputs from hypogastric nerves; **urethral α-adrenergic supersensitivity**.
- **Permanent pattern:** **impaired/absent contractility + fixed residual striated tone (not voluntarily relaxed) + open, non-functional smooth sphincter + ↓ compliance** → **storage and emptying failure**: leak across the distal sphincter with abdominal pressure (**obvious in women; prostate masks it in men**), or retention. **Positive bethanechol supersensitivity test.**
- **Therapeutic goal: low-pressure storage + periodic emptying.**
- **Avoid prostatectomy unless BOO is proven at the prostate** — otherwise it only weakens the sphincter.
- **Early post-operative period: CIC and wait — most dysfunction is transient; 6–12 months may pass before detrusor recovery.** Decreased compliance is more frequent after radical pelvic surgery than after conus/cauda equina injury.

### Simple and Radical Hysterectomy

- **Simple hysterectomy:** no consensus on neurogenic damage; total vs subtotal — **less UI after total**, both ↑ incomplete emptying (double voiding); vaginal route associated with more micturition and defecation symptoms than abdominal.
- **Radical hysterectomy:** potentially more debilitating, though one survey found no excess long-term dysfunction; **nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy reduces long-term urinary symptoms without oncological compromise (randomised trial)**; **minimally invasive approaches** lower long-term bladder dysfunction.

### Childbirth

- **No prospective long-term data; no trial shows caesarean section protects against UI.**
- **EPINCONT** (Epidemiology of Incontinence in the County of Nord-Trøndelag): **any UI higher after caesarean than nulliparity; moderate–severe UI lower after caesarean than vaginal delivery**; **UUI equal across groups**; **SUI linked to mode of delivery**.
- **Pregnancy itself** raises UI and faecal incontinence risk regardless of mode.

### Herpesvirus Infections

- **Herpes zoster** invading **sacral dorsal root ganglia/posterior roots** → **retention with areflexia days–weeks after the rash** (may precede rash with fever, perineal paraesthesia, obstipation). DO/UI also possible (root irritation, meningitis/myelitis, "**zoster cystitis**" — bladder vesicles at cystoscopy). **Resolves spontaneously in 1–2 months.**
- Voiding dysfunction ~4% of zoster admissions, rising to **~29% with lumbosacral dermatome involvement**. Three types: **herpetic cystitis (commonest)**, **neuritis → flaccid bladder retention**, **myelitis → DO**; all normal within 8 weeks.
- **Herpes simplex (anogenital):** retention **2–7 days after genital eruption**; with **CSF pleocytosis + sacral radiculitis + sphincteric dysfunction = Elsberg syndrome**; transient (may leave sacral neuropathic pain).

### Diabetes Mellitus

- **Commonest cause of peripheral neuropathy** in Europe/North America; **11.3% of US population** (28.5 million diagnosed adults). LUTS in 5–59% — distinguish referred from unselected cohorts. Nurses' Health Study: **UI more prevalent in diabetic women (odds ↑ ~20%), predominantly UUI; no SUI association**.
- **Classic diabetic cystopathy = sensory + motor neuropathy:** **impaired sensation → ↑ capacity → ↓ contractility → impaired flow → later ↑ PVR.**
- **Differential in men = BOO** (both give low flow) → **pressure–flow studies separate them**. **Smooth/striated dyssynergia not a feature** — false positives from abdominal straining (pseudodyssynergia; straining cannot open the bladder neck).
- **DO is also common** in diabetic series (often with coexisting cerebrovascular disease) → **urodynamics before treatment**.
- **Mechanisms (five sources):**
    1. **Muscarinic receptor change — M2 overexpression** (M2 preserves contraction when M3 lost).
    2. **Advanced glycation end products** → microvascular disease, endothelial dysfunction, ↓ nitric oxide.
    3. **Sorbitol (polyol, aldose reductase pathway)** → ↓ myo-inositol, ↓ Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, **reactive oxygen species/oxidative stress**.
    4. **Diacylglycerol → protein kinase C** → connective tissue (type IV collagen, fibronectin), neural and endothelial damage.
    5. **↓ NGF.**
- **Further findings:** Aδ- and C-fibre dysfunction impairs emptying; autonomic neuropathy associated with LUTS; ↓ nitric oxide–mediated urethral relaxation; ↑ muscarinic and purinergic expression; ↓ sodium-pump activity; mitochondrial and gap-junction abnormalities, collagen deposition (**time-dependent**); **polyuria (diuresis) itself enlarges the bladder**; anti–voltage-gated calcium channel antibodies (type 1 model); L-type channels and TRPV1. **Animal model matters** (alloxan → DO; sucrose-fed → normal; streptozotocin → pyuria).
- **Management:** **early timed voiding** prevents distension-induced decompensation; intensive glycaemic control slows neuropathy experimentally, but **LUTS in type 1 diabetes respond poorly to glycaemic control**.

**Key Point: Diabetes**

- **DO has been cited as the most frequent urodynamic finding** in diabetic cystopathy — possibly reflecting **timing of diagnosis within the natural history** (DO early, hypocontractility late).
</div>

### Guillain–Barré Syndrome

- **Acute inflammatory demyelinating disorder of peripheral somatic and autonomic nerves**: **rapid symmetric weakness, areflexia**, mild sensory signs; post-infectious molecular mimicry (**two-thirds preceded by respiratory or gastrointestinal infection**). **Demyelinating form 85%, axonal 15%.** Nadir within 2 weeks (75%) / 4 weeks (94%); mortality 5–8%.
- Autonomic complications: arrhythmia, BP lability, bowel, bladder, sexual dysfunction. **LUT dysfunction 25–80%; retention 11–30%.**
- Findings: **↑ PVR commonest**, then ↓ sensation, DO, ↓ compliance, DUA, DSD; **severity tracks motor grade/disability, not antibody titres**.
- **Manage reversibly (CIC, anticholinergics) pending recovery.**

<p class="tcap">Comparison — Herpes Zoster vs Herpes Simplex vs Guillain–Barré</p>

| | Herpes zoster | Herpes simplex | Guillain–Barré |
|---|---|---|---|
| Site | Sacral dorsal root ganglia | Sacral roots ± CNS | Peripheral nerves (demyelination) |
| Onset | Days–weeks after rash | 2–7 days after genital eruption | With ascending weakness |
| Usual LUT finding | Retention, areflexia; zoster cystitis | Retention | ↑ PVR, retention |
| Eponym | — | **Elsberg syndrome** (with CSF pleocytosis) | — |
| Course | **Resolves in 1–2 months** | Transient | Reversible therapy pending recovery |

**Concept —** distal to the cord the lesion hits the **peripheral reflex arc**, so the signature is **areflexia with a non-relaxing or fixed outlet**, and **compliance falls only when preganglionic parasympathetic motor fibres are damaged** — which is why urgent decompression, waiting and CIC outrank outlet surgery.

## Miscellaneous Conditions Definitely, Probably, or Possibly Related to Neuromuscular Dysfunction

### Detrusor Sphincter Dyssynergia

- **Dyssynergia** = kinesiologic dissociation of normally harmonious muscles; **DSD (DESD) = striated (external) sphincter** unless stated. Smooth sphincter = bladder neck and proximal urethral smooth muscle.
- **True DSD requires a lesion between the sacral cord and the brainstem PMC** — traumatic SCI, MS, transverse myelitis. **Without such a lesion, doubt the diagnosis and search exhaustively for neurology** — **exception: children with dysfunctional voiding (Hinman syndrome)**.

<p class="tcap">Comparison — Blaivas Types of DSD</p>

| Type | EMG pattern | Associated lesion | Treatment need |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Sphincter activity rises with detrusor pressure, **relaxes suddenly at peak** → unobstructed voiding | **Incomplete** | **Observe** unless persistent VUR, hydronephrosis or autonomic hyperreflexia |
| **2** | **Sporadic** sphincter contractions throughout detrusor contraction | Complete | Treat |
| **3** | **Crescendo–decrescendo** contraction → obstruction throughout | Complete | Treat |

- Alternative: **intermittent vs continuous** (continuous → complete injury); type matters less than **surveillance and prompt treatment**; **no link with level**.
- **Pseudodyssynergia** — EMG rise with pressure without true DSD: **(1) abdominal straining** (to initiate/augment or from discomfort); **(2) attempted inhibition** of an involuntary or uncomfortable contraction. Misdiagnosis leads to harmful treatment.
- **Untreated, ~half of men develop VUR, upper tract deterioration, stones, urosepsis, ureterovesical obstruction**; far less in women (lower pressures).
- **DLPP ≥40 cm H₂O after sphincterotomy = failure** (upper tract damage, persistent DSD) — applies to other treatments.
- **Treatment:** oral sphincter drugs disappoint. Options: **(1) CIC + DO control (preferred); (2) sphincterotomy; (3) sphincteric onabotulinumtoxinA; (4) indwelling catheter; (5) diversion.** **Urethral stents abandoned (complications; withdrawn).** **Sphincterotomy (surgical or chemical) only after counselling, for those unable/unwilling to use other options** — poor durability, repeat procedures.

### Dysfunctional Voiding

- Synonyms: **non-neurogenic neurogenic bladder, occult (neuropathic) voiding dysfunction, learned voiding dysfunction, Hinman syndrome** — **involuntary striated sphincter obstruction without neurologic disease**.
- **Proof requires pressure–flow–EMG showing emptying during involuntary sphincter contraction with no abdominal straining**; many reported cases rest on weaker evidence.
- Cause uncertain: **persisting transitional phase of continence development**, or **learned reaction to voiding discomfort** outlasting its cause.
- **Non-neurogenic chronic retention (consensus):** **PVR ≥300 mL persisting ≥6 months on ≥2 occasions**, stratified high/low risk and symptomatic/asymptomatic. **High risk** (hydronephrosis/hydroureter, chronic kidney disease, recurrent UTI, UI with skin sequelae) → **drainage** (intermittent or indwelling); **suprapubic preferred to urethral long term**; lifelong follow-up.

### Bladder Neck Dysfunction

- **Incomplete bladder neck opening during voiding**; synonyms: **smooth (proximal) sphincter dyssynergia, primary bladder neck obstruction, dysfunctional bladder neck**. "Smooth sphincter dyssynergia" = the neurogenic version with **autonomic hyperreflexia**.
- **Non-neurogenic form: young and middle-aged men with long-standing voiding and storage symptoms**, often labelled **psychogenic** (normal prostate, low PVR, normal cystoscopy); characterised by Turner-Warwick (1973).
- Differential: bladder neck contracture, benign prostatic enlargement/obstruction, dysfunctional voiding, prostatitis, neurogenic dysfunction, low-pressure/low-flow.
- **Diagnosis: urodynamics proves BOO → localise to bladder neck by videourodynamics, voiding cystourethrography or micturitional profilometry** (or infer from obstruction without stricture, prostate or DSD). DO/↓ compliance coexist in ⅓–½.
- Cause: abnormal muscle arrangement (narrowing not funnelling), **sympathetic hyperactivity** (anxious, "high-strung"; partial α-blocker response).
- **"Trapped prostate"** (Turner-Warwick): bladder neck dysfunction + prostatic growth → **double obstruction**; lobes expand into the urethra; lifelong poor voiding accepted as normal.
- **Treatment: α-blockers help some; definitive = bladder neck incision**; with trapped prostate → small TURP/ablation including the bladder neck or transurethral incision — patients "have never voided so well".

### Bladder Outlet Obstruction in Females

- **Uncommon**; female primary bladder neck obstruction (PBNO) rare.

<p class="tcap">Comparison — Definitions of Female BOO</p>

| | Nitti (videourodynamic) | Groutz (pressure–flow) | Solomon nomogram |
|---|---|---|---|
| Criterion | **Radiographic obstruction** bladder neck → distal urethra **with sustained detrusor contraction** (usually ↓ flow) | **Free Qmax ≤12 mL/s repeatedly + Pdet at Qmax ≥20 cm H₂O** | **Qmax and Pdet@Qmax → probability** of obstruction |
| Fluoroscopy | **Required** | Not required | Not required |
| Obstructed | ~23% of those studied | ~7% | Validated; good agreement with Nitti |
| PBNO share | ~16% | ~8% | — |
| Commonest cause | **Dysfunctional voiding**, then cystocele, prior anti-incontinence surgery | **Prior anti-incontinence surgery**, then severe prolapse, stricture | — |

- **Surgery for female bladder neck obstruction risks sphincteric UI** — approach cautiously; distinguish dysfunctional voiding from bladder neck dysfunction (uroflow, EMG, fluoroscopy); **multidisciplinary: biofeedback, behavioural, drugs**.
- **High-grade pelvic organ prolapse (POP) obstructs by urethral kinking** (over half); **test voiding with prolapse reduced before repair**; Qmax improves in most after correction.

### Low-Pressure and Low-Flow Voiding in Younger Males: Paruresis (Bashful Bladder)

- Low-pressure/low-flow = **decompensating detrusor (usually from BOO)** or **DO with detrusor underactivity (DO–DU**, formerly DHIC/DOIC). In young men: frequency, hesitancy, poor stream; endoscopy normal.
- **Paruresis (shy/bashful bladder)**: marked hesitancy **in the presence of others**; 3–19% of young men referred for urodynamics.
- **Obsessional rather than anxious**, over-control of micturition → **behavioural modification**. Ambulatory urodynamics finds a surgically correctable cause only **in men >40**; negligible yield **≤40**. Comorbid **anxiety and depression** common → psychological/multidisciplinary care.

### Urinary Retention: Fowler Syndrome in Young Females

- Female retention **unusual but not rare**; causes neurologic, pharmacologic, anatomic, myopathic, functional, psychogenic.
- **Fowler syndrome: retention in young women (<30; 20–35) without overt neurologic disease** — unable to void ≥1 day, **no urgency, rising discomfort**; **capacity >1 L without urge required**; normal brain/cord MRI; **polycystic ovaries** frequent.
- **Hallmark: concentric needle EMG of the urethral sphincter — complex repetitive discharges and decelerating bursts** → impaired relaxation; hypothesised **hormonally dependent channelopathy** (hence premenopausal); **urethral closure pressure very high (>100 cm H₂O)**, enlarged sphincter volume. Urethral afferent overactivity inhibits bladder afferents (**exaggerated cortical pro-continence activity**).
- **Urodynamics: detrusor acontractility.** EMG pattern occurs in ~8% of routine female studies; link to complete retention is rare.
- **Hormonal, drug and onabotulinumtoxinA therapy fail. SNM is the treatment — success ~70%**, even after long retention; **Fowler syndrome is a positive predictor of SNM response** vs idiopathic retention.
- Presumptive cases: confirmed in ~⅗; a third undiagnosed. Psychological comorbidity (somatisation, depression, anxiety, trauma, obsessive-compulsive disorder) overlaps with idiopathic retention → **multidisciplinary care**.

### Postoperative Urinary Retention

- Incidence **5–70%**; commonest after **LUT, perineal, gynaecologic and anorectal surgery**.
- **Contributing factors:** 1. traumatic instrumentation; 2. bladder overdistension; 3. ↓ bladder sensation; 4. ↓ contractility; 5. ↑ outlet resistance; 6. ↓ micturition reflex activity; 7. **nociceptive inhibitory reflex**; 8. pre-existing outlet pathology (BPH). **Anaesthesia/analgesia affect 2, 3, 4, 6**; a sympathetic nociceptive reflex could explain **4, 5, 6**.
- **Prevention:** **18–24 h catheter decompression after joint replacement lowers retention vs CIC** without more infection; **avoid acute overdistension** (reduces the detrusor response during and after distension).
- **Phenoxybenzamine prophylaxis** reduces retention (meta-analysis); tamsulosin before spinal surgery did **not** (randomised trial).
- **After spinal surgery exclude epidural haematoma → cauda equina syndrome** (saddle anaesthesia, leg weakness).

### Hyperthyroidism

- **Sympathetic overactivity/autonomic imbalance** → LUTS, especially in **women**: incomplete emptying, frequency, straining, ↓ flow, enuresis; ↑ PVR, large capacity, ↑ perineal EMG on voiding.
- **Resolve with treatment of thyrotoxicosis**; no correlation with hormone levels.

### Schizophrenia

- More **UUI and bed-wetting** than mood-disorder controls; involuntary contractions with childhood incontinence history → **possible brain pathology**; **antipsychotics** add UI via **α-blockade and hypodopaminergic state** (urgency, enuresis, DO).

### Gastroparesis

- Delayed gastric emptying without obstruction (diabetic, post-surgical, idiopathic); abnormal contractions and delayed sensation common. **Idiopathic → difficulty emptying; diabetic → frequency**; suggests a **common autonomic neuropathy**.

### Myasthenia Gravis

- **Autoantibodies to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors** → striated weakness. **Greatly increased incontinence after prostatectomy** (any disease lowering distal sphincter tone does this).
- De novo dysfunction (intrinsic sphincter deficiency, DO, areflexia) may mark an autonomic subset with worse prognosis. **Intradetrusor toxin and PTNS used without neurologic worsening.**

### Isaacs Syndrome

- **Continuous muscle activity** (fasciculation, myokymia, sweating, ↑ creatine kinase), **anti–potassium channel antibodies**; retention from **striated sphincter spasm** (catheter will not pass) + rectal spasm → **plasmapheresis, muscle relaxants, suprapubic drainage**; resolves.

### Wernicke Encephalopathy

- **Thiamine (B1) deficiency**, alcoholic or not; periventricular lesions (mammillary bodies, medial thalamus, hypothalamus, cerebellar vermis, PAG, midbrain tegmentum); thiamine deficiency also causes peripheral neuropathy.
- **Urgency UI with DO and small capacity** (medial thalamic–hypothalamic/PAG lesions), or **poor contractility with ED** in alcoholism — **both resolve with thiamine**.

### Systemic Sclerosis (Scleroderma)

- Collagen overproduction → skin, vessels, gut, heart, lung, kidney. **Areflexia ± ↓ compliance** with **detrusor collagen deposition**; other series find few abnormalities and no correlation with fibrosis — **fibrotic replacement ± autonomic dysfunction**.

### Ehlers–Danlos Syndrome

- Inherited connective tissue disorders (>10 subtypes): skin fragility/hyperextensibility, joint mobility. **Bladder diverticula (higher recurrence after repair) and VUR**; compliant diverticular tissue (matrix change).
- Female survey: **very high SUI (young women), UUI, pelvic pain**, POP, faecal incontinence — **urethral hypermobility** from collagen defect; UI worst for QoL.

### Myotonic Dystrophy

- **Autosomal dominant** myotonia, distal atrophy, cataracts, endocrine, cognitive, testicular, alopecia, conduction defects. **No sphincter myotonia; no characteristic pattern; symptoms do not predict urodynamics → study before treating.**

### Corticobasal Degeneration

- Rare, **unilateral**, cortex + basal ganglia; urinary symptoms common (1–3 years after onset), **nocturia first**; **DO commonest**, small capacity, hypocontractility, low compliance; **no DSD**.

### Sacrococcygeal Teratoma

- **Commonest congenital neoplasm**; post-resection urologic complications in ~⅓ (neurogenic bladder, UI, VUR); **prenatal hydronephrosis predicts sequelae**.

### Williams–Beuren Syndrome

- **Chromosome 7 deletion** (cardiovascular, facial, intellectual disability). **LUTS ~80%** (urgency, frequency, UUI); **bladder diverticula commonest anomaly**; DO common, some DSD, ↓ capacity.

### Amyloidosis

- **Hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR; formerly type 1 Portuguese familial amyloid polyneuropathy)**, autosomal dominant: early autonomic/genitourinary involvement; **↓ sensation and DUA (amyloid infiltration of nerves)** even in asymptomatic carriers; **SUI and ↓ anal tone**; LUTS in >80%.

### Machado–Joseph Disease

- **Spinocerebellar ataxia type 3 (SCA3)**, commonest dominant hereditary ataxia (MJD1 expansion, 14q32.1): spasticity, rigidity, dystonia, ophthalmoplegia, eyelid retraction. LUT dysfunction ~14%: **urgency, DO**, delayed sensation, ↑ PVR.

### Radiation

- **Early reaction (peak 4–6 weeks):** storage symptoms, ↓ first desire, ↓ capacity, ↓ compliance → **recovers by 6 months**.
- **Late effects:** less common but **progressive, intractable**; ↓ capacity, DO (up to ⅓), ↑ filling pressure. Rat model: **biphasic compliance loss (4–6 and from 10–12 weeks)** — **fibrosis did not explain it**; smooth muscle degeneration, **unmyelinated axon degeneration**, mast cells did.
- Prostate external beam: capacity falls modestly; symptoms correlate with **bladder dose**. Men with SUI after radiation: **more DO, smaller capacity**, more storage treatment. **Combined-modality prostate radiotherapy: long-term reconstruction or diversion in a substantial minority.**
- **Pelvic radiation is an independent LUTS risk factor in women** (frequency, urgency, SUI, ↓ compliance after radical hysterectomy + radiation).

### Defunctionalised Bladder

- Early fetal diversion: ↓ weight, connective tissue infiltration, smooth muscle disorganisation, ↓ carbachol response.
- **Previously normal defunctionalised bladder → ↓ capacity, involuntary contractions and/or ↓ compliance**; abnormal bladders keep prior pathology plus these. **Rehabilitate by cycling with increasing volumes.**
- **Renal transplantation into a defunctionalised bladder is feasible without augmentation** — threshold proposed: **capacity ≥100 mL and voiding pressure ≤100 cm H₂O** during rehabilitation; paediatric series and a randomised trial (cycling vs none) show recovery without graft compromise. Still **assess bladder function before transplant**.

### Disorders of Sexual Differentiation

- **Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH)** (after feminising surgery) → **more UUI and SUI**; another series (CAH, androgen insensitivity) found LUTS common but UI not increased — **effect uncertain**.

### Aging

- Autonomic axonal dystrophy; **↓ detrusor muscarinic receptors**; healthy women show **age-related ↓ contractility, ↓ filling sensation, ↓ MUCP**. **Age cannot be separated from coexisting disease.**
- **Occult neurology** (multiple cerebral infarcts, cervical and lumbar spondylosis) masquerades as BOO (Olmsted County) → **urodynamics in older men with complex LUTS**.

### Benign Joint Hypermobility Syndrome

- Inherited connective tissue disease; **UI and anal incontinence more frequent**, childhood history; **VUR frequent in children**; attributed to collagen metabolism.

### Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder

- **3–5% of children**; more **enuresis, urgency, holding manoeuvres**, voiding symptoms; continence achieved later; treatment of ADHD may improve LUTS.

### Other Conditions

- **Visceral cross-talk:** altered afferent input from other pelvic organs (interstitial cystitis, irritable bowel); experimental colitis causes **reversible detrusor contractility change without histologic change**.
- **Further conditions with reported LUT dysfunction:** adrenoleukodystrophy/adrenomyeloneuropathy; adult polyglucosan body disease; Behçet disease; Brown-Séquard syndrome; central cord syndrome; cystic fibrosis (incontinence); Down syndrome; Duchenne muscular dystrophy; familial dysautonomia; intramedullary epidermoid cyst; Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome; lateral meningocele (Lehman) syndrome; neurofibromatosis; pituitary adenoma; Sjögren syndrome; spinal cord tumours; spinal muscular atrophy.

**Concept —** these conditions are grouped because the **bladder behaves as the lesion predicts, not as the diagnosis suggests**: true DSD demands a pons-to-sacral-cord lesion, female and young-male "obstruction" is often functional, and a sphincter already weakened by neuromuscular disease (MSA, myasthenia, radical pelvic surgery) turns outlet surgery into incontinence.

## Evaluation, Surveillance, and Treatment of Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction

### Overview

- Practice varied widely for lack of evidence; now guided by the **AUA/SUFU Guideline on NLUTD (2021)** and **EAU Guidelines on Neuro-urology (2022)** — still limited evidence.
- **Gaps:** sexual and bowel dysfunction, urologic cancer screening, transitional care, fertility.

### Initial Evaluation

- **Risk stratification** (AUA/SUFU) — **prevention of renal damage is the primary goal** (renal failure was the leading SCI death before World War II).
- **Minimum for all: history, physical examination, urinalysis.**
    - History: neurologic condition (onset, level, disability, prognosis), current and past bladder management, symptoms and bother, **goals of care**, prior surgery.
    - Examination: cognition, mobility, scars/stomas/tubes, pelvic examination (women) or digital rectal examination (men), **spasticity, sensation, hand dexterity, contractures**, perineal/buttock skin and pressure sores.
    - Urinalysis interpreted in light of catheterisation. **PVR in anyone voiding spontaneously**; consider diaries, uroflowmetry, patient-reported outcome measures.
- **Risk by lesion:** suprapontine (DO, low PVR) → lower risk; elevated PVR → moderate; sacral/infrasacral → acontractile, low pressure but **compliance may fail**; **infrapontine–suprasacral → DO + DSD → unclear risk → urodynamics**.

<p class="tcap">TABLE 109.2 Risk Stratification of Patients With Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction (adapted from the AUA/SUFU 2021 Guidelines)</p>

| Risk | Typical lesion | LUTS | Urodynamics | Imaging | Renal function |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Low (must meet all)** | Suprapontine, or distal to cord/peripheral, uncomplicated | **Spontaneous voiding, low PVR, storage symptoms, stable** | (If done) synergic voiding, DO | (If done) normal | Normal/stable |
| **Unknown** | Infrapontine–suprasacral; sacral/infrasacral | Storage + voiding; **any change**; ↑ PVR; recurrent UTI | Not yet assessed | Any abnormality: hydroureteronephrosis, stones | — |
| **Moderate** | — | — | **Neurogenic retention; DO with incomplete emptying** | Normal | Normal/stable |
| **High (any one)** | — | — | **Poor compliance, ↑ storage pressure, DO, DSD, VUR** | **Hydroureteronephrosis, scarring, parenchymal loss, large/increasing stones** | **Abnormal/unstable** |

- **Anyone not clearly low risk is "unknown" until urodynamics, upper tract imaging and renal function reclassify them as moderate or high.**
- **Low risk needs no routine imaging, renal function or urodynamics** unless results would change diagnosis, prognosis or treatment.
- **Trap:** DO alone is compatible with low risk (suprapontine, synergic, low PVR); **DO in the high-risk row means DO with the pressure consequences of infrapontine disease**.
- **Cystourethroscopy not routine** — for haematuria, stricture/false passage, suspected stones or anything altering management.
- **Defer full assessment during spinal shock or acute brain injury (cerebral oedema)**; **no irreversible intervention on symptoms during this unstable phase**.

### Management of Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction: Surgical and Nonsurgical

- Therapies are classified by **function** — **facilitate storage vs facilitate emptying** — and by **target** — **bladder vs outlet**.

<p class="tcap">BOX 109.2 Voiding Dysfunction: Goals of Management</p>

- **Upper tract preservation/improvement; absence or control of infection; adequate storage at low pressure; adequate emptying at low pressure**; control over micturition; no catheter or stoma; social and vocational acceptability.

<p class="tcap">BOX 109.3 Reasons to Change or Augment a Given Regimen</p>

- **Upper tract deterioration; recurrent sepsis/urinary fever; lower tract deterioration**; inadequate storage, emptying or control (**"inadequate" includes high detrusor pressure, not just volume**); unacceptable side effects; skin changes from incontinence or appliances.

<p class="tcap">BOX 109.4 Patient Factors to Consider When Choosing Therapy</p>

- Prognosis (progressive/malignant disease); general health; **limiting factors (hand dexterity, transfers, habitus)**; mental status; motivation/compliance; wish to be catheter-free or avoid surgery; sexual activity; reliability; educability; family support; economic resources.

- **Principles:** results need not be perfect — **satisfaction and avoidance of adverse outcomes**; decisions shared with patient and carers (reversibility, side effects, best/worst case, follow-up, alternatives); **start with the simplest, most reversible therapy and climb**; **the patient decides when "enough is enough"**; combine treatments with different mechanisms and non-additive side effects.
- **Emptying:**
    - **CIC is the gold standard and foundation** for the willing patient/carer; **keep catheterised volumes <500 mL**.
    - **Credé/Valsalva not recommended** (outlet resistance).
    - **If chronic catheter needed: suprapubic > urethral** — similar UTI, stone and cancer rates, but urethral erosion avoided; patients prefer it.
    - CIC vs indwelling urethral: **less pyelonephritis, epididymo-orchitis and urosepsis**; **highest symptomatic UTI with indwelling urethral catheters; lowest with spontaneous voiding**.
    - **Hydrophilic-coated catheters lengthen time to first UTI** (randomised trial; systematic review).
    - **QoL does not always favour CIC** — some prefer indwelling catheters or surgery; dependence on carers harms independence → individualise.
    - **inFlow device** (magnetic intraurethral valve-pump, women): improves QoL but high dropout; for those unable to catheterise.
- **Storage:** **medical therapy first-line for NDO** (antimuscarinics most studied; **mirabegron** effective); refractory/intolerant → **intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA** (~60% continue long term).
- **DO + DSD: detrusor relaxation (drugs or toxin) + CIC preferred**; sphincterotomy for selected men (high failure, repeat procedures); **intrasphincteric toxin not routine**.
- **Neuromodulation:** dorsal penile/clitoral nerve stimulation suppresses NDO; pudendal stimulation (neurophysiologic guidance) ↑ capacity; **sacral deafferentation + anterior root stimulator (Brindley)** — high continence, fewer UTIs, stable kidneys, autonomic dysreflexia resolves; rhizotomy benefits even if stimulator switched off. **PTNS for spontaneous voiders with OAB (MS, PD, stroke)**; **SNM for NLUTD without SCI or spina bifida** (MRI-compatible devices).
- **Future:** lumbar-to-sacral ventral root rerouting, regenerative medicine — unstandardised.
- **Last resort — reconstruction/diversion** (e.g. unable to catheterise): **bladder neck closure + suprapubic catheter** (retropubic better than perineal — fewer fistulae; catheter care determines success); Mainz pouch or augmentation + fascial sling (both acceptable); **ileovesicostomy** (robotic: longer, costlier). **Reserve for failure of less invasive options, after discussion of goals.**

### Urinary Tract Infections and Antibiotic Use

- **UTIs near universal in long-term SCI**; bacteriuria common in the first year. **Risk factors: male sex, cervical injury, condom catheter.** Recurrence suggests **calculi, silent pyelonephritis or high residuals**.
- **Treat bacteriuria only when symptomatic** (consensus since 1992).
- **Recommendations:**
    1. Treat bacteriuria only if symptomatic.
    2. Choose agents with little collateral damage to normal flora.
    3. **Treat ≥5 days; 7–14 days for reinfection or relapse.**
    4. Correct structural and functional risk factors.
    5. **Prophylaxis only for recurrent UTI with no treatable cause, especially with dilated upper tracts.**
    6. **No prophylaxis for indwelling catheter or intermittent catheterisation users** (current guidelines agree).
- **Refractory recurrent UTI → upper tract imaging, cystoscopy, urodynamics.**

### Neurogenic Bladder and Bladder Cancer

- **Historical link with long-term catheterisation**: **squamous cell carcinoma** prominent (sometimes invisible endoscopically — found on biopsy), squamous metaplasia, leucoplakia; **risk factors: indwelling catheter, bladder stones, chronic UTI**.
- **Proposed features in SCI:** higher relative risk; higher squamous proportion; **younger age at peak**; **advanced stage at diagnosis**; **neither cystoscopy nor cytology reliable**; aggressive evaluation for multiple risk factors.
- **Counterview:** large multinational SCI data and spina bifida data show **incidence comparable to the general population** (meta-analysis ~0.3%), but **many present muscle-invasive** → **higher mortality**; **transitional cell carcinoma commonest** in contemporary data; **catheters and UTI, not SCI, are the risk factors**.
- **Screening: AUA/SUFU 2021 does not recommend surveillance cystoscopy** in NLUTD, catheterised or not — insufficient evidence; **overdetection of benign lesions** → biopsies and harm. **Cystoscopy for haematuria, cancer history or other indications.**

### Follow-Up

- Mortality falling → **lifelong urologic follow-up**; long-term SCI cohorts: **every patient has UTIs**; commonest other complications **stones, hydronephrosis, VUR**; also pyelonephritis, dysreflexia, urethral injury, urosepsis.

<p class="tcap">Comparison — Follow-Up Recommendations for NLUTD</p>

| Body | Frequency | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **American Paraplegia Society** (SCI) | **Annually for 5–10 years, then every other year if well** | Upper and lower tract evaluation and urodynamics at same intervals; nuclear renogram: **>20% fall in renal plasma flow → investigate**; **annual cystoscopy with indwelling catheter** |
| **Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine (2006)** | **Yearly** | No evidence on optimal tests or interval |
| **EAU** | Specialist review **every 1–2 years** (more often in MS, acute SCI, unstable disease) | **Dipstick at least 2-monthly** and when UTI suspected; **ultrasound (upper tract, bladder) + residual every 6 months**; **examination and bloods yearly** |
| **AUA/SUFU 2021** | **Risk-based** | **Low risk: no routine surveillance.** **Moderate/high: yearly history, examination, symptoms and renal function; upper tract imaging every 1–2 years**; repeat urodynamics for change; **stone history/risk: stone imaging every 1–2 years**; **bowel reconstruction: yearly history, examination, electrolytes/acid–base, renal function, upper tract imaging** |

- **Pattern:** older schedules were **calendar-based and SCI-specific**; AUA/SUFU is **risk-based** and exempts low-risk patients. **Trap:** annual cystoscopy for catheterised patients (APS) is **not** endorsed by AUA/SUFU 2021.

**Concept —** every evaluation and treatment decision serves one aim, **protecting the kidneys from sustained high bladder pressure**, so risk is judged by pressure, compliance and upper tract status, not by the diagnosis. Treatment then climbs from the most reversible option, and surveillance intensity follows risk.
