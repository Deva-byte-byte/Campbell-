<h1>Chapter 110 — Overactive Bladder</h1>

## Terminology and Definitions

- **Overactive bladder (OAB)** — International Continence Society (ICS): **"urinary urgency, usually accompanied by frequency and nocturia, with or without urgency urinary incontinence (UUI), in the absence of urinary tract infection (UTI) or other obvious pathology."**
- Component storage symptoms: **increased daytime frequency, nocturia, urgency, urgency (urinary) incontinence**. "Urge incontinence" was renamed **"urgency incontinence"** to match the abnormal sensation.

<p class="tcap">TABLE 110.1 Standardized Terminology and Definitions to Describe Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction Related to Overactive Bladder</p>

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| OAB syndrome | As above — **urgency is the defining symptom** |
| Increased daytime frequency | Patient considers he/she voids too often by day (**7 voids = upper limit of normal**) |
| Nocturia | **Waking one or more times** to void; each void preceded and followed by sleep |
| Urgency | **Sudden, compelling desire to void that is difficult to defer** |
| UUI | Involuntary loss of urine **associated with urgency** |

- **Bother:** all components reduce health-related quality of life (QoL); **urgency and UUI most strongly**; **UUI the most bothersome LUTS in both sexes**. Frequency bothers at **≥8 daytime voids (every ≤2 h)**, though fewer can bother; **nocturia bothers at ≥2 episodes, markedly at ≥3**. **Isolated nocturia** suggests nocturnal polyuria or poor sleep, not LUT dysfunction.
- **OAB ≠ detrusor overactivity (DO):** OAB is a **symptom syndrome**; DO is a **urodynamic observation** of involuntary detrusor contractions during filling (spontaneous or provoked). OAB patients may lack DO; DO may be asensate or an **artefact of instrumentation**.

### Mixed Symptoms Incorporating Urinary Urgency

- **Mixed urinary incontinence (MUI)** — involuntary leakage associated **with urgency and also with effort/exertion/sneezing/coughing** = **SUI + UUI ("OAB wet")** in one person.
- **SUI + "OAB dry" is not MUI** — describe descriptively ("SUI and urgency without UUI").
- **Urinary + faecal incontinence = double (dual) incontinence**, not MUI.
- MUI generally means **more severe leakage**; relative contributions hard to quantify, especially with **insensible incontinence** (unaware how leakage occurred).
- Fig. 110.1: incontinence spans **SUI → MUI → "OAB wet"**; "OAB dry" can coexist with SUI.

### Distinguishing Overactive Bladder From Bladder Pain Syndrome

- **Bladder pain syndrome (BPS):** persistent/recurrent chronic **pelvic pain, pressure or discomfort perceived as bladder-related + ≥1 other urinary symptom** (urgency, frequency). Pain is **required in BPS, not typical of OAB**.

<p class="tcap">Comparison — OAB vs BPS</p>

| | OAB | BPS |
|---|---|---|
| Driving fear | **Leakage** | **Pain** |
| Sensation with filling (Fig. 110.3) | **Phasic surges of urgency** that rise and recede → void or UUI | **Pain rises steadily, accelerating** with filling |
| Voided volumes | Variable | **More consistent** |
| Deferral | Difficult | **Possible, at the cost of more pain** |
| Site | Perineum / base of penis / urethra–vagina | **Suprapubic** ± perineal |
| UUI (Fig. 110.2) | Yes | **No** |
| Shared | Urgency, frequency, nocturia | Urgency, frequency, nocturia |

- **Overlap is large** (most women with bladder-centric pain also report urgency/UUI) → probably **overlapping pathophysiology: afferent hypersensitivity, central sensitisation, somatisation**.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**Key Points: Overactive Bladder Terminology**

- OAB = urgency ± frequency/nocturia ± UUI, without UTI or other obvious pathology.
- **OAB is symptomatic; DO is urodynamic.**
- Urgency = sudden, compelling desire to void that is difficult to defer.
- MUI = UUI + SUI.
- **"Fear of leakage" vs "fear of pain"** separates OAB from BPS, though both may express central sensitisation.
</div>

**Concept —** OAB is defined **by a sensation, urgency**, so every boundary in the terminology — DO, MUI, BPS, nocturia — is drawn by asking **what the patient feels and fears**, not what the bladder does on a trace.
{: .concept}

## Epidemiology and Economics

### Prevalence of Overactive Bladder and Related Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms

- **OAB 11.8–28.7%; UUI 1.5–17.3%** — ranges reflect differing definitions and samples.

<p class="tcap">TABLE 110.2 Studies Examining Prevalence Data for Overactive Bladder</p>

| Study | Population | Method | OAB definition | Prevalence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **NOBLE** (National Overactive Bladder Evaluation), 2003 | US, ≥18 y | Telephone (CATI) | ≥4 urgency episodes in 4 weeks **and** ≥8 voids/day or coping behaviours | **OAB 16.5%; with UUI 6.1%** |
| **EpiLUTS**, 2009 | Multinational, ≥40 y | Internet | Urgency at least "sometimes" and/or UUI | **OAB 28.7%; UUI 17.3%** (highest) |
| **OAB-POLL**, 2013 | US, 18–70 y | Internet | As EpiLUTS | OAB 23.3%; UUI 12.6% |
| **NHANES**, 2008 | US women, ≥21 y | Interview | Any incontinence in 12 months | UUI 7.9%; MUI 17% |
| **BACH** (Boston Area Community Health), 2006 | Boston, 30–79 y | Interview | AUA symptom index ≥8 | LUTS 18.7% |
| **EPIC**, 2006 | Multinational, ≥18 y | CATI | **ICS definitions** | **OAB 11.8%; UUI 1.5%** (lowest) |
| Milsom, 2001 | Multinational, ≥40 y | Interview | Any OAB symptom | 16.6% |

*CATI, computer-assisted telephone interviewing.*

- **Pattern:** **looser definitions ("urgency at least sometimes", internet) give the highest rates; strict ICS definitions (EPIC) the lowest.** Trap: comparing raw prevalence across studies with different case definitions.
- **Sex:** OAB **1.5–2× commoner in women**, **UUI 2–3×**. NOBLE: OAB similar by sex (~16–17%) but **UUI 7.6% women vs 2.6% men**. EpiLUTS and OAB-POLL: urgency and UUI roughly double in women.
- **Age:** rises in women from their **40s**, men from their **50s–60s**; UUI rises in women after ~44 and in men beyond ~64; **beyond 75 years ~40% of men and ~⅓ of women** have OAB symptoms vs ≤10% before 55. **Men overtake women in the oldest groups.**
- **Race/ethnicity:** consistently **highest in Black men and women** (EpiLUTS, OAB-POLL); NHANES — highest UUI in Black women.

### Incidence and Progression of Overactive Bladder

- **Dynamic, waxing–waning** condition. Incident OAB in women **~3–8%/year**; **progression from OAB dry to UUI ~1%/year; remission ~6%/year**.

### Health Burden of Overactive Bladder

- Impairs daily activity, sleep, self-esteem, relationships, sexual life; **UUI adds burden beyond dry OAB**; less leisure physical activity.
- **Work:** higher unemployment, more absenteeism (medical and disability) and impaired productivity.
- **Care-seeking is poor:** under half ever discuss symptoms despite seeing clinicians; reasons — **not thought important, never asked, "something to cope with"**; many unaware treatment exists.
- **Drug persistence is poor:** **<6% take medication**; antimuscarinics only **modestly superior to placebo** in aggregate; **~92% fail initial antimuscarinic within 2 years**, most stop within 6 months, half never refill. Reasons: **unmet expectations > side effects > cost**.
- **Costs:** patient costs mostly **containment (pads, laundry)**, heavier with severity and in Black and Hispanic patients and those with unmet social needs; oral drug costs high, no agent universally cheap. **Third-line cost-effectiveness: sacral nerve stimulation (SNS) costliest over 10 years; onabotulinumtoxinA 100 U most cost-effective vs conservative care.** US societal cost **~$66 billion (2007)**, projected ~$83 billion (2020).
- **Social determinants:** lower socioeconomic status associated with UUI (dose–response); food insecurity, financial strain, housing and toilet access, poor social support, interpersonal violence linked to OAB.

**Concept —** OAB is **common, age-related and female-predominant for UUI**, yet **under-reported and under-persisted** — the burden lies less in lack of effective treatment than in **failure to ask, expectations unmet, and cost**.
{: .concept}

## Pathophysiology and Etiology

- **Multifactorial, across all levels of neural control.** DO was once the **sine qua non** of OAB but is no longer: **only about half with DO have urgency; 44–69% with urgency show DO**.
- **No animal model of OAB** (urgency is subjective) → research uses surrogates: **sensory activity, motor control, LUT reflexes**.
    - **Sensory:** transduction → afferent traffic → gating → sensitisation → conscious perception; **urothelium/suburothelium** release mediators, cytokines and growth factors; gut inflammation sensitises bladder nerves; brain imaging identifies candidate centres.
    - **Motor:** detrusor motility and drivers (efferents, interstitial cells, mediators); **localised micromotions beside quiescent areas** summate into whole-bladder pressure.
    - **Integration (Fig. 110.4):** **sacral cord** — immature reflex voiding (infants, neurologic disease); **midbrain/brainstem (PAG, PMC) — main regulatory level**, homeostasis, voiding reflex, switching storage ↔ voiding; **forebrain** — conscious sensation, volition, emotion, decision to store or void; **periphery** — urothelium, interstitial cells, myovesical plexus.
- **Functional brain imaging:** insula and prefrontal cortex involved; OAB alters regional activity; **SNS, tibial nerve stimulation and antimuscarinics change brain activity**.

### Afferent Mechanisms in Overactive Bladder

- Afferent endings densest **beneath the urothelium**.

<p class="tcap">Comparison — Bladder Afferent Fibres</p>

| | Aδ fibres | C fibres |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | **Small-calibre, myelinated** | **Small-calibre, unmyelinated** |
| Normal role | **Sense volume and detrusor contractile state**; project to higher centres | **"Silent"** — respond only to high-intensity stimuli (extreme distension, cold, heat, chemical irritation) |
| In pathology | — | **Become spontaneously active and hypersensitive to low-intensity input** (via second-order spinal neurons) |

- **Two broad categories: ↑ afferent signalling from the bladder, or ↓ central capacity to modulate afferent signals.**

#### Mechanisms of Increased Afferent Activity

<p class="tcap">Comparison — Urothelium-Based vs Myogenic Hypotheses</p>

| | Urothelium-based | Myogenic |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Urothelium/suburothelium responds to mechanical, osmotic, inflammatory, chemical stimuli | **Detrusor smooth muscle** |
| Mechanism | Altered receptors/channels + **mediator release onto adjacent afferents** → excess afferent traffic → urgency, micturition reflex | **↑ spontaneous excitation + enhanced propagation** across too much of the bladder wall |
| Supporting features | Intrinsic homeostatic aberration, unknown local trigger | **Patchy denervation** (denervated cells upregulate receptors, altered membrane potential); structural change aiding spread; **exaggerated micromotions** |
| Link to urgency | Augments fullness signal | Micromotions distort wall → afferents sense filling without pressure change; **coordinated spread → urodynamic DO** |

- **Other increased afferent signalling — pelvic organ cross-talk:** signals from other organs converge at **dorsal root ganglia, spinal cord or brain**; **bowel–bladder** best understood (shared thoracic and sacral innervation). Rectal distension or inflammation alters detrusor activity via **convergent C-fibres**; colonic stimulation worsens bladder pain in cystitis models. Clinically, **rectal distension changes capacity, sensation and DO; constipation and straining impair emptying and worsen storage and voiding symptoms**.
- **Abnormal handling of afferent signals:** continuous **"afferent noise"** reaches the central nervous system (CNS); normally most is filtered below consciousness. **If CNS modulation fails, normally unconscious sensations are perceived as urgency.**

#### Other Pathophysiologic Mechanisms

- **Ischaemia–reperfusion** (atherosclerosis, arterial obstruction) → hypoxia, **oxidative stress** → damage to urothelium, muscle, microvasculature, nerves.
- **Chronic inflammation** — raised cytokines, chemokines, growth factors in tissue, urine and blood.
- **Urinary microbiome** differs in UUI/OAB — relevance to bladder–brain signalling unknown.

### Etiology and Attributable Risk Factors

- **Cause usually unknown and multifactorial.**
- **Aging** — clearly associated; multifactorial cellular and CNS change.
- **Sex** — higher prevalence (especially younger women) and **more UUI in women**.
- **BOO** — classic cause of DO in animal models, but in humans **not a direct cause**: **OAB prevalence similar in women (BOO rare) and men (BOO common)**; **DO often persists after prostate surgery**. BOO may act **indirectly, accelerating ageing changes**.
- **Race/ethnicity** — differences exist; mechanisms unknown.
- **Psychological factors:** **depression and anxiety** most studied; embarrassment, shame, low self-esteem; **OAB wet carries more psychological burden than OAB dry**. Possible **serotonergic** or **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis** mechanisms; **stress** acts via this route; anxious women show **greater temporal summation of heat pain (central sensitisation marker)**.
- **Voiding behaviours:** **convenience ("just in case") voiding, delayed voiding, straining, positional preference** may cause OAB; **coping strategies (mapping toilets, altering plans, fluid restriction)** associated with stress and anxiety independent of symptoms and **perpetuate a negative bladder perception** even after objective improvement.
- **Wider associations:** bowel function, **metabolic syndrome**, hormonal status, psychosocial burden.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**Key Points: Pathophysiology and Etiology**

- Afferent information is integrated at multiple CNS levels; **main regulation at midbrain/brainstem**.
- **Failed high-level integration may let normally unconscious sensations be felt as urgency.**
- The two bladder-centric theories are **urothelium-based** and **myogenic**.
- **BOO and OAB are probably separate consequences of ageing rather than cause and effect.**
</div>

**Concept —** urgency is the **perceived output of an afferent system** — too much signal from urothelium, muscle or neighbouring organs, or too little central filtering — which is why **DO, obstruction and bladder findings correlate only loosely with OAB** and why psychological state and behaviour modulate it.
{: .concept}

## Clinical Assessment

### Initial Evaluation

- OAB is a **symptom-based diagnosis**; **empiric treatment** may follow history and non-invasive testing.
- **Minimum (AUA/SUFU non-neurogenic OAB guideline): careful history, physical examination, urinalysis**; European Association of Urology (EAU) male and female LUTS guidelines agree and encourage **validated questionnaires and bladder diaries**.

#### History

- **Each OAB symptom and its bother**; other storage (SUI) and **voiding symptoms suggesting BOO**; post-micturition symptoms, dysuria, haematuria. Questionnaires help but are limited by **health literacy and language**.
- **Fluid intake** — caffeine, polydipsia; patients may already restrict.
- **Occult neurologic disease** — new OAB with **erectile dysfunction or tremor**, spinal surgery/disc disease.
- Obstetric/gynaecologic, surgical, radiotherapy, **bowel** (faecal incontinence 7–15% in general population, **~50% in women with OAB**) and medication history.
- **Comorbidities** that cause LUTS (polyuric diabetes), **relatively contraindicate antimuscarinics** (**uncontrolled closed-angle glaucoma, cognitive impairment, retention, impaired gastric emptying**) or **β3 agonists** (**uncontrolled hypertension, arrhythmia**), or ↑ urine output (**obstructive sleep apnoea, psychogenic polydipsia, lithium-induced diabetes insipidus**).

#### Physical Examination

- **Abdomen, pelvis, extremities, basic neurology, cognition and frailty — even when the diagnosis seems straightforward.**
- General appearance: gait, mobility, stature, frailty (performance status, neurology).
- Abdomen: scars, masses, hernias, tenderness, **suprapubic distension**.
- **Lower-limb oedema** — fluid shifts driving **nocturia**.
- **Pelvic/genital examination (± digital rectal examination in men) — the most important component**: women — pelvic floor tone, pain, **prolapse**, urethral prolapse/caruncle, labial adhesions, **vaginal atrophy (oestrogen status)**, supine stress test; men — **prostate size and abnormality**; all — **perineal sensation and rectal tone**.
- **Cognition** — observe during interview; **Mini-Mental State Examination** if concerned.
- **Frailty** predicts poor surgical outcome; **Timed Up and Go Test** (rise, walk 10 feet) is quick.

#### Urinalysis

- **Mandatory** — exclude UTI, haematuria, pyuria; culture if infection suspected; **microhaematuria → full evaluation**.

### Additional Assessment

#### Other Laboratory Testing

- **Renal function not routine** (review if available); **prostate-specific antigen (PSA)** in men if it alters management; **culture** for acute irritative symptoms with a negative dipstick (dipstick misses low counts).

#### Postvoid Residual Volume

- **Not required before lifestyle, behavioural or oral therapy in uncomplicated patients.**
- **Measure PVR in complicated patients:** retention risk, obstructive/voiding symptoms (**especially men**), prior anti-incontinence or prostate surgery, neurologic disease.
- **Portable bladder ultrasound preferred**; catheterisation if unavailable.

#### Bladder Diary

- Records **intake and voiding**: useful when history is poor, recall unreliable, or **polyuria** suspected; guides behavioural change; **objective response measure**; self-monitoring itself therapeutic.
- **3–7 days ideal; at least 24–48 h** (burden); time, volume, leakage, circumstances, urgency grade.
- **OAB diary = frequent small-volume voids.** **Daytime frequency counts voids while awake, including the last before sleep and the first on rising.** **Maximum voided volume = functional capacity** (tracks OAB and DO severity). Electronic/smartphone diaries improve compliance.

<p class="tcap">TABLE 110.4 Bladder Sensation Scale Used in the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire Bladder Diary (Bright et al., 2012)</p>

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **0** | No sensation — voided for **social reasons** ("just in case") |
| **1** | **Normal desire, no urgency** |
| **2** | Urgency that **passed before reaching the toilet** |
| **3** | Urgency, reached toilet still urgent, **no leakage** |

- **Trap:** grade 0 is **convenience voiding**, not a mild symptom; leakage is recorded separately, not as a sensation grade.

#### Patient-Reported Outcome Measures and Symptom Questionnaires

- PROMs capture frequency, severity and bother — for diagnosis and **monitoring response**.
- **International Consultation on Incontinence (ICI)** grades PROMs **A–C (+ for published content validity)**; **ICIQ modules** (from 1996) — standard, modular; **pair a symptom module with its HRQoL module**.
- **For OAB: ICIQ-MLUTS / ICIQ-FLUTS (sex-specific general), ICIQ-OAB + ICIQ-OABqol, core ICIQ-UI**.
- **AUA/SUFU recommends: Urogenital Distress Inventory (UDI), UDI-6, Incontinence Impact Questionnaire (IIQ), OAB-q.**
- **LURN Symptom Index** (Lower Urinary Tract Research Network) — **both sexes, full LUTS spectrum**; 29-item (27 + 2 sex-specific) and 10-item forms.

### Advanced Evaluation

- **Urodynamics, cystoscopy and renal/bladder ultrasound are not for the initial work-up of the uncomplicated patient.**
- **Urodynamics indicated when compliance, DO or other findings will decide for or against an invasive, irreversible or morbid treatment, or after failure of multiple treatments**; also **suspected BOO** (e.g. OAB after an outlet procedure). May improve satisfaction but **not proven to improve outcome for the index patient** (woman, low PVR, no significant prolapse, no neurologic disease, minimal SUI).
- **Aim: reproduce the symptoms**; two OAB diagnoses — **DO** and **early/exaggerated filling sensation** ("early and persistent desire to void" when DO absent).
- **Stopping antimuscarinics before testing:** stop → best chance of seeing DO; continue → explains refractory symptoms.
- **DO vs symptoms (Fig. 110.5):** DO correlates **best with UUI, worst with nocturia**. **OAB dry: DO in ~60–70% of men and 40–50% of women; OAB wet: DO in nearly all men**, but **absent in 30–40% of women with reported UUI** (SUI and pre-emptive voiding misread as urgency).
- **Technique:** use provocations (running water, standing); **fast filling provokes DO** and may obscure other parameters; test **seated/standing** (symptoms occur upright); if severe low-volume DO hides SUI → **second fill supine** (reduces DO).
- **Always assess BOO and emptying:** (1) OAB treatment may precipitate retention — **each 10 mL of PVR ↑ risk of catheterisation after onabotulinumtoxinA by ~27%**; (2) severe BOO associated with DO — OAB may not improve unless BOO treated, yet **relief of BOO does not guarantee improvement (DO in up to ~60% after TURP)**.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**Key Points: Overactive Bladder Clinical Assessment**

- **Minimum: history, examination, urinalysis.**
- Consider **malignancy, neurologic disease, systemic disease, elevated PVR**.
- Urine culture and PVR at the provider's discretion when indicated.
- **Diaries and validated PROMs** clarify symptoms and track response.
- **No urodynamics, cystoscopy or ultrasound in uncomplicated initial work-up.**
- **Urodynamics when findings will change an invasive/irreversible decision, or after multiple failed treatments.**
</div>

**Concept —** because OAB is a **symptomatic diagnosis of exclusion**, assessment is built to **exclude what mimics it** (infection, malignancy, neurology, retention, polyuria) with minimal testing; invasive testing is justified **only when its answer will change an irreversible decision**.
{: .concept}

## Management

- **OAB is chronic, affects QoL, and is not life-threatening** — counsel in that context. **Choosing no treatment is acceptable** after counselling (benefits may not outweigh costs; severe cognitive impairment).
- Address **mobility limits** (correcting leakage will not correct immobility); **incontinence-associated dermatitis** (pain, infection, pressure ulcers) may justify more aggressive treatment.
- **Set goals and realistic expectations** — improvement, not cure; gradual and irregular; goals may change; **critical before invasive therapy**.
- **Education** empowers and itself improves perception; **substantial placebo response in OAB trials** shows the power of non-pharmacologic intervention.
- **AUA/SUFU lines (first–fourth) balance benefit against invasiveness, adverse events and reversibility — not a mandatory linear path**; lines are often combined.

### Conservative Management

- Behavioural therapy and lifestyle: **bladder training, urge-control strategies, pelvic floor muscle therapy (PFMT), weight loss, fluid management** — mostly studied for UI in women; improvement, rarely resolution.
- **Fluid:** **~25% reduction meaningfully improves symptoms** (cost: thirst, underhydration); many already restrict.
- **Caffeine reduction improves urgency and frequency but not consistently UI** — let the patient judge the trade-off.
- **Weight loss:** reduces SUI and **UUI** (intensive programme in obese women); **bariatric surgery ~7–8% lower UI risk per 5% weight lost**.
- **PFMT and bladder training: incontinence episodes fall 50–80%**, negligible risk; **combine with drugs** according to preference.

### Pharmacologic Management

<p class="tcap">Comparison — Oral Drug Classes for OAB</p>

| | Antimuscarinics | β3 agonists (e.g. mirabegron) | α1-blockers (men) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Role | Established; cure ↑ 20–70% over placebo; **high withdrawal** | **Preferred agents (SUFU)** | **First-line for male voiding LUTS** |
| Key adverse effects | Dry mouth, constipation, **cognitive impairment**, blurred vision | — | — |
| Relative contraindications | Uncontrolled closed-angle glaucoma, cognitive impairment, retention, impaired gastric emptying | **Uncontrolled hypertension, arrhythmia** | — |
| Formulation | **Extended-release preferred** (less dry mouth and CNS effect) | — | Add antimuscarinic/β3 for storage LUTS |
| Dementia | **Chronic use (>3 months) → 10–65% ↑ new-onset dementia** | Avoids anticholinergic burden | — |

- **SUFU white paper: prefer β3 agonists, or extended-release trospium, darifenacin or fesoterodine** (limited CNS penetration); **shared decision-making** before anticholinergics.
- **Responses are idiosyncratic** — titrate; if inadequate or intolerable, change dose or agent.
- **Men with BPH + OAB:** α1-blocker + antimuscarinic beats either alone, but **acute retention up to ~3%** → **caution when PVR is raised**.
- **Initial failure → dose change, different agent or combination** before invasive work-up.

### Procedural or Surgical Management

- **"Third-line" (AUA/SUFU): after failure of or unsuitability for conservative and drug therapy** — may be chosen earlier for medical, social or personal barriers; more invasive, more adverse events.
- **Frail elderly:** respond well, but **frailty ↑ morbidity** (functional decline, discharge to care, high 1-year morbidity even after minor procedures).

<p class="tcap">Comparison — Third-Line Therapies for Refractory OAB</p>

| | PTNS (percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation) | Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA | SNS (sacral nerve stimulation) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Efficacy | **Response ~60–80%** | **UI episodes ↓ ~59%, urgency ↓ ~38%, frequency ↓ ~29%; ~¼ completely dry** | **5-year success ~70–80%** (↓ UUI, ↓ voids) |
| Main drawback | **Weekly office sessions for 12 weeks; response delayed to weeks 8–10** | **UTI ~20%**; CIC 0–12% in trials, **retention ~20% women, up to 35% men in practice** | **Revision >30% at 5 years**; battery replacement inevitable |
| Advantage | Minimal adverse events | **Most cost-effective (100 U)** | Rechargeable, longer battery, **MRI-compatible** devices |
| Cost | — | Lowest of third-line | **Costliest over 10 years** |

- **Choice (including declining treatment) is by patient preference after informed discussion of benefits and drawbacks.**

### Augmentation Cystoplasty or Urinary Diversion

- **Rarely** for intractable OAB; evidence mainly neurogenic → **little known of QoL in non-neurogenic OAB**; weigh against **containment (pads) or indwelling catheter**.

### Approach

- **ICI (sixth consultation)** grades evidence and gives care pathways for **men, women and the frail elderly** (Fig. 110.6); AUA/SUFU algorithm and SUFU OAB care pathway concur.
- **Common sequence:** **assess to confirm OAB and exclude alternatives → educate → conservative (lifestyle, behavioural, PFMT) → pharmacologic → invasive (tibial stimulation, SNM, chemodenervation)**; reassess symptoms, side effects and preferences at every step.
- **Minimum trial before judging efficacy: 4–12 weeks for behavioural and for drug therapy.**

| | Men | Women | Frail elderly |
|---|---|---|---|
| Presentation | Urgency/frequency ± incontinence | Incontinence with mixed symptoms | **Active case finding** |
| Initial assessment | Symptom score (incl. frequency–volume chart), QoL, urinalysis ± culture; **abdominal, rectal, sacral, neurologic examination**; pelvic floor function; **PVR** | Same general assessment; **abdominal, pelvic, perineal examination**; cough test; **oestrogen status**; voluntary pelvic floor contraction; PVR | **Treat reversible comorbidities**; goals incl. **carer preference**; **targeted examination incl. cognition**, neurologic and rectal; urinalysis; **frequency–volume chart or wet checks, especially with nocturia** |
| Initial management | Lifestyle; PFMT ± biofeedback; bladder training; products; **antimuscarinic (OAB ± UUI) and α-blocker if also BOO** | Lifestyle; PFMT for SUI or OAB; bladder retraining; **antimuscarinic** | Lifestyle, behavioural therapy; **trial of antimuscarinic**; **treat significant PVR** |
| On failure | **Specialist:** urodynamics, urinary tract imaging, urethrocystoscopy if indicated; **treat major component first** → α-blocker/5α-reductase inhibitor or correct BOO; antimuscarinic; **neuromodulation**; intermittent catheterisation | **Specialist:** assess prolapse, imaging, urodynamics; **treat most bothersome symptom first** → SUI surgery (bulking, tapes/slings, colposuspension) **or** botulinum toxin, neuromodulation, augmentation | Reassess comorbidity and function; **refer per patient preference and comorbidity** |

- **Pattern:** men — **identify and treat obstruction**; women — **stress vs urgency, treat the most bothersome first**; frail elderly — **comorbidity, function and carer goals before escalation**.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**Key Points: Clinical Management**

- **Conservative:** caffeine and fluid reduction, weight loss, bladder training, PFMT.
- **Pharmacologic:** antimuscarinics (oral or transdermal) or β3 agonists — **caution with chronic antimuscarinics (dementia risk)**.
- **Procedural:** tibial neuromodulation, botulinum toxin, sacral neuromodulation — **shared decision-making**.
- **Augmentation or diversion** for the refractory, weighed against containment or indwelling catheter.
</div>

**Concept —** OAB treatment escalates by **invasiveness and reversibility, not by severity alone**, because the condition is benign and chronic; every step is gated by **the patient's goals and tolerance**, and **no treatment** remains a legitimate endpoint.
