Chapter 155 — Prostate Biopsy: Techniques and Imaging

  • Transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS) is a standard urological tool underpinning initial and active-surveillance biopsy, low- and high-dose-rate brachytherapy, cryotherapy and high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU).
  • Radiotherapy: fiducial and radiofrequency markers for real-time tracking; polyethylene glycol hydrogel spacer in anterior perirectal fat to cut rectal dose and toxicity. Benign: benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) planning, some male infertility. Commonest use: cancer biopsy.
  • Detection improved with prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening and systematic biopsy; screening remains controversial.
  • Historic gold standard: 12-core TRUS-guided transrectal biopsy under local anaesthesia, now rapidly challenged by the TRUS-guided transperineal approach (Europe, Australia, United States).
  • Historical sequence: TRUS first described by Watanabe (1968) → digitally directed biopsy, common until the late 1980s → TRUS-directed biopsy → systematic sextant (six-core) protocol, Hodge (1989) → recommended cores increased to 12, while PSA screening expanded the biopsied population.
  • Up to 1.3 million biopsies a year in the United States. With significant and insignificant cancer both prevalent, effort centres on indications, technique and limiting complications.

ULTRASONOGRAPHIC ANATOMY OF THE PROSTATE

  • Between bladder neck and urogenital diaphragm, directly anterior to the rectum: ideal for transrectal imaging.
  • Pathological zones: anterior fibromuscular stroma (no glandular tissue), transition zone (TZ), central zone (CZ), periurethral zone and peripheral zone (PZ). They are not visible sonographically as distinct entities.
  • What TRUS does separate, especially in glands with significant BPH:
    • Posterior CZ and PZ, where most adenocarcinomas arise: homogeneous and echogenic.
    • Anterior TZ: heterogeneous.
  • Corpora amylacea: surgical-capsule calcifications outlining the PZ–TZ plane. Diffuse calcification is normal and age-related; larger symptomatic calculi may reflect infection or inflammation and need evaluation.
  • Prostatic urethra: midline, so seen whole only in the sagittal plane; hypoechoic lumen, periurethral calcification may outline it. Internal sphincter (bladder neck → verumontanum) forms a hypoechoic ring, funnelling the proximal urethra. At the verumontanum it angles anteriorly, giving an anteriorly concave sagittal course.
  • Seminal vesicles (SVs): paired, posterior at the base, smooth, saccular and symmetrical. Normal size 4.5–5.5 cm long × 2 cm wide.
    • Cystic SV mass: presumptively benign. Solid SV mass: significant probability of malignancy, especially with a primary neoplasm elsewhere. Consider schistosomiasis for a solid mass in endemic areas.
    • Absent SV → ipsilateral renal agenesis in 79%.
  • Vasa and ejaculatory ducts: ampullae run above the ipsilateral SV, dive caudally medial to it and fuse with it to form the ejaculatory duct, which enters posteriorly (occasionally hypoechoic) and empties at the verumontanum, paralleling the distal urethra.

Concept — Zonal anatomy is pathological, not sonographic. TRUS reliably shows only the posterior echogenic PZ/CZ versus the anterior heterogeneous TZ, and that is the distinction biopsy needs, because cancer is mostly posterior.

GREY-SCALE TRANSRECTAL ULTRASONOGRAPHY

  • Commonest prostate imaging modality: cancer detection, infertility, volume for brachytherapy and BPH.
  • Probes: side-fire, end-fire, biplane; 6–10 MHz; presets on modern machines. Biplane (axial + sagittal linear array) suits brachytherapy, ablation and transperineal work. End- and side-fire scan an arc of nearly 180°. End-fire vs side-fire: no difference in detection, initial or repeat (prospective studies).

  • Coupling medium: ultrasound does not cross air, so gel sits between transducer and rectum; with a sheath, both inside and outside the sheath.

Machine Settings

  • Magnification: low for measurement (whole gland on one screen); high for biopsy (to follow the needle).
  • Gain: set per patient (sometimes per region) so the normal PZ is medium grey, the reference for hypoechoic (darker), isoechoic, hyperechoic (lighter) and anechoic (black).

Techniques

  • Complete evaluation: scan in sagittal and transverse planes to calculate volume → inspect CZ and PZ for hypoechoic lesions and contour abnormalities → assess periprostatic fat planes → visualise SVs and vasa fully.

Volume Calculations

  • Planes: transverse and anteroposterior (AP) at the widest axial diameter; longitudinal sagittally, just off midline (bladder neck obscures the cephalad extent). AP is better sagittal (perpendicular to longitudinal); axial AP overestimates if the plane is oblique.

  • Despite the geometric assumptions, all formulas correlate >0.90 with radical prostatectomy specimen weight, because 1 cm³ ≈ 1 g of prostate.
  • Normal size: mature gland 20–25 g, stable until about age 50; average at 60–70 years ≈ 48 g.
  • AUA BPH/lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) guideline (2021): assess size (TRUS, abdominal ultrasound, cystoscopy or cross-sectional imaging) before intervention, as drugs and operations suit specific size ranges; size also shapes cancer surgery.
  • Planimetry, used when precision matters (brachytherapy planning): lithotomy → probe mounted on a stepper → serial transverse images at set intervals (3–5 mm) through the gland → area of each → sum and integrate to give volume.
  • PSA density (PSAD) = serum PSA ÷ volume: separates benign from malignant causes of raised PSA with a benign digital rectal examination (DRE). >0.15 ng/mL/cc suggests malignancy. For repeat-biopsy cancer: moderate sensitivity, poor specificity; high operator variability. Free-to-total PSA, other biomarkers and multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) now give similar information.

Cystic Lesions of the Prostate

  • Common on TRUS. Simple cysts look the same as elsewhere in the body: thin-walled, anechoic, with posterior acoustic enhancement. Congenital or acquired; rarely clinically significant.

Prostate Cancer Imaging on Transrectal Ultrasonography

  • Once thought pathognomonic; now sample every hypoechoic PZ lesion, but absence does not preclude biopsy: 39% of cancers are isoechoic, up to 1% hyperechoic.
  • Split evidence: one large series found equal detection with or without a lesion; another found lesion cores almost twice as often positive, so targeting remains worthwhile.
  • False-positive hypoechoic lesions: commonest are BPH foci and acute or chronic prostatitis; also granulomatous prostatitis, prostatic infarct and lymphoma.
  • Note contour abnormalities and PZ asymmetry. Extraprostatic extension (EPE): focal loss of bright periprostatic fat; microfoci invisible.

Key points: grey-scale transrectal ultrasonography

  • Zonal anatomy is not visible, but PZ is distinguishable from TZ, so biopsies can be aimed at the PZ.
  • Benign cysts: thin-walled with through-transmission; may become symptomatic if infected.
  • Planimetry with a template is needed for highly accurate volume (brachytherapy).
  • Sample hypoechoic foci, but up to 39% of cancers are invisible on grey scale.

PROSTATE BIOPSY: TECHNIQUES AND OUTCOMES

Indications for Prostate Biopsy

  • Screening debate: Chapter 157. Pre-PSA: DRE suspicion → digitally directed biopsy; PSA screening made TRUS biopsy standard of care. TRUS without biopsy has little value for cancer.
  • Initial biopsy detects cancer in ≈30%. Core needle biopsy is the technique of choice; fine-needle aspiration is obsolete.
  • Screening raised organ-confined, curable disease but brought overdiagnosis and overtreatment.
  • Historic trigger: PSA >4.0 ng/mL. The Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial (PCPT) showed no safe PSA threshold at any age: with PSA ≤4.0, 15% had cancer, and nearly 15% of those were Gleason ≥7.
Screening trials: ERSPC versus PLCO
TrialResult on prostate cancer mortality
European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer (ERSPC)Net benefit

Reanalysis of both using mean lead-time estimatesBoth give “compatible evidence that screening reduces prostate cancer mortality”. The accompanying editorial: the question is no longer whether screening can do good, but whether behaviour can change so that it does more good than harm
  • Organisations range from informed decision-making to rejecting screening. Weigh health, age, family history, options, wishes and growing concern over biopsy morbidity and mortality; online nomograms help.
  • Elevated risk: age >50; family history and >45; African ancestry; baseline PSA >1 ng/mL at 40 or >2 ng/mL at 60.
  • Absolute PSA cut-offs largely abandoned for risk stratification and PSA kinetics. Adjuncts (free-to-total ratio, velocity, PSAD) are not uniformly reliable; genomic markers address PSA's poor specificity; inherited risk increasingly recognised.

Contraindications to Prostate Biopsy

  • Contraindications: significant coagulopathy; severe immunosuppression; acute urinary tract infection (UTI) or prostatitis.
  • Painful anorectal disease or anal stenosis → general or regional anaesthesia.
  • No anus (after abdominoperineal resection) → in-bore MRI biopsy, or transperineal biopsy with an end-fire probe on the perineum (theatre under general, or local/regional by an experienced sonographer). Limitation: the prostate lies centimetres away, so definition is poor, especially base and SVs.

Preparing Patients for Biopsy

  • Informed consent; stop herbal supplements (undeclared agents); a little urine in the bladder shows the prostatovesical junction.

Antibiotic Prophylaxis

  • Rising complications, mostly infective admissions, renewed the focus on prophylaxis.
  • Transrectal biopsy is Class III (contaminated): unlike other lower-tract procedures, prophylaxis for every patient, regardless of risk factors.
  • AUA 2019 best practice: fluoroquinolones; first- or second-generation cephalosporin ± aminoglycoside; third-generation cephalosporin (evidence level Ib). Intramuscular acceptable for all; oral only for quinolones. Give ≥1 hour before; ≤24 hours.
  • Transient bacteraemia is common, usually silent. Endocarditis or prosthetic joint, pacemaker or defibrillator risk: IV ampicillin (vancomycin if penicillin-allergic) + gentamicin → oral fluoroquinolone for 2–3 days.
  • Food and Drug Administration warning (2016): tendinitis and rupture, peripheral neuropathy, central nervous system effects, myasthenia gravis → AUA caps fluoroquinolone prophylaxis at 24 hours.
  • Cochrane review (2011): antibiotics cut bacteriuria, bacteraemia, fever, UTI and admission versus placebo; longer or multi-dose courses no better than short or single-dose.

Table 155.2 Risk Factors for Prostate Biopsy Infection

  • Urological history: previous UTI or prostatitis; previous biopsy infection; greater cumulative number of biopsies.

Concept — The transrectal needle inoculates rectal flora into the prostate with every core. Prophylaxis therefore has to be universal, and its failures are driven by fluoroquinolone resistance.

Cleansing Enema

  • Self-administered; improves the acoustic window; no proven effect on infection, so some omit it.

Analgesia

  • TRUS-guided periprostatic block: 1–2% lidocaine, 7-inch 22-gauge spinal needle via the biopsy channel. Variants: 5 mL each side at the hyperechoic SV–prostate junction fat pad (authors'); 10 mL from SV junction along the lateral gland, base to apex; intraprostatic injection augments. Saturation: up to 22 mL of 1%; avoid intravascular injection.
  • Topical intrarectal anaesthetic is inferior to periprostatic block; useful when probe insertion is tender.

Patient Positioning

  • Left lateral decubitus, knees and hips at 90°, arm board, pillow between knees, buttocks flush with the table edge; right lateral or lithotomy if needed.
  • Lithotomy preferred for: transperineal biopsy, brachytherapy planning, Doppler and contrast studies (gravity distorts vascular patterns), fiducial markers.

Transrectal Prostate Biopsy Techniques

  • Sequence: DRE (nodularity, anal pathology) → volume → transverse and sagittal survey from base to apex, noting lesions (hypo- or hyperechoic, calcification, contour, cysts) → biopsy.
  • Spring-driven 18-gauge gun through the needle guide; path best seen sagittally on a ruled puncture line.
  • Mechanics: throw 15–25 mm; core 1.0–1.5 cm; tip runs 0.5 cm beyond the sample, so advancing past the capsule samples anterior tissue and misses the PZ. Technique: indent the capsule → withdraw a few millimetres → fire.
  • Less bleeding and pain: never reposition with the needle on the rectal wall; compress the mucosa with the probe (like pulling skin taut for phlebotomy).
  • Specimens: 10% formalin. AUA white paper: site-specific labelling not shown to change management; ≤2 cores per jar (overpacking lowers detection).

Sextant Biopsy

  • Hodge (1989): base, mid and apex bilaterally, parasagittal; a major advance on digitally and lesion-directed biopsy.
  • Flaw: parasagittal cores took much TZ, little PZ, while most cancers arise in the posterolateral PZ (McNeal): hence sextant false negatives.

Extended-Core Biopsy Techniques

  • Laterally directed cores came first and consistently raise detection; six cores are now inadequate.
  • Extended 12-core (adds apical and far-lateral cores): maximal detection, fewer repeat biopsies, fewer insignificant diagnoses; AUA-endorsed.
  • 6 → 12 cores raises detection; 12 → 18–21 (saturation) initially does not. Yet initial saturation meant less cancer on later biopsy, and later cancers were mostly insignificant. Saturation is mainly for prior negatives.
  • TZ and SVs not routinely sampled (low initial yield). TZ/anterior cores: persistent PSA after negative biopsy, or initially if gland >50 mL; MRI now finds anterior tumours. SV biopsy: palpable abnormality; some add PSA >30 ng/mL or pre-brachytherapy.

Repeat and Saturation Prostate Biopsy

  • Negative biopsies with persisting concern is common; yield falls with each successive biopsy.
  • ERSPC: after initial sextant, yield on biopsies 2–4 was 22% → 10% → 5% → 4%. Positive predictive value (PPV) held steady for biopsy-naive men across rounds but fell for previously negative men; repeat biopsies were a quarter of all biopsies yet found under a tenth of aggressive cancers.
  • Saturation (>12 cores) after negatives: early series improved detection, but anaesthetic needs mean hospital, and a later large prospective study found no significant gain.
  • Second biopsy justified if concern persists; third and fourth only for high suspicion or poor prognostic features; target the apex. Now add genomic testing of prior tissue and mpMRI.

Transurethral Prostate Biopsy

  • Once used for TZ cancer or after negative TRUS. Solitary TZ cancers: <5%. Now largely obsolete: TRUS under local samples the TZ, MRI (especially fusion) finds anterior cancers, and transperineal biopsy reaches them.

Concept — Biopsy strategy evolved by following where cancer actually lies, from palpable nodules to the posterolateral PZ and then to anterior and apical tumours. Each gain came from better geometry, not more cores: 12 beats 6, but 21 does not beat 12.

Risks and Complications of Transrectal Prostate Biopsy

  • Serious complications needing admission: <2%, but in a large population series 30-day admissions rose (1.0% → 4.1%), mostly infective.
  • PLCO and ERSPC: no excess mortality; infection <1%. Fear alone should not deter healthy men with long life expectancy.

Postbiopsy Infections

  • Fluoroquinolone-resistant E. coli dominates, bred by fluoroquinolone use in health care and the food supply. Admissions and fatal sepsis are rising, but sepsis mortality is no higher than for similar infections.
  • Sepsis: systemic inflammatory response to infection (fever, hypothermia, tachypnoea, tachycardia, confusion, hypotension). Septic shock: hypotension despite adequate fluid resuscitation. Any post-biopsy fever → assess for sepsis.
  • Prevention: swab-targeted prophylaxis has some utility; no bowel preparation helps; transperineal is promising.
  • Sensitivities: resistant to fluoroquinolones, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, ampicillin; susceptible to second- and third-generation cephalosporins, amikacin, carbapenems. Risk factors as Table 155.2, plus number of prior biopsies (active surveillance).
  • Treatment: no empirical fluoroquinolone → carbapenem for sepsis until cultures → up to 2 weeks if admitted.

Bleeding

  • Intermediate bleeding-risk procedure: hold antithrombotics and herbal remedies by class; low-dose aspirin may continue.
  • Persistent brisk rectal bleeding, escalation: direct pressure (probe or finger) → tamponade with an inflated probe sheath or Foley balloon → anoscopy or colonoscopy with adrenaline and polidocanol or other sclerosants → angiographic embolisation → transrectal exploration and suturing.

Other Complications

  • Vasovagal reaction stems from anxiety and probe discomfort. Any biopsy may raise the IPSS, but only transiently.
  • Erectile dysfunction: first blamed on neurovascular bundle injury, but data conflict. It is worse in men diagnosed with cancer, which implicates anxiety.

Concept — Transrectal biopsy's complications split by route. Bleeding is frequent but trivial; infection is rare but dangerous, and the danger tracks antibiotic resistance. This asymmetry drives both the prophylaxis rules and the move to the perineum.

  • Doppler beats grey scale alone, but many cancers stay invisible to every sonographic mode.
  • Rationale: angiogenesis raises microvessel density, which tracks metastasis, stage and disease-specific survival and is higher in cancer than surrounding tissue.
  • Prognosis: colour flow in the dominant tumour → markedly more PSA recurrence after radical retropubic prostatectomy, higher grade, more SV invasion; power Doppler signal also tracks Gleason score.
  • Unenhanced Doppler cannot see tumour microvessels (10–15 µm); it detects larger feeding vessels.
  • Microbubbles (as in echocardiography): 1–10 µm air or heavy gas in albumin or polymer shells, or lipid or surfactant coats; they amplify microvascular signal.
  • Contrast-enhanced TRUS (CE-TRUS): higher sensitivity, specificity preserved; targeting enhancing lesions improves detection; recommended for routine care by a European multicentre trial; 3D enhanced power Doppler adds accuracy (Fig. 155.7: lesion seen only after contrast).
  • Flash-replenishment imaging (software): high-power flash pulses destroy microbubbles → low-power pulses show replenishment → maximum-intensity composite of the vascular architecture, down to capillary level → real-time targeted biopsy. Targeted cores are more often positive than systematic cores.
  • Elastography may outperform Doppler: stiff tissue suggests malignancy (Fig. 155.8: stiff right-base focus, Gleason 4+4=8). Early: manual compression strain, operator-dependent; newer: beam compression or shear-wave velocity, operator-independent. Shear-wave stiffness rises with Gleason score, but targeted biopsy often missed cancer in a large series, so it complements, not replaces, systematic biopsy.

Other Newer and Investigational Imaging and Biopsy Techniques

  • Prostate HistoScanning: proprietary backscatter tissue analysis; unreliable for cancer and inferior to mpMRI.

Multiparametric Prostate Ultrasound

  • Ultrasound analogue of mpMRI: grey scale + vascularity (Doppler, contrast) + stiffness; alternative or complement to MRI.

Micro-Ultrasound

  • 29 MHz (versus conventional 7–9 MHz) → resolution 70 µm (versus 150–300 µm), close to histological architecture.
  • Prostate Risk Identification using Micro-Ultrasound (PRI-MUS): validated lesion scoring analogous to PIRADS.
  • Multicentre registry: micro-ultrasound (MUS) targeting matched or slightly beat mpMRI for sensitivity and negative predictive value (NPV); specificity and PPV low for both. No IV contrast: key for renal insufficiency. Needs validation.

Multiparametric Prostate Magnetic Resonance Imaging

  • Dynamic contrast enhancement (DCE) and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) improve detection; AUA policy statement (2017). Tissue still required. Real-time MRI biopsy is hard, hence three targeting techniques: in-bore, cognitive, fusion.
  • PIRADS: v1 European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR), 2012 → v2 American College of Radiology (ACR) + ESUR + AdMeTech, 2015 (published 2016), adding technical standards and a reporting lexicon.
  • Likert 1–5 for clinically significant cancer: Gleason ≥7 and/or >0.5 cm³ and/or EPE. PZ and TZ/CZ scored separately. T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) and DWI scored 1–5; DCE only positive or negative.
  • Lesion >1.5 cm on T2WI, or highly suspicious for EPE → automatically PIRADS 5.

Transperineal Prostate Biopsy

  • Transperineal adoption may overtake the transrectal gold standard: fewer infections, stewardship by omitting antibiotics, apex and anterior access, better local analgesia. Slogan: “TREXIT”.
  • Uptake: nearly doubled in the United Kingdom; over half of biopsies in Australia and New Zealand, with geographic and socioeconomic variation.
  • European Association of Urology (EAU) 2023: transperineal preferred over transrectal for systematic biopsy to detect clinically significant cancer (strong rating).

Techniques

  • History: the transperineal route preceded the transrectal one. Barringer (1922) perineal needle biopsy, with mixed success → open perineal cut-down → transperineal needle aspiration under rectal finger guidance → modern TRUS-guided biopsy.
  • Positioning. Lithotomy, biplanar probe in rectum, needle through perineum; scrotum taped up and forward to put the perineum under tension, removing laxity so landmarks and targeting are reproducible.

Analgesia

  • Formerly theatre-based (still reasonable for many-puncture mapping); now freehand office biopsy under local is strongly supported, with very rare abandonment.
  • Anaesthetise: perineal skin, deep transverse perineal muscle, levator ani, capsule (pudendal and perineal nerves, pelvic plexus branches). Options include pudendal block.
  • Periapical triangle block (commonest). Boundaries: lateral, medial border of levator ani; medial, rhabdosphincter; posterior, external anal sphincter; superior, prostatic apex.
  • Sequence: skin and tract 1–2 mL 1% lidocaine via 23-gauge needle → 5 mL into the periapical triangle via 20-gauge 7-inch spinal needle → 3 mL along the capsule (periprostatic) → each step under ultrasound, watching the hydrodissection.
  • Tolerability: peak visual analogue scale <4. Most uncomfortable to least: anaesthetic injection → biopsy → probe insertion.

Antibiotic Prophylaxis

  • Skin prepared with povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine: no rectal flora is carried in.
  • Meta-analysis of randomised trials: significantly less infection than transrectal; admissions similar; low certainty.
  • Hence skin-flora prophylaxis only, and increasingly none: antibiotics did not change the very low sepsis or infection rates (adjusted for cores, sepsis unchanged, infection slightly higher without).

Templates

  • The metric is clinically significant cancer detection; transperineal access reaches every region, including apex and anterior/TZ.

Cancer Detection Rate of Transperineal Prostate Biopsy

  • At least non-inferior to transrectal. Evidence: sextant TRUS vs six transperineal fan cores (PSA >4): comparable, slightly favouring transperineal → randomised 6 vs 12 transperineal cores: 12 superior (mirroring TRUS) → randomised transperineal vs TRUS, 12 cores (8 PZ + 4 TZ), spinal, PSA 4–20: no difference → meta-analysis under local: comparable to historical TRUS, varying by technique (targeted, freehand, PrecisionPoint, coaxial).
  • Anterior-only cancers: 6–20%, which the transperineal route is uniquely placed to sample, as it is the apex.

Summary of Advantages of Transperineal Prostate Biopsy

  • All segments accessible, minimal learning curve, office-based, and potentially antibiotic-free and device-free. Randomised trials are testing whether it should become the new gold standard.
  • Future: genomic markers to select for initial and repeat biopsy; refined Doppler, contrast, elastography, multi- and biparametric MRI, transperineal TRUS/MRI fusion; PSMA-PET-CT/MRI for risk stratification and targeting.
  • Until trials report, systematic grey-scale TRUS 12-core biopsy ± targeting remains the gold standard in this chapter, although EAU 2023 already prefers transperineal.

Concept — The transperineal route wins on anatomy and microbiology at once. The needle enters through sterilisable skin rather than colonised rectum and runs along the gland's long axis, reaching the apex and anterior zone. Its only weakness, the lack of a single agreed template, is organisational rather than biological.

Key points: prostate biopsy

  • Imaging alone cannot diagnose cancer; tissue is required. Local anaesthesia is routine.
  • Transrectal: prophylaxis ≤24 hours; systematic biopsy ≥12 cores (AUA).
  • Prebiopsy mpMRI whenever possible; fusion or cognitive targeting improves accuracy.
  • Transperineal under local, with or without antibiotics, is an emerging alternative. Advanced ultrasound and PSMA imaging may improve detection.
