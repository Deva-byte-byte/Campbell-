Chapter 80 — Surgery of Testicular Tumours

Campbell-Walsh-Wein Urology · Part VII Male Genitalia · Chapter Notes

Management of the Testis Mass

History and Physical Examination, Ultrasonography, and Preorchidectomy Evaluation

  • Any testicular mass needs prompt, complete evaluation, especially at peak-incidence ages: the cancer is often rapidly progressive, and delay raises the intensity and morbidity of curative therapy.
  • Four components: history (growth rate, symptoms) → examination → scrotal ultrasound → serum tumour markers.
  • Examination covers the supraclavicular nodes, breasts and abdomen, then the scrotal contents and the mass itself.
  • Ultrasound is not mandatory but characterises the mass and records laterality; its key role is the contralateral testis (bilateral synchronous masses about 1%).
  • Serum tumour markers (STMs) support the diagnosis of germ cell tumour (GCT), give staging information and set the post-orchidectomy baseline.
  • A testicular prosthesis should be discussed before surgery.

Radical Orchidectomy

  • For suspected testicular malignancy, radical orchidectomy is the diagnostic and therapeutic treatment of choice.
  • Inguinal approach, giving early control of the spermatic cord, then removal of testis, epididymis and cord to the internal inguinal ring.

Technique

  • Set-up: supine. Prepare from above the umbilicus to mid-thigh, and the genitalia back to the perineum. Drape to expose the scrotum, ipsilateral anterior superior iliac spine and pubic tubercle. Examination under anaesthesia locates the external ring.
  • Sequence: 3–5 cm transverse incision along Langer lines over the canal (extend hockey-stick over the scrotum, or angle caudally from the outset, if needed) → incise external oblique fascia along the canal for about 4 cm (Weitlaner or Gelpi retractor) → identify and preserve the ilioinguinal nerve on the anterior cord → mobilise the cord at the pubic tubercle and encircle it with a Penrose drain → divide external spermatic fascia and cremaster → deliver the testis with upward pressure on the hemiscrotum → divide the gubernaculum → dissect the cord to the peritoneal reflection at the internal ring.
  • Vas and gonadal vessels are ligated and divided separately with non-absorbable suture, leaving a 1–2 cm tail, so the gonadal stump can be found and excised at a later retroperitoneal lymph node dissection (RPLND). Some surgeons drop the stump preperitoneally, deep to the internal ring. Because the vas is separated, it need not be removed at RPLND.
  • Closure: irrigate and check haemostasis → lay the ilioinguinal nerve back in the canal → close the external oblique aponeurosis → subcutaneous tissue in 1–2 layers → skin → scrotal support with fluffed dressings for 2–3 days to limit swelling and haematoma.

Partial Orchidectomy

  • Rationale: with survival now the norm, the aim is less long-term treatment morbidity without lost efficacy.
  • Indications (all three, highly selected): tumour is polar, ≤2 cm, and the contralateral testis is compromised or absent. It is justified only when the risk of anorchia outweighs the risk of local recurrence.
  • If malignancy is uncertain, perform inguinal exploration with early cord control and excisional biopsy ± frozen section.
  • With a normal contralateral testis, elective testis-sparing surgery should not be attempted.
  • Emerging: very small masses (<5 mm) with negative markers have a low rate of malignancy. They may be candidates for partial orchidectomy or, highly selectively, close surveillance.

Technique

  • Sequence: same as radical until delivery → isolate the testis with towels → intraoperative ultrasound to localise → hypothermia can be omitted if resection time is <30 minutes → incise the tunica albuginea sharply → excise the mass with a rim of normal seminiferous tubules (negative margin) → close the tunica with absorbable suture → three-point fixation in the dependent scrotum (gubernaculum and septum).
  • Incision orientation: vertical (long axis) for a ventral-midline approach vs horizontal for lesions medial or lateral to the midline. This spares the segmental arteries running just deep to the tunica.
  • Intratubular germ cell neoplasia in the surrounding parenchyma is debated: completion radical orchidectomy vs adjuvant radiotherapy to the remnant. The surgeon's view of this risk decides whether frozen section is used.
  • Traditionally, confirmed GCT received 18–20 Gy adjuvant radiotherapy. Spermatogenesis is then permanently lost, so in a solitary testis the main benefit of sparing is Leydig cell (androgen) function.
  • Selected patients can be observed without radiotherapy, even with carcinoma in situ: a meta-analysis of case series showed low local recurrence (about 7.5% at 3–5 years). More may be observed in future; this needs study.
  • Not debated: ipsilateral local recurrence → completion radical orchidectomy, regardless of prior adjuvant therapy or residual testicular function.

Delayed Orchidectomy

  • A minority present with diffuse metastatic and/or symptomatic GCT that needs immediate chemotherapy. Diagnosis is by biopsy of a metastatic site, or presumptively from clinical features ± markers.
  • Delayed radical orchidectomy is recommended for all such patients, whatever the response elsewhere, because chemotherapy response is discordant between the testis and other sites.
  • An apparent retroperitoneal/extragonadal primary is more controversial. Testicular biopsy shows intratubular germ cell neoplasia in up to 42%, and about 5% develop metachronous testicular cancer if observed after chemotherapy.
  • Retroperitoneal disease that lateralises in a testicular-primary pattern → orchidectomy on that side. Post-chemotherapy specimens mostly show teratoma or necrosis, i.e. a "burned-out primary" or complete response.
  • If observation is chosen: monthly testicular self-examination plus regular clinician examination.

Postorchidectomy Evaluation

  • Clinical stage = pathology + imaging + markers.
  • Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), with intravenous and oral contrast, images the retroperitoneum; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is an alternative.
  • Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) and lymphangiography have no important role in initial diagnosis and staging.
  • Repeat STMs after surgery: α-fetoprotein (AFP), β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). The trend guides subsequent management.

Key points: management of the testis mass

  • Suspected malignancy → inguinal radical orchidectomy.
  • Partial: polar, ≤2 cm, compromised/absent contralateral testis.
  • Chemotherapy-first presentation → delayed orchidectomy in all.

Concept — Testis-mass surgery is built to avoid violating the scrotum and to leave a findable cord stump for later staging surgery. Organ sparing is the exception, allowed only when anorchia is the greater harm, and the testis is always removed eventually because it responds to chemotherapy differently from metastases.

Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection

  • Spread is to the retroperitoneal nodes in a predictable, side-specific pattern; the technique is broadly the same in every setting.
  • Retroperitoneal node regions (Fig. 80.1): 1 paracaval · 2 precaval · 3 interaortocaval · 4 pre-aortic · 5 right iliac · 6 para-aortic · 7 left iliac · 8 inter-iliac · 9 right suprahilar · 10 left suprahilar.

Preoperative Planning

  • Review the CT for mass location and anatomical variants (e.g. duplicated renal vessels), avoiding unplanned intraoperative consultations.
  • Timing: CT within 6 weeks and STMs within 7–10 days of surgery. No bowel preparation.
  • Sperm banking is offered and recommended to all who want future paternity.
  • Bleomycin: pulmonary toxicity occurs in about 11% after bleomycin-based first-line chemotherapy vs 1.7% without, and grade 3–4 toxicity is fivefold higher. So: pulmonary function tests → any deficit to a pulmonologist → alert anaesthesia and all perioperative staff → low fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) and conservative fluids → confirm the plan directly at the surgical time-out.
  • Chest CT if there are prior lung lesions or simultaneous lung resection is planned.
  • Inferior vena cava (IVC) thrombus: total occlusion → IVC resection; incomplete occlusion → allograft reconstruction as needed.

Surgical Technique

  • Open RPLND: supine, arms in a T slightly below 90°. An orogastric tube is almost always enough. Midline incision from xiphoid to just below the umbilicus.
  • Transperitoneal (traditional) approach: enter sharply → divide the round ligament → release the falciform ligament towards the superior IVC, avoiding hepatic injury from retraction.
  • Extraperitoneal approach (selected patients): same incision, but the peritoneum is peeled off the fascia starting infraumbilically, so the bowel stays contained. It may shorten stay and speed return of bowel function.

Exposure of the Retroperitoneum

  • Sequence (Fig. 80.2): self-retaining retractor → incise from the tip of the caecum to the medial side of the inferior mesenteric vein (IMV) → for full mobilisation with bowel on the chest, incise the white line of Toldt up the ascending colon to the foramen of Winslow → for large left-sided tumours, divide the IMV → for a modified left approach, incise the white line of Toldt of the descending colon.
  • Develop the plane between mesentery and retroperitoneal fat, staying anterior to the gonadal vein and ureter. Free the duodenum and inferior pancreas off the renal vessels, IVC and aorta. Liberal clips here reduce postoperative chylous leak.

Split and Roll Technique (Video 80.1)

  • Top-down start over the left renal vein or IVC; ligate the cephalad lymphatics at the renal veins. Mass size often dictates the order.
  • Starting on the aorta: account for the postganglionic sympathetic nerves on the anterior aorta and the superior hypogastric plexus, which the right-sided fibres lead to.
  • Split at 12 o'clock on the aorta just below the left renal vein → carry caudally to the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) (Fig. 80.3). In nerve sparing, identify and preserve the postganglionic nerves at or before the IMA before going further caudally.

Left Para-Aortic Packet

  • Template: roll the descending colon medially (white line of Toldt). Bilateral: through the mesenteric root, small bowel cephalad.
  • Divide the left gonadal vein where it crosses the ureter, or move it medially. Retract the ureter laterally.
  • Split along the aorta and iliac vessels caudally to the ureteric crossing (about half of the left common iliac artery) → roll laterally → doubly ligate the lumbar arteries up to the renal hilum.
  • There are usually three pairs of lumbar arteries, evenly spaced from hilum to common iliacs. The second pair arises at the level of the IMA, which makes it a landmark.

Interaortocaval Packet

  • Right nerve-sparing: split-and-roll on the anterior IVC, ligating the gonadal vein. Non-nerve-sparing, starting on the aorta: control the medial aorta first.
  • Caudally along the IVC to the iliac bifurcation, then up the right common iliac artery to the ureteric crossing, where the right gonadal vein is ligated and the ureter retracted.
  • Lumbar veins (usually 2–3, variable): a superior right lumbar vein just below or behind the renal veins, and a central common lumbar vein, the largest, which may cross beneath the aorta and then need para-aortic ligation as well.
  • Relationship at the IMA level: the IMA, the common lumbar vein and a postganglionic sympathetic nerve (second infrarenal lumbar splanchnic nerve) crossing onto the anterior aorta. Identify the nerve prospectively and dissect it free.
  • Sequence: control the medial IVC → control the medial aorta → roll the tissue medially → ligate the three medial lumbar arteries (Fig. 80.4) → release the lymphatics cephalad towards the crus and anterior spinous ligament at the right renal artery and vein, ligating the cephalad lymphatics (chylous leak) → proceed caudally over the anterior spinous ligament to the left common iliac vein.
  • Watch lumbar vessels entering the body wall; identify the right renal artery and any accessory arteries.

Right Paracaval Packet

  • Smallest and easiest; tapers to almost nothing at the right renal vessels. In bilateral split-and-roll, release it at the right renal artery and pass it beneath the IVC en bloc with the interaortocaval packet.
  • Release it from psoas, sparing the genitofemoral nerve, down to the right common iliac vessels and ureteric crossing.
  • The right sympathetic trunk lies posterolateral to the IVC. It is often mistaken for lymphatic tissue or a vessel and should be preserved, especially in nerve sparing.

Gonadal Vein

  • Incise the overlying peritoneum, sweep the ureter off posteriorly (a left vein reached through the mesentery passes under the sigmoid), then resect with gentle traction to the internal ring and the orchidectomy suture.

Nerve Sparing

  • Antegrade ejaculation depends on four postganglionic sympathetic efferents (L1–L4), which vary. Surgically they often fuse into about three right fibres emerging beneath the IVC and about three left para-aortic fibres. Both run over the anterior aorta to the superior hypogastric plexus (Fig. 80.5).

Closure and Postoperative Care

  • Inspect for residual tissue, lymph leak and bleeding; clip or ligate leaks; warm irrigation reveals vessels in spasm.
  • Optional: reapproximate the posterior parietal peritoneum (bowel adhesion to the great vessels) and the mesenteric root (volvulus). Always inspect the bowel, liver, stomach and pancreas.
  • No routine drain. Consider one after large retroperitoneal, retrocrural or bowel resection, where third-spacing is large: a non-suction drain, removed when output is serous and <100 mL/24 h on a regular diet.
  • No nasogastric tube → clear fluids the evening of surgery → low-fat diet on days 1–2. Non-opioid analgesia (ketorolac, paracetamol, gabapentin) reduces opioid use and bowel dysfunction, and possibly stay. Mobilisation mandatory on day 1. Discharge at 3–5 days (target day 3). Individualised care overrides a standardised enhanced-recovery pathway.

Concept — RPLND is a landmark-driven vascular operation. Lymphatics are ligated at the cephalad limit (to prevent chyle leak), lumbar vessels are controlled at their predictable levels, and the sympathetic fibres are found by their fixed relationship to the IMA, the lumbar veins and the aorta. Nerve sparing means recognising the anatomy before anything is divided.

Auxiliary Procedures

  • Rare at primary RPLND; needed in 23–45% of PC-RPLND. Commonest: nephrectomy, then vascular. Larger residual mass → greater need.

Nephrectomy

  • Nephrectomy is the commonest auxiliary procedure (5–31%). The testes' primary lymphatic drainage lies at the great vessels near the renal hilum, and left-sided tumours drain to the para-aortic/left hilar region, so the left kidney is most at risk. Most adjunctive nephrectomies are left-sided.
  • Risk is higher in salvage, desperation and reoperative RPLND. The incidence has declined over three decades.
  • Need rises with histology: fibrosis < teratoma < viable cancer.
  • Strongest predictor: residual post-chemotherapy mass >10 cm (odds ratio about 9). The highest-risk combination is a left-sided residual mass >10 cm.
  • Why it matters: patients may receive 4–8 cycles of cisplatin; chronic kidney disease could limit future systemic therapy, though young patients' renal reserve usually avoids dialysis.

Major Vascular Reconstruction

  • Plan for vascular reconstruction in every PC-RPLND: fibrosis encases the great vessels, and subadventitial aortic injury can cause fatal haemorrhage.
  • High-volume series need vascular procedures in about 9–15%. The aorta is involved most often, then the IVC, iliac vessels and renal vessels.
  • Small wall injury → bovine/porcine pericardial patch, avoiding bypass. Complete vessel resection → polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) or Dacron graft (Fig. 80.6).
  • Tumour can invade the vessel wall with thrombus: intraluminal thrombus in about 6% at PC-RPLND, almost all in the IVC and/or renal vein; aortic thrombus is exceptional.

Inferior Vena Cava Resection

  • Options: interposition bypass graft, patch venoplasty, or lateral venorrhaphy/primary repair.
  • Primary repair is preferred when it will not cause significant narrowing, because the low-pressure IVC thromboses more readily than the arterial system.
  • Unstable patient with life-threatening bleeding → caval ligation or lateral venorrhaphy.
  • Historically 5–10% of PC-RPLND needed caval wall or whole-IVC resection (fibrosis, invasion, thrombus). Predictors are mass size and International Germ Cell Consensus Classification Group (IGCCCG) intermediate/poor risk. Mass >5 cm: about 20% need IVC intervention vs about 3% if <5 cm.
  • Ligation (long the Indiana standard) → chronic venous sequelae very rare in this young population. Reconstruction rarely thromboses and reduces lower-limb oedema compared with acute ligation.
  • Rule: expected narrowing <25% → primary repair/lateral venorrhaphy; >25% → patch venoplasty or interposition graft. A patch suits cases where the full circumference need not be resected (bovine/porcine pericardium, or cadaveric vein if xenograft is unwanted).

Aortic Reconstruction

  • Aortic dissection is dangerous in PC-RPLND, especially for seminoma.
  • Small injury → primary repair. Significant injury → almost always a graft. There is no ligation option, unlike the IVC.
  • A multidisciplinary (vascular) team must be immediately available.
  • Commonest injury: subadventitial dissection of a densely adherent mass → PTFE or Dacron replacement.
  • Long duodenal serosal injury with aortic injury → graft, to reduce the risk of delayed aortoduodenal fistula.
  • About 1% need aortic replacement, mostly after salvage chemotherapy or at desperation RPLND; a third were disease-free, with no graft complications. Aortic resection is justified for complete resection when systemic options are few.

Hepatic Resections

  • IGCCCG (1997): non-pulmonary visceral metastases = poor risk (about 48% 5-year survival). About 6% of advanced cases have liver metastases.
  • Addressed after primary, salvage or third-line chemotherapy: all residual disease is resected, including imaged liver lesions.
  • Liver and retroperitoneal histology are often discordant (about half), and most liver specimens show necrosis only. Retroperitoneal necrosis is highly predictive of hepatic necrosis (about 94%), yet about 1 in 5 with retroperitoneal fibrosis only still have teratoma or viable GCT in the liver. So residual liver masses without marker-positive disease should be resected or ablated.
  • Resection vs ablation: resection is more morbid, with equal recurrence, except higher after percutaneous ablation. So a residual lesion <3 cm not safely resectable → open microwave ablation at RPLND (limited GCT data). Small lesions in difficult sites may be biopsied and observed.

Pelvic Resections

  • Rare outside prior groin surgery, repeat RPLND or late relapse; mostly excised with PC-RPLND.
  • Histology: teratoma in over half, viable GCT in about a fifth, sarcomatous transformation, necrosis in about a sixth. The fibrosis rate is less than half that of PC-RPLND specimens, so resect completely whenever feasible. Surgery cures most patients.

Management of Supradiaphragmatic Disease

  • 10–20% have supradiaphragmatic disease at presentation or later. Spread is haematogenous → lung or lymphatic → mediastinal and cervical nodes. About 80% of mediastinal metastases are lower–middle mediastinal. Anterior mediastinal disease suggests a primary mediastinal GCT.
  • Retroperitoneal–thoracic histology discordance: 25–50%.
  • After thoracic resection long-term survival is common, equal for lung and mediastinal sites. Residual pathology: teratoma in about half, necrosis in about a fifth, persistent NSGCT, and degenerative non-germ cell cancer in about 10%. Predictors of survival: older age, non-germ cell cancer in the testis, residual pathology. → Resect residual thoracic disease >1 cm.
  • Thoracotomy series: retroperitoneal necrosis predicted thoracic necrosis in about 89%. → Defer thoracic management until after RPLND. If the retroperitoneum shows fibrosis only, observing the chest may be warranted. Involve a multidisciplinary team.

Resection of Retrocrural Disease

  • Hard to reach from the midline. Options: thoracoabdominal, left thoracotomy, thoracoscopic ± robotic; thoracotomy's morbidity favours combined approaches that avoid it.
  • Transabdominal transdiaphragmatic sequence (Fig. 80.7): standard RPLND via laparotomy → divide the gastrohepatic ligament → incise parallel to the right crus through the muscular diaphragm → lower posterior mediastinum. Masses above T8 → extend with partial sternal division; full sternotomy for subcarinal, pulmonary or anterior mediastinal disease. It gives excellent survival with low complication rates.
  • Retrocrural dissection at the same sitting is reasonable. Extensive thoracic resection (pulmonary metastasectomy, mediastinal resection) → staged procedure, because of prolonged anaesthesia and higher complication risk.

Key points: auxiliary procedures

  • Nephrectomy commonest (left-sided, large masses).
  • Plan for aortic replacement; primary IVC repair preferred to graft.
  • Discordance common: chest after RPLND; resect >1 cm unless retroperitoneum fibrosis only.
  • Retrocrural at RPLND; extensive thoracic → staged.

Concept — Chemotherapy response differs between organs, so histology at one site never guarantees histology at another. The retroperitoneum is cleared first because it is the best, though imperfect, predictor of what remains elsewhere, and the surgeon must be ready to take kidney, cava or aorta to make the resection complete.

Surgical Decision Making

Management of Clinical Complete Remission to Induction Chemotherapy

  • About 70% of patients with clinical stage II or higher GCT treated with cisplatin achieve a complete radiographic (no residual mass >1 cm) and serological remission. The options are observation or PC-RPLND. This remains controversial.

Use of Modified Templates in Primary Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection

  • Historical RPLND: bilateral infrahilar plus interiliac region to the common iliac bifurcation, often bilateral suprahilar, via thoracoabdominal incision: high morbidity, most anejaculatory, accepted before curative chemotherapy existed.
  • Revolution, 1970s–1980s: curative cisplatin chemotherapy (Einhorn and Donohue, 1977) + recognition of side-specific lymphatic spread + nerve-sparing techniques → lower-morbidity modified templates.
  • Ray (1974): the first templated modifications, focused on the primary landing zones of right- vs left-sided tumours.
  • Donohue mapping study (1982): left tumours → left para-aortic; right tumours → interaortocaval and precaval. Contralateral and suprahilar spread is rare but increases with retroperitoneal bulk; interiliac spread is very rare.
  • Weissbach and Boedefeld (1987): a reduced left template (para-aortic and pre-aortic) in non-bulky pathological stage II, with intraoperative frozen section to trigger a full template.
  • Consensus: omit interiliac and suprahilar regions in the absence of bulky disease. Omitting suprahilar dissection ↓ chylous ascites, renal injury, and pancreatic/duodenal injury. Omitting interiliac plus contralateral dissection helps preserve antegrade ejaculation. Omitting the contralateral side remains controversial.
  • Against unilateral templates: a high-volume series of primary RPLND in clinical stage I/IIA applied a full template (suprahilar usually omitted). Depending on the template, 3–23% of node-positive patients had a positive node outside the modified template, more with right-sided tumours. The authors recommended full bilateral infrahilar nerve-sparing RPLND as standard for clinical stage I/IIA.
  • No randomised trial. Both achieve CSS approaching 100% (Table 80.3); comparison is confounded by differing adjuvant policies, so there is no one-size-fits-all template.

Use of Modified Templates in Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection After Chemotherapy

  • Standard of care: resect the residual mass + full bilateral infrahilar RPLND. This has been the standard since the first PC-RPLND report (Donohue, 1982): contralateral crossover is frequent with bulky initial disease and viable tissue cannot be recognised intraoperatively. The cost is morbidity and ejaculatory dysfunction where nerve sparing is not feasible.
  • Modified templates after chemotherapy (Table 80.4): extra-template nodes vary widely, yet recurrence stays low.
  • Candidate criteria for modified-template PC-RPLND (all three):
    1. Well-defined lesion ≤5 cm, confined to the primary landing zone on pre- and post-chemotherapy imaging (Fig. 80.9);
    2. Normal post-chemotherapy STMs;
    3. IGCCCG good or intermediate risk.
  • With these criteria: in-field recurrence 0–4%, CSS 98–100% (up to about 10 years), antegrade ejaculation >85%. No randomised comparison exists, so full bilateral dissection remains standard.

Concept — How far to operate depends on how predictable the disease is. In untreated low-volume disease the landing zones are reliable enough to narrow the field and to observe after resection. Histologic Findings at Post-Chemotherapy Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection and Survival Outcomes

  • Three findings at PC-RPLND: fibrosis/necrosis about 40%, teratoma about 45%, viable GCT about 15%. Viable GCT has become less frequent as chemotherapy regimens have been refined.

Outcomes by Histology

  • CSS is excellent after teratoma or fibrosis; the concern is viable GCT (Table 80.5).
  • Histology after BEP ×3 vs EP ×4 (good risk) is debated, but long-term survival is similar. Series vary by era, pretreatment risk and follow-up.

Fibrosis/Necrosis

  • CSS and RFS approach 95%. Chemotherapy has rendered all malignant cells non-viable, and subclinical deposits have most likely been cleared as well.

Teratoma

  • RFS 80–90%, CSS 85–95%. Recurrence risk is higher with somatic-type malignancy and mediastinal primaries.

Viable Malignancy

  • Persistent viable disease means primary resistance to chemotherapy. Long-term survival is 50–70%.

Post-Chemotherapy Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection in High-Risk Populations

Salvage Retroperitoneal Node Dissection

  • Performed in particularly high-risk, partly platinum-resistant disease, typically after at least 7–8 cycles of first- and second-line chemotherapy to normalise markers.
  • Viable malignancy is about 15–20% after first-line chemotherapy but substantially higher after salvage (at least double), with worse OS. In one salvage series: fibrosis/necrosis 50%, teratoma 23%, viable 27%. OS after salvage RPLND 60–75%.
  • After third-line high-dose chemotherapy (HDCT) with stem cell transplantation: extremely rare. Necrosis about a quarter, teratoma about a third, viable cancer about 40%; 5-year OS about 70%. This needs careful selection and multidisciplinary decision making, but RPLND still has a significant role.

Desperation Retroperitoneal Node Dissection

  • A main indication for RPLND after any line of chemotherapy is normalisation of STMs. Desperation RPLND is the special case of persistently elevated STMs despite chemotherapy. It must never be undertaken outside a multidisciplinary setting, because relapse and progression risk is very high.
  • Largest series: 5-year OS about 54%. Independent predictors of poor OS: desperation RPLND after salvage chemotherapy, elevated pre-operative β-hCG, and repeat RPLND.
  • Markers that fell after first-line chemotherapy and then plateaued at a detectable level → >75% chance of fibrosis or teratoma. Paradoxically, fibrosis here may mean a worse prognosis: the markers must then be coming from disease outside the retroperitoneum.
  • Normalisation of markers after RPLND is the only variable linked to improved OS.

Reoperative Retroperitoneal Node Dissection

  • Usually a result of technical failure of the first operation, which left residual disease at primary or post-chemotherapy RPLND. This is why RPLND belongs with experienced high-volume surgeons. Laparoscopic or robotic RPLND must obey the same rules of complete dissection as open surgery.
  • Volume: most RPLNDs in board recertification logs are done by urologists who have performed only one. >2 per year = top 25%; 3 per year = top 10%.
  • In the largest series, local recurrence was predicted by incomplete lumbar vessel division at the first operation and by teratoma in the reoperative specimen. Worse outcome: active cancer at reoperation, M1b stage, salvage chemotherapy before reoperation.
  • Most recurrences lie in the primary landing zone, and about half are retroaortic or retrocaval, i.e. left behind. Viable GCT with somatic-type malignancy in 15–20%. Complication rates are higher and cancer-specific outcomes worse.
  • → Thorough dissection with complete mobilisation of the great vessels at the first RPLND is mandatory.

Late Relapse

  • Definition: recurrence more than 24 months after primary treatment with an initial complete response, outside observation. Rare: 2–4%.
  • Commonest site: retroperitoneum. Yolk sac is often the dominant histology, and somatic-type malignancy is more frequent. Both are chemoresistant.
  • → Resectable disease: RPLND first. Chemotherapy is reserved for widespread or unresectable disease, to downsize it towards resectability.
  • OS after surgery is nearly 60%, so select cases carefully. Adverse factors: viable GCT, somatic-type malignancy, prior salvage chemotherapy, incomplete resection.

Surgical Outcomes, Functional Considerations, and Complications of Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection

Lymph Node Counts

  • Proposed as a surgical quality measure, as in other cancers; results mixed (higher counts linked to node positivity in one series, not in others).
  • Counts also depend on how pathology submits and processes tissue, not only on the surgeon.
  • National datasets link higher counts to better oncological outcomes and to academic/high-volume centres. Whether the count is a valid surrogate for quality is unproven.

Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection and Fertility

Fertility in Patients Undergoing Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection

  • RPLND does not affect testicular function or spermatogenesis, but subfertility is common at diagnosis: abnormal semen in 40–60% of newly diagnosed men. This confounds paternity rates after treatment.
  • Live birth after orchidectomy and primary RPLND is about one-third (small cohort).

Ejaculatory Dysfunction and Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection

  • Physiology: seminal emission (smooth-muscle contraction of the vasa, seminal vesicles and prostate, and prostatic secretion) and bladder-neck closure (which prevents retrograde ejaculation) both depend on the L1–L4 postganglionic sympathetic fibres. These coalesce at or below the IMA in the hypogastric plexus and run caudally in the interiliac space over the sacral promontory.
  • Pre-modification RPLND removed these fibres → anejaculation.

Complications of Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection

  • Overall complications: primary RPLND 10.6–24%; PC-RPLND up to 56%, less at experienced centres.
  • Risk factors: tumour size, IGCCCG intermediate/poor risk, elevated markers, prior RPLND, longer operative time, auxiliary procedures.
  • The mean number of RPLNDs per board-certified urologist per year is 1, which explains much of the variability in reported outcomes.

Pulmonary Complications

  • Major pulmonary complications are rare after primary RPLND but occur in 3–5% after PC-RPLND, falling as teams become more aware of bleomycin.
  • Bleomycin-treated patients are at increased risk of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and prolonged ventilation.
  • Prevention: avoid aggressive intravenous fluid intra- and postoperatively; keep FiO₂ as low as safely possible; use a bleomycin-aware anaesthetic team. Warn recovery and ward staff that tachycardia is common after RPLND and is not on its own a trigger for fluid. Use nasal oxygen judiciously and accept oxygen saturation of 88–90%.

Ileus

  • Up to 26% after primary or PC-RPLND. The extraperitoneal approach may significantly reduce it. No routine nasogastric tube; some enteral feeding on day 1 unless circumstances prevent it.

Lymphocele

  • Mostly subclinical; symptomatic ≤1.7%: fullness or flank pain (ureteric compression); CT shows a thin-walled cyst in the bed.
  • Air within it and/or rim enhancement → suspect infection.
  • Symptomatic or infected → percutaneous drainage. Antibiotics only if infected, and then consider leaving a drain. Prevention: clip or ligate lymphatic channels.

Chylous Ascites

  • Lipid-rich lymph in the peritoneal cavity: 0.2–2.1% after primary RPLND vs 2–12% after PC-RPLND.
  • Increasing girth, fullness, anorexia, weight gain; dyspnoea if large. Diagnosis: suspicion plus CT.
  • Paracentesis: milky fluid, protein >3 g/dL, triglyceride 2–8 × serum.
  • Suprahilar dissection raises the risk by disrupting the cisterna chyli, which is variable: at L1–L2, at or just above the renal vessels, most consistently behind the aorta under the left crus. A distinct cisterna is present in only about half.
  • Management ladder: paracentesis without a drain → if rapid reaccumulation, a drain capped for interval release → very low-fat, high-protein diet + medium-chain triglycerides + intramuscular octreotide (proven for chyle leak after hepatopancreaticobiliary surgery, not in urology) → failure: nil by mouth + parenteral nutrition → persistent high volume: observe, or invasive last resort: lymphangiography/lymphoscintigraphy ± percutaneous embolisation, peritoneovenous shunt (resolved about 90% of refractory cases in a small series), or surgical exploration.
  • Invasive options are for leaks not improving over days to weeks, or with unmanageable electrolyte and protein loss. After resolution, continue a low-fat diet for at least 4 weeks.

Venous Thromboembolism

  • Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is low in this young, healthy population: <1% after primary RPLND, 0.1–3.1% after PC-RPLND.
  • Sequential compression devices from the holding area; mobilise on day 0, with long periods out of bed.
  • Pharmacological prophylaxis is unstudied in RPLND but proven in general surgery. Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) occurs in about 13% during first-line bleomycin chemotherapy. → Pharmacological prophylaxis advocated, especially with prior VTE, hypercoagulable states, obesity or prior chemotherapy (particularly bleomycin).

Neurologic Complications

  • Rare peripheral nerve injury from positioning or retractors (femoral neuropraxia); prevent by careful positioning.
  • Bulky mediastinal or retroperitoneal disease after chemotherapy → increased risk of paraplegia.

Mortality

  • Primary RPLND: exceptionally rare. PC-RPLND: <1%, most often from pulmonary embolism, duodenal fistula, myocardial infarction or chylous ascites.

Key points: surgical outcomes, functional considerations and complications

  • Antegrade ejaculation expected after most primary RPLNDs.
  • PC-RPLND: bleomycin lung — FiO₂, fluids, anaesthesia.
  • Chylous ascites prevented by lymphatic ligation.

Concept — Morbidity follows the chemotherapy history. Bleomycin lung, fibrotic planes and bulk turn a low-morbidity staging operation into a major one, so prevention (low FiO₂, restrictive fluids, lymphatic ligation, thromboprophylaxis) is planned before the incision. Ejaculatory function depends only on whether the L1–L4 fibres are recognised and spared.

Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection in Unique Situations

Post-Chemotherapy Retroperitoneal Node Dissection for Seminoma

  • Pure seminoma is exquisitely chemosensitive: complete response 70–90% with platinum, even when widely metastatic, so residual masses are uncommon.
  • Surgery is hard and rarely rewarding: dense desmoplastic fibrosis, viable tumour only about 10%, no teratoma, more complications than NSGCT.
  • The 3 cm rule: residual masses <3 cm were all fibrosis, while >3 cm harboured viable tumour or teratoma in about 30%. → Observe all masses <3 cm; resection of >3 cm was recommended, but 70% of those are fibrosis, so many still undergo morbid surgery. Indiana data found no correlation between residual size and relapse and advised observing all masses unless they progress serologically or radiologically.
  • → FDG-PET decides for masses >3 cm.

Post-Chemotherapy Retroperitoneal Node Dissection for Sex Cord Stromal Tumours

  • Sex cord stromal tumours (SCSTs) are <5% of testicular neoplasms: Leydig cell, Sertoli cell and granulosa cell tumours, pure or mixed. Adult granulosa cell tumour is the rarest testicular tumour (fewer than 50 reported cases). Series are small.
  • Malignancy is hard to diagnose without metastases. Features associated with malignant behaviour: older age, size >4 cm, necrosis, mitoses >3–5 per 10 high-power fields, moderate–severe nuclear atypia, infiltrative margins/invasion of adjacent structures, lymphovascular invasion. Some classify two or more features as malignant, but prospective histological classification remains difficult.
  • Sertoli plus Leydig: about 0.4–0.6% of testicular tumours (registries).

  • Seminoma: <3 cm observe; >3 cm PET; PET-avid → surgery, salvage or HDCT.
  • Malignant SCST: RPLND of no proven survival benefit.

Concept — Both unique situations break the NSGCT rules. Seminoma is chemosensitive and has no teratoma, so residual masses are mostly scar, and imaging (size, then PET) rather than routine resection decides surgery. SCSTs are refractory once metastatic, so the operation that cures NSGCT offers them little.

Conclusion

  • Testicular cancer is one of oncology's great successes: cure in more than 90%. This is largely due to cisplatin-based multiagent chemotherapy, but surgery remains central.
  • The urologist usually diagnoses and guides the patient, within a multidisciplinary team.
  • Success is now measured as cure with the least morbidity and treatment burden. Surgical refinements aim to protect quality of life and avoid long-term sequelae without losing oncological efficacy.
