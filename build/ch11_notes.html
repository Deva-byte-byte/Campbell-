Chapter 11 — Perioperative Care in Urologic Surgery

  • Perioperative care keeps evolving with new tools and an ageing population; economics drive more outpatient surgery, shorter stays and fewer complications.
  • Standard of care: even complex urological operations are admitted on the day of surgery and discharged the day after.
  • Despite the shift to office-based and non-surgical care, the diversity of genitourinary disease requires every urologist to command perioperative principles.

PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION

  • Patient acuity is rising with age and comorbidity.
  • Surgeon and patient share responsibility for evaluation by the wider team, so the patient arrives in, and leaves, theatre optimised.
  • Appropriate specialist consultation improves outcomes and reduces cancellations.

PRESURGICAL TESTING

  • Goal: find an undiagnosed comorbidity, an undertreated condition, or a significant exacerbation that may affect the operative course.
  • Individualise by age, history, examination and procedure.
  • Routine testing beyond history and examination has never been shown cost-effective, and predicts morbidity less well than American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) status or American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) risk guidance; a systematic review found no support for routine testing before elective non-cardiac surgery.
  • Typical panel: full blood count; basic metabolic panel; prothrombin time (PT), partial thromboplastin time (PTT), international normalised ratio (INR) (controversial); electrocardiogram (ECG); ABO/Rh group and antibody screen; chest radiograph.

American Society of Anesthesiologists Classification and Risk

  • About 27 million US operations a year; about 8 million (30%) in patients with significant coronary or other cardiac disease — hence the cardiovascular focus.
  • ASA classification (1961, revised to six classes; Box 11.1) grades overall physical status, not surgical risk; subjective yet an independent predictor of mortality.
  • Multivariate scores: Goldman's criteria (Table 11.1) sum weighted points; the Modified Cardiac Risk Index (Table 11.2) simplifies to six predictors.

PREOPERATIVE CARDIOVASCULAR EVALUATION

  • Initial history, examination and ECG seek coronary disease, heart failure, symptomatic arrhythmia, pacemaker/implantable defibrillator, and orthostatic hypotension; define severity and stability.
  • Cardiac risk is modified by functional capacity, age, diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, renal dysfunction and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).
  • ACC/AHA guidelines use three categories: clinical markers, functional capacity, and type of surgery.

  • Timing after MI: the old 3–6-month wait is abandoned; ACC stratifies by infarct severity and reinfarction risk on stress testing; without trial data, waiting 4–6 weeks before elective surgery is reasonable, considering stents/bypass and antiplatelet needs.

Functional Capacity

  • Measured in metabolic equivalents (METs); 4 METs ≈ climbing two flights of stairs — cheap and simple.
  • Duke Activity Status Index (DASI) (Table 11.3), validated repeatedly, quantifies capacity.
  • ≥4 METs → no further invasive cardiac evaluation needed.

  • Determined by type of surgery and degree of haemodynamic stress.

Concept — Cardiac risk is the product of three independent axes: how sick the heart is (clinical markers), how much reserve the patient has (METs), and how much stress the operation imposes (surgery type). A patient with good reserve undergoing low-stress surgery needs no further testing whatever the history.

PULMONARY EVALUATION

  • Most important for thoracic and abdominal surgery.
  • Pulmonary function testing (forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV₁), forced vital capacity, diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide) for known lung disease, significant smoking history or symptoms — gives a baseline.
  • FEV₁ <0.8 L or <30% predicted → high risk.
  • Specific risk factors: COPD, smoking, sputum production, pneumonia, dyspnoea, obstructive sleep apnoea.
  • Smokers: 4-fold postoperative pulmonary morbidity and up to 10-fold mortality.
  • Restrictive fares better than obstructive disease — preserved maximal expiratory flow allows an effective cough with less sputum.
  • General factors: age, low albumin, obesity, impaired sensorium, prior stroke, immobility, acute renal failure, chronic steroids.

HEPATOBILIARY EVALUATION

  • Advanced liver disease survival has improved, so these patients come to surgery more often; mild–moderate disease is often asymptomatic and must be sought.
  • History: prior hepatitis, timing and precipitants (important if staff have a blood-borne exposure); pruritus, bleeding, distension, weight gain.
  • Examination: jaundice/scleral icterus visible at bilirubin >3 mg/dL; caput medusae, palmar erythema, spider naevi, clubbing; severe — ascites, encephalopathy, asterixis, cachexia.

SPECIAL POPULATIONS

Elderly

  • By 2050 Americans >65 will more than double to 89 million, >20% of them over 85; octogenarian and nonagenarian surgery is increasing.
  • Whether age or comorbidity drives risk is unsettled, but age independently predicts morbidity and mortality; after radical cystectomy, 90-day mortality climbs across the <70, 70–80 and >80 bands.
  • Over 80: hypertension and dyspnoea are the commonest comorbid risks; preoperative transfusion, emergency surgery and weight loss best predict morbidity; longer operative time raises mortality in octogenarians.
  • Delirium — often dismissed as "sundowning" — may be the first sign of metabolic or infective complication; assess cognition, function and mood preoperatively.
  • Reduce delirium: minimise noise, familiar surroundings, consistent sleep, regional anaesthesia where possible, minimal opioids.

Morbid Obesity

  • Weigh operative risk against natural history when timing surgery; BMI ≥45 kg/m² costs 8–13 years of life expectancy.
  • Cardiac symptoms are non-specific and examination underestimates dysfunction; >3 coronary risk factors → consider non-invasive cardiac evaluation.
  • Comorbidities: ASCVD, heart failure, hypertension, sleep-apnoea pulmonary hypertension, hypoventilation, arrhythmias, deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, poor exercise capacity; ventilation–perfusion mismatch.
  • Positioning injury and wound infection risk → consider laparoscopy when appropriate.

Pregnancy

  • Commonest reason: renal colic/stones, found on fetal ultrasound or during colic.
  • Fetal radiation risk highest from preimplantation to ~15 weeks; malformation threshold 10 cGy → ultrasound first (CT abdomen ~1 cGy; intravenous pyelogram ~0.3 cGy).
  • Delay surgery until the fetus is mature unless mother or fetus would be harmed.
TrimesterAnaesthetic/surgical risk

  • Analgesia: opioids acceptable (no human birth defects at normal doses; wean early — fetal dependency); avoid NSAIDs — premature closure of the ductus arteriosus.

Nutritional Status

  • Malnutrition impairs host defence, wound healing, immunity, bowel recovery and organ function.
  • Assessment: weight loss plus lymphocyte count and albumin (visceral protein); >20 lb (~9 kg) loss in 3 months = severe malnutrition; validated tools include the Subjective Global Assessment.

Concept — Each special population reshapes the same risk calculation: the elderly lose reserve and present atypically (delirium), the obese mask cardiac disease, pregnancy adds a second patient with a time-dependent vulnerability, and malnutrition removes the capacity to heal — so the gut should be used whenever it works.

PREPARATION FOR SURGERY

Preoperative Optimisation Strategies

Optimisation of Comorbid Illness

Cardiac.

  • Of nitrates, calcium-channel blockers and β-blockers, only β-blockade improved outcome.
  • Mangano (NEJM, 1996), landmark: prophylactic atenolol improved outcomes after non-cardiac surgery; a very large retrospective study linked perioperative β-blockade to lower mortality in high-risk major non-cardiac surgery.
  • Goal-directed therapy (judicious fluids, inotropes, oxygen to physiological targets) further reduces risk; an early trial showed a large fall in mortality.

Pulmonary.

  • Stop smoking ≥8 weeks before surgery. Stopping <8 weeks may raise risk — loss of smoke irritation reduces coughing and pulmonary toilet. >6 months' abstinence → risk equals non-smokers.
  • COPD: preoperative bronchodilators dramatically reduce complications; treat infection with antibiotics; steroid pretreatment for asthma; epidural/regional anaesthesia, physiotherapy and continued bronchodilation help.

Diabetes.

  • Hyperglycaemia impairs healing and raises infection; hypoglycaemia under anaesthesia may go unrecognised.
  • Stop long-acting oral hypoglycaemics; prefer short-acting agents or sliding-scale insulin; target glucose 80–250 mg/dL; frequent fingerstick checks postoperatively; resume usual regimen when eating.
  • Insulin pump: continue basal infusion on the day of surgery, correct with the pump; know the patient's sensitivity factor.

Thyroid.

  • Either dysfunction → endocrinologist; defer elective surgery until euthyroid.

Management of Medications — Antithrombotic Therapy

  • Warfarin and antiplatelets cause significant postoperative bleeding (unlike venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis) → balance bleeding against thrombosis.
  • Warfarin (atrial fibrillation, mechanical valves, prior VTE): half-life 36–42 h → stop 5 days before surgery for INR <1.5.
  • Direct oral anticoagulants (apixaban, dabigatran, rivaroxaban) differ pharmacologically; the surgeon must know each.
  • Bridging depends on thrombotic risk (Table 11.4): 2012 American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) — bridge moderate- and high-risk patients with therapeutic subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) or IV unfractionated heparin (UFH).

Bowel Preparation

  • Rationale: fewer faeces and bacteria → less anastomotic leak, abscess, wound infection.
  • Flora: aerobes — Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis; anaerobes — Bacteroides, Clostridium. Concentration (organisms/g): jejunum 10–10⁵; distal ileum 10⁵–10⁷; ascending colon 10⁶–10⁸; descending colon 10¹⁰–10¹².
  • Evidence is inferred from colorectal surgery.

  • Exceptions where preparation persists: enema before transrectal prostate biopsy (portal of entry, bacteraemia); laparoscopy (believed to improve exposure and ileus — unproven).

Both feature in enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways.

Concept — Optimisation means reversing what can be reversed before the insult of surgery, but the timing rules are counterintuitive: late smoking cessation, premature antiplatelet withdrawal and routine mechanical bowel preparation can each do more harm than good.

INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT

Patient Environment

  • Hypothermia is harmful in elective surgery (therapeutic only in some trauma/brain injury).
  • Mechanism: induction → peripheral vasodilatation redistributes core heat (early drop); thereafter radiation and conduction account for most loss.
  • Normothermia = core 36–38°C; even 1–2°C of hypothermia is harmful — mild hypothermia increases blood loss and transfusion (meta-analysis of RCTs) via impaired clotting enzymes and platelet aggregation.
  • SSI risk rises with mild hypothermia (34–36°C) — impaired immunity and vasoconstriction-driven tissue hypoxia.
  • Kurz (1996), landmark, 200 colorectal patients: hypothermia → 3× wound infection and +2.6 days' stay. The Surgical Care Improvement Project (SCIP) includes normothermia.
  • Warming: blankets, warmed IV fluids, warmed irrigation (transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP), long endoscopy), warmed humidified CO₂ in laparoscopy, higher theatre temperature.

Patient Safety

  • Harvard Medical Practice Study (Brennan, 1991): 48% of adverse events accompanied an operation → inspired the Institute of Medicine's To Err Is Human. The operating theatre is the commonest venue of preventable surgical injury; the surgeon is "captain of the ship" but every member must attend to detail.
  • Three immediately preventable injuries: retractor, thermal and positioning.
  • Self-retaining retractors → femoral neuropathy: lateral blades must cradle rectus only, never compress psoas; reinspect periodically.
  • Thermal: diathermy, argon beam, bipolar, lasers; light-cable ends burn through drapes → off/standby when not in use.
  • Morbid obesity: hydraulic table, long instruments, extra padding, wide compression sleeves, table extensions.

Patient Positioning

  • Shared team responsibility; knowledge drawn from anaesthesia. The ASA Closed Claims Database (1999) found neuropathy the second-leading cause of liability → ASA practice advisory (Box 11.2).
  • Mechanism of position-related neuropathy: stretch, prolonged compression or ischaemia.

Antibiotic Prophylaxis

  • CDC (1999): SSIs are ~40% of nosocomial infections in surgical patients and add 7–10 days in hospital; nationally ~1 million extra inpatient days. Level 1 evidence supports prophylaxis across procedures; Medicare withholds payment for some SSI admissions → prevention is mandatory.
  • Adjuncts: hand-washing, scrub, sterile field; hair removal does not reduce SSI but if done use clippers or depilatory cream, not razors.
  • Three determinants of need: (1) patient susceptibility (Box 11.3); (2) procedural risk — wound class (Box 11.4); (3) consequence of infection (e.g. cystoscopy for microhaematuria: no prophylaxis in a young healthy patient, yes in an elderly insulin-dependent diabetic).

Concept — Prophylaxis is justified by host susceptibility × inoculum × consequence, and succeeds only if a tissue level is present at the moment of contamination — hence the obsession with timing, the single dose, and redosing for long cases.

VENOUS THROMBOEMBOLIC PROPHYLAXIS

  • Postoperative VTE is the second commonest cause of excess stay, charges and mortality among surgical discharges.
  • Urology incidence without prophylaxis historically 10–40%; now 1–5% with mechanical prophylaxis and early ambulation; in the prospective @RISTOS study VTE followed open urological surgery despite high prophylaxis rates; radical cystectomy carries the highest incidence.
  • VTE is the most important cause of non-surgical mortality in urology.
  • Mechanical prophylaxis is near-universal; pharmacological only after weighing bleeding (Table 11.6). In general surgery, pharmacological prophylaxis increased minor bleeding (bruising, haematoma) but not major bleeding; TURP and partial nephrectomy bleed more.
  • Surgical factors: general vs neuraxial anaesthesia; supine vs lithotomy; abdominal vs pelvic ± lymphadenectomy; open vs laparoscopic. Patient factors: Box 11.5.
  • ACCP 2008: hospitals need a formal VTE strategy; moved from individual risk models to routine group-specific prophylaxis for each surgical group. AUA (2009) combines individual risk with procedure type — high-risk patient/low-risk surgery or low-risk patient/high-risk surgery may each warrant drugs (Table 11.7).
BOX 11.5 Patient-Related Factors Increasing Risk for Venous Thromboembolism
  • Surgery; trauma; immobility/paresis; cancer (active or occult) and cancer therapy (hormonal, chemotherapy, angiogenesis inhibitors, radiotherapy); venous compression; previous VTE; increasing age; pregnancy/postpartum; oestrogen contraceptives or hormone replacement therapy; selective oestrogen-receptor modulators; erythropoiesis-stimulating agents; acute medical illness; inflammatory bowel disease; nephrotic syndrome; myeloproliferative disorders; paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria; obesity; central venous catheter; thrombophilia. Concept — VTE prophylaxis is a bleeding-versus-clotting trade-off: mechanical methods carry no bleeding cost but less efficacy, heparins the reverse, and the AUA model escalates from ambulation to combined therapy as patient and procedural risk accumulate.

ANAESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS

  • Anaesthesia delivers hypnosis, amnesia and analgesia with good operating conditions; most urology is done under monitored anaesthesia care, regional or general anaesthesia, combining inhalational and IV agents, analgesics and benzodiazepines.
  • Risk is now very low: ambulatory all-cause mortality of the order of 1 in 50,000.

Selection of Mode of Anaesthesia

  • Urologist helps choose the mode: patient factors (comorbidity, airway, preference) and procedural factors (complexity, duration, site, expected fluid and blood loss).

Monitored Anaesthesia Care

  • Conscious sedation under an anaesthetist, ranging from minimal sedation to brief general anaesthesia; usually IV opioid + benzodiazepine.
  • Suits short endoscopy, transrectal ultrasound procedures, and (with local) superficial genital surgery.
  • Office sedation: The Joint Commission requires equivalent monitoring — trained monitoring assistant, immediate airway/resuscitation equipment, pre- and post-procedure evaluation.

Regional Anaesthesia

  • Local, spinal, epidural. Local: avoid intravascular injection; lidocaine vs bupivacaine differ in onset and duration; pre-incision infiltration reduces nociceptor sensitisation and postoperative pain.
  • Spinal/epidural (lidocaine or bupivacaine) → sensory, motor and sympathetic block.

SKIN PREPARATION

  • Antiseptics: alcohol, povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine; apply in concentric circles from the centre and let dry before incision.
  • Cochrane: some evidence that 0.5% chlorhexidine in methylated spirits beats alcohol-based povidone-iodine for clean surgery; no conclusive superiority overall.
  • CDC recommends preoperative showering, but antiseptic bathing does not reduce infection.
  • Hair removal, if done: immediately before surgery, with clippers (shaving breaches skin → colonisation).

TRANSFUSION CONSIDERATIONS

  • Urological organs are vascular; know transfusion risks and alternatives (e.g. Jehovah's Witnesses).
  • Before the acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) epidemic: transfusion for haematocrit <30%; infection fears → National Institutes of Health consensus (1988) → ASA guidelines (2006).

  • Component therapy: packed red cells (PRBCs) = whole blood minus plasma; haematocrit 40% whole blood vs ~70% PRBC; given with crystalloid.
  • Platelets: not empirical; give if <50,000/mm³ with planned surgery, or 50,000–100,000 with high-risk surgery or platelet dysfunction.

  • Untreated pain adds physiological stress and complications; analgesia blocks nociception somewhere between receptor, afferent nerve, cord and cortex.

Key points
  • Proper preoperative evaluation prevents cancellations and reduces complications.
  • Cardiac testing depends on functional capacity, cardiac risk factors and surgery-specific risk.
  • SSI is a leading complication; prophylaxis is indicated in virtually all procedures.
  • VTE: AUA recommends mechanical or pharmacological prophylaxis (both for the highest risk).
  • Anaesthetic pharmacology lets the urologist help choose the mode.
  • Transfuse at haematocrit <21% in most, unless cardiopulmonary risk.
  • Hypothermia of even 1–2°C increases blood loss and SSI.
