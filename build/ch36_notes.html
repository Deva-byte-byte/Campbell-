Chapter 36 — Pediatric Urogenital Imaging

  • Age-specific differences: studies routine and tolerable in adults may need anaesthesia/sedation or specially trained technicians in children; doses must be adjusted and equipment sized for the smaller patient — the urologist must know these differences to order and interpret tests.
  • Weighing the decision to image: radiation dose per study, likelihood of interval follow-up (further exposure), need for contrast and need for sedation → set against the anticipated clinical benefit.
  • ALARA ("as low as reasonably achievable") principle is the consensus standard (International Commission on Radiological Protection, ICRP). Individual radiosensitivity cannot be predicted, so every patient is treated as radiation sensitive — especially in pregnancy, fetal life and childhood.
  • Guidelines on imaging selection come from the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), Italian Society of Pediatric Nephrology, Canadian Paediatric Society (CPS), Polish Society of Pediatric Nephrology (PSPN) and European Association of Urology/European Society for Paediatric Urology (EAU/ESPU) — all driven by the aim of reducing unnecessary invasive testing, built on best available data plus expert opinion.
  • American College of Radiology (ACR) Appropriateness Criteria — Haematuria–Child (2018) and Urinary Tract Infection–Child (2024): balance radiation exposure against diagnostic yield, based on the prevalence of findings in specific clinical scenarios.
  • Organising frame of the chapter: non-ionising then ionising modalities; for each, three levels (kidney/ureter; bladder/urethra; scrotum/testes/internal genitalia) and five indications (obstruction → cysts/masses → infection → other congenital malformations → trauma).

Technique

Non-ionising

  • Sonography is the archetypal minimally invasive study — hence its widespread use.
  • Magnetic resonance (MR) is not minimally invasive in practice: long acquisition times mean many children need sedation or anaesthesia.
  • Sedation/anaesthesia risk usually exceeds contrast risk: it is typically higher than the risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis from gadolinium (poor renal function, or immature neonatal renal function) and of iodinated-contrast nephropathy in poor renal function.
  • Allergic reaction: both contrast classes carry a higher (but still very small) risk.
  • Radiofrequency heating: MR radiofrequency pulses can cause patient heating → must be monitored; hyperthermia reported rarely.
  • Long-term MR safety questioned because magnetic-field exposure alters gene expression experimentally; no human data, but fetal MR is avoided in the first trimester.
  • Ultrasonography has shown no gene-expression changes → regarded as safe at all ages and all stages of gestation.

Ionising Radiation

  • More harmful in children than adults because of (1) greater radiosensitivity of developing tissues and (2) potential for higher cumulative lifetime dose.
  • Stochastic effects of repeated exposure (internal cell injury) → radiation-induced malignancy after a long latent period.
  • Reading the risk literature: determine whether risk is estimated, modelled or measured; measured studies are confounded by technique, age, size and intervening studies over long intervals.
  • Despite these limits, there is reasonable evidence of a dose-related risk in children and even in utero.
  • Responses: dose reduction per test while preserving diagnostic accuracy; better decision making; non-ionising alternatives.

Concept — Every paediatric imaging choice is a trade between three harms — ionising radiation, sedation and contrast — against diagnostic yield; the child's long remaining lifespan is what tips the balance toward sonography and ALARA.

Ultrasonography

Fundamentals of Sonography in the Fetus and Child

  • Prenatal sonography can diagnose many urological conditions with near certainty by assessing: presence and quality of renal cortex → laterality → kidney position and relation to other organs → amniotic fluid volume and urine in the bladder.
  • Amniotic fluid origin: before 8–10 weeks it is a dialysate of fetal blood across permeable fetal skin; fetal urine production begins at 10–12 weeks; by 20 weeks sonography detects most urological abnormalities.
  • Normal urinary tract: two kidneys in the renal fossae; cortex isoechoic or slightly hypoechoic to liver; discrete corticomedullary interfaces; no cortical cysts, masses, or dilatation of collecting system or bladder.
  • Fetal bladder should be visible in all fetuses by 10–12 weeks; cycling may be seen with patience; it never empties completely (small residual always present).
  • Umbilical cord placement and anterior abdominal wall integrity must be checked — abnormalities are highly associated with bladder exstrophy and cloacal exstrophy.
  • Newborn kidney trap: distinct corticomedullary differentiation (hypoechoic pyramids) plus slightly hyperechoic parenchyma can be mistaken for hydronephrosis.
  • Hydronephrosis is underestimated on an early postnatal scan (up to 2 days after birth) and in dehydration.
  • Prenatal sonography limitations: low amniotic fluid, maternal habitus, maternal bowel gas, maternal pelvic bones, fetal position — in these respects MRI is superior.

Kidney/Ureter

Obstruction

Hydronephrosis versus cystic renal diseases.

  • Commonest prenatal sonographic findings: collecting-system dilatation and parenchymal cysts.
  • Differential: hydronephrosis (obstructive and non-obstructive), multicystic dysplastic kidney (MCDK), polycystic kidney disease (autosomal recessive and autosomal dominant), cystic nephroma.
  • Reporting is not standardised across specialties. The Society for Fetal Urology (SFU) classification is most used by paediatric urologists — based on calyceal dilatation, minor-calyx involvement and parenchymal thinning. Others use renal pelvic anteroposterior diameter or combined systems.
  • Because reporting varies, the clinician must review the actual images before deciding.
  • Natural history: most hydronephrosis is benign and improves or resolves. Profound developmental abnormalities present as moderate–severe hydronephrosis from high-grade obstruction; the transition from dilated to non-dilated tract localises the obstruction — ureteropelvic junction (UPJ), ureterovesical junction (UVJ) or urethra.

  • Grade 3 → 4 step is defined by the parenchyma, not by further dilatation.

Dilated ureters.

  • Usually a hallmark of obstruction, but ureteric dilatation also occurs without obstruction, e.g. vesicoureteral reflux (VUR).
  • Megaureter and ureterocele have obstructive and non-obstructive variants → distinguish with a functional drainage test (diuresis renography or MR urography).

Functional interpretation of sonography of the kidney.

  • Standard ultrasound is anatomic; function is only inferred.
  • Key clinical question in hydronephrosis: obstruction vs VUR vs normal variant (neither).
  • Bilateral hydroureteronephrosis + thickened bladder + poor emptying → voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) to exclude posterior urethral valve (PUV).
  • Otherwise, diuretic renography is the gold standard for evaluating hydronephrosis.
  • Emerging: computer-aided shape analysis and ultrasound elastography (parenchymal/pelvic tension as indirect measure of obstruction) — if validated, could greatly reduce ionising functional studies.

  • Renal signs: echogenic parenchyma, moderate–severe hydronephrosis, renal cortical cyst (renal dysplasia); bladder view: marked bilateral distal hydroureter.

Cysts/Masses/Stones

Renal cysts. Cysts can mimic hydronephrosis but are distinguished because cysts do not communicate, whereas dilated calyces communicate with the renal pelvis.

  • Trap: ARPKD appears as large, bright kidneys rather than discrete cysts — the echogenicity comes from dilated collecting tubules.

Renal and abdominal solid masses.

  • Differential: neuroblastoma, congenital mesoblastic nephroma, Wilms tumour, renal vein thrombosis, renal artery thrombosis, adrenal haemorrhage, rare solid tumours (rhabdoid, clear cell, angiomyolipoma).
  • Sonographic hallmarks + age + presentation usually suggest the diagnosis, but contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) commonly follows.

Renal stones.

  • Stone-forming children face a lifetime of imaging → minimise radiation; inform parent and older child so they can advocate for this plan at unfamiliar emergency facilities.
  • Sonography may suffice for initial screening and follow-up in most children with acute renal colic, rather than reflex CT or kidney–ureter–bladder (KUB) radiograph.
  • Ideal for hydronephrosis/hydroureter, directs further imaging if the stone is not seen; reliably detects intraparenchymal calculi and nephrocalcinosis.
  • Calculus = echogenic with acoustic shadowing vs parapelvic fat = echogenic without shadowing.
  • "Twinkling" artefact on colour Doppler distinguishes calculi from other hyperechoic foci.

Ureteral stones.

  • Proximal ureter may or may not be dilated, depending on the degree of obstruction.
  • Distal/pelvic ureteric stones are easier to image in children than adults (shorter distance to the stone); twinkling also seen.

Infection

  • Role: initial screening for obvious anatomical abnormality; not routine at onset of treatment, but failure to improve or worsening on antibiotics → sonography.
  • Sensitive for significant renal abnormality, but much less for uncomplicated VUR, duplication anomalies, acute pyelonephritis (APN) and focal scarring. Alone, rarely useful in most febrile and non-febrile urinary tract infection (UTI) → often combined with VCUG when a urological abnormality is suspected.
  • Imaging algorithms are mostly applied after treatment, to identify risk factors for recurrence and anomalies predisposing to complicated UTI and renal injury.
  • Hospitalised child: screening sonography before discharge to exclude obstruction; otherwise imaging is deferred until after discharge.
  • Hydronephrosis without VUR → diuresis renography; the combination reliably localises the obstruction.
  • Grey-scale sonography is inferior to technetium-99m dimercaptosuccinic acid (99mTc-DMSA) for APN and scarring.
  • A normal sonogram neither risk-stratifies a child with APN nor predicts VUR.

Contrast-enhanced urosonography (CEUS).

  • Agent: gas-containing sulfur hexafluoride lipid-type A microspheres (Lumason/SonoVue); US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved for intravenous or intravesical use.
  • Contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography (CeVUS) — commonest application: VUR detection, with a five-point grading system analogous to VCUG.
  • Cost: initially higher (time-consuming), offset by being a non-ionising method for detecting and monitoring VUR.
  • Single-centre retrospective paired comparison with VCUG: moderate sensitivity and specificity for VUR, with identical performance between two readers (good inter-rater reliability).
  • Systematic review/meta-analysis (Table 36.1): first- and second-generation agents equally accurate, overall false-negative rate 3% → authors recommend CeVUS for VUR detection without reservation.
  • CeVUS may also replace contrast cystography for VUR after renal transplantation and during video-urodynamics.

  • Trap: "non-ionising = less accurate" — here it does not; the limitation is cost/time and grading comparability, not detection.

Intrarenal reflux (IRR) on CeVUS.

  • IRR marks renal regions at risk of injury from APN; applying CeVUS to the "top-down" approach is attractive.
  • Bottom lines from recent series: CeVUS detects IRR in a minority of renal units, with excellent concordance with VCUG; dilating VUR and IRR are both commoner in males; IRR is seen mostly during voiding, most often in the lower pole, rises with each VUR grade and is associated with renal scarring.
  • Polar distribution of IRR on CeVUS mirrors the distribution of acute and chronic DMSA lesions — IRR is the anatomical substrate of pyelonephritic scarring.

Trauma

  • Sonography compares favourably with contrast-enhanced CT for the initial evaluation of renal injury in acute paediatric trauma.
  • Contrast-enhanced sonography is as sensitive as contrast-enhanced CT for paediatric blunt abdominal trauma.

Bladder and Urethra

Obstruction

  • Anatomical detection: ureteroceles, large diverticula, bladder calculi, debris, suspicious masses (Figure 36.7: ureterocele, diverticulum, calculi with twinkling).
  • Bladder wall thickness varies with volume and age; volume-indexed standards are too complex/variable for wide use — but a grossly thickened bladder with a voiding-dysfunction picture is suspicious.
  • Basic function: emptying quantified by prevoid and postvoid volumes.
  • "Keyhole sign" = thickened bladder + dilated posterior urethra → highly suggestive of PUV (Figure 36.8: prenatal bilateral hydroureteronephrosis + thick bladder + keyhole = bladder outlet obstruction).

Cysts/Masses

Midline pelvic cysts.

  • Differential: hydrometrocolpos (urogenital sinus anomalies), ovarian cyst, distended bladder, urinary ascites, dilated prostatic utricle.
  • Hydrometrocolpos = uterus and vagina distended with mucus or blood, from vaginal obstruction (imperforate hymen, vaginal atresia, transverse vaginal septum) or retrograde urine flow in urogenital sinus and cloacal malformations.
  • Dilated prostatic utricle = remnant of the müllerian duct opening → fluid-filled midline structure posterior to the bladder in males.

Figure 36.9 — Cloacal anomaly.

  • Oligohydramnios + dual pelvic cysts below a dilated bladder (34-week female fetus) → postnatal sonography shows hemivaginas → contrast via the single perineal opening opacifies both hemivaginas, bladder and distal colon = cloaca.

Bladder tumours.

  • Bladder as full as possible to assess contents and wall.
  • Masses: clot, benign and malignant tumours, foreign bodies, pseudotumours.
  • Clot vs tumour (child with gross haematuria): image supine then prone → clot moves, tumour stays fixed.
  • Wall tumours arise from urothelium, mesenchyme, neuroendocrine cells or in response to viral infection; sonography does not discriminate histology → further clinical assessment.

Infection

  • Viral cystitis: thickened bladder wall, sometimes enough to mimic a mass; clinical triad → viral upper respiratory prodrome + gross total haematuria + marked urgency/frequency.

Other Congenital Malformations

Abdominal wall defects. Bladder exstrophy and prune belly syndrome — abdominal wall formation defects with urogenital anomalies.

Trauma

  • No bladder/urethral sonographic trauma content is given under this heading; lower-tract trauma is imaged with contrast studies (see VCUG and CT sections).

Scrotum/Testes/Internal Genitalia

Acute Scrotum

  • Genital imaging covers four conditions: acute/enlarged scrotum, testicular/paratesticular masses, cryptorchidism, ambiguous genitalia. Ultrasonography is always the initial and usually the only modality.
  • Overuse is widespread — most scrotal problems are diagnosed by history and examination; avoid over-interpreting imaging that contradicts the clinical examination.
  • Colour Doppler helps when the examination is equivocal for torsion; never delay exploration for imaging if torsion is suspected and delay threatens salvage.
  • "Whirlpool sign" — high-resolution imaging of the twisted spermatic cord itself, developed because colour Doppler gives false negatives; raises sensitivity to about 96% (from about 76% with colour Doppler), specificity about 99%.
  • Salvageability is hard to predict except: no arterial flow + parenchymal heterogeneity → all testes non-viable at exploration.
  • Epididymo-orchitis: hyperaemia of epididymis and/or testis — but must be correlated clinically because recent spontaneous detorsion also gives hyperaemia.
  • Torsion of testicular/epididymal appendage: upper-pole/epididymal hypoechoic avascular nodule with surrounding hyperaemia (skilled operator).
  • Acute idiopathic scrotal oedema: "fountain sign" — hyperaemia of a thickened scrotal wall with none of the above diagnoses.

Figure 36.11 — Torsion. Whirlpool sign in the right cord with absent Doppler flow vs ample flow on the non-torsed left side (19-year-old; incidental left varicocele).

Cysts/Masses

  • Interpret with age and full clinical picture; generally well characterised by ultrasound.
  • Commonest prepubertal primary testicular tumour = benign teratoma — heterogeneous: solid + cystic + calcified components.
  • Yolk sac tumour — homogeneous, well-vascularised mass (Figure 36.13: enlarged testis in a 2-year-old).
  • Epidermoid cyst — alternating hyper/hypoechoic "onion rings" with no internal flow.
  • Congenital adrenal hyperplasia: bilateral hypoechoic, hyperaemic, heterogeneous testicular adrenal rests with inadequate steroid replacement or poor compliance.

Undescended Testis

  • Do not image routine cryptorchidism (American Urological Association "Choosing Wisely") — poor sensitivity for detecting/localising the testis and it does not change management.
  • Reasonable exception: a difficult-to-examine obese child, where finding an inguinal testis simplifies the surgical approach.
  • Primary modality in ambiguous genitalia or severe hypospadias with non-palpable gonads → detects müllerian structures to guide work-up.

Differences of Sex Development

  • Sonography is the basis for identifying internal genital structures in differences of sex development (DSD) — hypospadias with unilateral/bilateral non-palpable testes, or female genital anomalies.

Concept — Sonography is first-line at every level because it is safe, repeatable and anatomic; its weakness is function (obstruction, reflux, pyelonephritis), and the rest of the chapter is essentially about which test fills that functional gap at the least cost in radiation and sedation.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Fundamentals of MRI in the Fetus and Child

  • Used to characterise complex congenital anomalies when sonography lacks detail; not helpful for infection, stones or trauma.
  • Precontrast series may suffice for ectopic ureters, duplication anomalies, renal cysts, calyceal diverticula.
  • Limits: cost, access, sedation in young children.
  • Fetal MRI is performed alongside a third-level obstetric ultrasound after about 20 weeks; any anatomy visible on ultrasound is more easily appreciated on MRI.

Protocol

  • MR urography (MRU) gives anatomy and function. Sequence: intravenous normal-saline hydration + Foley catheter → furosemide (before or during) → T2-weighted fast spin echo (FSE) sequences (rapid single-shot for anatomy; longer 2D/3D FSE for resolution; 3D reconstructable) → contrast → dynamic T1-weighted gradient-recalled echo (vasculature, parenchymal enhancement, excretion) → delayed images for ureteric course.
  • Hydration + diuretic → faster, better distension and fewer artefacts on contrast imaging.
  • Patlak method — graphic analysis using linear regression of tracer clearance — is the usual functional analysis of dynamic data.

Sedation in the child.

  • Standard protocol takes up to 1 hour; a shortened ~30-minute format exists but needs larger validation.
  • Propofol conscious sedation or general anaesthesia is often needed under 6 years.
  • Avoiding sedation: respiratory-triggered (breath-hold) sequences — at the cost of longer studies; child life specialists (preparation and coping techniques).

Kidney/Ureter

Hydronephrosis/Obstruction

  • MRU is a plausible alternative to diuretic renography: far better anatomical resolution, no radiation; pinpoints anomaly, gives differential function and drainage for surgical planning.
  • Protocols resemble mercaptoacetyltriglycine (MAG3) diuretic renography; gadolinium-based contrast; well hydrated; furosemide 1 mg/kg (max 20 mg); timing of furosemide, catheterisation and positioning vary by protocol.
  • Sequence: precontrast T2 → 3D T2 fat-saturated, 1-mm slices → slow contrast injection → T1 fat-saturated dynamic scan until contrast fills the ureters.
  • Barrier to adoption: complex post-processing by a specialised clinician; free software (e.g. "chop-fmru", ImageJ from the National Institutes of Health) assists.
  • Status: promising, but equivalence to diuretic renography unproven and interpretation training still needed.

Cysts/Masses/Stones

  • Poor for stones; useful for complex renal cysts and renal masses.
  • MRI vs CT for paediatric renal tumours: similar for capsular penetration and lymph node metastasis; MRI better for synchronous contralateral lesions.

Infection

  • Gadolinium-enhanced MRI images APN with accuracy similar to DMSA, single-photon emission CT (SPECT) and contrast CT, but is impractical (long sedation) and rarely changes care.

Other Congenital Malformations

  • Complex anatomy → MRU gives helpful anatomical detail.

Bladder/Urethra

  • Rarely more sensitive than a well-performed sonogram for the lower tract, but extremely useful for bladder masses, especially rhabdomyosarcoma.
  • Sequences: coronal wide-field short tau inversion recovery (STIR); diffusion-weighted imaging (b = 0, 100 or 600 s/mm²); non-enhanced and gadolinium-enhanced pelvis; 3D volumetric T1; axial and sagittal T2 fat-saturated.
  • Urine masks T2-hyperintense lesions → acquire non-enhanced T1 and fast sequences before gadolinium accumulates in tissue.
  • Rhabdomyosarcoma: uniformly low T1; heterogeneous high T2 and on contrast-enhanced T1 fat-saturated images (heterogeneity attributed to necrosis).
  • Useful for staging, but no MRI feature predicts subtype.

Scrotum/Testes/Internal Genitalia

Acute Scrotum

  • MRI has no role.

Cysts/Masses

  • No role in characterising scrotal cysts/masses; may be considered for staging testicular cancer — MR gives information predictably similar to the historical contrast CT of chest and abdomen.
  • Paediatric renal tumours: MRI preferred for locoregional staging; CT preferred by some for distant metastasis.

Undescended Testis

  • MR is marginally better at best than sonography for internal structures and gonadal detection.

Differences of Sex Development

  • Sonography + urogenitography usually suffice for diagnosis and surgical planning; MRI adds anatomical information only in a few very complex cases.

Concept — MRI is the non-ionising problem-solver for complex anatomy, tumour staging and (potentially) functional obstruction studies; its ceiling is set not by image quality but by sedation, cost and post-processing expertise.

Conventional Radiography

Plain Abdominal Radiography

  • KUB used for radiopaque stones and for constipation in children with voiding dysfunction.
  • Few data support its accuracy for diagnosing or monitoring constipation, but it gives persuasive visual evidence to sceptical parents.

Intravenous Pyelography

  • Intravenous pyelography (IVP) has fallen out of favour, replaced by high-resolution anatomical and reproducible functional studies.
  • Technique: sequential radiographs through excretion and drainage, in different focal planes.
  • A good study localises obstructive lesions and radiopaque stones; drainage time assessed, but less precisely than diuretic renography.

Retrograde Pyelography

  • Contrast injected into the ureteric orifice — needs anaesthesia in a child; best for intraoperative localisation of a lesion already found on other studies.
  • Trap: free retrograde flow ≠ unobstructed antegrade flow (and vice versa), especially after prior repair — e.g. small postsurgical tissue flaps obstruct antegrade flow yet look unobstructed retrogradely.

Concept — Conventional radiography survives only where it answers a question nothing safer does; retrograde flow is a valve-direction test, so it cannot prove antegrade patency.

Voiding Cystourethrography/Micturating Cystourethrogram

Fundamentals of VCUG

  • Assesses lower urinary tract function; routinely done without sedation, though invasive → pain/anxiety mitigation protocols exist.
  • After UTI: defer until the child has had several days of antibiotics and is clinically improving.
  • Catheter check: brief fluoroscopic image confirms tip in bladder and not too much catheter advanced.
  • Catheter not midline → malposition: e.g. utricle/posterior urethral abnormality in males, entry into an ectopic ureter in females.
  • Too much catheter → knotting.

Kidney/Ureter

Obstruction

  • Routine VCUG for all hydronephrosis has been challenged; most algorithms reserve it for hydronephrosis with dilated ureter(s).

Cysts/Masses

  • No renal cystic disease or mass is specifically evaluated by VCUG.

Infection

  • Historical 4–6-week delay after UTI (fear that trigonal inflammation causes transient VUR) is unnecessary — clinically significant VUR rarely disappears after treatment.
  • Early VCUG during febrile UTI: endotoxin-induced ureteric dilatation may overestimate the VUR grade.

Other Congenital Malformations

  • Only UVJ anomalies among upper-tract malformations benefit from VCUG.

Trauma

  • No renal or ureteric trauma is specifically evaluated by VCUG.

Bladder/Urethra

Obstruction

  • Excellent high-resolution anatomical study: bladder wall (trabeculation, ureterocele, diverticula, bladder-neck hypertrophy, tumours), urethra (PUV, stricture, diverticulum), bladder stones, rupture, foreign bodies; assesses emptying.
  • Scout film can reveal many spinal abnormalities.

Figure 36.15 — VCUG findings.

  • Highly trabeculated bladder; ureterocele seen within the bladder and then everting; large diverticulum with elongated posterior urethra in PUV; PUV with bilateral grade 5 VUR; short distal bulbar stricture.

Cysts/Masses

  • Dilatations/masses seen: urachal diverticula, bladder diverticula, dilated utricles, fibroepithelial polyps.

Infection

  • Single or few non-febrile UTIs (cystitis/urethritis) → VCUG adds little; with increasing severity or recurrence, some obtain VCUG to exclude anatomical risk factors.
  • Circumcised males: UTI risk so low that any UTI may merit evaluation.

Other Congenital Malformations

Occult VUR.

  • Evidence for "occult" VUR: new contralateral VUR appears in 7–33% of unilateral VUR; VCUG finds VUR in only about 40% of DMSA-proven APN.
  • Mechanisms: variation between voiding cycles; infection-induced UVJ dysfunction.
  • Positional instillation of contrast (PIC) cystography: cystoscopically directed stream of contrast, hung 1 m above the bladder, aimed at the ureteric orifice to challenge it.
  • Indication: negative VCUG + recurrent febrile UTIs; identifies occult VUR in up to 39% with unilateral VUR and 59% with negative VCUG + abnormal DMSA.
  • Debate: critics — mostly low-grade and transient; proponents (with concomitant endoscopic treatment) cite a 58% reduction in recurrent UTI.

Trauma

  • Pelvic trauma, especially with blood at the meatus and/or difficulty voiding → contrast imaging of bladder and urethra; urethral injury most likely; prompt intervention mandatory.
  • Blood at meatus → retrograde urethrogram before any catheterisation → urethra cleared → advance catheter → fill bladder by gravity until distended.
  • Expected bladder capacity (mL) = (age in years + 2) × 30, maximum 400 mL.

Figure 36.16 — Bladder injury with pelvic fracture. Screening CT suggests injury → contrast cystography confirms: extreme elevation of the bladder neck ("pie-in-the-sky") and contrast collecting in the retroperitoneal (extraperitoneal) space.

Scrotum/Testes/Internal Genitalia

  • Not useful for routine genital anomalies, except urethral, bladder and internal genital anatomy in cloaca, urogenital sinus and DSD.
  • Males with ectopic ureters or dilated utricles may have recurrent UTI (epididymitis, febrile/symptomatic UTI) → best evaluated with VCUG.

Differences of Sex Development

  • Urogenitography is essential for surgical planning and classification.
  • Technique: catheter in the single perineal opening → contrast under fluoroscopy → define the confluence of urethra and vagina and orientation; alternatively Foley balloon inflated and pressed to the perineum for retrograde filling.
  • Classified by Shopfner's classification.

Figure 36.18 — Genitogram, 46,XX ovotesticular DSD. Low confluence of urethra and vagina with reflux into the right fallopian tube → Shopfner type III.

Concept — VCUG is the anatomical reference test for the bladder outlet and urethra; its limits are that it is invasive, ionising and a snapshot — which is why reflux can be "occult" and why its timing after infection and its trigger criteria have been narrowed.

Computed Tomography

Fundamentals of CT Imaging in Children

  • CT's paediatric role rests on availability and speed, not superior diagnosis — best realised in blunt abdominal trauma and polytrauma; its need is dwindling as sonography and MR advance.

Kidney/Ureter

Obstruction

  • Little role in hydronephrosis — little advantage over ultrasound, plus radiation.
  • With IV contrast and delayed images, a delayed nephrogram and ureteric drainage vs the contralateral side signals obstruction, but is hard to quantify.
  • Helpful anatomy in complex anomalies (ectopic kidney, ectopic ureter, megaureter, duplication, renovascular anomalies) when sonography or MR are inconclusive or unavailable.

Cysts/Masses

  • Contrast-enhanced CT for paediatric renal tumours is commonplace.

Infection

  • Limited role; reserved for when non-ionising modalities are inconclusive.
  • CT and MR help in renal abscess, extraurinary fistulisation, postoperative complications, or infection secondary to obstruction.

Figure 36.17 — Renal abscess. 6-year-old: abdominal pain, raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein, inability to stand erect → Doppler sonography shows heterogeneous lower-pole collection → CT confirms abscess.

Calculus Disease

  • CT's most obvious advantage — better than sonography at detecting and quantifying stone burden, but the extra accuracy is seldom clinically impactful.
  • Apply ALARA, especially in stone-formers facing lifelong repeated imaging.

Trauma

  • CT with and without IV contrast is routine in paediatric abdominal trauma.
  • Timing trap: images taken only in the arterial phase enhance cortex alone → show contusion/laceration but miss collecting-system and ureteric injuries → delayed (excretory) images are required.

Bladder/Urethra

  • Little role in lower-tract obstruction, infection or malformation; may image bladder masses, but MR preferred for soft-tissue resolution.

Trauma

  • Lower-tract CT reserved for bladder or pelvic trauma.
  • CT cystography: antegrade/delayed filling or retrograde catheter filling → detects bladder or augment rupture.
  • Advantage over conventional cystography: better anatomy and immediate classification as intraperitoneal vs extraperitoneal, which determines management.

Scrotum/Testes/Internal Genitalia

  • No role in acute scrotum, genital cysts/masses, undescended testis or DSD; part of staging for testicular/paratesticular malignancy (metastases).

Concept — In children CT is chosen for speed, not superiority; outside trauma and tumour staging almost every CT question is answerable by sonography or MR without radiation — and when CT is used, phase timing decides whether the collecting system is actually seen.

DMSA Renal Cortical Scintigraphy

  • Mechanism: DMSA is taken up by proximal tubular cells, a process dependent on renal blood flow; APN reduces focal blood flow → photon-deficient areas.
  • Lesions may be focal, multifocal or diffuse; distinguishing acute from chronic is difficult when APN is superimposed on old scars.
  • Validation (piglet autopsy model, histopathological reference): sensitivity 87%, specificity 100% for APN; SPECT gives slightly higher sensitivity, lower specificity, equivalent accuracy to planar (pinhole) imaging.

MAG-3 Diuresis–Induced Renal Scintigraphy

  • Diuretic renography is the gold standard for distinguishing obstructive from non-obstructive hydronephrosis/hydroureter; tracer 99mTc-diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid (DTPA) or, more commonly, 99mTc-MAG3.
  • Strict protocol required for reproducibility (the "well-tempered" diuretic renogram).

Radionuclide Testicular Scanning

  • In use since the 1970s, to distinguish torsion from inflammation (epididymitis, orchitis, appendage torsion).
  • Technique: IV 99mTc-pertechnetate → dynamic and static gamma images of the pelvis.
  • Photon-deficient = poor flow (torsion); photon-hyperdense = inflammation (epididymitis).
  • In experienced centres, sensitivity and specificity up to 100%, potentially better than sonography — but limited availability, invasiveness and radiation prevent adoption over ultrasound.
  • Best use: cases equivocal on examination and sonography, and only if it does not unreasonably delay potentially testis-saving surgery.
Key points: imaging techniques
  • ALARA governs all paediatric imaging.
  • Non-ionising: contrast-enhanced sonography and machine-learning-enhanced sonographic analysis should be adopted as they become available — may obviate more invasive tests.
  • Obstruction: sonography is the initial test and the follow-up test; ancillary VCUG and/or diuresis renography per findings; normal infant kidney (hypoechoic pyramids, distinct corticomedullary junction) can be mistaken for hydronephrosis; diagnosing obstruction on renography requires review of all test parameters.
  • Cysts/masses/stones: MCDK = randomly distributed cysts of varying size, scant hyperechoic parenchyma; ARPKD = bilateral, enlarged echogenic parenchyma; ADPKD = bilateral, parenchyma replaced by cysts; renal masses → contrast-enhanced CT follows sonography.
  • Infection: guidelines use sonography as initial screen despite low yield; a normal sonogram does not risk-stratify febrile UTI nor predict VUR.
  • "Bottom-up" vs "top-down": see comparison below.
  • Other congenital malformations: sonography is the initial imaging for suspected DSD — müllerian structures and their relation to the urinary tract.
  • Trauma: abdominal sonography compares favourably with contrast CT and suits longitudinal follow-up of renal injury (perinephric haematoma, urinoma); ionising studies with IV or intraluminal iodinated contrast evaluate genitourinary trauma; evaluation is incomplete without delayed CT views of parenchyma and collecting system, or lateral projections of bladder, bladder neck and urethra.
  • Acute scrotum: history, examination and scrotal sonography discriminate inflammation, neoplasm, trauma and torsion; nuclear scintigraphy adds little over sonography.
  • Undescended testis: sonography unreliable — do not use, except to seek an inguinal testis in an overweight boy.
