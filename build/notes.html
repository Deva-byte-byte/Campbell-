Chapter 51 — Pathophysiology of Urinary Tract Obstruction

Pathophysiology of Paediatric Urinary Tract Obstruction

  • Definition: any anatomic or functional abnormality that impedes urine flow, injuring the kidney and lower urinary tract; may occur anywhere from calyces to urethral meatus, upper and/or lower tract.
  • Four classifying axes: acute vs chronic · partial vs complete · intrinsic vs extrinsic · unilateral vs bilateral.
  • The governing risk is permanent damage to the developing kidney.

Epidemiology

  • Overall incidence ≈ 1 in 500 live births worldwide.
  • Fetal lower urinary tract obstruction (LUTO): ≈ 2–3 per 10,000 fetuses (per-pregnancy figure under Urinary Tract Sites).
  • Prevalence varies by population and region; severity forms a spectrum.

Association With End-Stage Renal Disease and Kidney Transplantation

  • Untreated severe obstruction → chronic kidney disease (CKD) → end-stage renal disease (ESRD).
  • High-grade obstruction: lifetime ESRD risk up to 30%.
  • ESRD means lifelong dialysis or transplantation, each with significant morbidity — hence the urgency of early detection.

Key points

  • Urinary tract obstruction is relatively common in children.
  • Severe uncorrected obstruction → CKD, ESRD or renal failure.

Concept — paediatric obstruction strikes a kidney that is still developing: the same pressure that a mature kidney partly tolerates can permanently cap nephron number, so the subject is organised around detecting early and decompressing before development is lost.

  • Presentation depends on site, severity, developmental stage at onset and duration.

Key points

  • Manifestations depend on location, severity, age at onset and duration; location dictates the symptoms.
  • Prenatal detection is by routine US; postnatal presentation is more variable, from asymptomatic to life-threatening.
  • LUTO carries significant long-term morbidity and mortality; UPJO accounts for the vast majority of upper tract obstruction.

Concept — LUTO is a bilateral, whole-fetus disease (kidneys and lungs fail together through loss of amniotic fluid), whereas upper tract obstruction is usually a unilateral, organ-level problem with a normal contralateral kidney — which is why LUTO dominates mortality despite being far rarer than UPJO.

Definition of Obstruction

  • Obstruction is an impedance of urinary flow at any level, involving one or several components.
PartialHigh-grade / complete
Fewer signs and symptoms; may resolve spontaneouslyMore signs and symptoms; requires intervention
  • Causes: congenital malformation, structural abnormality, inflammation, tumour, extrinsic compression (Table 51.1).
  • Spectrum: incidental imaging finding → recurrent UTI, retention, hydronephrosis, impaired renal function.
  • Consequences: untreated severe obstruction → CKD; when prenatal, also pulmonary hypoplasia and limb deformity.
  • Management aims: relieve severe obstruction → preserve renal function → prevent long-term complications, by conservative, pharmacological, endoscopic or surgical means chosen by cause, severity and clinical status.

Key points

  • Obstruction may be partial or high grade, at any level, intrinsic or extrinsic, congenital or acquired.
  • Treatment aims to relieve obstruction, preserve renal function and prevent complications.

Concept — obstruction is defined by its effect on flow and function, not by dilation: a dilated system can be unobstructed and resolve alone, which is why the partial/high-grade distinction, not the image, decides treatment.

Diagnosis of Urinary Tract Obstruction

  • The central task: separate children who need surgery to preserve renal function from those who will resolve spontaneously.
  • Imaging and laboratory studies grade severity and time treatment.

Timing of Obstruction: Prenatal Diagnostic Testing

  • Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) are among the commonest prenatal US anomalies: 1 in 250 – 1 in 1000 pregnancies.
  • Severity is judged by degree of hydronephrosis, associated urinary tract changes and amniotic fluid volume; fetal blood and urine biomarkers and proteomics may add prognostic information.

Imaging

Ultrasound. The primary prenatal modality — cost-effective, widely available, part of standard anomaly screening; grades obstruction by APD, calyceal dilation, parenchymal thickness and appearance, and bladder and ureteral abnormalities.

  • Landmarks: kidneys visible from 9 weeks; bladder filling by 12 weeks — not seen by 15 weeks is abnormal; normal fetal ureters are not visualised, so a visible ureter is dilated.
  • UTD classification: unified prenatal and postnatal grading from eight societies (Society for Pediatric Urology, Society for Fetal Urology, American Society of Pediatric Nephrology, Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine, American College of Radiology, American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine, Society for Pediatric Radiology, Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound), stratified before and after 28 weeks.
  • European Rare Kidney Disease Reference Network (ERKNet) consensus, May 2022: APD is the most reliable parameter; obstructive uropathy is considered at APD ≥ 7 mm at 16–27 weeks and ≥ 10 mm at ≥ 28 weeks.
  • Below these thresholds, obstruction is not considered unless there is megacystis and/or reduced amniotic fluid.

Magnetic resonance imaging. Fetal MRI is a selective adjunct to US (Fig. 51.2).

  • Indications: US technically limited (oligo- or anhydramnios, maternal obesity); complex anatomy in severe obstruction; planning fetal intervention.
  • Advantages: not limited by fetal position; multiplanar. Three-dimensional reconstruction and functional renal imaging are developing.
  • Fig. 51.2 — T2-weighted fetal MRI of cloacal malformation: dilated ventral bladder, with uterus didelphys and massive hydrometrocolpos behind it.

Laboratory Studies

Bladder taps. Fetal vesicocentesis — US-guided aspiration of fetal urine to distinguish obstructive from non-obstructive hydronephrosis and predict postnatal renal function; tests sodium, chloride and calcium (most important), osmolarity and low-molecular-weight proteins (β2-microglobulin, proteomics). Supporting evidence is still accruing.

Urinary markers. Utility debated. Used to separate obstructive from non-obstructive hydronephrosis and megacystis, predict renal function and monitor fetal intervention for LUTO.

  • Megacystis: enlarged bladder failing to empty over 40 minutes of observation at ≥ 18 weeks.
  • Three serial samples; the last reflects current renal function (earlier samples are stagnant urine).

Glomerular filtration rate. GFR is the fundamental measure of renal function and guides treatment; the most accurate paediatric estimate is cystatin C–based, being less dependent on muscle mass and age.

Biomarkers. First-year panel — serum creatinine, serum and urine cystatin C, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), retinol-binding protein (RBP), microalbuminuria — alone and combined is a promising non-invasive way to identify infants needing expedited surgery.

Pressure-Flow Studies

The Whitaker test. Invasive assessment of upper tract obstruction.

  • Indications: (1) equivocal less invasive tests; (2) suspected obstruction with poor renal function; (3) suspected intermittent obstruction; (4) gross dilation with positive diuresis renogram.
  • Technique: percutaneous access → high-rate perfusion of the renal pelvis with saline or dilute contrast → serial renal pelvic and bladder pressures.
  • Interpretation: unobstructed → drains freely, no appreciable pressure rise; obstructed → pressure > 12 cm H2O or a continuous rise.

Uroflowmetry. Measures voided volume, maximum flow rate (Qmax) and flow curve to diagnose lower tract dysfunction or obstruction.

Key points

  • Prenatal: imaging (US, MRI) and laboratory studies (bladder taps, urinary markers, biomarkers).
  • Postnatal: laboratory studies plus primary, secondary and tertiary imaging, as tabulated above.

Concept — each postnatal study answers one question — US for anatomy, MAG3 for drainage, DMSA for scar, VCUG for the bladder outlet and reflux — invasive or irradiating tests are escalations used only when these fail to separate obstruction from dilation.

Pathological Changes of the Urinary Tract With Obstruction

  • Driven by mechanical stress, inflammatory responses and molecular signalling.

Lower Urinary Tract

Pathological Changes in the Bladder

  • BOO → detrusor hypertrophy (larger fibres, more mass) and hyperplasia (more smooth muscle cells) → wall thickening and reduced compliance.
  • Changes span urothelium, suburothelium, detrusor cells, extracellular matrix (ECM) and nerves.
  • Remodelling stimuli: cyclic stretch → raised hydrostatic and cyclic hydrodynamic pressure and hypoxia.
  • Three-stage remodelling: hypertrophy → compensation → decompensation.
  • Compliance is mediated by the ECM: chronic inflammation releases interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) → ↑ collagen and fibronectin → fibrosis → poorly compliant, hostile bladder.
  • High-pressure bladder (> 40 cm H2O) → risk of renal damage and failure.

Concept — bladder failure in obstruction is a matrix disease, not only a muscle disease: hypertrophy compensates, but inflammatory collagen removes compliance, and lost compliance — pressure above 40 cm H2O — transmits injury to the kidneys.

Haemodynamic Changes With Renal Obstruction

  • Sequence: urine production continues → proximal pressure rises → net filtration pressure falls → reduced perfusion and ischaemic injury.
  • Bilateral obstruction or a solitary kidney → more profound disease.
  • GFR and renal blood flow fall in direct relation to the high-pressure system, which compensatory hypertrophy and smooth muscle stretch perpetuate as hydronephrosis.
  • Ischaemic injury — inflammation, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis — begins within 2 hours of complete obstruction.

Effects of Obstruction on Renal Tubular Function

  • Sustained obstruction → hyposthenuria (concentrating defect) and impaired acidification → polyuria and electrolyte derangement.
Urinary concentration ability — early versus prolonged obstruction
Early — wastingProlonged — retention
Sodium, potassium, bicarbonate, magnesium, calcium, phosphatePotassium, hydrogen, chloride, ammonium
  • Distal renal tubular acidosis is characteristic; potassium is wasted early and retained late.
  • Ion transport changes: obstruction downregulates aquaporins (AQP1–4) — proximal tubule, descending thin limb, collecting duct — and sodium transporters throughout the nephron → concentrating defect and salt wasting; impaired H+ transport → metabolic acidosis in severe cases.

Egress of Urine From the Obstructed Kidney

  • Partial vs complete obstruction sets how far egress is compromised; retained urine → progressive pelvicalyceal dilation (hydronephrosis) → raised intrarenal pressure → long-term damage.

Key points

  • Changes in both tracts are caused by mechanical stress, inflammation and molecular signalling.
  • Lower tract: detrusor hypertrophy and hyperplasia with deleterious ECM change. Upper tract: impaired nephrogenesis and abnormal development.
  • Chronic obstruction → scarring, impaired tubular and concentrating function, altered ion transport; haemodynamic change; reduced perfusion → inflammation, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis.

Concept — the age of the kidney decides the injury: the immature kidney dies and fails to develop, the mature kidney proliferates then scars — and in both, falling perfusion and transporter loss explain the polyuria, acidosis and salt wasting seen clinically.

Clinical Impact of Renal Obstruction

Hypertension

  • Obstruction causes secondary hypertension; renal parenchymal disease accounts for 60–80% of secondary hypertension in children.
Bilateral ureteral obstructionUnilateral ureteral obstruction
≈ 77% hypertensive≈ 20% hypertensive; renin secretion lateralises to the affected kidney in most
  • Mechanism: ↑ intrarenal pressure → ↓ perfusion and GFR → renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) activation → ↑ angiotensin II and aldosterone → vasoconstriction and sodium retention.

Compensatory Renal Growth

  • Significant functional loss → compensatory hypertrophy driven by insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), TGF-β1 and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF): glomerular enlargement and tubular hypertrophy — glomerulomegaly, not nephrogenesis.

Renal Recovery

Chronic renal insufficiency risk. Injury begins within 2 hours; prolonged obstruction → scarring and atony → end-stage renal failure over days to months. Treatment can reverse damage; incomplete recovery is common.

Acute-on-chronic renal injury. Acute obstruction on pre-existing CKD → rapid deterioration into acute kidney injury (AKI).

Renal reserve. The capacity to raise function under demand — compromised by obstruction, limiting the response to further insults.

Postobstructive Diuresis

Unilateral obstructionBilateral obstruction
Postobstructive diuresis (POD) in ≈ 2%POD in ≈ 50%
  • Polyuric phase after acute relief of obstruction, restoring euvolaemia and plasma composition.
  • Mechanism: aquaporin downregulation, loss of medullary tonicity and tubular transporter dysfunction from apoptosis.
  • Severe fluid and electrolyte shifts → monitor urine output and serum and urine electrolytes closely (see Chapter 90).

Key points

  • Obstruction-related hypertension is secondary hypertension; renal parenchymal disease causes 60–80% of secondary hypertension in children.
  • Significant functional loss → compensatory hypertrophy. Prolonged or recurrent obstruction raises CKD risk; POD follows acute relief with fluid and electrolyte imbalance.

Concept — bilaterality is the multiplier: with a normal contralateral kidney, hypertension, POD and renal failure are uncommon because the healthy kidney compensates and regulates volume; remove that buffer and each complication becomes the rule.

Treatment of Obstruction

  • Approach set by location and aetiology; multidisciplinary because decompression carries medical complications. Aims: relieve symptoms, preserve renal function, improve outcomes.

Pain Management

  • Sources: baroreceptor activation by obstruction, stent discomfort, infection, decompression haematuria.
  • Agents: non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) only if renal function is not compromised, paracetamol, α-adrenergic blockers, anticholinergics, occasionally opioids.

Management of Oliguria

  • In severe obstruction or AKI: treat the cause, optimise fluid and electrolytes, support renal perfusion.

Management of Postobstructive Diuresis

  • Monitor intake and output, correct electrolytes, replace fluid as needed.
  • Usually resolves within 24 hours; seldom lasts beyond 48 hours.
  • Do not overcorrect — over-replacement perpetuates POD.
  • Risk factors after valve ablation: oligohydramnios (prolonged, severe obstruction); preterm birth (low aquaporin-2 expression); raised serum creatinine (renal impairment).

Management of Chronic Kidney Disease

  • Blood pressure control, dietary modification and medication to slow progression; severe disease or ESRD → peritoneal dialysis, haemodialysis or transplantation.

Emerging Therapeutic Options With Precision Medicine

  • Individualised treatment based on patient characteristics and genetics may allow more targeted interventions.

Key points

  • Treatment depends on location, aetiology and duration of obstruction.
  • It aims to relieve obstruction and manage disease- and treatment-related complications.

Concept — treatment runs on a temporise-then-define logic: drainage buys renal function and stability, definitive reconstruction follows, and the complications of relief are managed as carefully as the obstruction itself.
