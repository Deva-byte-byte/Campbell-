Chapter 9 — Occupational Health in Urology

  • Urology is physically and emotionally demanding and practised in a potentially hazardous environment; occupational health affects performance, surgical outcomes and well-being.
  • Surgical culture devalues self-care and rewards grit, efficiency and self-sacrifice, so attention to occupational health is countercultural — yet managing it optimises the surgeon's health, performance and quality of care.

PHYSICAL ERGONOMICS

Musculoskeletal Strain and Injury: The Importance of Surgical Ergonomics

  • Work-related musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) affect 46–90% of urologists during their careers (survey data).
  • Pain degrades performance: reduced range of motion, strength, motor control and mental focus; increased irritability.
  • Site of MSD tracks the type of surgery:
    • Open → neck, shoulder, low back.
    • Robotic/endoscopic → eye strain, neck pain, hand/wrist strain.
  • Non-modifiable risk factors: younger age, inexperience, anthropometrics (short stature, small glove size, low grip strength), female sex, high body mass index (BMI), and medical conditions (arthritis, neuropathy, varicose veins, poor circulation).
  • Prevention targets modifiable factors (Table 9.1): prolonged static postures, repetitive motions and awkward positions.
  • Environmental adjustment — table height, monitor location/height, foot-pedal position — optimises posture; targets differ by technique (Figs. 9.1–9.4).
  • Strain reducers: antifatigue mats, two-piece lead, lightweight headlamps, lightweight steep-declination loupes.
  • "Postural resets": regularly check position, identify awkward postures, correct toward neutral.
  • Outside theatre: regular exercise and strength training appear protective; daily 2-minute neck resistance exercises improved chronic neck discomfort in a trial of non-surgeons.
  • Intraoperative microbreaks/stretches reduced pain and improved subjective focus without lengthening cases (several small studies); a free web app prompts stretches at set intervals.
  • Exoskeletons (open) and robotic flexible ureteroscopy improve ergonomics and pain but are not yet widely used.
  • Many urologists will need medication, physiotherapy, massage, time off or case-mix change during their careers; prevention prolongs careers.

Presbyopia and Ergonomics in Urologists

  • About a decade into practice most urologists need near-vision correction for surgery; existing wearers move to bifocals, trifocals or progressives.
  • General-purpose multifocals create ergonomic strain: looking through a narrow lens segment drives neck extension and long static neck positions.
  • Prefer "workspace" lenses correcting only near and intermediate vision; if standard progressives/bifocals are used, lower the computer monitor.

Computer Workstation Ergonomics

  • Desk work compounds MSD (electronic records, messaging, video visits).
  • Set-up: knees 90°, feet flat (or footrest); elbows 90°, wrists straight, arms supported.
  • Monitor top at or slightly below eye level → gaze-down about 20°; screen 20–40 inches (50–100 cm) away.
  • Eye rests and walking breaks; a laptop is almost impossible to use ergonomically without a riser or separate keyboard (Video 9.1).

Concept — Musculoskeletal injury is a cumulative-load problem: static, awkward postures sustained over years. Every intervention either restores neutral posture (set-up, eyewear, monitors) or interrupts the load (microbreaks, resets, varying cases), which is why cheap, simple measures work.

OCCUPATIONAL EXPOSURES

Exposures to Infectious Diseases

  • Core controls: hand hygiene, personal protective equipment (PPE), immunisation.
  • Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends vaccination of health care workers against hepatitis B, influenza, and measles–mumps–rubella.
  • Bloodborne pathogens — human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV) — are the concern because of intraoperative sharps injuries.
  • Reduce by safe handling/disposal protocols and safety-engineered devices; institutions provide needlestick training, reporting systems and post-exposure protocols.

Surgical Smoke Inhalation

  • Hazards: particulate matter, toxic gases, bioaerosols — a respiratory risk to the whole team.
  • Mitigation: smoke evacuation, ventilation, respiratory protection, smoke-evacuating diathermy pencils.
  • Carbon dioxide laser ablation of genital condylomata → N95 mask + smoke evacuator with tip within 2 inches (5 cm) of the site, to prevent human papillomavirus transmission to the team's airways.

Chemical Exposures

  • Sources: disinfectants, anaesthetic agents, chemotherapeutics; mitigate with ventilation, PPE, safe handling and guidelines.
  • Intraoperative intravesical chemotherapy → two pairs of chemotherapy gloves, impermeable disposable gown, face shield and goggles; a closed-system drug-transfer device adds protection against spills.

Ionising Radiation Exposure

  • Fluoroscopy raises risk of cataract, infertility and cancer.
  • Protect: lead apron, thyroid shield, lead glasses, lead gloves, plus dose-reduction techniques (Table 9.2) — keep exposure as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA).
  • Institutions must supply dosimeters and ongoing radiation-safety training; training correlates with lower occupational dose.
  • Pregnant surgeon: fetal dosimeter at waist level under the apron; maternity or double-thickness lead; minimise participation in radiation cases where possible.

Intraoperative Distractions and Noise

  • Distractions and flow disruptions impair focus, raise cognitive load and jeopardise safety — but some (urgent pages, instrument updates) are necessary.
  • Protect critical portions: music paused, no non-emergency pages, no team breaks; state expectations at the briefing or time-out.
  • Noise (equipment, conversation, alarms, movement) impairs communication, increases errors, task load and fatigue; prolonged high-intensity exposure causes hearing loss.
  • Measured theatre noise exceeds recommendations for high-concentration work but does not usually reach hearing-damage levels.
  • Mitigate: closed-loop communication, quiet critical phases, consider equipment noise in capital purchases; keep modifiable sound comfortable for the team and safe for the patient.

Concept — Each exposure is controlled by the same hierarchy: remove or reduce the source, interpose a barrier, then protect the individual. Radiation (time, distance, shielding), smoke (evacuation, then N95) and chemotherapy (closed transfer, then double gloves) are the same principle applied to different hazards.

PHYSICAL HEALTH OF SURGEONS

  • Heart disease, unintentional injury and suicide are top-five causes of death at 25–54 years and top-ten at 55–64; each can be aggravated by occupational exposure and culture.
  • Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is the most threatening modifiable cause of death at 35–64.
  • Chronic job stress worsens cardiovascular and mental health risk; sleep deprivation raises risk of fatal car crash; burnout leads to depression, substance misuse and suicide.
  • Physicians see their own primary care provider less than non-physicians, surgeons least of all. Surgeons who use primary care and do regular aerobic and strength exercise report higher quality of life and less burnout.
  • Why urologists avoid care:
    • Time constraints — schedules crowd out appointments.
    • Culture of self-sacrifice — reluctance to burden colleagues with cover.
    • Medical knowledge — self-diagnosis and self-treatment delay care.
    • Fear of judgement — by colleagues or regulators, especially for mental health care.
  • Surgeons are a costly, long-trained asset; neglect of fitness, nutrition, lipid screening/treatment and stress shortens careers.
  • Treat lipids and hypertension to prevent myocardial infarction and stroke; resilience training offsets chronic stress; age-appropriate cancer screening finds disease at treatable stages.
  • Evidence-informed biomarkers worth early attention: low-density lipoprotein cholesterol; visceral fat and muscle mass (DEXA with body composition); cardiorespiratory fitness (VO₂ max); insulin sensitivity (oral glucose tolerance test) — targets for preventing ASCVD, type 2 diabetes and cancer.
  • Nutrition is key but judged beyond the chapter's scope because of controversy.

Physiologic Response to Surgery

  • During routine surgery: heart rate ↑, heart-rate variability ↓, salivary cortisol ↑ — back to baseline about 30 minutes after.
  • Magnitude varies between surgeons, possibly with negative affectivity and trait anxiety; the long-term effect of repeated exposure is unknown.

Sleep

  • National Sleep Foundation: 7–9 hours' sleep opportunity (age 25–64); 7–8 hours (≥65).
  • Sleep hygiene: no large meals, caffeine or alcohol before bed; fixed bed and rise times; dark, quiet, cool room; no screens in the bedroom; daytime activity.
  • Barriers: workload, on-call, administrative frustration, worry about ill patients or adverse events.
  • Sleep deprivation degrades objective performance by at least 12% (task time, economy of motion, errors). Acute vs chronic effects are incompletely studied; individuals and systems should remove barriers.

Fitness

  • CDC: 150 min moderate or 75 min vigorous exercise per week + muscle strengthening on 2 days, covering all major muscle groups.
  • Muscle strength and cardiorespiratory fitness correlate with longevity; target exercise and treatment at areas of ergonomic stress.

Concept — The surgeon's commonest killers are modifiable and are amplified by the job; the failure is not of knowledge but of uptake, driven by time, culture and fear of judgement. The chapter's remedy is to treat the surgeon as a patient with the same screening and prevention offered to others.

MENTAL AND EMOTIONAL HEALTH OF SURGEONS

  • Job-related stress is common and underlies several distinct but interrelated conditions: burnout, compassion fatigue and others.

Burnout

  • Coined by Freudenberger (1980): occupational syndrome of emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and low sense of personal accomplishment.
  • Personal effects: damaged relationships, substance misuse, depression/anxiety, suicide risk. Professional effects: poorer care, lower patient satisfaction, higher turnover.
  • Prevalence: medical providers 40–60%; urology about 50%; general working population 28%.
  • Risk factors in urologists: more hours, more patient encounters per week, mid-career rather than early career; chronic MSD pain also raises burnout and shortens careers.
  • Future detection: wearable physiological monitors (burnout raises blood pressure and heart rate); DNA methylation or hair cortisol as biomarkers.
  • No universal solution; tailor to practice and person. Effective levers: more autonomy, efficient work processes, less administrative burden, peer/community support.
  • Faculty spending ≥20% of time on personally meaningful work have lower burnout.
  • Institutions should monitor burnout and weigh well-being in safety and financial plans; self-care must not displace system-level change.

The Traumatised Provider ("Second Victim Syndrome")

  • Leriche: every surgeon carries "a small cemetery" of failures.
  • After an adverse event or error: post-traumatic stress symptoms, anxiety, depression; guilt, shame, lost confidence; insomnia, anorexia, headache.
  • Terminology: "second victim" can offend patients, families and providers; "traumatised caregiver" is an alternative.
  • Consequences: higher burnout, substance misuse, early retirement and suicide.
  • Barriers to help: embarrassment, shame, fear of judgement, licensure/credentialing implications of mental-health treatment.
  • Response: training should teach recognition in self and others; institutions provide "psychological first aid", ongoing support and counselling. Peer support reduces distress and improves coping; programmes improve safety culture.

Psychological Stress and Management Strategies

  • Drivers: organisational, environmental, technological, personal, team and patient factors; stress affects the surgeon's technical and non-technical performance and that of the team.
  • Two levels of defence: prevent stressors (usually system-level) and build individual stress-management skills.
  • Stress Continuum Model (US military): leaders identify each colleague's zone and deploy resources to return them to readiness; nearer "ill" → greater need for professional help (Fig. 9.5).

  • Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and mindfulness increase resilience under stress (trials). CBT: thoughts, emotions and behaviours are interconnected — identify and challenge distorted thoughts, replace with rational ones; deliverable by a professional or self-taught.
  • Dialectical behavioural therapy (DBT) = CBT + mindfulness, emphasising acceptance and validation of emotion alongside behaviour change.
  • Both rest on recognising cognitive distortions (Table 9.3). Imposter syndrome — persistent inability to believe success is deserved — is a distortion (personalisation) linked to burnout, commonest in trainees, manageable with CBT.
  • Mental practice (cognitive rehearsal) and relaxation (breathing) reduce stress in surgical trainees; energy/attention management, goal setting and performance routines work in other high-stress fields.
  • Personal strategies: supportive relationships; workload management (prioritise, delegate, realistic goals); mindfulness; exercise, nutrition, sleep; hobbies and non-work pursuits.

Conclusion

  • Occupational health spans ergonomics, exposures, physical and mental health; evidence-based individual strategies plus institutional policy protect surgeons, enhance patient safety and optimise outcomes.

Concept — Stress is a continuum, not a binary: the same person moves between zones, and the response must escalate with the zone. Self-care handles the green–yellow end; the orange–red end needs workload change, peers and professionals, and institutions own the stressors that individuals cannot fix.

Key points
  • Work-related musculoskeletal injury is common, from isometric contraction, awkward postures and poor monitor positioning.
  • Short intraoperative stretch breaks reduce strain and improve focus.
  • Minimise radiation: lowest settings, least time, lead apron and thyroid collar.
  • Physical health: exercise, diet, sleep hygiene, standard primary-care screening.
  • Stress: CBT and mindfulness, peer support, institutional action on sources of frustration.
