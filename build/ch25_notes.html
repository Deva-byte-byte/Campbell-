Chapter 25 — Molecular Genetics and Cancer Biology

  • Molecular understanding of cancer follows from Watson and Crick's structure of DNA (1953); the first human genome sequence (early 2000s) and expanding sequencing technology accelerated discovery of genetic and epigenetic determinants of cancer.
  • Carcinogenesis is multistep and often takes many years; a normal cell must clear multiple hurdles.
  • At least eight attributes of the malignant cell: (1) genetic instability and mutation; (2) autonomous growth; (3) insensitivity to antiproliferative signals; (4) resistance to apoptosis and other induced cell suicide; (5) unlimited division potential; (6) angiogenesis; (7) local invasion — the feature that uniquely distinguishes malignant from benign; (8) immune evasion.
  • Cancer cells must also tolerate the stresses of their abnormal physiology and increase energy metabolism; tumour-associated inflammation can drive preneoplastic lesions to invasion; many acquire the lethal attribute of metastasis.

Basic Molecular Genetics

Transcription

  • DNA → messenger RNA (mRNA) → protein. RNA polymerase II synthesises pre-mRNA complementary to the template, containing exons (coding) and introns (non-coding).
  • Three nuclear modifications before translation: 5′ capping, 3′ polyadenylation, splicing (introns removed, exons joined).
  • Alternative splicing lets one gene encode several proteins. Androgen receptor (AR) splice variants lacking the ligand-binding domain are active without ligand → may drive prostate cancer refractory to androgen ablation.

Protein Synthesis

  • Translation occurs in the cytoplasm on ribosomes.
  • Frameshift vs substitution: a single-base insertion or deletion shifts the frame and usually gives a non-functional protein; a single-base substitution may or may not change the amino acid, and may or may not alter function.
  • Transfer RNA (tRNA): 75–85-base adapter carrying one amino acid and a three-nucleotide anticodon. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): ~80% of cellular RNA, part of the ribosome.
  • Stages: initiation (often rate-limiting; 40S subunit binds mRNA; start codon AUG → every protein begins with methionine) → elongation (fastest; several ribosomes may read one mRNA at once) → termination at one of three stop codons.

Chromosome and Gene Structure

  • Linear chromosomes are packaged by histones into chromatin; local chromatin structure governs expression and differs by cell type.
  • Euchromatin (actively expressed DNA) vs heterochromatin (inactive, possibly structural).

Tumour Suppressor Genes

  • Negatively regulate growth; roles in cell cycle, DNA repair, signalling. Loss → dysregulated growth and division despite stress and damage → further mutation.
  • Both alleles usually must be lost, by: (1) homozygous deletion; (2) loss of one allele + mutation of the other; (3) mutation of both; (4) loss of one allele + epigenetic silencing (promoter methylation) of the other. Classic examples: RB1, TP53.
  • Knudson's two-hit hypothesis (retinoblastoma): familial cases — often bilateral — inherit one non-functional allele, so a single somatic hit suffices.
  • Exceptions to two hits: dominant negative — the mutant protein disables the wild-type partner where proteins act as multimers (e.g. TP53); haploinsufficiency — one lost allele lowers protein enough to raise carcinogen susceptibility (e.g. CDKN1B / p27Kip1).

Oncogenes

  • Mutated, activated forms of normal proto-oncogenes that drive proliferation. Activation by: (1) activating mutation; (2) amplification (↑ copy number [CN] → ↑ transcript and protein; arises from replication errors, aneuploidy); (3) chromosomal rearrangement.
  • Examples: ERBB mutation → constitutively active epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). MYC (early-response transcription factor): amplified in prostate cancer, linked to immortalisation of prostate epithelium; amplified/overexpressed in bladder cancer (more than half of papillary and invasive tumours). MET (hepatocyte growth factor receptor): overexpressed in RCC, more in high grade; germline missense activation in hereditary papillary RCC. TMPRSS2–ERG fusion in a large proportion of prostate cancers.

Key Points: Tumour Suppressor Genes and Oncogenes

  • Somatic and germline testing identify alterations governing cancer risk and progression.
  • Tumour suppressors restrain growth; lost by four routes; some escape two-hit rule (dominant negative, haploinsufficiency).
  • Proto-oncogenes activated by mutation, amplification or rearrangement.

Cell Cycle Deregulation

  • Division is tightly balanced against cell loss; human cells integrate external growth-stimulatory and growth-inhibitory signals to choose division or quiescence. In cancer, activated oncogenes and lost suppressors tip the balance permanently towards proliferation.
  • G0 = reversible quiescence, the default. The cycle is ordered and unidirectional: G1 → S (DNA synthesis) → G2 → M (mitosis), about 24 hours; each step requires completion of the previous.
  • Checkpoints monitor DNA integrity → pause for repair → apoptosis if irreparable. Oncogenes and suppressors act largely by disabling checkpoints and apoptosis; loss of damage sensing → genetic instability, driving progression, resistance and metastasis.

Cyclin-Dependent Kinases and Cyclins

  • Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs): conserved serine/threonine kinases that phosphorylate phase-specific substrates.
  • Activity depends on cyclins (12 in mammals), whose levels rise and fall with the phase and are rate-limiting; after each phase the cyclin is polyubiquitinated and degraded by the proteasome, inactivating its CDK.
  • Further control: activating/inhibitory phosphorylation of the CDK and CDK inhibitors (CDKIs) — all commonly altered in cancer.

Mitosis

  • Sequence: chromosomes condense (condensins); sister chromatids held by cohesins → centrosomes move to opposite poles and assemble spindle microtubules, which attach at centromeres via kinetochores → nuclear envelope and lamins dissolve (cyclin B–CDK1) → chromosomes align at midline (metaphase) → motors pull complements to poles (anaphase) → nuclei re-form, chromosomes decondense → cytokinesis.
  • Mitotic abnormalities in cancer were noted by von Hansemann (late 1800s); Boveri (1914) proposed they cause tumours.

Mitotic Arrest: The Spindle Assembly Checkpoint

  • Failed attachment → non-disjunction: one daughter trisomic, the other monosomic = aneuploidy, a near-universal feature of solid tumours and a common route to gene amplification and deletion; expression tracks CN, so aneuploidy may drive initiation and progression.
  • Checkpoint-gene mutations (e.g. hBUBR1) are rare; abnormal expression of checkpoint proteins better explains instability — ↓ MAD1/MAD2 in RCC, ↓ MAD2 in testicular cancer.

Retinoblastoma (RB1) Tumour Suppressor

  • RB1 controls the late-G1 restriction point, beyond which the cell is irreversibly committed to divide; continuous proliferation in cancer is largely from loss of RB1 signalling.
  • Sequence: hypophosphorylated RB1 binds E2F and recruits histone deacetylases (HDACs) → S-phase genes silent → during G1, cyclin D–CDK4/6 and cyclin E–CDK2 phosphorylate RB1 at eight sites → hyperphosphorylated RB1 releases E2F → E2F (at least seven members) dimerises with DP1–3 → transcribes S-phase genes (DNA polymerase, S-phase cyclins) → after mitosis RB1 is dephosphorylated and represses E2F again.
  • Inputs converging on RB1 (Fig. 25.3): TGF-β via p15/p27; contact inhibition; DNA damage via ATM/ATR → TP53 → p21; oncogene stress via p19ARF; replicative senescence.
  • Ways cancers disable RB1: mutation or deletion; promoter hypermethylation; overexpression/amplification of cyclin D or E2F; loss (mutation, deletion, methylation) of CDKIs. The RB1 pathway (cyclins D/E, CDK4, E2F, p16, p27) is probably altered in almost all human cancers.

TP53 Tumour Suppressor

  • TP53 links checkpoints to genome integrity: DNA damage (via ATM/ATR → CHK1/CHK2) or oncogene activation (via ARF, which blocks MDM2) → phosphorylated, active TP53 → cell-cycle arrest, DNA repair, senescence or apoptosis; MDM2 is its negative regulator.
  • TP53 is the most commonly mutated gene in cancer and in a pan-cancer screen of metastatic disease; prominent in GU malignancies.

Concept — The cycle is a one-way ratchet driven by cyclin–CDK pulses and braked by CDKIs; RB1 guards entry (G1/S) and TP53 guards integrity (G1/S and G2/M). Cancer needs to break both, which is why these two pathways are lost in almost every tumour.

Key Points: Cell Cycle Deregulation

  • Phase-specific CDK phosphorylation orchestrates G1 → S → G2 → M.
  • G1 → S depends on RB1 phosphorylation; RB1 mutations common in urological cancer.
  • Main control points G1/S and G2/M use CDKIs in response to DNA damage, contact, cytokines, hypoxia.
  • TP53: arrest and repair, or apoptosis if damage irreparable.

DNA Damage and Repair

  • DNA polymerase plus error correction: about 3 incorrect bases per division in a genome of more than 3 billion.
  • Cancer-causing change still arises via mutation, CN alteration and epigenetic change (methylation, chromatin condensation, promoter access), from endogenous (mitochondrial respiratory by-products) and exogenous (chemicals, radiation) agents.
  • Prevention: free-radical scavengers (α-tocopherol, vitamin C, carotenoids, bilirubin, urate) and detoxifying enzymes (superoxide dismutases, glutathione peroxidases, glutathione transferases).

DNA Repair Mechanisms

  • About 10 000 oxidative lesions per cell per day, plus spontaneous depurination, depyrimidination and deamination.
  • DNA damage response (DDR) = replication proofreading + specific repair systems, tightly integrated with the cycle: first step is arrest; ATM and ATR activate TP53, CHK1 and others.
  • Loeb's mutator hypothesis: the spontaneous mutation rate is too low to explain the number of mutations in most cancers → early loss of replication-fidelity genes produces a hypermutable phenotype, which is harder to treat because therapy rapidly selects resistant clones.
  • Recessive cancer-predisposition syndromes caused by repair defects — xeroderma pigmentosum, Bloom, Werner, ataxia telangiectasia, hereditary breast and colon cancer — implicate hypermutability in causation.
  • Three systems repair damage to bases themselves: NER, BER, MMR.

Double-Strand Break Repair

  • DSBs are potentially the most disruptive lesion; unrepaired → apoptosis — the basis of radiotherapy and many chemotherapeutics, so cells that abrogate this response resist therapy. Misrepair → mutations, deletions and the structural rearrangements nearly ubiquitous in solid tumours.
  • DSB response sequence: break recognised → ATM autophosphorylates/activates → phosphorylates TP53, CHEK1, CHEK2 (arrest) → phosphorylates histone variant H2AX → γH2AX, which spreads up to 30 Mbp either side and recruits DDR factors into repair foci.

Key Points: DNA Damage and Repair

  • DDR = damage recognition and repair; damage rarely causes cancer because repair is redundant.
  • NER — ultraviolet/chemicals; BER — deamination, oxidation, alkylation; MMR — polymerase mispairs; DSB repair — ionising radiation, free radicals.
  • Inherited repair defects → marked cancer susceptibility.

  • Irreparable DNA damage → apoptosis → replacement from undamaged cells; hence defective apoptosis promotes cancer.
  • Apoptosis also underpins immune killing of tumour cells (surveillance and immunotherapy) and the response to chemotherapy and radiotherapy — defects confer resistance.

Regulators of Apoptosis

  • Balance of positive and negative signals; feed-forward loops make it irreversible once started. Signals converge on caspases (cysteine proteases with aspartate specificity; at least 13).
  • Initiator caspases 8, 9, 10 → executioner caspases 3, 6, 7; made as inactive procaspases, activated by cleavage in an amplifying cascade.
  • Executioner targets: anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 and Bcl-XL (cleavage releases pro-apoptotic fragments) → repair/replication proteins (DNA-PKcs, replication factor C) → nuclear structure (lamins, NuMA, SAF-A) → cytoskeleton (keratin, actin) → adhesion (β-catenin, focal adhesion kinase).

  • Apoptosis is regulated, energy-requiring and irreversible.
  • Intrinsic: Bcl-2 family governs cytochrome c release; extrinsic: death receptors activate caspases.
  • TP53 is central to apoptosis but mutated in over half of cancers.

Senescence, Telomeres, and Autophagy: Protection Against Cancer

  • Senescence and autophagy (linked to apoptosis) handle oxidative damage and stress of ageing and limit proliferation and immortalisation; once cancer exists they also mediate resistance to radiotherapy and cytotoxics.

Replicative Senescence

  • First seen in cultured cells after replicative exhaustion (Hayflick); linked to loss of DNA methylation and telomere shortening. DNA damage, oncogenic signalling and oxidative stress cause induced/accelerated senescence.
  • Senescent cells are viable, metabolically active but growth-arrested (G1 or G2/M) via p16Ink4a, p21Waf1/Cip1, p27Kip1; they persist, unlike cells dying by apoptosis, autophagy or mitotic catastrophe.
  • Senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP): cytokines, chemokines, matrix-remodelling enzymes — immunostimulatory but potentially pro-tumour.
  • Linked to ageing, prostate cancer and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). Morphology: flattened, enlarged, prominent nucleus, granular cytoplasm; stains for senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal).
  • Often bypassed in cancer. p53-dependent route (ATM, Chk2, ATR → p21, key effector; forced p21 induces senescence even without p53; p63, p73 also involved; ARF stabilises p53 by blocking MDM2) vs p53-independent routes; transformed cells lacking p53 and Rb retain some capacity to senesce.
  • Therapy-induced senescence (TIS): ADT induces senescence in a subset of prostate cancers, and SASP may explain ADT-associated inflammation; enzalutamide can induce senescence via ↓ replication-licensing factor CDC6, partly through GATA2. Senescent stromal fibroblasts may promote cancer; removing senescent cells may improve chemotherapy → senescence is a double-edged sword.

Telomeres and Telomerase

  • Telomeres: repetitive chromosome-end sequences protecting against degradation and recombination; lose ~100 bp per division (lagging-strand RNA priming; polymerases cannot copy the ends) → the "mitotic clock".
  • Telomere sequence to cancer (Fig. 25.7): proliferation, oxidative stress, DNA damage → moderate shortening → DDR → senescence or apoptosis (barrier 1) → if checkpoint inactive, further erosion → severe shortening → chromosomal instability (CIN) generating oncogenic mutations but also lethal damage (barrier 2) → telomerase activation → stabilised telomeres and immortalisation → cancer; without it, death from CIN.
  • Most cancers have abnormally short telomeres; telomere-specific fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) shows prostate cancer cells shorter than adjacent normal epithelium. Premalignant bladder and prostate lesions already short → early, causal role.
  • Short telomeres in primary prostate cancer predict recurrence independent of age, Gleason sum and nodal status; short, variable telomeres in stroma → higher prostate cancer death risk.
  • Telomerase: specialised reverse transcriptase adding telomeric repeats; restricted in somatic tissue to rapidly proliferating tissues (intestinal epithelium, endometrium, testis, activated lymphocytes). Forced expression → immortalisation. Supplies two functions: quelling CIN and unlimited replication. 85–90% of human tumours express high telomerase.
  • Alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT): telomerase-independent recombination, associated with ATRX or DAXX inactivation.
  • Therapy: imetelstat (telomerase inhibitor) in myelodysplastic syndrome; small molecules, peptides and hTERT-based immunotherapies (UV1, GX301, INO-9012) in early prostate, renal and bladder trials.

Autophagy

  • Conserved intracellular self-defence: organelles and cytoplasm sequestered in autophagic vesicles → fused with lysosomes → degraded. Discovered in yeast (Nobel Prize, Ohsumi).
  • Low basal level; upregulated by starvation, growth-factor withdrawal, aggregated proteins, damaged organelles, intracellular pathogens. Above the "autophagy threshold" → death by necrosis, mitotic catastrophe or apoptosis. Autophagy-dependent cell death is a defined form of regulated death (Nomenclature Committee on Cell Death).
  • Signalling: ROS, growth factors, metabolic signals → mTOR and AMPK → ULK1/ULK2 → phosphorylates Beclin-1 → autophagy-related (ATG) genes (ATG5, ATG12; about 30 known).
  • Context-dependent role: BECN1 loss raises cancer in mice and is frequent in human breast and prostate cancer (tumour suppressor); in early (e.g. KRAS-driven) tumours autophagy limits instability, inflammation and damage; but in hypoxic, nutrient-poor tumour cores it supports survival; in BRAF models it suppresses benign tumours yet its inhibition accelerates advanced cancers.
  • Therapy-induced autophagy is usually cytoprotective. KRAS-inhibited tumours become autophagy-dependent → MEK/ERK + autophagy inhibitors. Chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine (lysosomotropic, raise pH) may synergise with chemotherapy; after enzalutamide, clomipramine or chloroquine increased apoptosis in CRPC cells. No impact yet on urological cancer management.

Key Points: Senescence, Telomeres and Autophagy

  • Replicative senescence = growth arrest from methylation loss and/or telomere shortening.
  • Telomeres = mitotic clock; shortening is a barrier to unrestrained growth.
  • Autophagy clears aggregates, organelles, pathogens; extreme stress beyond the threshold kills.

Cancer Epigenetics

  • Disrupted epigenetic programming is a hallmark of cancer: DNA methylation, histone modification and non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) alter chromatin accessibility → silence repair and cell-cycle (suppressor) genes and permit growth-promoting genes.
  • Reversible → drug target: monotherapy success in haematological tumours; combinations with hormonal, chemo- or immunotherapy promising in solid tumours.

DNA Methylation

  • Methyl group on carbon 5 of cytosine → 5-methylcytosine, mainly at CpG dinucleotides; essential for embryonic development. CpG is depleted genome-wide because methylcytosine deaminates to thymine.
  • CpG islands lie upstream of ~40% of gene promoters. Hypermethylation → silencing (altered chromatin, blocked transcription-factor binding, excluded machinery); hypomethylation → expression.

  • Nucleosome: octamer of two each of H2A, H2B, H3, H4 wrapped by ~146 bp of DNA. Key Points: Epigenetics

  • 5-methylcytosine deaminates → C→T transitions.
  • Promoter hypermethylation silences suppressors; hypomethylation → oncogene expression, instability.
  • Histone modifications change chromatin conformation and expression.
  • siRNA: one target; miRNA: many. ncRNAs drive oncogenesis and resistance; detectable in liquid biopsy.

Angiogenesis, Hypoxia, and HIF-1

  • Angiogenesis is essential for proliferation and metastasis and a major target in renal and other cancers; it reflects a balance of pro- and anti-angiogenic regulators from stroma, leucocytes and tumour cells.

Hypoxia-Inducible Factor (HIF) and Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) Pathway

HIF regulation: normoxia vs hypoxia (Fig. 25.10)
NormoxiaHypoxia (or VHL loss)
HIF-α (HIF-1α or HIF-2α) prolyl-hydroxylated → bound by VHL (E3 ubiquitin ligase with elongins, CUL2, RBX1) → ubiquitinated → proteasomal degradationHIF-α–VHL binding falls → HIF-α accumulates, dimerises with HIF-1β (ARNT) → nucleus → binds hypoxia-response elements → VEGF, EphA1, angiopoietin and other genes that raise oxygen delivery
  • HIF-1 is the general oxygen sensor; it also drives vasculogenic mimicry (Claudin-4, vimentin, Twist1): tumour cells, not endothelium, line tortuous, hyperpermeable channels.
  • VEGF upregulation correlates with microvessel density, advanced stage, progression and poor prognosis in renal tumours.

Hypoxia and Metabolism

  • Tumour oxygen tension is low; via HIF-1, cells shift to ↑ glucose transport, glycolysis to pyruvate and lactate, ↓ mitochondrial metabolism and mass.
  • CA9, MCT4, NHE1 keep intracellular pH alkaline (proliferation) and extracellular pH acidic (invasion); HIF-1α represses angiogenesis inhibitors such as thrombospondin-2.
  • Warburg effect (Nobel Prize 1931): aerobic glycolysis — best understood in clear cell RCC, where VHL loss by deletion or methylation → constitutive HIF-1α and HIF-2α. VHL is inactivated in roughly 60% of RCC, by somatic mutation (~50%) or promoter methylation (~10%) (clear cell-specific figures: see below).
  • Hypoxia induces N- and E-cadherin, vimentin, MMP2, MMP9 → invasion and metastasis.
  • Immune effect: HIF-1α, VEGF, IL-6, IL-10, PD-L1 and TGF-β enhance MDSC suppression, recruit tumour-associated macrophages and block DC maturation → modulating this environment is central to modern renal cancer therapy.

Concept — VHL loss is permanent pseudohypoxia: the cell behaves as if starved of oxygen, and angiogenesis, Warburg metabolism, invasion and immunosuppression all follow from that single signal — which is why clear cell RCC responds to VEGF, HIF-2α and immune blockade.

Cell Plasticity and Pluripotency

  • Stem cells: self-renewal, generation of progeny, specialised niches, ability to form all cell types of an organ; share with cancer cells expanded replicative potential, telomere elongation, damage resistance, undifferentiated state — debate whether cancers co-opt stem properties or arise from stem cells.
  • Lineage plasticity: epigenetic and phenotypic change, usually after extreme pharmacological targeting of a driver; cells trans-differentiate to another lineage with new drivers and biomarkers.

Next-Generation Sequencing Techniques

  • Next-generation sequencing (NGS): rapid, cheap, high-throughput massively parallel sequencing of millions of fragments; profiles genomes, transcriptomes and epigenomes.

Next-Generation Sequencing Workflow

  • Sequence (Fig. 25.12): extract and fragment DNA/RNA → ligate adapters with barcodes → amplify into a library → immobilise on a solid surface → add fluorescently labelled nucleotides, record emissions → millions of short reads → align to reference or assemble de novo → bioinformatic analysis (variants, expression, epigenetic marks).

Bladder Cancer Somatic Alterations

  • Urothelial carcinoma: commonest urinary-tract malignancy — 90% bladder, 10% upper tract (mainly renal pelvis, ureter).
  • TCGA 2014 (MIBC): high somatic mutation rate; recurrent pathway hits in receptor tyrosine kinase/MAPK (FGFR, ERBB) and PI3K/AKT/mTOR; four expression clusters — cluster I papillary-like, FGFR3-enriched; I and II ERBB2-enriched; cluster III basal/squamous-like (like basal breast cancer).
  • TCGA 2017: commonest altered genes TP53 (most frequent), KMT2D, KDM6A, ARID1A, PIK3CA, KMT2C, RB1, EP300, FGFR3, STAG2, ATM; mutational load driven by APOBEC-signature mutagenesis; five subtypes (basal squamous, luminal papillary, luminal-infiltrated, luminal, neuronal).
  • Consensus classification (six subtypes): basal/squamous (35%, largest), luminal papillary (24%), luminal unstable (15%), stroma-rich (15%), luminal non-specified (8%), neuroendocrine-like (3%) — the basis for future trials.

Testicular Cancer Somatic Alterations

  • Commonest solid malignancy of young males; seminoma vs non-seminoma.
  • Low mutation rate, distinct methylation profiles, frequent uniparental disomy. Commonest changes: gain of 12p; KIT, KRAS, NRAS mutations (especially seminoma). TP53 mutations are rare, unlike other solid tumours.
  • miR-371a-3p: >90% sensitivity and specificity, outperforms classical serum markers for diagnosis and relapse monitoring; validation ongoing.

Key Points: RCC, Bladder, Testicular Cancer

  • VHL is the commonest clear cell RCC mutation → pseudohypoxia.
  • PBRM1, BAP1, SETD2 alter prognosis.
  • Papillary RCC: MET pathway.
  • Urothelial: FGFR, HER2, DDR targets.
  • Testis: few mutations; miRNA predicts relapse.

General Principles of Clinical NGS Assays

  • Three classes: DNA panels, RNA panels, cell-free DNA (cfDNA) panels.

Interpretation of Clinical DNA-Sequencing Results

  • Variant allele frequency (VAF) = proportion of reads carrying the altered allele; shaped by zygosity, CN (amplification), loss of heterozygosity (LOH) and tumour purity.
  • Worked example: heterozygous, CN-neutral alteration (50% of tumour DNA) × 60% purity → VAF 0.5 × 0.6 = 30%.

  • Variants of unknown significance (VUS) are common: a high-VAF VUS in a gene frequently altered in that cancer is more likely pathogenic (selection pressure). Molecular tumour boards help.

RNA-Sequencing Panels

  • Whole-transcriptome sequencing (comprehensive, costly — research discovery) → targeted panels (cheaper, deeper, more accurate).
  • Expression carries information DNA cannot; cell-cycle/proliferation signatures underpin most clinical prognostic biomarkers in localised prostate cancer. Older assays use arrays or quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) rather than RNA-seq.

Cell-Free and Circulating Tumour DNA

  • Metastatic biopsies are infrequent → cfDNA "liquid biopsy" (blood; increasingly urine for GU cancer), mostly targeted panels.
  • Only a small (sometimes tiny) fraction of cfDNA is circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) → very deep sequencing; more false positives and negatives than tissue, worse without germline.
  • Clonal haematopoiesis (CHIP): common age-related myeloid mutations; since most non-tumour cfDNA comes from leucocytes, they mimic somatic tumour variants at single-digit VAF.
  • ctDNA fraction is prognostic across cancers → minimal residual disease (MRD) assays: WES of primary → bespoke hypertargeted panel of clonal alterations → ultra-deep sequencing after definitive treatment → guide adjuvant therapy.
  • Emerging: cfDNA methylation (confounded by ctDNA fraction) and fragmentomics (fragment size and position; detects cancer even at low ctDNA fraction).

Circulating Tumour Cells

  • Haematogenous spread requires tumour cells in blood. Detection by enrichment (e.g. anti-EpCAM antibody, size) or capture-free imaging; enumeration is prognostic.
  • Molecular profiling: AR-V7 (splice variant lacking ligand-binding domain; constitutively active), neuroendocrine markers, PD-L1 on RCC circulating tumour cells (CTCs).

Clinical Application of Molecular Biomarkers in Genitourinary Cancers

Diagnostic vs prognostic vs predictive biomarkers
Diagnostic BiomarkersPrognostic BiomarkersPredictive Biomarkers
QuestionIs cancer present?How aggressive?Will this treatment help?
MetricsSensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPVRecurrence-/progression-free and overall survivalBiomarker–treatment interaction
Prostate examplesPSA (main; limited sensitivity, especially specificity); urine TMPRSS2–ERG, PCA3Preoperative PSA, T stage, Gleason → NCCN risk groups; molecular and artificial-intelligence (AI) testsBRCA1/2 → PARP inhibitor; PORTOS (postoperative radiotherapy dose escalation, validated in SAKK 08/09); AI pathology → short-term ADT; germline HSD3B1
  • Diagnosis of clinically significant (intermediate/high-risk) disease is what matters; better detection of low-risk cancer worsens overdiagnosis. With multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) and fusion biopsy, the role of urine tests is less clear.
  • Prognostic: treatment usually intensifies as prognosis worsens, or is de-intensified/omitted (active surveillance) — but this is not universal, hence predictive markers.
  • A biomarker can be both: NCCN risk groups predict ADT duration with radiotherapy — short-term ADT only for unfavourable intermediate risk, long-term only for high risk.
  • Relative vs absolute risk reduction: with a fixed relative risk reduction (RRR), absolute benefit scales with baseline risk. 30% metastasis risk × 50% RRR = 15% absolute risk reduction (ARR) — measurable; 3% × 50% = 1.5% — undetectable in most trials.
  • HSD3B1 (1245A>C) germline variant boosts dihydrotestosterone synthesis → ADT resistance, yet greater sensitivity to abiraterone.
  • Statistical proof of prediction needs a biomarker–treatment interaction test in a randomised trial including both biomarker groups — large samples, so many phase III trials enrol biomarker-positive patients only.

Fig. 25.14 Predictive and Prognostic Biomarkers

  • Interaction plots of 10-year recurrence-free survival against biomarker score: parallel treated/untreated lines = prognostic only (with or without overall benefit); intersecting lines = predictive — benefit in high-score, none in low-score patients.

Concept — A prognostic marker tells you the baseline risk; a predictive marker tells you the treatment effect. Many "predictive" uses of prognostic markers work only through absolute risk — the same relative benefit is invisible when baseline risk is low.

Key Points: Biomarkers

  • Liquid biopsies allow serial, non-invasive monitoring with important limits.
  • Interpret panel results by pathogenicity evidence, VAF and presence of germline sequencing.
  • Prognostic → risk; predictive → treatment benefit; some do both.
