Chapter 33 — Urologic Aspects of Pediatric Nephrology

  • Different training, different viewpoints: paediatric urologists and paediatric nephrologists reach urinary tract abnormalities by separate pathways; optimal acute and chronic care needs collaborative expertise of both.

Renal Function, Fluid, and Electrolyte Homeostasis

Renal Development

  • Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) — the spectrum produced by abnormal or dysregulated renal development — are the commonest cause of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) in children.
  • Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) = volume of filtrate crossing the glomerular filtration barrier per unit time; renal blood flow is one determinant, and both rise together through fetal and postnatal development.
  • Renal blood flow rises from 3–4% to 12–16% of cardiac output in the first year: ↑ cardiac output + marked ↓ renal vascular resistance (relative to the cardiac output rise).
  • Mechanisms of falling vascular resistance: intrarenal flow redistributes from deep juxtamedullary glomeruli → outer cortical glomeruli; rising angiotensin II ↑ mean arterial pressure and constricts the efferent arteriole → ↑ glomerular capillary pressure → ↑ GFR.
  • Renin, angiotensin II and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) rise in late gestation and are higher in the first 2 weeks of life than in adulthood.
  • Prenatal and neonatal GFR is much lower than adult GFR. Creatinine in the first 48 h reflects maternal function; by day 7 it reflects the neonate's own (normally <0.5 mg/dL at term).
  • GFR doubles over the first 2 weeks in term infants, then rises steadily to adult levels by 2 years; tubular maturation runs in parallel.
  • Total body water 70–90% of body weight in term and preterm neonates (higher than older children).
  • Dilute well, concentrate poorly: neonates dilute to 50 mOsm/L but concentrating ability is limited → negative free-water balance; normal free-water loss 1–2% body weight/day over the first 5 days (more in preterm).
  • Why concentration is limited: ↓ responsiveness to antidiuretic hormone (ADH); short tubules (esp. thick ascending limb, which lengthens with age); low Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase activity (rises after birth, peak at 2–3 weeks); low medullary interstitial urea, needed for the countercurrent multiplier.

Haematuria (Microscopic and Gross)

  • Gross haematuria = red or "tea-coloured" urine. Microscopic haematuria = clear yellow urine, dipstick positive, >5 red blood cells (RBCs) per high-power field on freshly spun urine.
  • Isolated microscopic haematuria ≈1% of school-age children; <25% remain positive on repeat testing and most of those resolve within 1 year. Usually benign, yet a definitive diagnosis is reached in <30%.
  • Gross haematuria ≈ slightly >0.1% of paediatric emergency visits; prompts urgent evaluation and a cause is found in most.
  • History: painful/painless, intermittent/persistent; trauma, pain, fever, dysuria, voiding dysfunction, rash, arthralgia, swelling/weight gain, exercise, headache, visual change. Family: haematuria, bleeding disorders, hearing loss, stones, sickle cell, renal disease. Examine: blood pressure (BP), rash, joints, oedema, flank pain, masses, perineum.
  • Critical step = fresh urine with microscopy. Red/bloody → non-glomerular; brown/"tea-coloured" → glomerular.
  • Gross haematuria, heme-positive dipstick, no RBCs → haemoglobinuria or myoglobinuria.
  • Sediment: crystals → urolithiasis; white cells/bacteria → infection; RBC casts → glomerulonephritis (then quantify proteinuria).
  • Suspected glomerular disease, proteinuria, hypertension, oedema or systemic disease → renal function, electrolytes, full blood count, albumin, complement C3/C4, antistreptolysin O (ASO) or anti-DNase B → refer to paediatric nephrology.
  • Urinary tract infection = commonest cause of gross haematuria in children → culture if fever, abdominal pain, dysuria or frequency.
  • Trauma, flank/abdominal pain or clots → urological evaluation, often computed tomography (CT). Family history of stones, pain, passage of gravel → stone imaging. Renal cysts (mostly polycystic kidney disease) can bleed. Rare: obstruction, tumours, coagulopathy, vascular malformation.
  • Microscopic haematuria: routine ultrasound is low-yield; if persistent for months, image only on clinical grounds. Voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) and cystoscopy are not indicated for isolated microscopic haematuria. Positive family/personal history, abnormal examination or other urinary findings → fuller evaluation.

Proteinuria

  • Urine normally contains protein; children may show low-level dipstick proteinuria physiologically (filtered plasma + secreted tubular proteins).
  • Urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) normal: <0.7 mg/mg newborn, <0.2 mg/mg older children. Timed collection: <4 mg/m²/h. Adolescents/adults: <150 mg/24 h.

Nephrotic Syndrome

  • Clinical: dependent oedema (eyelids, scrotum, labia); hypertension atypical; microscopic haematuria ≈20%, macroscopic unusual.
  • Definition: proteinuria >40 mg/m²/h or UPCR >2.0 mg/mg + hypoalbuminaemia + hyperlipidaemia. In children it usually occurs in isolation rather than as part of nephritis.
  • Three groups: congenital/infantile, primary (inherited or idiopathic), secondary (≈10%). Mutations in ~30 genes identified in inherited or sporadic primary disease.
  • CNS: onset within 3 months of birth; genetic or perinatal infection; proteinuria often begins in utero. Immunosuppression not recommended — unresponsive.
  • Primary nephrotic syndrome: no causative agent; no biopsy before steroids — classified clinically by steroid response.

Glomerulonephritis

  • Classic tetrad: haematuria + proteinuria + hypertension + acute kidney injury (AKI); "tea/cola" urine with dysmorphic RBCs and RBC casts.
  • Systemic diseases may present as GN → look for multisystem signs. Initial labs: AKI severity, metabolic derangement, and complement — hypocomplementaemia sharply narrows the differential.

Hypocomplementaemic Glomerulonephritis

  • Acute post-streptococcal GN = commonest PIGN: pharyngitis or pyoderma several weeks before; school-age, rare <3 years; sporadic or epidemic.
  • AKI usually mild; severe cases → rapidly progressive GN (RPGN) needing renal replacement therapy (RRT).
  • Diagnosis: C3 usually <50% of normal + raised ASO or anti-DNase B.
  • Recovery timeline (conservative care): improvement within 1 week → oedema resolves 5–10 days → hypertension 2–3 weeks → C3 normal by 8–12 weeks → proteinuria up to 6 months → microscopic haematuria >1 year.
  • Persistently low C3 or atypical course → biopsy.
  • MPGN and C3GN: rare, late childhood, rare <5 years; biopsy required.
  • SLE: suspect in any child with systemic disease + GN + low C3 and C4; ranges from asymptomatic haematuria/proteinuria to RPGN; almost all hypocomplementaemic, ANA-positive and anti-double-stranded DNA-positive.

Normo-complementaemic Glomerulonephritis

  • IgA nephropathy (IgAN) = commonest primary GN worldwide, children included; 2nd–3rd decades; biopsy is the only definitive diagnosis.
  • Recurrent painless macroscopic haematuria concurrent with or immediately after upper respiratory infection (contrast the several-week latency of PIGN); resolves in days, microscopic haematuria/proteinuria may persist; occasional loin pain.
  • IgAN progression risk: persistent proteinuria, hypertension, raised creatinine; 20–30% reach ESKD, another 20% progressive chronic kidney disease (CKD).
  • HSP: self-limited vasculitis; purpuric rash, arthralgia, abdominal pain, GN — may appear asynchronously over days–weeks. Nephritis mostly asymptomatic haematuria + mild proteinuria; gross haematuria up to 25%; nephritic/nephrotic presentation in a minority, RPGN rare.
  • HSP is a clinical diagnosis (no specific marker); biopsy only guides treatment in nephrotic syndrome or RPGN. Resolves in 1–2 weeks; recurrences common for 4 months; urinary abnormalities up to 3 months, and up to 2 years in 10–20%; most achieve full remission.
  • Alport syndrome: defect of type IV collagen α3/α4/α5 chains of the glomerular basement membrane; presents with persistent microscopic haematuria ± intermittent gross.
  • Alport hallmark = bilateral sensorineural deafness, high-frequency first, late childhood; family history of renal failure or deafness in a haematuric child is highly suggestive. Later: macular disease, anterior lenticonus; rarely leiomyomata (oesophagus, trachea, female genitalia).
  • Alport inheritance: mostly X-linked COL4A5; autosomal COL4A3/COL4A4 — heterozygous = dominant, biallelic = recessive. X-linked males: function preserved in 1st decade → proteinuria late childhood/adolescence → hypertension → renal insufficiency; ~50% ESKD by 25 years.
  • ANCA-associated GN: rare in childhood; aggressive; untreated 90% mortality within 2 years; intensive immunosuppression has transformed survival, but ~one-quarter reach ESKD. Eosinophilic GPA (Churg-Strauss) extremely rare.

Tubulopathies

  • Isolated (e.g. distal RTA) or generalised (Fanconi); primary or secondary.
  • History: family history of renal/metabolic disease; failure to thrive, poor feeding, recurrent vomiting, dehydration, irritability, tetany, seizures, jaundice.
  • Initial tests: full blood count; metabolic panel with Ca, Mg, PO₄; venous/arterial blood gas; quantitative urine K, Na, Ca, PO₄, Mg.
  • Renal tubular acidosis (RTA): inappropriately high urine pH in metabolic acidosis; hallmark = hyperchloraemic (normal anion gap) metabolic acidosis, urine pH >5.5, raised urine anion gap.

Cystic Renal Disease

  • Common cause of CKD and ESKD in children; found prenatally or during work-up of hypertension, haematuria, abnormal function, polyuria, or incidentally (abdominal pain, constipation). Imaging or family history often diagnostic; genetic testing preferred when possible.
  • Multicystic dysplastic kidney (MCDK): ultrasound = large reniform mass of non-communicating cysts; non-functional; usually unilateral — bilateral → near-universal neonatal death.
  • MCDK associations in up to 25%: renal hypoplasia/dysplasia, vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), ureteropelvic junction obstruction. Contralateral compensatory hypertrophy; MCDK involutes over months–years.
  • VCUG recommended, especially with hydronephrosis and/or hydroureter.
  • MCDK surveillance (renal function, BP, ultrasound): 3-monthly in year 1 → 6-monthly until involution, or at least to 5 years; emerging risk of early hypertension and CKD → growing consensus for annual review into adulthood.

Urolithiasis: Medical Management

  • Medical and surgical management of paediatric urolithiasis is covered in Chapter 54.

Hypertension

  • Definition: repeated BP >95th percentile for age, sex and height. Prevalence 3–4% — far below adults — but rising with childhood obesity.
  • American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP): ages 1–13 vs adolescents ≥13; normal = <90th percentile (children) or <120/80 mm Hg (adolescents).
  • White-coat hypertension: up to one-quarter with raised office BP are normal outside the office.
  • Staging sets urgency of work-up and predicts end-organ damage.

Acute Kidney Injury

  • Definition: abrupt fall in function — creatinine rise ≥0.3 mg/dL (within 48 h) or ≥50% from baseline, or oliguria <0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥6 h; a continuum linked to outcome.

KDIGO = Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes. Modified from Kellum and Lameire, KDIGO AKI Guideline Work Group, 2013.

  • Staging logic: creatinine by fold-rise (1.5 → 2 → 3×); urine output by duration (6 → 12 → 24 h). The paediatric-only stage III criterion is a clearance <35, because no absolute creatinine defines paediatric AKI.
  • Epidemiology: ~4/1000 paediatric admissions; up to one-third of intensive care admissions; dialysis-requiring AKI 1–2% of critically ill children; mortality 15% vs 0.6% without AKI.
  • Causes have shifted away from primary renal disease → multiorgan failure, ischaemia, nephrotoxins, congenital heart disease, bone marrow transplantation, sepsis.
  • Creatinine is insensitive and late: normal values vary with age, muscle mass and size; ~50% GFR loss before creatinine rises; lags GFR by 24–72 h.

  • Complications and causes: hyponatraemia (free-water retention); hypernatraemia (sodium given); hyperkalaemia (↓ filtration and secretion); anion-gap acidosis (↓ H⁺ excretion, ↓ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption/regeneration); hyperphosphataemia (↓ excretion); hypocalcaemia (↑ phosphate, impaired vitamin D).
  • Prerenal AKI → prompt, vigorous isotonic fluid; no potassium until urine output established and severity defined.
  • Oliguria → fluid restriction to insensible losses + output; if overloaded, do not replace output and give diuretics.
  • Avoid nephrotoxins; dose drugs to residual function; in early AKI assume GFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m² whatever the creatinine.

Key points
  • AKI → markedly increased mortality; prerenal → vigorous isotonic fluid; CRRT = standard of care.

Concept — creatinine is a lagging, relative marker in children: AKI is staged by change from baseline, early AKI is managed as if GFR were already <15, and a tubule that still conserves sodium (FeNa <1%) means the injury is still reversible with fluid.

Chronic Kidney Disease

  • Definition: structural or functional abnormality persisting >3 months; Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) stages by GFR because complications appear at set GFR levels regardless of cause.
  • Paediatric caveats: staging excludes <2 years (term infant GFR ≈40 mL/min/1.73 m², adult by ~2 years); the 3-month rule excludes infants <3 months.

  • Causes differ from adults and by age: young children → CAKUT (hypo/dysplasia; obstruction — posterior urethral valves, prune belly) and cystic disease (MCDK, cystic dysplasia, juvenile nephronophthisis, ARPKD); late childhood/adolescence → glomerular disease (nephrotic syndrome, FSGS, HUS, MPGN, MN, lupus) predominates.
  • Complications worsen with stage; KDOQI and KDIGO issue stage-based care guidelines.

Concept — paediatric CKD is a disease of growth: its causes are congenital and urological, its unique harms (short stature, epiphyseal bone disease, neurodevelopment) arise because the child is still growing — hence no protein restriction, and BP control as the one lever on progression.

End-Stage Kidney Disease: Dialysis and Renal Transplantation

  • ESKD = needing chronic dialysis or transplantation to live. No absolute creatinine or BUN mandates dialysis.
  • Start dialysis: CKD stage V (GFR <15) or CKD with uncontrolled hypertension, volume overload, metabolic derangement, poor growth, or uraemic complications.

FeaturePeritoneal dialysisHaemodialysis
Typical patientCommonest initial modality <9 years and <20 kgPredominant >10 years (USA)
ScheduleOvernight, 8–12 h → steadier metabolic and BP controlIn-centre 3–4 × weekly, 4 h; home haemodialysis nightly for select families (steadier control)
FormsManual continuous ambulatory; automated continuous cycling (most US children)In-centre or home

ContraindicationsOmphalocele, gastroschisis, diaphragmatic hernia, bladder exstrophy (commonly regarded)—
  • Goal is transplantation, not dialysis. <18 years = <5% of US kidney transplants; living donors mainly parents; pre-emptive transplant promoted with a living related donor.
  • Benefits unique to children: growth, neurocognitive development, QoL → children listed before 18 get deceased-donor priority.
  • Nearly all should be considered. Absolute contraindications: active malignancy, uncontrolled infection. Cognitive impairment/intellectual disability does not automatically exclude.
  • Size threshold: many centres wait for ≥10 kg and 65 cm (technical issues, graft thrombosis).
  • Urological prerequisite: repair or reconstruct urinary tract anomalies (CAKUT) before transplant; complete immunisation.
  • Native nephrectomy (peritransplant) for chronic/recurrent pyelonephritis, refractory hypertension, uncontrolled nephrotic syndrome.
  • Primary graft dysfunction <3%; delayed graft function and rejection by donor type — see table.
  • Immunosuppression: antibody induction (thymoglobulin or basiliximab) → maintenance CNI (tacrolimus or ciclosporin) + antiproliferative (MMF); trend to steroid-free maintenance in lower-risk children.
  • Infection: young children may lack immunity to cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus and BK virus. Malignancy (renal cell, thyroid, skin) risk ≈7-fold vs general paediatric population.
  • Antibody-mediated rejection increasingly recognised.
  • Chronic allograft nephropathy has no therapy → nearly all eventually need retransplantation or dialysis.

Concept — size and the bladder decide the pathway: small children get peritoneal dialysis because venous access fails, transplant waits for 10 kg/65 cm, and the urologist's job is a safe, reconstructed lower tract before the graft goes in; living donation wins on every graft metric.

Summary

  • Effective partnership between paediatric urology and nephrology — each with full-spectrum expert teams — has improved outcomes and reduced disease burden for children with kidney and urinary tract disorders.
