Chapter 35 — Urologic Evaluation of the Child

Triage of common paediatric urology consultations

  • Spectrum: simple problems (communicating hydrocele, nocturnal enuresis) to complex anomalies — posterior urethral valves (PUV), bladder exstrophy, differences of sexual differentiation (DSD).
  • Lifelong consequences: many decisions affect the patient for life, including fertility.
  • Guardians share decision making; instrumentation and surgical options are constrained by age and size.
  • Acuity set from child, parents and referrer → emergency department vs routine clinic (Table 35.1).

Emergent referrals

Acute scrotum

  • Definition: new-onset pain, swelling and/or tenderness of the intrascrotal contents.
  • Commonest diagnoses: testicular appendage torsion, spermatic cord torsion, epididymitis. Most time-sensitive = spermatic cord torsion (delay → testicular loss).

Testicular appendage torsion.

  • Commonest cause of acute scrotum; mostly prepubertal; supportive care.
  • Early, before diffuse inflammation → point tenderness over the torsed appendix.
  • Ultrasonography rarely shows the appendage itself — shows epididymal hyperaemia (mimics epididymitis).

Spermatic cord torsion.

  • Prevalence ≈ 1 in 4000 males; any age, majority after age 10.
  • Acute severe pain, frequently with nausea and vomiting.
  • Commonest signs: generalised testicular tenderness, abnormal testicular orientation, absent cremasteric reflex. A present cremasteric reflex does not exclude torsion.
  • Clinical diagnosis — if suspected, forgo imaging → immediate exploration; viability is inversely related to duration.
  • Manual detorsion may relieve symptoms without fully untwisting the cord → does not obviate urgent exploration.
  • At detorsion/orchiopexy → contralateral orchiopexy to prevent metachronous torsion.

Neonatal (extravaginal) torsion.

  • Can occur before birth → usually testicular nubbin / vanishing testis.
  • Signs: enlarged, firm testis without apparent pain or distress.
  • Acute perinatal diagnosis → elective vs urgent exploration is controversial; authors prefer urgent intervention.

Ambiguous genitalia in the newborn

  • Emergency on three counts: medical stabilisation, diagnosis, assignment of sex of rearing (DSD in detail: Chapters 59–60).
  • Stabilisation = recognise/prevent/treat salt-wasting congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) — commonest cause of newborn ambiguous genitalia; commonest type 21-hydroxylase deficiency (≈95%).
  • Three presentations: virilisation + salt wasting; simple virilisation without salt wasting (ambiguous genitalia in females; sexual precocity at 2–3 years in males); neither.
  • Classic virilised 46,XX: no palpable gonads, possible clitoromegaly, common urogenital sinus.
  • Salt wasting (either sex): failure to regain birth weight, weight loss, dehydration → death from hyperkalaemia, dehydration, shock.
  • Crisis usually begins day 4–15 → do not discharge early if suspected.
  • Essential tests: 17-hydroxyprogesterone + serum electrolytes. Newborn screen checks only 21-hydroxylase and may not return before discharge.
  • Treatment: lifelong glucocorticoid + mineralocorticoid. Other CAH variants (not screened) may lack salt wasting but still cause adrenal crisis.

Bladder/cloacal exstrophy

  • Rare (≈2.15 per 100,000 US live births); spectrum epispadias → bladder exstrophy → cloacal exstrophy (cloacal rarer still; multidisciplinary).
  • Bladder mucosa at birth: pink, healthy but delicate.
  • Protection sequence: tie cord with 2-0 silk (plastic clamp damages mucosa) → cover with nonadherent film (cling film/Tegaderm) → at each nappy change gentle saline irrigation + new wrap.
  • Immediate transfer to a paediatric centre with full diagnostic and specialist services.
  • Initial examination: bladder plate size/quality, pubic diastasis, urethral plate length/width, penile length, associated anomalies.

Obstructive uropathy with urinary tract infection and/or acute kidney injury

  • Commonest acute presentation of obstruction with infection = obstructing urolithiasis.
  • Active infection + obstruction → prompt drainage: ureteral stent or nephrostomy.
  • Children compensate for shock longer; hypotension may appear only when shock is decompensated.
  • Weigh morbidity and quality-of-life cost of a nephrostomy in a small child when choosing decompression.
  • Ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) / ureterovesical junction (UVJ) obstruction with infection, extrinsic compression or AKI → consider cutaneous pyelostomy or ureterostomy.
  • Large ureterocele → ureteral and bladder outlet obstruction → urethral catheter or prompt ureterocele decompression.
  • Bladder outlet obstruction (e.g. PUV) → may need cutaneous vesicostomy.

Renal vein thrombosis

  • Rare (≈2.6 per 100,000 live births); consider with palpable flank mass and/or gross haematuria in a newborn.
  • Male and left-sided predominance; two-thirds present within first 3 days.
  • Risk factors = hypercoagulability: prematurity, umbilical venous catheter, congenital heart disease, respiratory distress syndrome, maternal gestational diabetes, infection.
  • Classic triad: haematuria + flank mass + thrombocytopenia.
  • Diagnosis: Doppler ultrasound; no evidence-based treatment guidelines.
  • Most infants → complete, cortical or segmental infarction and/or hypertension → ↑ chronic kidney disease (CKD) and death regardless of treatment.
  • Treatment reported: supportive care → anticoagulation → thrombolysis; surgery rarely warranted.

Paraphimosis

  • Foreskin trapped behind the glans in an uncircumcised male — emergent.
  • Pitfall: swollen uncircumcised penis mistaken for circumcised → always ask circumcision status. Rarely occurs after circumcision if a cicatrix has formed.
  • Severe oedema within hours → harder reduction; unreduced → necrosis, gangrene.
  • Differential: hair tourniquet — circumcised or not; same emergent sequelae; inspect closely.
  • Reduction: manual compression of glans + distal traction on oedematous foreskin; analgesia by topical anaesthetic, penile block or ketamine procedural sedation.
  • Adjuncts to reduce oedema: ice pack 5 min; Coban wrap 15–30 min; granulated sugar 1–2 h; multiple needle punctures.
  • Manual failure → dorsal slit or circumcision under anaesthesia.

Priapism

  • Definition: full or partial erection ≥4 hours unrelated to sexual stimulation; urological emergency; paediatric prevalence unknown, uncommon.

Ischaemic priapism.

  • Commonest cause: sickle cell disease → other haemoglobinopathies, sickle cell trait, leukaemia, pelvic tumours, parenteral nutrition, Henoch–Schönlein purpura; severe constipation also reported.
  • Drugs: phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE-5) inhibitors, antipsychotics, antidepressants, stimulants, antihypertensives, anaesthesia, recreational drugs (cocaine).
  • Work-up: history, examination → consider urinalysis + urine and plasma toxicology.
  • Sickle cell precipitants: nocturnal erections, sexual activity, dehydration, fever, cold exposure.
  • Opioid analgesia sometimes needed (inhibits erection in animal models).
  • “First aid” while awaiting urology: physical exercise (running up stairs), urination, cold bath, ejaculation, ↑ fluids.
  • Cold packs — reduce pain, ischaemic damage, vasoconstrict — only if sickle cell disease not suspected; they can induce priapism in sickle cell disease.
  • In hospital: oxygen + intravenous fluids, especially in haemoglobinopathy.
  • Refractory: corporal blood gas confirms low-flow IP; colour Doppler is highly sensitive and an alternative.

Nonischaemic priapism. Children with perineal/penile trauma need a primary and secondary survey.

Trauma

Renal trauma.

  • Kidney = commonest injured genitourinary organ (detail: Chapter 62). Paediatric kidney more susceptible; congenital dilation (e.g. UPJ obstruction) commoner than in adults.
  • Blunt trauma by far commonest: motor vehicle collision, falls, sports, pedestrian vs vehicle, increasingly all-terrain vehicles.
  • Flank tenderness, ecchymosis, rib fractures as in adults — but hypotension, tachycardia and haematuria are less reliable → mechanism of injury becomes the key predictor.
  • 2020 American Urological Association (AUA) urotrauma guideline — image stable blunt trauma if gross or microscopic haematuria + systolic BP <90 mm Hg, or concerning mechanism/examination (rapid deceleration, significant flank blow, rib fracture, significant flank ecchymosis, penetrating flank/abdominal/lower chest injury).
  • CT preferred; renal ultrasonography acceptable.
  • American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) renal Organ Injury Scale (OIS) predicts intervention and complications in children as in adults → use identically.
  • Nonoperative management ideal; intervention usually minimally invasive (stent, nephrostomy/drain, angioembolisation).

Straddle injury.

  • Blow to perineum from striking an object with own body weight; summer; bicycle crossbars, ladders, pools, playgrounds.
  • Always consider sexual abuse (≈1 in 7 males, 1 in 3 females abused in childhood).
  • Investigate for sexual assault if: age <9 months; perianal, hymenal or vaginal injury; extensive/severe injury; concurrent non-urogenital injuries; history inconsistent with findings.

Blunt penile injury.

  • Rare; in the USA often toilet-seat crush — mean age ≈3.9 years, almost all at home; long-term effects unknown.
  • Heterogeneous; often expectant. Gross haematuria, meatal blood or inability to void → ultrasound, RUG or flexible cystoscopy to find surgical cases (penile fracture, urethral injury).

Acute urinary retention

  • ED incidence ≈8.5–30 per 100,000 visits. Bladder scanner or ultrasonography estimates volume.
  • Expected bladder capacity (mL) = (age + 2) × 30 — distinguishes retention from reduced urine output.
  • Causes: drugs; infection (UTI, balanoposthitis); post-instrumentation/surgery; neurogenic; trauma; functional (pelvic floor dysfunction, constipation/faecal impaction); benign or malignant mass.
  • Work-up: examination, renal ultrasonography, abdominal radiograph → no cause found → neurological evaluation ± spinal imaging.
  • Not all need a catheter: oral analgesia, warm sitz bath, rectal disimpaction/enema may induce voiding.

Urgent referrals to be evaluated within 24 hours

Newborn hydronephrosis (bilateral or solitary kidney)

  • Mostly prenatally detected; some present with failure to thrive, feeding intolerance, abdominal mass or febrile UTI.
  • Postnatal ultrasound before discharge if prenatal hydronephrosis (third-trimester anteroposterior renal pelvic diameter >7 mm or Urinary Tract Dilation (UTD) A2–3) in a solitary kidney, bilateral, or with suspected bladder outlet obstruction.
  • Timing: ideally after 48 h (earlier scans underestimate dilation) — but immediately if outlet obstruction suspected.
  • Serum creatinine reflects maternal creatinine for 48 h → follow for a downward trend.
  • Moderate–severe bilateral (UTD P2–P3): review prenatal images/history — oligo-/anhydramnios and gestation of onset, other anomalies; respiratory status, vitals, whether voided (voiding ≠ normal emptying).
  • Differential: outlet obstruction (PUV, urethral atresia, megalourethra, neurogenic bladder, obstructive ureterocele, hydrocolpos); bilateral UPJ obstruction; vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) or UVJ obstruction.
  • Examination: spinal, anorectal, genital anomalies, patent urachus; palpable kidney/bladder.
  • Male + bilateral hydronephrosis, thick bladder wall, hydroureter or dilated posterior urethra → voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) before discharge.
  • High suspicion of outlet obstruction → feeding tube/catheter per urethra pending imaging, especially if unstable.
  • Catheter impossible (urethral atresia) or supra-urethral obstruction → other decompression. High-grade obstruction bilaterally/solitary kidney → vesicostomy, ureterostomy, nephrostomy or vaginostomy; some suit early reconstruction (e.g. neonatal pyeloplasty for high-grade UPJ obstruction in a solitary kidney).
  • Identify obstruction threatening upper tract, infection or function; nephrology if parenchymal abnormality or renal insufficiency.
  • Most need no neonatal intervention but moderate–severe bilateral/solitary cases need close follow-up.
  • Educate every family: neonatal fever → properly collected urine; also urine output change, haematuria, vomiting, poor feeding, palpable kidney/bladder, poor weight gain.

Gross haematuria

  • Definition: visible blood with red blood cells (RBCs) confirmed on urinalysis/microscopy.
  • Discriminating symptoms: flank/abdominal pain, fever, dysuria, incomplete emptying, trauma, joint pain, oedema, recent illness (viral cystitis, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis); character — dark vs bright red, clots.
  • Baseline for all: urinalysis + microscopy, urine culture, renal–bladder ultrasound.

Renal tumour

  • Presentation: palpable, firm, non-tender abdominal mass that rarely crosses the midline.
  • Wilms tumour = commonest renal cancer <15 years; peak 1–5 years; ≈10% syndromic.
  • Differential: congenital mesoblastic nephroma (neonate), neuroblastoma, renal cell carcinoma, clear cell sarcoma, rhabdoid tumour, renal medullary carcinoma (sickle cell trait/disease), metastasis, leukaemia/lymphoma (management: Chapter 63).

Testicular tumour

  • ≈1% of paediatric solid tumours (0.5–2 per 100,000); bimodal — <3 years and postpubertal.
  • Usually painless mass; keep suspicion in every scrotal examination, especially testicular size asymmetry; palpate each testis (consistency, size, position) and paratesticular structures.
  • Mass found → look for metastasis: abdominal mass, leg oedema, supraclavicular nodes. Precocious puberty (pubic hair, penile enlargement) → Leydig cell tumour.

Semiurgent referrals

Primary amenorrhoea

  • Definition: no menses by 16 years with normal secondary sexual development, or by 14 years with none.
  • History/examination: secondary sexual characteristics, family history, cyclic abdominal pain.
  • Differential: constitutional delay; hypogonadotropic hypogonadism; chronic illness (e.g. hypothyroidism); hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (primary ovarian failure, e.g. gonadal dysgenesis); pregnancy; androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS); Müllerian agenesis; polycystic ovary syndrome.
  • Cyclic pain → genital outflow obstruction (transverse vaginal septum, imperforate hymen).
  • Initial evaluation: are Müllerian structures present? (examination, pelvic ultrasound, endoscopy) + FSH, LH ± karyotype.

Interlabial mass

  • Mass appearing to protrude from the introitus.
  • Neonate: periurethral/Skene gland, Bartholin or Gartner duct cysts; ureterocele; imperforate hymen; rhabdomyosarcoma. Older child: add urethral prolapse.
  • Most urgent step: exclude upstream obstruction — pelvic/renal ultrasound for hydrometrocolpos (imperforate hymen) or hydronephrosis (prolapsed ureterocele); ultrasound also stages rhabdomyosarcoma (Chapter 58).

Routine referrals

Asymptomatic hernia or hydrocele

  • Indirect inguinal hernia → inguinal bulge, inguinoscrotal swelling or hydrocele; commoner after prematurity.
  • Pathology: patent processus vaginalis → fluid (hydrocele) or abdominal contents (hernia) between visceral and parietal tunica vaginalis.
  • Swelling ↑ with crying, vomiting, constipation, cough. Fluctuating size → communicating; classically smaller in the morning after lying supine.
  • Normal clinic examination → provoke with Valsalva (sit-up, blowing bubbles, standing with abdominal flexion). A reliable parental history of intermittent swelling suffices for diagnosis.
  • Operate if: hernia on examination, or compelling history with normal examination (parental history, prior positive examination, photograph).
  • Communicating hydrocele and hernia → same operation: high ligation of processus vaginalis at the internal ring (Chapter 55).
  • Hernia precautions for any hernia, hydrocele or undescended testis: pain, firm/non-reducible bulge, erythema, inconsolable infant ± vomiting.
  • Resolved incarceration or bowel-containing hernia → timely surgery.

Undescended testicle

  • Detail: Chapter 57. Empty hemiscrotum at birth = undescended testis or in utero torsion.
  • Descended at birth, newly empty later → retractile vs ascending testis.
  • History: age first noticed, prematurity, inguinal bulge, prior groin surgery, fluctuating size; often patent processus → hernia risk.
  • Inspect for micropenis, hypospadias, chordee → abnormal findings raise DSD suspicion.
  • Systematic palpation: inguinal canal, mons, scrotum, perineum, upper thigh (ectopic testis).
  • Ultrasound not recommended — poor sensitivity and specificity for location.

Varicocele

  • ≈15% of adolescents; parental anxiety about fertility; mostly incidental; few with pain/heaviness; may cause testicular size asymmetry.
  • Examination: testicular volume, consistency, Tanner stage, varicocele grade, supine and standing; teach self-examination.
  • Objective volume by orchidometer or ultrasound; Lambert formula = length × width × height × 0.71.
  • Non-decompressing supine, especially right-sided → retroperitoneal ultrasound to exclude tumour compressing the gonadal vein.
  • Management of asymptomatic adolescent varicocele is controversial (Chapter 55).

Meatal stenosis

  • Almost exclusively circumcised males.
  • Symptoms: dorsally deflected, thin or forceful stream, spraying, split stream, dysuria, haematuria, hesitancy, frequency, distal penile pain; some asymptomatic.
  • Diagnosis on examination: meatus <5 Fr; some have a ventral web.
  • Uroflowmetry: plateaued or peaked curve; incomplete emptying, even retention. Long-standing → bladder wall thickening/trabeculation.
  • Symptomatic → meatotomy.

Labial adhesions

  • Commonest interlabial abnormality; peak 13–23 months; rare in newborns (maternal oestrogen).
  • Asymptomatic → observe; spontaneous resolution with age.
  • Symptoms: vaginal pooling, recurrent UTI, deflected stream, hesitancy, pain/itch, post-void dribbling on standing (vaginal voiding — evacuation of retained vaginal urine when rising).
  • Examine in frog-leg/butterfly position; to see introitus, pull labia majora straight out, not laterally → vulva funnels open without tension.
  • Treatment if symptomatic/desired: teach caregivers positioning → topical oestrogen, steroid or petroleum-based product; butterfly position after bathing to check recurrence without touching; manual lysis under topical anaesthetic or sedation.
  • Recurrence 7–55%.

Bladder bowel dysfunction

  • Bladder bowel dysfunction (BBD): spectrum of impaired storage/elimination of urine and stool.
  • Paediatric lower urinary tract symptoms have different aetiologies from adults: frequency, urgency, incontinence, enuresis, delayed toilet training, straining, recurrent non-febrile UTI, genital irritation/vaginitis, haematuria, encopresis, constipation.
  • Treatment: behavioural → medical → biofeedback → pelvic floor physiotherapy → psychological care; rarely surgery.
  • History from child and guardian (perspectives differ); validated questionnaires give an objective measure over time — Dysfunctional Voiding Scoring System, Dysfunctional Voiding and Incontinence Scoring System, Incontinence Symptom Index, Vancouver Scale, ICIQ-Pediatric LUTS, Childhood Bladder and Bowel Dysfunction Questionnaire, Iowa Voiding Questionnaire.
  • Usually after toilet training; associated with behavioural diagnoses.
  • Exclude occult spinal dysraphism: leg weakness/sensory change; gluteal cleft asymmetry, sacral dimple, hair tuft, haemangioma → spinal imaging or neurosurgery; gait abnormality/toe walking; sensory level if wheelchair-bound; scoliosis (Chapter 47).

Microscopic haematuria

  • Dipstick blood must be confirmed by microscopy, and UTI excluded, before diagnosis.
  • Definition: >5 RBCs/high-power field (hpf) on centrifuged urine on more than two occasions.
  • Symptoms guide referral: urology, nephrology or both; wide differential (online eTable 35.2).
  • Seek family renal history, immunological history, hypertension, crystals, proteinuria, casts.
  • Mostly benign → observe and retest; screening renal–bladder ultrasound common but its utility is debated.
Key points
  • Spermatic cord torsion is a true emergency and a clinical diagnosis; if strongly suspected, defer imaging and explore immediately.
  • Ambiguous genitalia in the newborn is an emergency — exclude life-threatening conditions, establish the diagnosis and sex of rearing; a multidisciplinary team is standard of care.
  • Gross haematuria differential is narrowed by character (tea-coloured vs bright red) and associated symptoms (dysuria, flank pain, recent illness).
  • BBD is categorised and treatment focused using voiding questionnaires.

Concept — triage asks one question: what is lost by waiting? Torsion, salt-wasting CAH, infected obstruction and paraphimosis cost an organ or a life within hours; children also hide shock until it decompensates, so clinical suspicion, not imaging or blood pressure, drives the emergent tier.

Evaluation of the paediatric patient

History

  • Engage the child, set expectations; dispel fears of surgery/examination so the child participates.
  • Privacy for older children: sexual/social history may be taken alone; ask preferences at the start; explain prior traumatic tests and what to expect.
  • Identify everyone present and their relationship (social worker, foster parent); others may leave.
  • Prenatal history (most relevant to hydronephrosis, not testicular complaints): natural conception vs in vitro fertilisation (IVF); intrauterine growth restriction, maternal diabetes; prenatal anomalies — cardiac, spinal dysraphism, ambiguous genitalia, discordant cell-free DNA/karyotype/ultrasound genital appearance, imperforate anus, omphalocele, abdominal cyst, Müllerian/cloacal anomalies.
  • Serial prenatal ultrasound — gestation at detection, change with growth, amniotic fluid volume → proxy for fetal renal function (amniotic fluid is largely fetal urine).
  • Surgical history may start prenatally (fetal dysraphism repair, vesicoamniotic shunt); some families meet urology antenatally.
  • Prenatal exposures: maternal drugs, environment, illness, teratogens, folic acid deficiency; amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling, genetic tests.
  • Birth history for all: gestational age; neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) stay (anomalies, feeding, respiratory support); procedures (circumcision).
  • Medications, family history and siblings' surgical history — some conditions are familial.
  • Social history: medical power of attorney, foster care, caseworker, care facility; daycare/school; household; who holds legal consent.
  • Consent from guardian, assent from child — age- and procedure-appropriate agreement matters.
  • All carers need education and supplies (catheterisation, postoperative care), sometimes across homes; sports seasons affect timing/restrictions.
  • Safety: bullying/abuse may surface during sexual or voiding history; understand gender identity as the child develops.
  • Developmental delay; in neurogenic bladder — mobility, hand dexterity, sensory level (self-catheterisation, independent transfer).
  • Comorbid psychological conditions — attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), anxiety, depression, autism, intellectual disability — matter in bladder/bowel and chronic complaints.

Abdominal examination

  • Distinguishes abdominal pain causes; detects distended bladder, constipation, stigmata of chronic disease (lax abdominal wall — prune belly syndrome).
  • Costovertebral angle tenderness → pyelonephritis or renal colic. In infants, renal mass or severe hydronephrosis often palpable.

Male genital examination

  • Explain first; tell young children only parents and doctors (parent present) may examine.
  • Adolescents: privacy, gowns. Prepubertal: parent at the head. Parent absent → documented chaperone.
  • Penis: foreskin retractability (uncircumcised); adhesions and skin bridges (circumcised); other anomalies.
  • Scrotum/inguinal canal: testicular position, consistency, size, symmetry; hydrocele, mass, bulge. Prepubertal → supine/frog-leg; adolescent → standing; Valsalva for hernia or varicocele.
  • Tanner staging (five stages): males by genitalia + pubic hair, females by breast + pubic hair → guides delayed/precocious puberty differentials.

Female genital examination

  • Frog-leg position; adolescents sometimes stirrups with draping.

Neurologic examination

  • Observe mobility and interaction. Neonates/infants: limb tone, head circumference. ≥12 months: play, gait, movement symmetry.
  • Spinal dysraphism (myelomeningocele, tethered cord) ↔ neurogenic bladder and bowel → inspect lumbosacral skin for occult dysraphism.
  • High- vs low-risk markers are poorly defined; predictive data lacking.
  • “Simple dimple” = within the gluteal cleft, <5 mm, base clearly seen → low correlation with pathology.
  • Neurosurgeon consensus (asymptomatic child): palpable lipoma → image (unanimous); simple dimple → observe without imaging unless symptomatic; image for coccygeal tag >5 mm, deep dimple, atypical dimple, deviated gluteal crease. Laboratory studies

Urine studies

Urinary tract infection.

  • Collection is hard in the non-toilet-trained child; dipstick, microscopy and culture all contribute.
  • American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) 2011 guideline (under revision): urinalysis with pyuria and/or bacteriuria + ≥50,000 colony-forming units/mL of a uropathogen from a catheterised or suprapubic aspirate specimen.
  • Contamination rises as invasiveness falls; AAP → catheter or suprapubic aspirate for both urinalysis and culture in febrile infants <24 months — bag specimens give very high false positives (methods: Chapter 37).
  • Collection route matters less when not testing for infection (haematuria, proteinuria).

Haematuria.

  • Microhaematuria on screening in 0.5–3% of children; gross haematuria rarer → more aggressive evaluation.
  • Isolated microhaematuria usually self-limiting and benign; three commonest causes: thin basement membrane disease, immunoglobulin A (IgA) nephropathy, hypercalciuria.
  • Dipstick blood → always confirm by microscopy — false positives from haemoglobinuria and myoglobinuria.
  • Microhaematuria → repeat over 2–3 weeks → persistent → imaging.
  • Gross or persistent microhaematuria → random urine calcium/creatinine. Hypercalciuria → high fluid intake + modest sodium restriction; it is a stone risk factor → renal ultrasound and urology referral.

Proteinuria.

  • With haematuria → suggests glomerular cause. Many RBCs can falsely raise dipstick protein (lysis releases haemoglobin).
  • Reflects disrupted glomerular filtration barrier: transient (fever, strenuous exercise), persistent benign (orthostatic), or persistent from renal disease.
  • First-morning samples because orthostatic proteinuria is common. Low-level dipstick protein in healthy children is usually transient/false positive; AAP does not recommend screening.
  • Confirm with random urine protein/creatinine. Persistent non-orthostatic proteinuria on two early-morning samples = pathological until proven otherwise → nephrology.
  • Paediatric differential skews to congenital renal dysplasia (reflux nephropathy) and obstructive uropathy, as well as glomerular disease.

Specific gravity. Reflects concentrating ability and hydration; physiological range 1.002–≈1.040.

Serum studies

Complete blood count.

  • Anaemia prevalent in CKD; haemoglobin falls once glomerular filtration rate (GFR) <43 mL/min/1.73 m².
  • Paediatric reference values matter for postoperative bleeding. Post-newborn clamp circumcision bleeding is rare but can need admission, transfusion, reoperation.
  • Trap: haemoglobin may be normal initially after acute bleeding, then fall precipitously. Neonates tolerate hypovolaemia worse than adults → watch pallor, shallow/rapid breathing, weak or absent peripheral pulses.

Serum creatinine and cystatin C.

  • Both predict GFR, alone or combined; best paediatric method still under study, especially with comorbidity.
  • Ideal GFR marker: filtered, not secreted or reabsorbed by tubules.

Urologic imaging

  • Overview of modalities in Chapter 36. ALARA (“as low as reasonably achievable”) is paramount; paediatric dose is hard to quantify (variable technique, size, age).

Key points
  • Paediatric history is directed at both child and caregiver; set expectations and goals up front.
  • Frog-leg or butterfly position favours genital examination in prepubertal males and females.
  • Simple sacral dimples generally need no magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or neurosurgical consultation.
  • AAP diagnosis of UTI requires urinalysis evidence of infection (pyuria and/or bacteriuria) with a positive culture in a symptomatic child.
  • Minimise radiation (ALARA) whenever imaging a child.

Concept — every paediatric norm is age-indexed: vital signs, bladder capacity, penile length, urine ratios and BMI all move with age, so an adult threshold applied to a child is the commonest error — and the child's physiological reserve masks deterioration until late.

Multidisciplinary care

  • Needs arise from the psychological impact of diagnosis and complex underlying pathology.
  • Goals: centralise care, improve efficiency and access, foster innovation for complex pathology.
  • Forms: multidisciplinary clinic (several providers in one visit) or multidisciplinary conference (plan relayed to family).
  • Established areas: prenatal diagnosis, spina bifida, nephrolithiasis, BBD, DSD, complex pelvic anomalies; radiology and pathology input adds value.

Special considerations for child comfort with evaluation

  • Children often cannot understand the reason for intervention and react to separation, pain, anxiety → adult awake procedures (catheterisation, flexible cystoscopy, priapism treatment) often not feasible awake.
  • Distraction and child life specialists: tablet ≈ midazolam for presurgical anxiety, emergence delirium, length of stay; virtual reality ↓ preoperative anxiety; extendable to catheterisation, VCUG, urodynamics, office procedures.
  • Preprocedural sedation (anxiolytic, sedative, analgesic) can avoid general anaesthesia for minor procedures.
