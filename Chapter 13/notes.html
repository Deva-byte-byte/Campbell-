Chapter 13 — Incisions, Exposure, and Drains

  • Open-surgery exposure has fallen with the expansion of laparoscopy and robotic-assisted laparoscopy, but open skill remains necessary. Open versus minimally invasive is decided by complexity and size of the pathology, patient preference and the surgeon’s skill set.

Abdominal Incisions

Midline Incision

  • Most versatile incision: reaches the extraperitoneal, peritoneal and retroperitoneal spaces. Either half can be carried past the umbilicus.
Midline incision — variants

  • Incise along the linea alba (the midline aponeurosis of the abdominal wall muscles), not through rectus abdominis. This avoids the vessels that run deep to and perforate the muscle, so it bleeds less (Fig. 13.1). It is harder in obesity, multiple prior laparotomies and hernia.
  • Entry: extraperitoneal → open posterior fascia as low as possible near the pubis → expand bluntly. Intraperitoneal → posterior fascia → peritoneum.
  • Closure in one layer, under more tension than incisions that follow Langer lines. Fascia running or interrupted figure-of-eight, strong absorbable suture (monofilament polydioxanone or braided polyglycolide). Bites 1 cm from the edge, 1 cm apart (Fig. 13.2).
  • No closure method is superior for dehiscence or incisional hernia, but avoid continuous closure with a rapidly absorbable suture: it raises hernia rates.

Pfannenstiel Incision

  • Adult and paediatric; extraperitoneal or intraperitoneal pelvic surgery, benign or malignant; donor nephrectomy and post-nephrectomy specimen extraction.
  • Lower morbidity and incisional hernia rates than midline or port-site extension. Good cosmesis and less pain, because the rectus is separated, not cut.

Sequence: transverse semilunar skin incision two fingerbreadths above the pubis → anterior rectus sheath opened transversely → rectus bluntly freed from the sheath above and below → linea alba attachment taken down superiorly and inferiorly → midline opened → extraperitoneal space or peritoneum.

  • Extensions: laterally for exposure; into a Gibson for the ureter; skewed toward the operative side for extraction.
  • Closure: peritoneum and muscle reapproximated; fascia running or figure-of-eight absorbable.

Gibson Incision

  • Traditional incision for renal transplantation and transplant nephrectomy. Muscle-splitting, extraperitoneal access to the iliac vessels, lower ureter and bladder. Also the hand-port or extraction site in minimally invasive nephrectomy.
  • Traditional oblique vs “hockey-stick” (J-shaped): oblique runs from a few centimetres medial to the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) toward the inguinal fold, ending just lateral to rectus. The J can be extended further superiorly, but retrospective series show higher hernia rates.

Sequence: external oblique split → internal oblique → transversus abdominis (incise if needed) → peritoneum mobilised medially → ureter found crossing the iliac artery. Open the peritoneum only for a hand port. Close in two layers, running absorbable.

Concept — The midline gives the widest, fastest field but is one layer under tension. The transverse and muscle-splitting incisions (Pfannenstiel, Gibson) give a smaller field for less pain and fewer hernias. Staying in avascular planes such as the linea alba is how bleeding and nerve injury are avoided.

Anterior Approaches to the Kidney and Retroperitoneum

  • Six anterior incisions (Fig. 13.6): midline transperitoneal, subcostal, chevron, modified thoracoabdominal, transverse, paramedian. The choice is driven by oncological and patient factors and surgeon preference.
  • Transverse upper abdominal: mainly paediatric — Wilms tumour resection, open pyeloplasty.
  • Paramedian: described for extraperitoneal nephroureterectomy and paediatric bladder augmentation. Lateral to rectus it risks the nerves within rectus and ventral hernia, so it is kept for avoiding the midline in a complex abdomen (e.g. colostomy).

  • What the table tests: anterior = early vascular control and bilateral access, at the cost of bowel complications; flank = extraperitoneal, at the cost of the pleura. Only transcostal adds flank bulge; only supracostal spares the rib.
  • Trap: thoracoabdominal appears in both groups — the modified (anterior) version is transperitoneal, while the flank version can be completely extraperitoneal.

Thoracoabdominal Incision

  • Indications are shrinking with incidental detection and nephron-sparing surgery. A large, morbid incision that gives the best exposure of the thorax, retroperitoneum and great vessels, and so early vascular control.
  • Indications: level III caval thrombus, T4 renal tumour, large upper-pole tumour, adrenal tumour, upper retroperitoneal mass.
  • Pain is no worse than a flank incision over the first month — counter to surgical intuition.

Sequence: exaggerated flank position, ipsilateral arm on a Mayo stand or trapeze → incision over the 9th–10th rib bed (supracostal or with rib resection), continued onto the abdomen and to the midline if needed → muscles divided → pleura opened, sparing lung → diaphragm opened to join the cavities → costochondral junction divided.

  • Modified version: thoracotomy through the 8th rib bed + subcostal + longitudinal abdominal incision.
  • Closure: chest tube (may be omitted if the parietal pleura is uninjured) → all layers with costal cartilage reapproximated → diaphragm in two layers, running absorbable → abdominal wall. Nonabsorbable mesh (Marlex) with anchoring figure-of-eight sutures has been described to reduce costochondral displacement and discomfort; the author does not use it.

Chevron and Subcostal Incisions

  • Subcostal transperitoneal: renal mass with caval thrombus. Supine subcostal vs flank subcostal: supine gives better hilar control for large anterior tumours.
  • Extensions (Fig. 13.7): midline to xiphoid → suprahepatic IVC; add sternotomy → thrombus reaching the heart (rarely used by urologists); across the midline → “three-quarters” → full chevron for a left-sided caval thrombus or locally invasive tumour.

Sequence: supine or modified flank → from midline two fingerbreadths below the costal margin to the tip of the 11th rib → anterior rectus sheath incised, rectus cut with cautery, lateral muscles divided in sequence → preferably intraperitoneal → incise the white line of Toldt to the hepatic or splenic flexure, colon mobilised medially → self-retaining retractor (Bookwalter), guarding liver and spleen → two-layer running absorbable closure.

Concept — Anterior approaches buy bilateral access and early control of the pedicle and IVC by entering the peritoneum. Each step up the scale (subcostal → chevron → thoracoabdominal → sternotomy) is chosen by how far the tumour or thrombus reaches.

Flank Incisions

  • Often cut muscle and/or remove rib. Careful technique limits pain and long-term complications, and they are not more painful than thoracoabdominal. In the minimally invasive era they are reserved for large or complex oncological resections and simple nephrectomy for xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis (XGP).
  • Three muscle/fascial layers: external oblique → internal oblique → transversus abdominis, usually divided with cautery.

True Flank Incisions

  • Flank position: lateral recumbent, lower leg flexed, upper leg extended, operative side made convex, incision over the table break.
  • Options by target and size (Fig. 13.8): 12th rib supracostal, 11th rib transcostal, subcostal (also thoracoabdominal, Foley muscle-splitting). Start anterior to the 12th rib laterally, going no further medially than the edge of rectus.

12th Rib Supracostal and 11th Rib Transcostal

  • More posterior access → less bowel risk; more thoracic risk (pleura). Resecting the rib tip → significant pain; flank bulge if as high as the 11th rib.

Sequence: skin over rib → external oblique and latissimus dorsi divided over the rib, beyond its tip → periosteal elevator strips muscle and periosteum anteriorly and along both edges → Doyen rib stripper posteriorly → rib cutter → edges blunted, bone wax → rib bed divided → lumbodorsal fascia → pleura identified with large anaesthetic breaths → retroperitoneum entered as posteriorly as possible, peritoneum pushed medially → neurovascular bundle between layers 2 and 3 freed → retractors → close in two layers, running absorbable.

Pleural injury: red rubber catheter through the tear → tear closed around it with running absorbable → diaphragmatic muscle closed → catheter end under water → sustained high-volume breaths → withdraw slowly once bubbling stops. Large tear → formal chest tube.

  • Post-operative chest radiograph only if a pleural tear was recognised and repaired (or there is clinical suspicion). Routine films after open nephrectomy have a very low pneumothorax yield.
  • Rib-sparing alternative: incise above the 11th rib or between 11th and 12th (infra-11th, Fig. 13.9) without excising rib. This avoids rib-resection pain and complications, still exposes kidney and hilum, and stays extraperitoneal.

Reopening a Prior Incision

  • Main danger is bowel injury on transperitoneal re-entry; there is little evidence to guide it.
  • Principle: extend the incision into unviolated tissue and enter the peritoneum there, where planes are anatomical and adhesions fewer. Then dissect organs stuck to the old scar under direct vision.
  • Involve a general surgeon when heavy adhesions are expected: prior cystectomy with urinary diversion, abdominal trauma, bowel surgery.

Concept — The flank trades the peritoneum for the pleura: each step higher on the rib cage improves upper-pole reach but adds pleural risk, pain and bulge. That is why rib-sparing supracostal or infra-11th variants are preferred where they give enough exposure.

Inguinal Incisions

Incisions for Specific Surgeries

Scrotal Incisions

  • Elastic skin, easy closure. Only contraindication: presumed testicular or intrascrotal malignancy → inguinal incision.
  • Unilateral: transverse or vertical on that side. Bilateral: vertical midline through the median raphe.
  • Indications: varicocelectomy, hydrocelectomy, spermatocelectomy, vasectomy, exploration for torsion, orchidopexy. Also reaches the penile base for an inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) or artificial urinary sphincter (AUS).
  • Rich blood supply → close dead space (or leave a temporary Penrose) to prevent haematoma. Dartos in one or two layers, absorbable; skin interrupted or horizontal mattress.

Penile Incisions

  • Peyronie surgery is covered elsewhere. Every urologist must manage circumcision, penile fracture and distal shunting for priapism (shunts: Chapter 70).

Adult circumcision — two-incision “sleeve” (commonest, Fig. 13.13): retract foreskin → mark first circumferential incision 5–10 mm from the glans (not too close) → reduce prepuce → mark second at the coronal sulcus (seen through the prepuce) → recheck both → cut with a No. 15 blade, guarding the frenular vessels ventrally (either first; author cuts the outer, preputial one first) → develop plane between prepuce and dartos/shaft → divide collar longitudinally and remove → meticulous haemostasis → 3-0 or 4-0 absorbable (chromic gut), simple interrupted. Continuous closure is possible but risks strangulation.

Perineal Incisions

  • Expose the proximal urethra and penile base: urethroplasty, perineal urethrostomy, male sling, IPP, AUS; historically radical perineal prostatectomy.
  • Urethral surgery: 8–10 cm vertical incision from the perineoscrotal junction. Inverted-Y (Lambda) vs midline: Lambda has higher 90-day wound complications.
  • Closure: layers according to dissection → Colles fascia closed, usually continuous, to obliterate dead space → skin in interrupted mattress sutures to stop the wound opening in this position.
  • Radical perineal prostatectomy: large inverted horseshoe, apex ~2 cm from the anal verge, ischial tuberosities as landmarks. Largely replaced by the retropubic route. Single-port robotic platforms may revive the perineal route for prostatectomy, cystectomy and PLND.

Use of Retractors

  • Exposure creates the workspace. Handheld (Richardson, Army-Navy) for entry and closure; self-retaining with padding to hold the space and protect organs.

Use of Surgical Drains

  • Purpose: prevent collections of urine, blood or pus.
  • Passive vs active: Penrose (passive) now mainly in the scrotum; closed suction (Jackson-Pratt, Blake) in most other surgery.
  • Placement: separate stab incision, avoiding the inferior epigastric vessels in the muscle and the bowel; secured with nonabsorbable suture.
  • Little evidence supports routine drains; routine use is questioned after robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy, partial nephrectomy and robotic cystectomy → surgeon’s discretion. Drain the renal bed when findings demand it, e.g. partial nephrectomy, simple nephrectomy for XGP (Fig. 13.9).

Concept — Specific-site incisions follow one rule: tissue that is elastic and richly vascular (scrotum, penis, perineum) closes easily but haematomas readily. Dead-space closure and meticulous haemostasis matter more than the incision itself.

Key Points

  • Open approaches still have indications; choice rests on pathology size and complexity, patient preference and surgeon skill.
  • Know the abdominal wall anatomy; staying in the midline avoids key vessels and nerves.
  • Anterior incisions reach pelvic, intraperitoneal, retroperitoneal and extraperitoneal structures; lower midline is the commonest urological incision for bladder and prostate.
  • The kidney is usually approached through an anterior or flank incision, each with trade-offs.
  • Inguinal incisions, above or below the ligament, reach the cord, testis and ilioinguinal nodes.
  • Adult circumcision: two-incision sleeve.
  • Scrotal and penile incisions need meticulous haemostasis.
  • Perineal incisions may return with new robotic technology.
