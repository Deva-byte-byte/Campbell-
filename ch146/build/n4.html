Malignant Adrenal Neoplasms

Adrenocortical Carcinoma

Overview and Epidemiology

  • Rare: 0.7–2 per million. Bimodal — children in the first decade, adults in the 4th–5th decades (most patients are adults); slight female predominance (1.5–2.1:1).
  • Mostly sporadic and unilateral; 2–6% bilateral, possibly hereditary.
  • Associated syndromes: Li–Fraumeni, Beckwith–Wiedemann, Lynch, Carney complex, MEN-1, NF-1, familial adenomatous polyposis, McCune–Albright.
  • Surgery is the cornerstone and the best chance of cure; advanced disease needs multimodal therapy.
  • Despite more incidental imaging, there has been no stage or size migration and no survival improvement over two decades; administrative data show only a weak relationship between size and metastasis or survival.

Clinical Characteristics

  • Most still present advanced and symptomatic: hormone excess 40–60%, abdominal pain/fullness ≈30%, remainder incidental.
  • Functional in 50–79% of adults and 90% of children; functional status may not cause symptoms (e.g., steroid precursors) and may change as the tumour grows; several hormones are often co-secreted.
  • Cortisol is commonest (50–80% of functional tumours) — ACTH-independent Cushing syndrome, often with more marked virilisation than functional adenomas; ACC causes ≈8% of Cushing syndrome; cortisol secretion independently predicts poor outcome.
  • Androgens second (40–60% of functional tumours): in women, virilisation (male-pattern baldness, hirsutism, oligomenorrhoea) related to 17-ketosteroids; virilisation without raised 17-ketosteroids → consider testosterone from adrenal or ovary.
  • Oestrogen → feminisation in men (testicular atrophy, gynaecomastia), partly peripheral conversion of androstenedione — highly suggestive of malignancy.
  • Cortisol plus androgen in up to half of functional ACCs. Aldosterone is rare — hypertension and hypokalaemia more often come from other steroids.
  • Non-functional ACC: fullness, back pain, nausea, vomiting, constitutional symptoms.

  • Size: average 10–12 cm; >90% exceed 5 cm; carcinoma in 4–5% of incidentalomas <4 cm, 10% >4 cm, 25% >6 cm → consider resection above 4–6 cm.
  • Ominous: lymphadenopathy, local extension into IVC or adjacent organs. Commonest metastatic sites: lung and liver → CT chest, abdomen, pelvis; bone and brain imaging only if symptomatic.
  • CT: irregular borders and enhancement, calcification, necrosis with cystic change; unenhanced mean ≈39 HU vs ≈8 HU in adenoma; classically no brisk washout (exceptions reported).
  • MRI: T1 isointense to liver/spleen, T2 intermediate–high, marked gadolinium uptake; essential for venous tumour thrombus.
  • 18F-FDG PET: ACC is avid (high sensitivity and specificity for malignancy) but cannot distinguish ACC from other malignancies or metastases.

Pathological Evaluation

  • Two questions: origin (steroidogenic factor 1 (SF1) staining = adrenocortical) and malignancy — obvious with metastasis or invasion, difficult when organ confined (Fig. 146.27: ACC — high-grade atypia with necrosis; adenoma — orderly trabeculae, minimal atypia; phaeochromocytoma — alveolar/trabecular, granular amphophilic cytoplasm; metastasis — pleomorphic hyperchromatic cells).
  • Weiss criteria (1984): nine features of structure, cytology and invasion; ≥3 criteria = malignant (sensitivity 100%, specificity 96%). Mitotic rate grades ACC (low vs high) and correlates best with outcome. Use caution in paediatric and oncocytic tumours — alternative criteria recommended.

Management

  • Most present advanced; localised disease still has high local and distant recurrence (60–80%) → multidisciplinary, multimodal care. Only one randomised trial exists in metastatic disease; the rest is retrospective.
Surgery
  • Complete excision is essential — best chance of cure, control of hormonal and local symptoms.
  • Open surgery preferred for tumours >6 cm or with local invasion (higher peritoneal seeding with minimally invasive approaches); smaller organ-confined tumours may be approached laparoscopically with caution (spillage risk; outcomes may favour open).
  • En bloc resection of involved organs for negative margins; regional lymphadenectomy for control and staging; IVC thrombus may need bypass, replacement or interruption.
  • Metastatic disease: cytoreduction if >90% of tumour burden can be removed — may palliate and facilitate other therapy, survival benefit uncertain. If complete debulking is impossible, do not operate — it delays systemic therapy.
  • Resect recurrences (improved survival in retrospective series), best in low-grade tumours with long disease-free intervals; radiofrequency ablation or angioembolisation if unresectable.
  • Surveillance: CT chest, abdomen, pelvis every 3 months for 2–3 years → every 6 months to 5 years; monitor initially raised hormones; PET for indeterminate findings; beyond 5 years individualised (<3% recur after 5 years).
Radiation
  • Treatment of choice for bone and brain metastases; limited role for the primary.
  • Adjuvant radiotherapy lowers 5-year local recurrence (≈86% without vs ≈11% with) but not disease-free or overall survival; best for patients without metastasis with positive or indeterminate margins.
Medical therapy
  • Mitotane — oral dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT) derivative, the most used agent: adrenolytic (direct cortical cytotoxicity, free-radical damage, steroidogenic enzyme inhibition) → steroid replacement needed. Toxicity: gastrointestinal upset, lethargy, depression, feminisation in men, rash, raised liver enzymes and binding globulins, hypothyroidism, dyslipidaemia, thrombocytopenia → titrate to tolerance and serum levels.
  • Single-agent response 14–36%; streptozotocin combination ≈36%; etoposide–doxorubicin–cisplatin combination ≈49%.
  • Symptom control while mitotane takes effect: ketoconazole, metyrapone, aminoglutethimide, etomidate (adrenostatic) and mifepristone (glucocorticoid receptor antagonist).

Prognosis

  • Overall 5-year survival 20–47% — advanced presentation, frequent recurrence, weak systemic therapy.
  • Adverse factors: stage, size >12 cm, age, positive margins, high mitotic rate, atypical mitoses, cortisol production, necrosis, high Ki-67. The effect of functional status overall is debated.

Key points: Adrenocortical carcinoma
  • Increasing size raises malignancy risk.
  • Most ACCs are functional at presentation.
  • Multimodal therapy — surgery, radiotherapy, systemic therapy — is often needed.

Concept — ACC is a disease of recurrence rather than of the primary: even complete resection is followed by relapse in most patients, so outcome depends on an intact, unspilled first operation. That is why open en bloc surgery, no preoperative biopsy and hormonal markers for surveillance dominate its management.

Neuroblastoma

  • Neural-crest malignancy (adrenal medulla, sympathetic ganglia); commonest extracranial solid tumour of childhood — covered in Chapter 63.

Adrenal Sarcoma

  • Extremely rare primary sarcomas: epithelioid angiosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, liposarcoma, Kaposi sarcoma, alveolar and synovial sarcoma.
  • Cannot be distinguished preoperatively from other adrenal malignancies (benign adenoma is excluded by the usual work-up); definitive diagnosis can be hard even after resection.
  • Wide local excision with negative margins; mixed carcinoma–sarcoma features → treat as sarcomatoid carcinoma.

Metastases

Overview and Epidemiology

  • Common site: at autopsy, >25% of patients with melanoma or lung carcinoma have adrenal metastases; many other primaries (RCC, breast, medullary thyroid, contralateral ACC, gastrointestinal, prostate, cervix, pancreas, urothelial, seminoma, others) also spread there.
  • With a history of malignancy, >50% of new adrenal lesions are metastases.
  • RCC reaches the adrenal directly or haematogenously, yet ipsilateral adrenalectomy at radical or partial nephrectomy only if the gland is radiologically abnormal or thrombus reaches the adrenal vein.
  • Resection of an isolated metastasis may improve survival in melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC); a randomised trial now supports metastasectomy in lung cancer.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Characteristics

  • Adrenal insufficiency from metastases is exceedingly rare (though Addison described it); bulky (>4 cm) bilateral disease seems necessary. The oft-quoted “20% of metastatic RCC have adrenal insufficiency” rests on a single small abstract. Advanced cancer often mimics addisonian symptoms without biochemical insufficiency.

Diagnostic Tests

  • Metastases look like adenomas (well circumscribed, homogeneous, rarely necrotic) but lack lipid: >10 HU and poor washout (Fig. 146.28: 38-HU left adrenal RCC metastasis years after right nephrectomy).
  • Exceptions: up to 7% of metastases are <10 HU; RCC and hepatocellular carcinoma metastases can wash out like adenomas → growth kinetics become decisive; MRI signal intensity may help separate RCC metastasis from adenoma. Biopsy helps with a known primary.
  • Unlike renal metastases (usually with widespread disease), adrenal metastases may be early and solitary — making resection feasible.
  • Metabolic work-up still required — functional lesion rates are similar with or without prior malignancy, and pigmented phaeochromocytoma has been mislabelled as metastatic melanoma.

Treatment and Prognosis

  • RCC: resection of ipsilateral or contralateral adrenal metastasis as part of cytoreduction in selected patients; benefit unproven prospectively; role may change with targeted and immunotherapy.
  • Non-urological primaries: multidisciplinary decisions with the medical oncologist; best data for NSCLC and melanoma.
  • NSCLC: adrenal metastases usually accompany systemic disease; isolated deposits may reflect lymphatic spread. Resection of a solitary metastasis → 5-year survival >25%; synchronous and metachronous metastases fare equally (meta-analysis); level 1 evidence supports metastasectomy.
  • Melanoma: long-term survival and rare regression after adrenalectomy — retrospective, highly selected data.
  • Summary: suspect metastasis in any incidentaloma with a cancer history; percutaneous biopsy is safe (success depends on size, location, technique); adrenalectomy for solitary metastasis suits selected patients.
Key points: Adrenal metastases
  • Common, but bilateral bulky (>4 cm) disease is needed for biochemical adrenal insufficiency.
  • >50% of new adrenal lesions in patients with prior malignancy are metastases — metabolic work-up still recommended.

Concept — Prior malignancy flips the prior probability — most new adrenal lesions become metastases — but does not remove the need to exclude phaeochromocytoma. Because adrenal deposits can be early and solitary, this is the one setting in which biopsy and resection of a metastasis are both justified.

Non-neoplastic Adrenal Disorders

Cushing Syndrome/Cortisol Excess

Pathophysiology

  • Cushing syndrome = symptomatic hypercortisolaemia. Three groups: exogenous (iatrogenic — commonest), ACTH-dependent (extra-adrenal; up to 85% of endogenous cases), ACTH-independent (adrenal; relatively rare).

Clinical Characteristics

  • Classic features — central obesity, moon facies, buffalo hump, proximal weakness, easy bruising, striae — are individually non-specific; the combination should prompt screening. Systemic features overlap the metabolic syndrome (Fig. 146.30: depression/psychosis, glaucoma, immunosuppression, peptic ulcer, salt and water retention, hypertension, visceral obesity, insulin resistance, protein catabolism, skin thinning, muscle atrophy, osteoporosis, urolithiasis, hypogonadotropic hypogonadism).
  • Urological relevance: hypogonadotropic hypogonadism in men — low threshold to test men with low libido or erectile dysfunction, low testosterone and low gonadotropins; urolithiasis in up to 50% — test stone formers with cushingoid features. Treatment reduces but does not normalise stone risk.

Diagnosis

  • Establish cortisol excess, then its cause — see Testing for Hypercortisolism.

Exogenous Cushing Syndrome

  • Withdraw glucocorticoids gradually to let the HPA axis recover (weeks to months). Beware steroid withdrawal syndrome — intolerance of dose reduction despite normal HPA testing.

Adrenocorticotropic Hormone–Dependent Disease

  • 80–85% of endogenous Cushing syndrome; ≈80% of it pituitary (Cushing disease), the rest ectopic ACTH, and very rarely ectopic CRH.
Cushing disease
  • Harvey Cushing (1932); ≈70% of endogenous Cushing syndrome; usually a microadenoma (macroadenomas ≥1 cm ≈5%); 15% of functional pituitary adenomas secrete ACTH; up to two-thirds of patients are women.
  • Standard: trans-sphenoidal resection — cure only 60–80%, relapse up to 25%; macroadenomas <15% cured. Postoperative severe addisonian state is common (careful replacement for a year); hypopituitarism ranges widely and rises with reoperation; salvage radiotherapy is slow and risks hypopituitarism.
  • Bilateral adrenalectomy (the urologist’s role): usually after at least one failed pituitary treatment, or when hypercortisolism is life threatening. Advantages: very high, rapid success with no hypopituitarism. Costs: lifelong glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement, rare residual functioning adrenal tissue, and Nelson (Nelson–Salassa) syndrome.
  • Nelson syndrome: progressive growth of the pituitary adenoma after bilateral adrenalectomy — chiasmal compression, oculomotor deficits, rarely raised intracranial pressure; 8–29%; prophylactic radiotherapy inconsistent and controversial.
Ectopic ACTH syndrome
  • Nearly always malignant tumours; ≈10% of Cushing syndrome; first described by Liddle and colleagues in the early 1960s. Hypercortisolism may precede the tumour diagnosis by years; underdiagnosed in end-stage cancer.
  • Higher ACTH → larger, more hyperplastic adrenals than in Cushing disease (Fig. 146.32: bilateral enlargement with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma).
  • Best treatment: resect the primary — possible in only ≈10%. Unresectable or occult primary (up to 35%) → bilateral adrenalectomy with lifelong replacement is excellent.

Overview and Epidemiology

Clinical Characteristics

  • Chronic: non-specific fatigue and anorexia for months.

Diagnostic Tests

  • Clinical suspicion → morning cortisol and ACTH; primary disease shows abnormal aldosterone and renin; confirm with the corticotropin (ACTH stimulation) test; most autoimmune cases have anti-21-hydroxylase antibodies.
  • Secondary insufficiency needs specialist HPA-axis testing.

Treatment

  • Hydrocortisone or cortisone acetate, with half to two-thirds of the daily dose in the morning and the rest in one or two later doses (circadian mimicry). Fludrocortisone only in primary disease. Androgen supplementation for persistent constitutional symptoms (some experts).
  • Perioperative stress dosing is debated — usual dose is often enough, but coverage is given because the downside is negligible and crisis catastrophic. Mineralocorticoid is unnecessary for patients on chronic steroids (aldosterone intact).

Prognosis

  • Effect of primary insufficiency on life expectancy unknown, but quality of life is impaired (low energy, libido, psychological effects); secondary (hypopituitary) insufficiency causes premature death.

Summary

  • In urology: surgical or pharmacological loss of adrenal tissue; high index of suspicion; previous ipsilateral nephrectomy before contralateral surgery is the classic at-risk setting; consider in patients on chronic steroids and the critically ill; low threshold for endocrinology input.
Key points: Adrenal insufficiency
  • Mineralocorticoid deficiency only in primary disease; pituitary or hypothalamic failure (including chronic steroid suppression) spares aldosterone.
  • Prior partial or radical nephrectomy + contralateral renal or adrenal surgery = risk of postoperative adrenal crisis — review old reports and imaging for the remaining adrenal.

Concept — Whether insufficiency is primary or secondary decides whether aldosterone is lost, and that one fact separates the two clinically (hyperkalaemia, salt craving, pigmentation) and therapeutically (fludrocortisone). For the urologist, the danger is iatrogenic: the last functioning adrenal tissue is what an operation removes.

Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia

  • Autosomal recessive steroidogenic enzyme defect with low cortisol; 21-hydroxylase deficiency in >95%. Loss of negative feedback → high ACTH → cortical hyperplasia and androgen excess. Usually diagnosed in childhood (Chapter 59).
  • Patients and carriers are prone to benign cortical adenomas, not carcinoma; undiagnosed non-classic 21-hydroxylase deficiency can present with adrenal neoplasms later in life.
  • No CAH screening for incidentaloma patients — genetic screening has not shown excess undiagnosed CAH.

Conclusions

  • The adrenals are essential to life-sustaining homeostasis yet, despite frequent incidental lesions, are infrequent primary initiators of disease.
  • Significant abnormalities need a systematic multidisciplinary approach, in which the urologist — through minimally invasive and retroperitoneal expertise and the renal–adrenal link — naturally contributes evaluation and primary surgical management.
