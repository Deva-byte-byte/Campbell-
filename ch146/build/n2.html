Evaluation of Adrenal Lesions in Urological Practice

  • Urologists meet adrenal pathology as a referred new mass, an incidental mass in an existing patient, or occasionally a clinically suspected syndrome before imaging.
  • Two questions drive management: (1) could the lesion be malignant? (2) is it hormonally active — secreting outside normal regulatory control? Either → treatment, usually resection (Box 146.1).
  • Malignant potential is judged by imaging; hormonal activity by laboratory testing; biopsy helps only in rare scenarios.
  • The pregnant patient with an adrenal mass needs a tailored multidisciplinary plan (outside the chapter’s scope).

Imaging of Adrenal Masses

  • Imaging is the primary tool for malignant potential; several guidelines address it.
  • No imaging feature reliably separates functional from non-functional adenomas — endocrine testing can never be omitted on imaging grounds.

Intracellular Fat Content

  • Benign adenomas are lipid-rich and can often be diagnosed on this feature alone.
Unenhanced computed tomography
  • The first and perhaps single best, most easily interpreted test for intracellular lipid; diagnoses adenoma in >70% of cases.
  • <10 Hounsfield units (HU) = high intracytoplasmic lipid = adenoma in patients without another known malignancy.
  • ≤0 HU is 100% specific but insensitive (a minority of adenomas reach it) — hence the working 10-HU threshold: highly specific, sensitivity reported from about 70% to 100% in recent series; confirmed in surgical series.
  • Limitations: up to 7% of adrenal metastases measure <10 HU (European Society of Endocrinology–European Network for the Study of Adrenal Tumors, ESE–ENSAT), and rare low-density phaeochromocytomas exist — metabolic work-up is mandatory for all lesions.
  • Measure density yourself on digital images if the report omits it — avoids unnecessary further imaging.
Magnetic resonance imaging
  • Opposed-phase chemical-shift imaging: fat–water signal interference; signal loss on out-of-phase versus in-phase sequences = intracellular lipid = adenoma. Usually assessed qualitatively (quantitative methods use the spleen as reference).
  • Test characteristics broadly equivalent to unenhanced CT; possible advantage for lipid-poor adenomas is disputed. CT washout is more sensitive than chemical-shift MRI.

Contrast Enhancement

  • Post-contrast attenuation overlaps heavily between adenomas and non-adenomas — enhanced phases are rarely useful alone, except for lesions that do not enhance at all (haemorrhage, cyst fluid).
  • Histogram analysis of a region of interest on enhanced CT: if >10% of pixels are <0 HU, the lesion is probably an adenoma. Combination with fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET) CT is promising.

Washout Studies

  • Lipid-poor adenomas wash out contrast as rapidly as lipid-rich ones — the basis of the washout study, now routine for lipid-poor lesions; highly sensitive and specific.

  • Pitfalls: RCC and hepatocellular carcinoma metastases can wash out like lipid-poor adenomas. Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) data are limited (usually large at presentation); ACC classically fails to wash out, with reported exceptions.

  • Gadolinium MRI washout is not used clinically — gadolinium signal is less dose dependent than iodinated CT enhancement.

Size

  • Larger masses are more likely malignant (Fig. 146.9: benign lesions predominate below 4 cm; malignancy rises steeply above 6 cm).

  • 4 cm is the consensus threshold to consider resection in fit patients: a 4-cm cut-off gives high sensitivity but poor specificity for malignancy; 6 cm trades sensitivity for specificity.
  • Radiological size underestimates pathological size.
  • Benign imaging overrides size: in the largest series, no incidentaloma <10 HU was malignant regardless of size; in surgical series, all homogeneous adenoma-like lesions were benign whatever their size.
  • Age modifies the threshold: adenoma incidence rises with age, so small lesions in the young demand more caution, while lesions >4 cm in older comorbid patients may be better observed. Borders, lipid content and enhancement are weighed with size; there is no level 1 evidence.

Growth

  • Follow growth kinetics of unresected lesions — but most resected for growth prove benign. In a prospective cohort, a small minority grew ≥5 mm or ≥1 cm, and all resected for growth were benign.
  • In a literature review, about 9% grew >1 cm over 3 years; the rate of malignant transformation ≈1 in 1000.
  • ACC grows on average ≈1.9 cm per year.

Key points: Size and growth
  • Larger tumours are more likely malignant, yet imaging features remain informative in large masses; in healthy, especially young, patients, masses ≥4 cm should be resected or watched very closely.
  • Follow growth kinetics in lipid-poor masses or those >4 cm that are not resected.

Other Imaging Characteristics

  • Large heterogeneous masses invading surrounding structures suggest carcinoma; irregular margins and an enhancing rim are quite specific for malignancy. Cysts and acute/subacute haemorrhage have characteristic appearances.
Macroscopic fat
  • Macroscopic fat almost always means myelolipoma and is treated by most guidelines as definitively benign (reported 100% sensitive for myelolipoma).
  • No agreed definition: areas <−30 HU proposed as fat density; series have used −130 to −23 HU. Also easily seen on MRI.
  • Fat proportion discriminates: isolated myelolipomas are usually >50% fat; lesions with <10% fat were mixed myelolipoma with other pathology (hyperplasia, functioning or non-functioning adenoma). Rare fat-containing ACCs were symptomatic with <5% fat; “myelolipomatous degeneration” of adenomas had <20% fat. Teratoma, metastasis, lipoma and angiomyolipoma may contain fat (the latter two need not be distinguished clinically).
  • Distinguish from retroperitoneal liposarcoma: aggressive, irregular borders, infiltrative. Some experts biopsy when in doubt.
Calcifications
  • Some guidelines treat calcification as definitively benign, but it occurs in up to 30% of ACCs — the authors reject that confidence.
  • Fine punctate calcification suggests ganglioneuroma but is not pathognomonic.

Other Imaging Modalities

Ultrasonography
  • Suboptimal for detection and characterisation, yet the commonest source of incidentalomas where it is first-line imaging.
  • Right-sided lesions detected more often: the IVC and liver give a window on the right; the left adrenal is hidden or mistaken for spleen, pancreas, para-aortic nodes or stomach.
Functional imaging
  • Rarely needed; useful when the origin is unclear or malignancy is suspected but unconfirmed.
  • 18F-FDG PET: high sensitivity and specificity for malignant lesions (ACC, metastases) but reserved for inconclusive CT/clinical data; CT washout may be more helpful in patients with known cancer. Caution again with RCC and hepatocellular carcinoma metastases.
  • Cortex-specific tracers: NP59 (radiolabelled cholesterol analogue) is taken up by adrenocortical cells — separates adenoma from metastasis; planar imaging is limited for small lesions, improved by single-photon emission CT (SPECT-CT). 11C-metomidate and 123I-iodometomidate (inhibitors of 11β-hydroxylase and aldosterone synthase) identify cortical origin but cannot distinguish adenoma from ACC, and 11C has a short half-life.

Key points: Imaging
  • <10 HU on unenhanced CT = high intracellular lipid — extremely specific for adenoma; MRI is similar.
  • CT washout is the gold standard for adrenal imaging: it separates even lipid-poor adenomas (about 30% of adenomas) from other lesions; gadolinium MRI washout is not used.
  • RCC metastases may mimic lipid-poor adenomas.

Concept — Adrenal imaging is a lipid assay followed by a perfusion assay: unenhanced density detects the fat that defines adenoma, and washout rescues the lipid-poor adenoma. Everything that defeats the method — RCC and hepatocellular metastases, low-density phaeochromocytoma — is why imaging never replaces biochemistry.

Assessing for Hormonal Hypersecretion

Overview

  • All adrenal lesions >1 cm need testing for hormone excess: cortisol and catecholamines in every case; aldosterone if hypertension or unexplained hypokalaemia.
  • Rationale: >10% of incidentalomas are metabolically active (Table 146.4). Yet an estimated 75–80% of adrenal masses are inadequately evaluated.
  • Myelolipoma: pure myelolipomas are never active, but the authors still advise hormonal work-up.
  • Catecholamine testing in <10 HU lesions: observational data and a meta-analysis found ≥10 HU detected 100% of phaeochromocytomas, so testing may be unnecessary for <10 HU asymptomatic lesions — now incorporated in Canadian Urological Association (CUA) guidance.
  • Never biopsy, sample (AVS) or excise any adrenal lesion before excluding catecholamine excess — manipulation can provoke a fatal catecholamine surge.

Testing for Aldosterone Hypersecretion

  • Only about 1% of adenomas cause Conn syndrome, but up to ~5% of newly hypertensive patients may harbour an aldosterone-secreting adenoma.
  • 2016 Endocrine Society guideline (with nine other organisations) on primary aldosteronism (PA) — no competing guideline; the chapter follows it.
Indications for screening
  • Adrenal lesion plus hypertension or unexplained hypokalaemia. Accurate diagnosis and subtyping matter for target-organ risk, drug selection and potential surgical cure.

Screening test: aldosterone-to-renin ratio
  • Screening test of choice: morning plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC, ng/dL) ÷ plasma renin activity (PRA, ng/mL/h) = aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR), expressed as a unitless number; direct renin concentration (DRC) is an alternative.
  • Sample 8–10 am, preferably seated — the same window as the morning cortisol of the dexamethasone test.
  • Floor the renin at 0.2 ng/mL/h to avoid falsely high ratios; very low renin alone inflates the ARR, so a high ARR with low PAC (especially <15 ng/dL) suggests a false positive — but not absolutely, as some proven aldosteronomas have PAC <15 or even <10 ng/dL.
  • Hypokalaemia is an unreliable screen: fewer than 40% of patients with PA are hypokalaemic.

Confirmatory testing
  • Mandatory after a positive screen — only 50–87% of positive screens are confirmed. Refer to an experienced endocrinologist at this point.
  • Omit confirmation when there is spontaneous hypokalaemia + undetectable renin + PAC >20 ng/dL (high positive predictive value).
  • Most tests exploit failure of aldosterone to suppress with sodium loading; no test is superior, so choice is by patient and physician. Monitor blood pressure; correct hypokalaemia and stop mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists first.

Subtype differentiation
  • Essential because only some subtypes are surgically curable (Box 146.3). Non-surgical candidates skip subtyping and start medical therapy (Fig. 146.12).
  • Genetic testing not for all; consider with family history of PA, onset <20 years, or family history of early stroke.
  • Adrenal CT in all potential surgical candidates. Aldosterone-producing adenoma: unilateral, <10 HU, non-enhancing, average 1.6–1.8 cm, normal contralateral gland — but about 20% are <1 cm (Fig. 146.14: a typical ~1-cm aldosteronoma), so lateralisation is never based on CT alone. Idiopathic hyperplasia: multiple nodules, bilateral limb enlargement, or normal glands. MRI adds cost without advantage.
  • AVS establishes lateralisation in surgical candidates: CT-based decisions would have wrongly excluded 22% and wrongly recommended adrenalectomy in 25%. When done correctly, AVS sensitivity 95%, specificity 100%.
  • AVS exceptions: age <40 with a clear unilateral adenoma (>1 cm) and normal contralateral gland (incidental adenomas rare in the young), and suspected ACC.
  • SPARTACUS randomised trial (CT- versus AVS-guided treatment): no difference in antihypertensive intensity at 1 year, challenging routine AVS; CT and AVS lateralisation were discordant in half; persistent biochemical aldosteronism after surgery was more frequent with CT guidance (20% vs 10%).

Testing for Hypercortisolism

  • All lesions >1 cm; 5–8% of incidentalomas secrete excess glucocorticoid.
  • Overnight low-dose dexamethasone suppression test (LD-DST) is the favoured screen for adrenal masses. Late-night salivary cortisol (LNSC), 24-hour urinary free cortisol (UFC) and the 48-hour 2-mg test are valid for Cushing syndrome generally (2008 Endocrine Society) but secondary for adrenal masses.
  • Exclude exogenous steroids, including creams and nasal sprays, before testing. Before resection for clinically significant hypercortisolism, confirm suppressed/low morning ACTH.
  • Other conditions activate the HPA axis and mimic Cushing syndrome (Box 146.4) — endocrine expertise is advised.

Confirmatory testing
  • Clinical signs plus abnormal screen: confirmation probably unnecessary. Abnormal test with low suspicion, or normal tests with high suspicion → endocrinologist.
Identifying the cause of hypercortisolism (sequence)
  • Step 1 — exclude exogenous glucocorticoid: the commonest cause in the Western world; oral, topical or inhaled, even low dose; ask about herbal remedies, nasal sprays and surreptitious use (e.g., performance enhancement).
  • Step 2 — serum ACTH:
    • Low ACTH → ACTH-independent: adrenal adenoma (autonomous secretion) or PPNAD; also exogenous steroid. Normal adrenals on imaging → suspect exogenous steroid, rarely PPNAD. Up to 10% of adrenal Cushing syndrome is bilateral — AVS may help.
    • High ACTH → ACTH-dependent (pituitary adenoma or ectopic source) — outside urology; refer.
  • Step 3 — pituitary versus ectopic: difficult because both sources are hard to image and incidental pituitary, lung and pancreatic findings are common (10% of the population have a pituitary imaging abnormality; half of Cushing disease has a normal pituitary MRI). Gold standard: inferior petrosal sinus ACTH sampling after CRH stimulation — central-to-peripheral gradient = Cushing disease; no gradient = ectopic.
  • High-dose dexamethasone suppression is no longer routine: some ectopic tumours carry glucocorticoid receptors and also suppress.
Fig. 146.16 — cortisol algorithm (sequence)
  • Adrenal mass → exclude exogenous glucocorticoid → one of: overnight 1-mg DST, LNSC (×2) or 24-h UFC (×2) → normal = Cushing syndrome unlikely; any abnormal → endocrinologist for confirmation (repeat abnormal LNSC/UFC).

Testing for Adrenal Sex Steroid Hypersecretion

  • Exceedingly rare in incidentalomas. Commonest sex-steroid-secreting mass: ACC co-secreting cortisol. Pure androgen-secreting tumours (testosterone, 17-ketosteroids) occur mainly in women; about half are benign.
  • No routine testing; test when ACC is suspected: DHEA-S, androstenedione, 17-hydroxyprogesterone; testosterone in women; oestradiol in men and postmenopausal women — potential ACC tumour markers; diagnostic value for benign versus malignant not yet defined.
  • Dexamethasone suppression to separate adrenal from ovarian androgen is unreliable — replaced by imaging.

Testing for Catecholamine Hypersecretion

  • Phaeochromocytoma in about 5% of incidentalomas → all patients, including those with suspected metastasis, need testing.
  • Broad guideline consensus: plasma free metanephrines or urinary fractionated metanephrines; performance is similar overall.
  • Liquid chromatography methods (electrochemical/fluorometric or tandem mass spectrometry) are preferred to immunoassays.
  • Interference: paracetamol (acetaminophen), mesalazine, sulfasalazine cross-react with electrochemical detection — stop ≥5 days; tricyclic antidepressants and phenoxybenzamine cause false positives — stop; severe illness causes false positives.
  • Urine catecholamines and VMA are abandoned as first-line tests (lower accuracy).

Concept — Every hormonal screen is a suppression-or-rhythm test for autonomy: aldosterone that ignores a suppressed renin or a salt load, cortisol that ignores dexamethasone or the night, catecholamine metabolites produced continuously inside the tumour. Each false result comes from something that shifts the regulator or the assay — drugs, binding globulins, posture, renal function.

Biopsy of Adrenal Masses

Overview

  • Limited role: (1) imaging with clinical data diagnoses well; (2) histology cannot reliably separate adenoma from carcinoma; (3) biopsy carries risk.
  • ESE–ENSAT: no biopsy without a history of malignancy. Main role: confirm suspected metastasis (rarely necessary); tissue in metastatic ACC before systemic therapy.

Test Characteristics of Biopsy

  • Without known malignancy: solitary adrenal metastasis as first presentation is extremely rare; sensitivity for malignancy at best 81–84%, for ACC at best 70%; specificity ≈99%. 18–19-gauge needles improve yield without more complications; adequacy about 88% (67–100%).
  • With known extra-adrenal malignancy: >50% of new adrenal lesions are metastases; biopsy accurately separates metastasis from benign lesion, with excellent negative predictive value once non-diagnostic samples are excluded. Positive yield highest with lung cancer (>90%) and RCC (80%).

Complications of Biopsy

  • 2.8–12%; bleeding commonest; also pneumothorax, haemothorax, abscess, bacteraemia, pancreatitis. Biopsy (especially with haemorrhage) may complicate or prevent laparoscopic resection.
  • Needle-tract seeding in ACC — some advise avoiding biopsy if ACC suspected (only two cases reported).
  • Exclude phaeochromocytoma biochemically first — biopsy can precipitate a life-threatening hypertensive crisis (some inadvertent biopsies were uneventful, but due diligence is mandatory).

Clinical Usefulness of Biopsy

  • Community biopsies before tertiary referral never changed management in one review. Radiology reports calling lesions “amenable to biopsy” should be strongly discouraged.
  • Biopsy only when imaging limits are reached and the result will change management — chiefly a known primary cancer possibly recurring in the adrenal.
Fig. 146.17 — biopsy decision (sequence)
  • Can imaging characterise the mass? Yes → no biopsy. Metabolically active? Yes → no biopsy. ACC strongly suspected? Yes → no biopsy. Will biopsy change management, and does benefit justify risk (including harder laparoscopic surgery)? Yes → biopsy, with phaeochromocytoma excluded.
Key points: Biopsy of adrenal masses
  • Favourable test characteristics for benign versus metastatic disease.
  • Always exclude phaeochromocytoma before biopsy.

Concept — Biopsy answers only one question well — is this a metastasis? — because cytology cannot separate adenoma from carcinoma. It is therefore justified only when a known cancer may have recurred and the answer changes treatment.
