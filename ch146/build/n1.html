Chapter 146 — Pathophysiology, Evaluation and Medical Management of Adrenal Disorders

  • Small, deceptively simple glands (formerly the suprarenal glands) at an anatomical crossroads beside major vessels, nerves and viscera; now recognised as central to homeostasis and responsible for several major human diseases.
  • Medical management is multidisciplinary (endocrinology, nephrology, cardiology); surgical ownership has historically been split between urologists, general, oncological and endocrine surgeons, largely by local institutional tradition.
  • Evaluation and surgery sit naturally with urologists because of minimally invasive skill, familiarity with retroperitoneal anatomy and approaches, and the close linkage of renal and adrenal pathophysiology.

Historical Background

  • Overlooked by Galen, da Vinci and Vesalius; first described by Bartholomaeus Eustachius (mid-16th century).
  • Thomas Addison (1855) linked adrenal insufficiency to the glands at autopsy — the eponymous Addison disease.
  • Brown-Séquard (1856): bilateral adrenalectomy in animals was uniformly fatal — the adrenals are indispensable to life.
  • Osler (1896): first hormonal replacement — crude porcine adrenal extract produced weight gain in one patient with Addison disease.
  • Adrenaline discovered and localised to the medulla; shown to produce a sustained rise in blood pressure. Its failure to sustain life after bilateral adrenalectomy established Addison disease as a disorder of the cortex alone.
  • Cortisol isolated in the 1930s; its use in rheumatoid arthritis earned the 1950 Nobel Prize (Kendall, Hench, Reichstein). Aldosterone isolated from bovine adrenal in 1952.
  • Late 20th-century pioneers: Conn, Wilkins, Liddle, Sutherland.

Adrenal Embryology and Anatomy

Overview

  • Paired retroperitoneal organs of cortex and medulla: spiculated mustard-yellow cortex, distinct from perinephric fat; brown central medulla on cross-section.
  • 4–5 g each; 4–6 cm long, 2–3 cm wide. Right triangular, left crescent-shaped.
  • Position either “capping” the upper pole or “cradled” superomedially just above the renal vessels — visible on computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and surgically relevant, especially on the left and in patients with abundant perinephric fat, when identifying the adrenal vein.

  • The medullary migration through the cortex explains the brown stippling of the cortex.
  • Fetal adrenal at birth is twice adult weight but immature; the fetal cortex is resorbed by 12 months. Glomerulosa and fasciculata continue to develop, but the zona reticularis completes differentiation only at 3 years — reflecting the late importance of adrenal sex steroids. Adrenocortical stem cells may contribute to development and homeostasis.
  • Unilateral adrenal agenesis is rare; its reported association with renal agenesis is probably spurious (poor imaging of the ipsilateral adrenal) because adrenal and renal development are separate. With renal agenesis, malrotation or malascent the adrenal usually develops normally, discoid and in its normal position.
  • Adrenal rests (accessory tissue, cortical or medullary) lie along the path of gonadal descent: present in up to half of neonates, persisting in about 1% of adults.
  • In congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), testicular adrenal rests may become hyperplastic and present as a testicular mass — consider before orchiectomy in CAH.
  • Adrenal heterotopia: incomplete separation of adrenal mesoderm from liver or kidney, with partial or complete incorporation into that organ.

  • Redundant arterial supply from three sources: superior (inferior phrenic artery), middle (aorta directly), inferior (ipsilateral renal artery) → subcapsular plexus → direct medullary branches and cortical sinusoids.

Key points: Anatomy and embryology
  • Two embryologically and functionally distinct components: cortex (endocrine) and medulla (neurocrine).
  • Arterial supply variable; venous drainage predictable — short right vein to IVC, longer left vein to left renal vein in most patients.

Concept — The adrenal is two organs sharing a capsule: a mesodermal steroid factory and a neural-crest sympathetic ganglion. Its surgical anatomy is governed by one asymmetry — the short right vein on the IVC versus the long left vein on the renal vein.

Adrenal Physiology

  • Cortex and medulla are best conceived as two distinct organs — the former endocrine, the latter neurocrine.

Adrenal Cortex Physiology

  • All cortical steroids derive from cholesterol, chiefly from low-density lipoprotein (LDL); the ratio and type of enzymes in each zone determine its product (Fig. 146.4 — steroid synthesis from cholesterol to mineralocorticoid, glucocorticoid and androgen).
  • No membrane receptors: lipophilic steroids diffuse into the cell, bind intracellular receptors, and the hormone–receptor complex binds DNA to modulate transcription.

Zona Glomerulosa

  • The only zone expressing aldosterone synthase (CYP11B2) — hence the sole source of aldosterone.
  • Aldosterone acts on the distal nephron: reabsorbs Na+ and Cl−, secretes K+ and H+. Sodium reabsorption carries free water, so aldosterone changes total body fluid volume, not sodium concentration. Salivary and colonic effects are physiologically minor.
  • Regulated chiefly by angiotensin II (renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, RAAS) and directly by serum potassium; adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) is a weak stimulus — so the glomerulosa alone does not atrophy in pituitary failure.
  • Atrial natriuretic peptide is the main inhibitor (with somatostatin, dopamine) — a cardiac–adrenal–renal link.

Zona Fasciculata

  • Glucocorticoid synthesis via 17α-, 21- and 11β-hydroxylase. Cortisol is the principal human glucocorticoid, under tight ACTH control in a classic negative-feedback loop.
  • Strict circadian rhythm: most cortisol secreted in the early morning. Glucocorticoids are essential to life — metabolism, immunity, intravascular volume, blood pressure, mood, sleep and possibly memory.
Normal hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis physiology
  • Fasciculata secretes up to 20 mg cortisol per day.
  • Hypothalamic corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) (with oxytocin and vasopressin) → anterior pituitary corticotrophs cleave pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) to ACTH → adrenal cortisol and androgens → cortisol feeds back on hypothalamus (CRH) and pituitary (ACTH). Fig. 146.5 depicts the loop.
  • Stress, physiological or psychological, is the dominant modulator; splanchnic innervation also influences output.
  • ACTH is trophic as well as secretagogue: without it (e.g., suppression by exogenous steroid) all but the mineralocorticoid-producing cells stop synthesis and undergo apoptosis.
  • CRH follows the suprachiasmatic nucleus: cortisol peaks in the morning, nadir about 11 pm; even small perturbations of this rhythm are pathological.

Zona Reticularis

  • 17α-hydroxylase and 17,20-lyase → dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), sulphated DHEA (DHEA-S) and androstenedione; some androgen also from the fasciculata. ACTH-controlled and circadian.
  • The largest steroid output (>20 mg/day) but the least important for adult homeostasis.
  • Pharmacological suppression of adrenal androgens is an increasingly used strategy in advanced prostate cancer; developmental aberrations cause CAH.

Adrenal Medulla Physiology

  • <10% of adrenal mass; unrelated to the cortex in function and embryology; an integral part of the autonomic nervous system.
  • Chromaffin cells receive preganglionic sympathetic fibres from T11–L2 — analogous to sympathetic ganglion cells.
  • Secretes epinephrine 80%, norepinephrine 19%, dopamine 1% — catecholamines synthesised from tyrosine, modulating the stress response via organ-specific adrenoceptors (Table 146.3). Stored in vesicles, released by exocytosis.
  • Phenylethanolamine-N-methyltransferase (PNMT) converts norepinephrine to epinephrine and is relatively unique to the medulla (also brain and organ of Zuckerkandl). Glucocorticoids potentiate PNMT — one of the few cortex–medulla links — and explain why the medulla is the main source of circulating epinephrine.
Catecholamine metabolism (Fig. 146.6)
  • Tyrosine → L-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA) → dopamine → norepinephrine → (PNMT) → epinephrine.
  • Catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) methylates norepinephrine → normetanephrine and epinephrine → metanephrine. Most adrenal metabolism occurs within medullary cells themselves, not in liver or kidney.
  • >90% of circulating metanephrine and ≥20% of normetanephrine derive from the adrenal medulla — so their rise is the basis of phaeochromocytoma diagnosis. Urinary metabolites are largely sulphated.
  • Monoamine oxidase (MAO) and other enzymes produce vanillylmandelic acid (VMA), the end product, formed largely in the liver; sympathetic-nerve catecholamines also end as VMA.
  • Terminology: “free” = unconjugated; “fractionated” = individual compounds reported separately.

Key points: Physiology
  • Glomerulosa → mineralocorticoid, fasciculata → glucocorticoid, reticularis → androgen; zonal enzyme ratios determine the product.
  • Aldosterone is driven by angiotensin II and potassium; ACTH is minor, so the glomerulosa survives pituitary failure.
  • Cortisol is under tight ACTH control; adrenal androgen suppression is a target in advanced prostate cancer.
  • Catecholamines derive from tyrosine and modulate the systemic stress response.

Concept — Each zone is defined by which enzymes it expresses and which signal it obeys: the glomerulosa answers to angiotensin II and potassium, the inner zones to ACTH. That single split explains why pituitary failure spares aldosterone, and why intra-adrenal methylation makes metanephrines the diagnostic currency of the medulla.
