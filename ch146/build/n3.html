Adrenal Neoplasia

Adrenal Incidentaloma

Definition

  • Adrenal mass >1 cm found on cross-sectional imaging performed for an unrelated reason. Strictly excludes masses found during cancer staging or in patients later found to have adrenal-related symptoms.
  • Work-up aims to reclassify the incidentaloma into a specific neoplasm, which then dictates management.

Epidemiology

  • About 5% on imaging, matching autopsy data; rises with age from <0.5% in the 20s to up to 7% at ≥70 years.
  • The retroperitoneum allows silent growth beyond 20 cm, yet median size at presentation ≈3 cm.
  • “Incidental” does not mean “insignificant”: nearly 20% are potentially surgical (Table 146.4).

Adrenal Adenoma

Overview and Epidemiology

  • Commonest adrenal neoplasm, cortical in origin; about 6% at autopsy; >85% of incidentally imaged adrenal neoplasms are adenomas; prevalence rises with age.
  • Task: separate the non-functional benign adenoma from functional or malignant lesions.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Characteristics

  • Yellow from abundant lipid; histologically may resemble carcinoma — distinguished by the Weiss criteria and immunohistochemistry (Box 146.6).
  • Benign by definition; most metabolically silent: in pooled series ≈90% of incidentalomas were benign adenomas, of which ≈93% were inactive, ≈6% cortisol-secreting and ≈1% aldosterone-secreting.
  • Testosterone-secreting adenomas are exceedingly rare and may contain Reinke crystals (normally in Leydig cells).
  • Associations of non-functional adenoma with insulin resistance and left ventricular dysfunction are unconfirmed.
  • Most remain stable; about 9% grow >1 cm over 3 years.

Hormonally Inactive Adenoma

  • No immediate intervention. Repeat testing and imaging are controversial, with society disagreement.

Aldosterone-Secreting Adenoma

Overview and epidemiology
  • Conn (1955): hypertensive, hypokalaemic 34-year-old woman cured by removal of a 4-cm unilateral tumour.
  • PA is the commonest secondary hypertension: 3–13% of hypertensives in primary care, up to 30% at tertiary centres.
Pathophysiology
  • RAAS (Fig. 146.18): renin release is rate limiting, stimulated by low renal perfusion, renal sympathetic activity and low macula densa sodium → angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → (ACE) angiotensin II → vasoconstriction and aldosterone release (also potassium, ACTH).

Clinical characteristics
  • Usually diagnosed in the 3rd–6th decades during work-up of refractory, moderate-to-severe hypertension; prevalence of PA rises with hypertension stage.
  • Most are normokalaemic (63–91%); hypokalaemic symptoms (headache, polydipsia, polyuria, nocturia, palpitations, weakness) occur in a minority. Serum sodium resembles other hypertension because of escape.
  • Target-organ damage exceeds essential hypertension: left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and hypertrophy, prolonged QT; about 4-fold stroke, 6.5-fold myocardial infarction, 12-fold atrial fibrillation; more proteinuria, type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome. Treatment induces left ventricular remodelling.
Treatment and prognosis
  • Goal: remove the source or block aldosterone at target organs, chosen by subtype and surgical fitness (Fig. 146.12).
  • Lateralised secretion → consider adrenalectomy, usually laparoscopic (small tumours); open if ACC suspected.
  • Outcomes: blood pressure improves in most, but only 37–52% need no antihypertensives; biochemical success ≈94% — residual hypertension reflects other conditions.
  • Predictors of persistent hypertension: age >50, male, high body mass index, preoperative ARR, ≥2 preoperative agents, first-degree relative with hypertension, long duration, cardiovascular remodelling, renal insufficiency.
  • Age <40 with unilateral adenoma >1 cm → adrenalectomy without AVS.
  • Postoperatively: check PAC and ARR for biochemical cure; high-sodium diet and monitoring for hyperkalaemia for several weeks; blood pressure continues to change for up to 6 months.

Key points: Primary aldosteronism
  • Most patients are normokalaemic in contemporary series.
  • End-organ damage exceeds that of essential hypertension.
  • Accurate subtype differentiation ensures appropriate treatment.

Concept — Primary aldosteronism is diagnosed by the renin it suppresses and treated by the side it comes from: a unilateral source is cured by adrenalectomy, a bilateral source only blocked by mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists. That is why lateralisation, not imaging, decides operation.

Cortisol-Secreting Adenoma

  • About 10% of Cushing syndrome; usually a dominant unilateral hyperplastic nodule, sometimes multifocal bilateral hyperplasia. <10% of adrenal masses are bilateral and these are diagnostically challenging; radiologically invisible lesions may cause MACS.
Additional testing
  • Confirm hypercortisolism and its ACTH-independent cause before resection.
  • Screen for vertebral fractures (once at diagnosis — plain films, or existing CT for included levels), type 2 diabetes and hypertension (general-population guidelines for the latter two). No consensus on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry despite common osteoporosis.
  • Hypogonadism is common in men — test if symptomatic. Urolithiasis in up to 50% of Cushing syndrome — no screening recommendation.
Mild autonomous cortisol secretion
  • Adrenalectomy is controversial — individualise (ESE–ENSAT). Surgery may improve glucose control, hypertension and weight, but progression to overt Cushing syndrome is extremely unlikely.
  • Opposing views: operate only when signs present (hypertension, obesity, glucose intolerance, osteopenia) versus operate on all to prevent sequelae.
Postoperative care
  • Postoperative adrenal insufficiency: ≈65% after unilateral adrenalectomy for MACS; ≈99.7% for overt Cushing syndrome — autonomous cortisol suppresses and atrophies the contralateral gland.
  • Endocrine Society replacement: hydrocortisone 10–12 mg/m²/day in 2–3 doses, first dose on waking.
  • Common perioperative protocol: hydrocortisone 100 mg IV on the day of surgery → slow oral taper (cortisone acetate 25–37.5 mg/day or hydrocortisone 25–50 mg/day) once oral intake resumes.
  • Deaths from hypocortisolism have been reported after surgery for Cushing syndrome, none after MACS.
  • Alternative: dexamethasone 0.5 mg 6-hourly from surgery (not detected by the cortisol assay) → early-morning cortisol <50 nmol/L confirms successful resection → switch to hydrocortisone.
  • Taper: unstudied; one scheme checks morning cortisol 3-monthly and starts ACTH stimulation tests once it exceeds 7.4 µg/dL.
Prognosis
  • Median recovery of adrenal function: 6.5 months (MACS) vs 11 months (Cushing syndrome); a few (≈2% and ≈7%) never recover.
  • Normalising cortisol reduces but does not eradicate cardiovascular risk; cushingoid features resolve in 7–9 months in most, occasionally persisting for years.

Concept — A cortisol-secreting adenoma switches off the ACTH that sustains the opposite gland, so its removal leaves an atrophic contralateral adrenal. The degree of preoperative autonomy predicts how certain and how long the postoperative adrenal insufficiency will be.

Phaeochromocytoma

Overview and Epidemiology

  • Tumour of medullary catecholamine-producing cells; 1–2 per 100 000 per year; causes only ≈0.5% of hypertension.
  • ≈5% of incidentalomas; incidental discovery accounts for 10–25% of all diagnoses.
  • Paragangliomas = extra-adrenal phaeochromocytomas (10–25%; Fig. 146.20: adrenal 75–90%) arising from paraganglia along sympathetic and parasympathetic chains — head, neck, thorax, abdomen, pelvis including bladder. The organ of Zuckerkandl (aortic bifurcation to inferior mesenteric artery root) is a common site. Some reserve “paraganglioma” for head and neck tumours, which are usually non-functional.

Pathophysiology

Overview
  • Adrenal phaeochromocytomas possess PNMT and can make epinephrine; tumours vary in enzyme content and self-metabolism, so secretory profiles and clinical behaviour vary.

  • Malignancy is defined only by clinical metastases; histology and even local invasion predict poorly; no single histological criterion; Ki-67 may be the best marker but is unvalidated.

Hereditary disease
  • Younger, multifocal and/or bilateral. MEN-2: almost always adrenal; VHL ≈12% and NF-1 ≈6% extra-adrenal; SDHB/SDHD mostly extra-adrenal and multifocal, though solitary adrenal tumours occur.
Malignant disease
  • Metastasis much commoner in extra-adrenal tumours; with SDHB, a substantial minority (≈28%) present with adrenal lesions.
  • Malignant tumours tend to be larger (>5 cm) and dopamine secreting — but no preclinical diagnosis without metastasis.
  • Sites: bone, lung, liver, lymph nodes. Most metastases appear within 5 years, but >15 years is reported.

Diagnostic Tests

  • Biochemistry first, then imaging to localise. Consider phaeochromocytoma even in known malignancy and suspected solitary adrenal metastasis — misdiagnosis can be catastrophic.
Cross-sectional imaging
  • Well circumscribed; >10 HU unenhanced (mean ≈35 HU) — rich vascularity, low lipid; no rapid washout (rare <10 HU, brisk-washout tumours exist → biochemistry for every mass).
  • Iodinated contrast does not trigger hypertensive crisis — a discredited belief.
  • MRI: no out-of-phase signal drop-out. The “light bulb sign” (bright T2, best on fat suppression) is neither sensitive nor specific.
Functional imaging
  • 18F-FDG PET, 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT and MIBG — see Imaging of Adrenal Masses.

Biochemical Evaluation

  • Catecholamines and metabolites circulate largely sulphate-conjugated; old “total” assays could not separate free from sulphated forms and performed worse.
  • Catecholamine testing (sensitivity and specificity ≈85%) has been replaced by metanephrines because release is paroxysmal; urinary catecholamines are still often obtained with urinary fractionated metanephrines.
  • Metanephrine testing — the foundation: COMT converts catecholamines to metanephrines continuously inside the tumour, so plasma metanephrines are far more sensitive than episodic catecholamine surges. Choice of plasma versus urine remains debated.
  • VMA: formed via MAO in both medulla and sympathetic nerves (which lack PNMT and add only normetanephrine), so the rise is diluted → low sensitivity (<65%) but high specificity (≈99% in non-familial).
  • Clonidine suppression: α2 agonist suppresses sympathetic (not tumour) norepinephrine; compare normetanephrine before and after — secondary test for borderline results; risk of significant hypotension.
  • Chromogranin A: secretory-vesicle granin; sensitivity ≈85%; role in confirmation of mild (<4-fold) plasma metanephrine rises; renally cleared — specificity falls when GFR <80 mL/min.

Screening for hereditary phaeochromocytoma
  • Hereditary in more than one-third; nearly one-quarter of apparently sporadic cases carry germline mutations → all patients with phaeochromocytoma or paraganglioma should enter shared decision-making about genetic testing (2014 Endocrine Society guideline algorithm).

Treatment

Overview
  • Phaeochromocytoma is a surgical disease: complete resection whenever possible; laparoscopic adrenalectomy is standard, open for large or difficult tumours. Lifelong follow-up for all; familial and malignant disease need cardiology, endocrinology and oncology input.
Preoperative management
  • Intraoperative catecholamine release → hypertensive crises and arrhythmias; historical mortality up to 50% before routine blockade, now <3% (anaesthesia plus blockade).
  • First International Symposium on Pheochromocytoma (2005): preoperative blockade for all with abnormal biochemistry, even if normotensive and asymptomatic. No level 1 evidence on the best regimen.
  • Two strategies (Fig. 146.22): option 1 — aggressive α-blockade (NIH approach); option 2 — calcium channel blockade. A minority advocate no blockade in selected patients.
  • Cardiac evaluation for catecholamine cardiomyopathy — some advise routine echocardiography; at least cardiology or anaesthetic consultation.

Intravascular volume management
  • Perhaps the most important preoperative step. Liberal salt and fluid once blockade starts → admit the day before for IV fluids (1–2 L bolus the night before).
Postoperative management
  • Hypotension (lasting phenoxybenzamine effect) and rebound hypoglycaemia: catecholamine excess suppressed insulin via α2 receptors; withdrawal → rebound hyperinsulinaemia. Some advise overnight intensive care.
Follow-up
  • Repeat biochemistry after surgery; if metanephrines stay high, MIBG may unmask metastases once the primary is removed.
  • Annual biochemistry is mandatory; lifelong screening advised — 10-year recurrence up to 16%, even >15 years later. Suggested: testing at 6 months, then annually; postoperative imaging to document resection, further imaging guided by biochemistry.
Hereditary disease
  • MEN-2 and VHL: low malignancy, high bilaterality → partial cortical-sparing adrenalectomy to avoid lifelong steroid replacement. Requires expertise and planning; replacement may still be needed, addisonian crisis can still occur; recurrence poorly defined but significant.
Malignant disease
  • Largely palliative; metastasectomy of resectable disease is standard but unproven for survival versus α/β-blockade plus α-methyl-p-tyrosine. Ablation and embolisation for local control.
  • 131I-MIBG therapy (confirm uptake first): symptomatic response in up to two-thirds; catecholamine fall in >40%; tumour shrinkage ≈30%; complete response <5%. High-dose therapy produced durable complete responses in a quarter, with notable toxicity. FDA approval 2018 (Azedra) for unresectable phaeochromocytoma/paraganglioma, from a single-arm open-label study.
  • Cyclophosphamide, vincristine, dacarbazine (CVD): best-studied chemotherapy; >50% radiological and ≈75% biochemical response, but short-lived (≈2 years); used after MIBG failure or MIBG-negative tumours. CVD + MIBG undefined.
Prognosis
  • Excellent after resection; surgical mortality <3%; hypertension usually but not always resolves.
  • Recurrence up to 16%, half malignant; risk factors: extra-adrenal (hazard ratio 11.2), hereditary (3.4), right-sided (3.1), bilateral (1.4), larger (1.2 per cm).
  • Metastatic course varies — some survive >20 years; bone metastases carry the best prognosis; 5-year survival ≈50%.
  • Summary: consider phaeochromocytoma in every adrenal mass; refer to a tertiary centre if unfamiliar with its care.
Key points: Phaeochromocytoma
  • Nearly 25% of apparently sporadic cases are hereditary on genetic testing.
  • Plasma or urinary metanephrines underpin modern diagnosis.
  • Malignancy is defined only by metastases; recurrence even 15 years after resection → long-term follow-up.
  • Master perioperative management before operating.

Concept — Every step of phaeochromocytoma care follows from one fact: the tumour stores and releases catecholamines unpredictably but metabolises them continuously. Continuous metabolism makes metanephrines the diagnosis; unpredictable release makes α-before-β blockade and volume repletion the preparation; and delayed metastasis makes follow-up lifelong.

Nonfunctional Benign Adrenal Lesions

Myelolipoma

Epidemiology

  • Rare, benign, metabolically silent; all ages; <0.1% at autopsy. About 15% extra-adrenal, up to half of these presacral.
  • Equal sex ratio; no side preference; bilateral cases reported; may exceed 10 cm; usually asymptomatic, occasionally abdominal or flank pain.

Pathophysiology

  • Clonal stem-cell proliferation: mature fat plus trilineage haematopoietic tissue (megakaryocytes may abound). Cellularity does not fall with age; no contribution to circulating blood — capillary venous sinuses are absent.
  • Induced in rats by necrotic tissue and ACTH → the adrenal-stress-response hypothesis; cause unknown.

Diagnosis

  • Large macroscopic fat component on cross-sectional imaging; interdigitating enhancing myeloid tissue; calcification in over a quarter; haemorrhage common. Fig. 146.24: macroscopic fat in a 1.5-cm lesion is pathognomonic.

Additional Workup

  • Hormonal testing recommended for all, although some guidelines advise against it: over two dozen myelolipomas coexist with hormone excess, usually hypercortisolaemia, generally from a coexisting functional adenoma rather than the myelolipoma itself.

Management

  • Surgical excision is definitive when needed; embolisation only for acute haemorrhage. Most do not need resection (Fig. 146.23: a rare 15-cm symptomatic lesion).
  • Indications: symptoms, or concern about spontaneous haemorrhage (fewer than 50 cases; smallest bleeding lesion 6.3 cm; bleeders average 10–14 cm). No deaths reported; emergency surgery or transfusion rare → value of prophylactic excision unclear — individualise.

Adrenal Cysts

Overview and Epidemiology

  • 0.064–0.18% at autopsy; 1–22% of incidentalomas. Millimetres to >20 cm; uni- or multilocular; bilateral in 8–10%; female predominance, peak 3rd–6th decades; from prenatal life onwards.
  • Associations: hypertension, polycystic kidney disease, Klippel–Trénaunay–Weber and Beckwith–Wiedemann syndromes.

Clinical Characteristics

  • Cystic neoplasms mimic benign cysts, especially haemorrhagic or degenerate tumours resembling pseudocysts (ACC, adenoma, phaeochromocytoma). About 7% of reported cysts were associated with malignancy — all pseudocysts; likely overestimated because series are surgical.
  • Cystic neoplasms are larger (>7 cm) with thicker walls. Large cysts may be hard to assign to adrenal versus kidney, pancreas, spleen or liver.

Diagnostic Tests

  • Endocrine evaluation for all.
  • Imaging: well-defined, sharply marginated, fluid attenuation, no enhancement (Fig. 146.25: stippled wall calcification). Attenuation >30 HU suggests haemorrhage, debris or calcification. Peripheral, rim-like calcification in 15–70% (occasionally nodular). MRI: T1 hypointense, T2 hyperintense.
  • Imaging cannot exclude malignancy → aspiration or excision. Aspirate only after excluding phaeochromocytoma; send fluid for Gram stain, cytology, triglycerides, cortisol and amylase (left-sided); cystogram can assess the wall. Aspiration may be therapeutic.

Treatment

  • Driven by function, malignancy risk and symptoms. Natural history: in small series 60% enlarged and 25% shrank.
  • Observe small, asymptomatic, thin-walled, homogeneous near-water cysts; ESE–ENSAT: no follow-up for silent benign-appearing lesions <4 cm.
  • Excise cysts that are heterogeneous, >5 cm, thick-walled or symptomatic. Simple decortication is not advocated (malignancy risk).

Prognosis

  • Histology dependent: benign cysts are followed for reaccumulation and retreated if symptoms recur; malignant ones per their histology.

Summary

  • Symptomatic cysts → surgery; small, non-functional, asymptomatic, benign-appearing → conservative follow-up.
Key point: Adrenal cysts
  • Because of possible associated malignancy, observe adrenal cysts with caution.

Concept — The cyst that carries risk is the pseudocyst, because a haemorrhagic or necrotic tumour can masquerade as one. Size, wall thickness and heterogeneity are therefore surrogates for “is there a tumour behind the fluid?”.

Ganglioneuroma

Overview and Epidemiology

  • Extremely rare benign neuroectodermal tumour of ganglion and Schwann cells; 0.3–2% of incidentalomas.
  • Sites: mediastinum ≈39%, retroperitoneum ≈31%, adrenal ≈21%, occasionally pelvis and neck. Young: half aged 10–29; only 20% over 40.

Pathophysiology/Clinical Characteristics

  • Rare malignant transformation; peripheral nerve sheath tumours and composite ganglioneuroma–phaeochromocytoma reported; nodal ganglioneuroma cells may represent matured neuroblastoma.
  • Can grow very large and encase vessels without narrowing the lumen. May secrete vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (diarrhoea); most are asymptomatic.

Diagnostic Tests

  • No definitive preoperative diagnosis; imaging clues: well circumscribed; attenuation ≤ muscle on enhanced and unenhanced CT; calcification in 20–69% (classically fine and punctate, often coarse; limited value because ACC also calcifies).
  • MRI: T1 homogeneously low; T2 heterogeneous, intermediate to high. PET standardised uptake value usually <3.0.

Treatment and Prognosis

  • Diagnosis almost always at resection, so natural history is undefined; beware large size and encasement of retroperitoneal vessels.

Summary

  • Rare, largely pathological diagnosis, nearly always benign course.
Key point: Ganglioneuroma
  • Rare benign tumours of the young that can grow to encase critical structures.

Adrenal Oncocytoma

Overview and Epidemiology

  • Familiar from the kidney; also salivary and endocrine glands. Adrenal oncocytomas are exceedingly rare (about 50 reported).

Pathophysiology and Clinical Characteristics

  • Large, mitochondria-rich, granular eosinophilic cells; pathogenesis unclear.
  • Female:male ≈2.5:1; left:right ≈3.5:1; up to 20 cm; cases in pregnancy.
  • Mostly inactive; ≈10% secrete sex steroids, cytokines and/or cortisol.
  • Classically benign, but >30% of reported lesions malignant and a further 23% indeterminate, including IVC invasion.

Diagnostic Tests and Treatment

  • Diagnosed at resection → manage like any adrenal mass. No regular central stellate scar (unlike renal oncocytoma). Metastasectomy and radiotherapy for local recurrence may help malignant cases.

Prognosis

  • Uncertain but leans benign; even widely metastatic disease may progress slowly.

Summary

  • Extremely rare; despite a benign reputation, a proportion are malignant.
Key point: Oncocytoma
  • Unlike renal oncocytomas, a large proportion of adrenal oncocytic lesions are malignant.

Concept — The benign-sounding adrenal lesions are benign only once imaging or pathology has proved it. Macroscopic fat alone is diagnostic — hence myelolipoma alone escapes the 4-cm rule — while cysts, ganglioneuromas and oncocytomas are managed as adrenal masses until resected.
