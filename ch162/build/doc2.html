Chapter 162 — Radiation Therapy for Prostate Cancer

Radiation Therapy Background and Technologic Advances

Early Radiation Treatment With External Beam and Brachytherapy Approaches

  • Roentgen described the x-ray in 1896 ("Concerning a New Kind of Ray") and received the first Nobel Prize in Physics in 1901. The mechanism of tissue damage — ionisation causing deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage — was identified only decades later, after DNA itself had been described.
  • First prostate radiotherapy: Pasteau and Degrais, 1913 (published 1914), using an intraurethral radium source. Hugh Hampton Young, pioneer of the radical perineal prostatectomy, adopted and popularised it.
  • Kilovoltage external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), 1930s–1950s: these low-energy beams deposit their dose in skin and superficial soft tissue, so an effective prostate dose could not be reached without unacceptable toxicity.
  • Three requirements for treating a deep pelvic target. These drove every later advance:
    1. Penetrating beams that deposit little dose along their path, sparing skin and tissue.
    2. Sparing of adjacent organs: rectum, bladder and femoral heads.
    3. Minimal targeting error from physiological organ motion and tissue deformation between visits.

Linear Accelerators

  • 1960s: megavoltage EBRT made curative treatment possible, with acceptable normal-tissue damage and less skin toxicity.
  • Mechanism: electrons are accelerated along a linear path of oscillating electric potentials → strike a target (tungsten) → photons (x-rays) are produced → the beam is shaped by lead blocks, tungsten collimators and leaves.
  • Higher photon energy → deeper dose deposition and greater skin sparing. Orthovoltage (100–500 kV) and cobalt-60 (1.17–1.33 MV) units deposit their maximum dose at or just below the skin surface. Modern linear accelerators (6–20 MV) place the maximum dose several centimetres deep.
  • 1980s–1990s: computer-based planning → CT-based three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT), then intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT).

CT-Based Treatment Planning and Three-Dimensional Conformal Radiotherapy

  • Conventional radiation (until the mid-1980s): fields were designed from surface and skeletal landmarks. The prostate was localised on plain films using a contrast-filled Foley catheter or urethrogram and a rectal tube, with portals centred on the pubic symphysis and femoral heads.
  • 1990s: computed tomography (CT) entered radiation oncology. The prostate could now be seen at planning, and computing power allowed dose calculation corrected for the differing electron densities of bone, air and soft tissue.
  • Planning workflow: simulation (a CT scan in the immobilisation device used for daily treatment) → the radiation oncologist contours the target (prostate) and the normal tissues (regions of interest, ROIs) → prescribes the target dose, its acceptable variation and the normal-tissue constraints → dosimetrists and physicists calculate and optimise → dose–volume histograms (DVHs) record the dose each structure receives.
  • 3D-CRT: the beams conform to the target's shape. Its purpose is to improve the therapeutic index, escalating prostate dose while respecting adjacent organs. Planning without CT data produced frequent errors in field placement and portal size.

Basics of Fractionation in Radiation Therapy

  • Coutard (1932): many small fractions give disease control with acceptable normal-tissue tolerance.
  • Conventional fractionation = 1.8–2.0 gray (Gy) daily. One gray is the absorption of 1 joule per kilogram. This fraction size rests on early clinical experience, animal data and mathematical models.
  • Linear–quadratic model: the most widely used model for predicting a tissue's response to fraction size, based on the tissue's α/β ratio.
  • Why small fractions were preferred: late-responding normal tissues were thought more sensitive to fraction size than tumour. Late toxicity (≥90 days after treatment) is reduced by fractionation, so more fractions spare late-responding tissue more than tumour.

Radiobiologic Basis for Hypofractionation

  • Most models assume tumours have more cycling cells than late-responding normal tissue. Prostate cancer has relatively few cycling cells, so it may respond to fraction size and dose rate like a late-responding normal tissue.
  • Classic α/β estimates are ~3 Gy for normal tissue and ~10 Gy for tumour. Prostate cancer's α/β is estimated as low as ~1.5 Gy, lower than that of the surrounding late-responding organs.
  • Consequence: using fractions larger than 2 Gy allows a lower total dose, which means less normal-organ injury with the same or better cancer control.
  • Evidence: the first modelling study pooled brachytherapy and EBRT outcomes and found α/β ≈1.5 Gy best fitted the data. It was criticised for mixing modalities that differ in both dose per fraction and spatial dose distribution. Larger EBRT-only cohorts then confirmed α/β ≈1.0–1.7 Gy (varying with risk group) and ≈1.4 Gy.

Concept — Prostate cancer's low α/β reverses the usual fractionation logic. Because the tumour, not the rectum, is the tissue most sensitive to fraction size, larger fractions favour tumour kill over late toxicity, and every hypofractionation and stereotactic regimen later in the chapter follows from this.

Transrectal Ultrasound-Guided Brachytherapy

  • Brachytherapy (BT), from the Greek brakhus ("short"): radioactive sources placed in or near a tumour.
  • Historical sequence: Pasteau placed temporary radium needles through the urethra (1914) → Young used intracavitary radium in the bladder, rectum and urethra, "cross-firing" into the prostate (1922) → Barringer (Memorial Hospital, New York) implanted radon needles transperineally → Whitmore performed retropubic 125I implantation with lymph node dissection (1970s).
  • Retropubic "freehand" technique: open exposure; needles passed anterior to posterior, guided by a gloved finger in the rectum. Complications: bleeding from the periprostatic venous plexus, needles passing through the rectum or bladder, and infection.
  • Holm: transrectal ultrasound (TRUS)-guided transperineal technique, giving live image guidance. Set-up: high lithotomy → TRUS probe held on a stepper fixed to the table or a floor pedestal → a predrilled template with parallel holes sits on the perineum, its grid overlaid on the ultrasound image → needles inserted through the template → sealed seeds deployed as the needle is withdrawn.
  • Trans-sacral CT-guided and intraoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-guided implantation have been studied but are not in routine use.

Concept — Every technical step in this history (megavoltage, CT planning, conformal shaping, TRUS guidance) answers one of three problems: penetration, organ sparing and targeting accuracy. Fractionation adds a separate, biological way of controlling toxicity.

Contemporary Techniques and Advances in Imaging

Multiparametric Magnetic Resonance Imaging in Radiation Treatment Planning

  • Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) gives anatomical and functional information at diagnosis (see Chapter 152) and upgrades or upstages disease. It predicts seminal vesicle invasion more accurately than extracapsular extension.
  • Limitation: against whole-mount pathology in the Prostate Imaging–Reporting and Data System version 2 (PI-RADS v2) era, MRI still misses some clinically significant cancers, particularly those <10 mm.
  • So the workup before definitive radiotherapy uses systematic ± targeted biopsies. Several cores from the same target count as one core when calculating the proportion of positive cores (National Comprehensive Cancer Network, NCCN).
  • MRI simulation: MRI carries no electron-density information, so it cannot calculate dose on its own. It is fused with the CT simulation to delineate the prostate and tumour.
  • MRI is most useful when CT is compromised, for example by a hip prosthesis or a small prostate. Some centres also use it in post-implant quality assurance for LDR seed distribution.
  • Erectile vessel-sparing radiotherapy, planned on T2 sequences and MR angiography: a prospective single-arm study found most men still sexually active at 5 years. The radiosensitivity of neurovascular structures is poorly quantified, so the technique remains investigational.

Metabolic Imaging in Prostate Cancer

  • Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography (PET)/CT is appropriate before treatment for unfavourable intermediate-risk and high-risk intact disease (see Chapter 152). Prospective trials using 68Ga-PSMA or 18F-DCFPyL show that up to about half of these patients have their treatment recommendation changed.
  • After prostatectomy, 68Ga-PSMA, 18F-DCFPyL, 18F-fluciclovine and 18F-choline all detect microscopic disease better than conventional imaging.

Intensity-Modulated Radiation Therapy

  • IMRT intensifies dose to the prostate while reducing dose to the surrounding tissues. It evolved from 3D-CRT.
  • Evolution: static beam angles with moving shields in the linear accelerator head → inverse planning, in which the user enters the prescription, normal-tissue tolerances and structure priorities → the optimiser generates a candidate plan → clinical review.
  • IMRT produces steep dose gradients at the edge of the prostate. Fig. 162.2 — IMRT plan, 79.2 Gy (7920 cGy) in 44 fractions to the prostate, sparing most of the rectum and bladder.
  • Planning studies show IMRT spares the rectum, bladder, femoral heads and small bowel better than 3D-CRT. No randomised trial has compared dose-escalated IMRT with 3D-CRT, yet almost all prostate radiotherapy in the United States was IMRT by 2009.
  • Volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT): a subtype of IMRT that treats continuously during a 360° gantry arc. Treatment time is about halved.

Heavy Particle Beam/Proton Therapy

  • Particles in clinical use: electrons (shallow penetration → skin and superficial tumours); protons; neutrons; helium ions; heavy ions (neon, argon, carbon); negative ions. Heavy particles need large infrastructure and are hard to control.
  • Physical advantages: a higher linear energy transfer (LET), the energy deposited per unit path length → denser DNA damage and less repair → a slightly higher relative biological effectiveness (RBE) than photons.
  • Bragg peak: a sharp rise in dose, then a cut-off at the end of the particle's range. Tumours are thicker than a single peak, so the beam is modulated into a spread-out Bragg peak (SOBP).
  • Fig. 162.6 — dose against depth: photons deliver a surface dose and an exit dose; protons have a lower entrance dose, a Bragg peak and no exit dose, and the SOBP covers the tumour depth. (From Leeman et al., Lancet Oncol, 2017.)
  • Uncertainties: range uncertainty is far more sensitive to set-up, organ motion and tissue heterogeneity than with photons, and RBE varies at the edges of the SOBP. Either can underdose the target or overdose normal tissue.
  • Protons are the most available heavy particle (produced by a linear accelerator, cyclotron or synchrotron). Neutrons were historically more common, and helium and carbon ions are used in a few countries outside the United States.

Contemporary Techniques: Brachytherapy

Low-Dose-Rate Brachytherapy Technique and Trends

  • LDR BT: sources are implanted permanently and deliver dose gradually until they have fully decayed. "Low dose rate" describes the rate, not a low dose.
  • Low photon energy (~21–30 kiloelectron-volts, keV) → dose falls off within a few millimetres. This spares surrounding tissue but demands precise placement, or tumour-bearing regions are underdosed.
  • Utilisation: BT for low-risk disease rose through the 1990s (Cancer of the Prostate Strategic Urologic Research Endeavor [CaPSURE] database), then fell steeply (by about 60%). Proposed causes: competition from ultrahypofractionated EBRT, robotic prostatectomy, and urology practices owning linear accelerators. Evidence that adding BT to EBRT improves control and survival in intermediate- to high-risk disease may reverse the decline.
  • Planning: computer algorithms arrange the sources to maximise prostate ablation while limiting dose to the intraprostatic urethra and rectum (Fig. 162.7 — (A) preoperative MRI-based plan; (B) intraoperative ultrasound-based plan).
  • Peripheral (modified peripheral) loading: more seeds at the edge of the gland and fewer centrally → urethral sparing → less urinary morbidity.

Assessment of Permanent Implant Quality

  • Post-implant imaging at 14–30 days (isotope-dependent), by CT and/or MRI: seed count and arrangement are checked to confirm a tumoricidal prostate dose with minimal dose to adjacent structures (Fig. 162.8 — post-implant dosimetry: isodose lines at 50–200% of prescription and the DVH for prostate, urethra, rectum and bladder).

High-Dose-Rate Brachytherapy

  • High-dose-rate (HDR) BT: temporary catheters (hollow transperineal needles) → imaged by TRUS, CT or MRI → a single high-activity iridium-192 (192Ir) source (gamma, mean 380 keV) in a remote afterloader steps through set dwell positions → treatment over minutes → the source is withdrawn.
  • Conformality comes from varying dwell time and position. Robotic source control protects staff. Because dose is calibrated in real time and the source is removed, no post-implant dosimetry is needed.
  • Evolution: first used as a boost with EBRT in intermediate- to high-risk disease; now given as multifraction monotherapy for more favourable disease.

High-Dose-Rate Monotherapy

  • Established schedules: 27–28 Gy in 2; 31.5 Gy in 3; 34–38 Gy in 4 fractions. They give excellent biochemical control with limited toxicity.
  • Single-fraction 19 Gy fails. A prospective series gave disappointing disease-free survival and substantial biopsy-proven local failure at about 4 years. In the randomised comparison of 19 Gy × 1 vs 27 Gy in 2 (median follow-up 60 months), 5-year biochemical disease-free survival was 73.5% vs 95% and 5-year local failure 29% vs 3%. Multifraction HDR monotherapy remains the standard for low- and intermediate-risk disease.
  • The only randomised LDR vs HDR monotherapy trial used HDR 19 Gy × 1. LDR gave a deeper PSA nadir (<0.4 ng/mL) at the cost of more urinary irritative change (International Prostate Symptom Score [IPSS], EPIC). Final outcomes are pending.

Patient Selection for Brachytherapy

  • The main acute toxicity is obstructive and irritative uropathy, which leads to a temporary catheter in a small proportion of patients. This is why urinary function drives eligibility.
  • Guidelines: ABS and Groupe Européen de Curiethérapie–European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology (GEC-ESTRO) consensus guidelines for LDR and HDR.
  • Prerequisites: the patient must be fit for general anaesthesia (the standard; local anaesthesia appears only in limited reports) and able to undergo TRUS, because the technique depends on strict spatial accuracy and TRUS visualisation.

Relative contraindications to brachytherapy

  • Large TURP defect, or TURP within the previous 3–6 months → incontinence risk and technical difficulty.
  • Poor urinary function or obstruction: maximum flow rate (Qmax) <10 mL/s; IPSS >20; large median lobe.
  • Prior pelvic radiotherapy.
  • Connective tissue disease or inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Pubic arch interference: needles cannot reach the anterior or lateral prostate.
  • Lithotomy position not possible.
  • Rectal fistula.
  • Cytoreduction of a large gland or median lobe: a short course of ADT or androgen receptor blockade shrinks the gland by up to ~30%, but carries toxicity including increased cardiovascular mortality. 5α-reductase inhibitors shrink it by only ~20% but are a less morbid alternative.
  • ADT with BT: usual in high-risk disease treated with BT + EBRT; its role is unclear in unfavourable intermediate risk, or high risk with few risk factors.

Brachytherapy Combined With External Beam Radiotherapy

  • The combined regimen is 45 Gy of conventionally fractionated EBRT + BT at ~60–70% of the monotherapy dose. EBRT covers periprostatic tissue and raises the gland dose.
  • A pooled analysis of two randomised trials showed that biochemical failure rose with risk group (favourable intermediate → unfavourable intermediate → high) with no measurable benefit from adding EBRT to BT.
  • With ADT, biochemical disease-free survival after combined therapy in high-risk disease is 80–90%. HDR series with ≥5 years of follow-up show control of 83–100% in intermediate risk and 74–91% in high risk.

Role of Androgen Suppression Combined With Radiation

Background and Potential Mechanisms

  • Huggins and Hodges (1941) showed that prostate cancer is androgen-dependent. This provided the rationale for adding hormonal therapy to RT.
  • From 1967, hormonal therapy was added to RT for stage C (T3) tumours. These were often large, and cytoreductive ADT gave better geometry for irradiation and less tumour burden. Its use remained controversial through the 1970s–1980s, when early series were often negative.
  • RTOG 75-06, an early favourable report: patients with histologically unfavourable tumours given androgen suppression + EBRT had disease-free and overall survival similar to patients with favourable tumours who did not receive it.
  • Mechanisms of synergy:
    • Apoptosis: androgen-responsive cells undergo programmed cell death when deprived of androgen. Like radiation-induced death, this is p53-independent.
    • Radiosensitisation: castration → less Ku70 repair protein → impaired nonhomologous end-joining → more unrepaired double-strand breaks after RT.
    • RT upregulates androgen receptor (AR) signalling durably. Patients with a post-RT rise in AR-regulated hK2 were about threefold more likely to fail biochemically. Adjuvant ADT after RT works by blocking this AR upregulation.

  • Serum PSA is the accepted surrogate endpoint. Unlike after RP, residual benign glands keep producing some PSA after RT, but the "acceptable" level is far below age-matched normal because non-malignant acini atrophy.

Significance of Nadir Value and Doubling Time

  • Conventional-dose RT (~66–72 Gy): median nadir 0.4–0.5 ng/mL with NED, >1.0 with local failure, >2 with distant failure.
  • Nadir thresholds after EBRT: nadir >2 ng/mL at 1 year predicts biochemical failure, distant metastasis and death. >1.5 ng/mL at 2 years is an independent risk factor for distant metastasis. >0.5 ng/mL predicts all-cause mortality (unfavourable risk, 70.2 Gy ± 6 months ADT).
  • After BT: nadir <0.5 ng/mL → better long-term freedom from biochemical failure. PSA <0.2 ng/mL at 5 years → excellent 10-year control. PSA ≤0.2 ng/mL at 4 years → disease-free survival beyond 10 years in a very large multi-institutional analysis. With 125I D90 of 160 Gy, only ~10% have PSA >0.2 at 5 years (two-thirds still falling), and the proportion below 0.2 keeps rising for 7–8 years.
  • BT gives a deeper and later nadir than EBRT: mean nadir about ten-fold lower after LDR or HDR + EBRT (~0.05–0.1 ng/mL) than EBRT alone (~0.5 ng/mL). Time to nadir is about 2.5 years after BT vs 1.5 years after EBRT.
  • PSA doubling time (PSADT): <3 months → associated with prostate cancer mortality. <12 months → markedly worse disease-specific survival (RTOG 92-02 analysis; in TROG 96.01 the HR for prostate cancer death was ~23). Practical use: consider earlier intervention if PSADT <12 months, and observation is more comfortable if it is longer.
  • Caveat: PSADT is an imperfect surrogate. Its reliability depends on having many values over a wide range.

Brachytherapy and the Benign Bounce Phenomenon

  • Bounce (spike) = a rise >0.2 ng/mL followed by a durable decline. It occurs after LDR and HDR BT and also after EBRT.
  • Seen in about a third of men after LDR, and up to about two-thirds of younger men. The mean rise is ~1 ng/mL (up to 10 ng/mL); it can last 12–18 months, and double peaks occur.
  • A bounce is associated with better outcome: improved biochemical control and survival compared with no bounce.
  • A biopsy during a bounce may show residual cancer with treatment effect (indeterminate). MR spectroscopy shows a diffuse rise in choline:citrate across the gland, not the focal change expected with tumour. There is no reliable way to tell a bounce from failure in the first 3 years.
  • Management of a rising PSA after BT: within 30 months → monitor. If it approaches 10 ng/mL → systemic staging. If it persists beyond 30 months → prostate biopsy.

Biochemical Failure and Impact of Neoadjuvant Hormones

  • With neoadjuvant ADT, PSA is often already undetectable when RT starts, so a true nadir and time to nadir cannot be determined.
  • When testosterone recovers after EBRT + ADT, PSA often bounces (median ~2 years after treatment, ~0.8–0.9 ng/mL), then falls again as the RT effect continues. Many more of these bounces meet the ASTRO definition of failure than the Phoenix definition.
  • Only about a fifth of bounces occur during testosterone recovery; the rest come after testosterone has normalised. This suggests a delayed testosterone response, inflammation or instrumentation. More frequent testing detects more bounces.
  • Phoenix (nadir + 2) makes fewer false calls of failure than ASTRO (three rises).

Post–Radiation Therapy Biopsy, Timing, and Interpretation

  • PSA cannot distinguish local from systemic failure, so biopsy assesses the local effect. The problems are timing, indeterminate biopsies with marked radiation effect and sampling error.
  • Timing: radiation causes postmitotic cell death, meaning fatally damaged cells survive a few divisions before dying. Biopsies taken as early as 6 months were once called failures. Histological resolution runs parallel to the PSA nadir. The optimal biopsy time is 30–36 months, although 24-month biopsies strongly predicted 7-year disease-free survival.
  • Grade only when radiation effect is absent or minimal. Gleason grading was built on untreated surgical specimens and depends on gland architecture, which RT disrupts. Grading disintegrating glands gives a false-positive, falsely high-grade result and unnecessary salvage. ADT-treated cancer can also mimic Gleason 4 or 5.
  • Radiation atypia in benign glands can mimic cancer. High-molecular-weight cytokeratin labels the basal cell layer, which is present in benign glands and absent in cancer.
  • Radiation-effect scoring systems (Box 162.1) matter because they separate biopsies with no or minimal effect from those with marked effect. Ignoring the distinction dilutes the prognostic value of the biopsy and leads to inappropriate salvage. Proliferation markers (proliferating cell nuclear antigen, Ki-67) help judge residual tumour.
  • Prognostic value: 2-year biopsy status predicts 10-year biochemical NED, freedom from distant metastasis and disease-specific survival. Biopsies showing marked treatment effect behave as negative. Biopsy status and Gleason score are the two strongest determinants of biochemical disease-free survival.

Imaging and Sampling Error

  • TRUS alone is of limited use after RT: fibrosis changes the echogenicity of the irradiated gland.
  • MRI far surpasses CT and ultrasound for soft tissue, and mpMRI detects recurrence better than T2-weighted imaging alone. Adding MR spectroscopy improves accuracy; diffusion weighting improves it; dynamic contrast enhancement added nothing further in one study.
  • Local recurrence arises at the site of the original dominant nodule, as confirmed on whole-mount salvage specimens.
  • MRI–TRUS fusion biopsy is well established, and in-bore MRI-guided biopsy is safe and effective.

Ultrahypofractionation

  • ≥5 Gy per fraction = ultrahypofractionation (SBRT, SAbR). The evidence is mostly phase I/II series (Table 162.6) plus the phase III trials in the SBRT trials table above.
  • Pooled phase II analysis (eight institutions): 5-year biochemical relapse-free survival 95% low, 84% intermediate, 81% high risk; neither total dose nor ADT changed the outcome in any risk group.
  • Toxicity: some series show clearly more late morbidity than moderate hypofractionation (grade 2 urinary toxicity >30% in some). Rare events: a grade 4 rectal ulcer at 50 Gy/5 and a rectal fistula.
  • Dosimetric risk factors for high-grade rectal toxicity: rectal wall volume receiving 50 Gy, and >35% of the rectal circumference receiving >39 Gy.
  • Hydrogel rectal spacer at 45 Gy/5 (phase II): periprostatic rectal ulcers fell from an expected near-universal rate to a small minority, with no late grade ≥3 GI toxicity.

Outcomes for Brachytherapy

  • Post-implant biopsies from early series showed a very low rate of positive biopsies at 2 years after implants with good dosimetry. Biopsy has limits, as discussed above, so biochemical surrogates and survival are now used.
  • PSA is the most useful marker of relapse: a rising PSA predicts local and/or distant relapse years before clinical recurrence. It is hard to interpret because of variability at low levels and suppression by even short ADT. A bounce occurs in about a third of patients after BT and has been linked to better cancer control.

Comparative Efficacy of External Beam Radiation Therapy Versus Brachytherapy

  • No randomised trial has compared BT alone with EBRT alone. The evidence is retrospective.
  • Intermediate risk: 125I BT (145 Gy) gave better long-term freedom from biochemical failure than EBRT (~75 Gy), with no difference in distant metastasis, disease-specific or overall survival. BT remained independent after propensity matching, although BT patients were younger, had less aggressive disease and received less ADT.
  • cT1–T2, all risks: only EBRT <72 Gy was inferior. Otherwise the modality did not predict outcome, and RP and EBRT + BT were equivalent.
  • HDR monotherapy for low-risk disease shows acceptable toxicity and early biochemical control similar to permanent seeds.

Brachytherapy Combined With External Irradiation

  • Here BT acts as a boost to escalate dose within the prostate and periprostatic tissue. Control rates are given in the brachytherapy section above.
  • RTOG 0232 design: intermediate risk (cT1c–T2b by the 6th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer [AJCC] manual, with Gleason 2–6 and PSA ≥10 but <20, or Gleason 7 and PSA <10). BT alone (103Pd 125 Gy or 125I 145 Gy) vs partial-pelvis EBRT 45 Gy followed 2–4 weeks later by BT (103Pd 100 Gy or 125I 110 Gy). Limitations: not stratified into favourable and unfavourable intermediate risk, and ADT was at investigator discretion.
  • ASCENDE-RT design: stratified by NCCN risk group. All patients had 12 months of ADT starting 8 months before RT, then EBRT 46 Gy to the prostate, seminal vesicles and regional nodes. They were then randomised to a further 32 Gy EBRT prostate boost or a 125I implant to 115 Gy 2–3 weeks after EBRT.
  • Toxicity: several (not all) studies show more GI and GU toxicity with combined BT + EBRT than with either alone. Patients must be counselled that the control benefit is partly offset by morbidity.

Concept — BT's dose advantage shows up as better biochemical control. For low and intermediate risk, BT alone is enough. Adding EBRT improves control only where disease may extend beyond the prostate, and always at a toxicity cost.

Outcomes of Modern Technique

Treatment Morbidity and Quality-of-Life Outcomes

  • 70 Gy was long considered the maximum safe dose. Conformal RT made higher doses possible, but toxicity tracks dose to the organ. In the MD Anderson trial, 5-year grade ≥2 complications were about twice as common at 78 Gy as at 70 Gy. Rectal complication risk rose substantially when more than 25% of the rectum received 70 Gy.
  • So technology that cuts organ dose, such as IMRT, reduces toxicity.

Urinary Toxicity and Management

  • IMRT to high dose (up to 86.4 Gy): 10-year grade ≥2 urinary toxicity ~20%; grade 3 rare. Image guidance halved late grade ≥2 GU toxicity (3-year rates ~10% vs ~20%).
  • Patient-reported symptoms: dysuria, weak stream and frequency peak at about 2 months, improve by 6 months and are back to baseline by 12 months. Incontinence is uncommon after RT.
  • RTOG 94-08 (intact prostate): acute grade ≥2 / ≥3 urinary toxicity 30.8% / 3.8%; late at 5 years 18.3% / 3.3%. After prostatectomy (RTOG 96-01), acute grade ≥2 was lower (14%) and the other rates were similar.
  • After BT: IPSS rises immediately and falls at a rate set by the isotope's half-life. Chronic grade ≥2 urinary toxicity after permanent implants is ~20%. α-blockers started before implantation may reduce the severity and duration of symptoms.
  • Predictors after LDR: the length of urethra receiving high dose (grade 2–3 morbidity when about 20 ± 11 mm received >400 Gy with 125I); gland volume >60 cm³; and pretreatment IPSS. IPSS >20 carries ~29% retention risk; IPSS <10 only ~2%.
  • HDR: a similar pattern, with acute grade ≥2 urinary symptoms in 15–18% and chronic grade ≥3 in 5–10%.
  • ASCENDE-RT: 5-year grade 3 urinary toxicity 18.4% with the LDR boost vs 5.2% with the EBRT boost (P < 0.001). This is the price of the halved biochemical failure.
  • Incontinence after TURP: higher risk after BT with a previous TURP, and higher still after multiple TURPs. Without urethral trauma it stays at ~3%.

Rectal Toxicity and Management

  • Acute rectal symptoms are self-limiting. Late radiation proctitis with bleeding usually appears 6–18 months after treatment.
  • Management ladder: steroid suppositories or sucralfate enemas (for minor, self-limited cases) → argon plasma coagulation (APC) or hyperbaric oxygen → diverting colostomy in the worst cases. APC controls bleeding in most patients but can cause ulceration and, rarely, colovesical fistula.
  • IMRT to 81 Gy: 10-year grade ≥2 GI toxicity ~5%, grade 3 <1%. Low with or without fiducial-based image guidance.
  • Patient-reported bowel symptoms: urgency and frequency peak at 2 months and partly recover.
  • ProtecT: RT (3D-CRT) caused more bowel bother than active monitoring or RP, worst at 6 months and improving thereafter, with a residual increase in bloody stools at 2–6 years. No difference in loose stools or faecal incontinence.
  • BT: significant rectal complications in 1–2% and bleeding in ~1%, lower than after EBRT. Recto-urethral fistula is rare but feared; a colostomy rate of ~0.3% was found in Medicare claims.
  • Rectal dose limits for BT: complications are rare when the rectal wall receives ≤85% of the prescription. For BT alone, rectal V100 <1.0 cm³ on day-1 dosimetry or <1.3 cm³ on day-30 dosimetry (ABS). For BT + EBRT, bleeding risk rises with rectal V100 >0.5 cm³ and EBRT rectal V30 >35%.

Rectal-Sparing Strategies

  • Options: precise MRI delineation of the anorectum; endorectal balloon; a saline-filled balloon between prostate and rectum; and, most widely reported, a transperineal spacer (biodegradable hydrogel or hyaluronic acid) placed in Denonvilliers' fascia to separate the prostate from the anterior rectal wall (Fig. 162.9 — axial T2 MRI showing hydrogel between the prostate/seminal vesicles and the rectum).
  • Safe with LDR, HDR, EBRT, SBRT and combined therapy. It can be placed before treatment or after suboptimal dosimetry from a permanent implant.
  • Hydrogel randomised trial (IG-IMRT 79.2 Gy): late rectal toxicity 2.0% vs 7.0% (P = 0.04).
  • Hyaluronic acid phase III trial (60 Gy/20; 2:1; spacer + fiducials vs fiducials alone): grade ≥2 rectal toxicity 2.9% vs 13.8%.

Erectile Dysfunction and Management

  • The structure responsible for radiation-associated erectile dysfunction (ED) is unknown. The mechanism is predominantly vascular, and penile bulb dose may correlate.
  • Poor erections rise over 24 months after RT, mostly in patients who also received ADT; the decline is modest with RT alone. After SBRT, about half of potent men remain potent at 5 years. Predictors: baseline health-related quality of life and age at 2 years, then quality of life and body mass index at 5 years.
  • Potency preservation by modality, best to worst: BT alone (~76% at 5 years) > EBRT + BT > EBRT + BT + ADT (~52–58%). BT alone also beats prostatectomy and EBRT. Adding EBRT and diabetes mellitus (multivariate) and age (univariate) predict loss of potency.
  • Phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (PDE5Is) are effective in about three-quarters of men who use them. After SBRT, sexual aids still worked at 5 years in most men who had used them before RT, and in most who had not.
  • Men treated with RT were less likely than prostatectomy patients to try non-PDE5I aids (urethral alprostadil [MUSE], injections, vacuum device). It is unclear whether this reflects referral practice or less dysfunction.
Key points: treatment morbidity and quality-of-life outcomes
  • Rectal bleeding mostly mild; spacers reduce proctitis; incontinence uncommon; PDE5Is usually effective.

Concept — Toxicity depends on the dose each organ receives, not the dose to the prostate. Organs outside the target (rectum) benefit from IMRT, IGRT and spacers. The organ inside it (urethra) does not, so GU toxicity is the persistent limit. ED is vascular and driven by ADT, and BT alone preserves potency best.
