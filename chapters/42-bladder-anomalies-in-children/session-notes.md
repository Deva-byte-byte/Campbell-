### CONGENITAL BLADDER ANOMALIES

#### Chapter opening

- **ICD-10-CM (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification)** classifies birth defects as **structural or functional**; the bladder is affected across the whole spectrum.
- **Structural anomalies:** bladder exstrophy, duplication, agenesis, urachal anomalies, diverticular disease.
- **Functional anomalies are usually secondary** — to bladder outlet obstruction or neurogenic dysfunction.
- **Some are both** — e.g. the bladder of **prune-belly syndrome (PBS)**.
- Because many congenital anomalies are **detected prenatally**, embryology and normal/abnormal fetal imaging are the frame for interpreting them; the chapter concentrates on the **less common congenital and rare acquired** paediatric bladder conditions (the common entities are covered elsewhere in the text).

#### Embryology of the Urinary Bladder

- **Definitive nephrons begin urine production at ~10 weeks** → the earliest point at which lower urinary tract anomalies can become evident.
- Developmental sequence: **weeks 4–6: urorectal septum divides the endodermal cloaca** → ventral **urogenital sinus (UGS)** + dorsal rectum → **cranial UGS (continuous with the allantois) → bladder + pelvic urethra**; **caudal UGS → phallic urethra (male) / distal vagina (female)**.
- **Male vs female urethra:** the **entire female urethra derives from the pelvic UGS**; the male urethra has pelvic and phallic parts.
- **Allantois:** extra-embryonic cavity from the yolk sac, joined to the cranioventral cloaca (the future bladder).
- Urachal formation: **months 4–5: allantoic duct and ventral cloaca involute as the bladder descends into the pelvis** → the duct, not growing with the embryo, **elongates and narrows** → obliterates into a thick fibrous cord, the **urachus** → becomes the **median umbilical ligament**, joining bladder apex to umbilicus.

*Concept —* **Urachal obliteration is driven by bladder descent**: every urachal anomaly is simply a failure of this epithelialised tube to close at some point between bladder apex and umbilicus.

#### Normal and Abnormal Antenatal Sonographic Findings of the Bladder

- **Normal is defined by exclusion** — the absence of abnormal findings; physiological variation and **bladder cycling** must be accounted for before an abnormality is called.
- **Appearance:** elliptical, anechoic, fluid-filled pelvic structure. **Borders: laterally the umbilical arteries; anteriorly the pubic bones; posteriorly the rectosigmoid.**
- **Wall thickness ≤3 mm**; mucosa and muscle of similar echogenicity to other pelvic structures.
- **Visibility tracks urine production:** seen in about half of fetuses at 10 weeks → rising through weeks 11–12 → **virtually always by 13 weeks**.
- **Transvaginal > transabdominal ultrasound** for image quality and first-trimester detection.
- **Fetal bladder empties every 15–20 minutes** → **non-visualisation mandates a second scan in the same session**.
- **First-trimester bladder diameter should not exceed 6–8 mm.**
- Three adjuncts shape interpretation of an abnormal bladder:
  - **Fetal sex:** unreliable before 14 weeks; judged by **visualising the testes, not the presence or absence of a phallus**.
  - **Amniotic fluid:** until **16 weeks' gestational age (WGA)** it is mainly **placental transudate**; thereafter **predominantly fetal urine** → liquor volume becomes a surrogate for fetal urine output.
  - **Umbilical cord:** **two arteries, one vein**, and **no fluid-filled urachus**.

*Concept —* **The fetal bladder is interpreted as a triad — bladder, kidneys, liquor — because it is designed to fill and empty**: a single image means nothing until cycling has been observed and liquor and renal development are known.

##### Nondilated or Absent Bladder

- A non-visualised bladder is one of three things: **absent, malformed, or not filling** (from **renal dysfunction** or **ectopic drainage** of urine).
- **Renal development and amniotic fluid volume** point to the cause.
- **Repeat after 15–20 minutes** before diagnosing an absent bladder — the fetus may simply have voided.

*Bladder agenesis.*
- **Pathogenesis unclear:** the hindgut is usually normal, so cloacal division is normal → the defect is either **atrophy of the cranial UGS** or **failure to incorporate the mesonephric ducts and ureters into the trigone**.
- **Associations:** neurological, orthopaedic and urogenital anomalies — renal dysplasia or agenesis; absent prostate, seminal vesicles, penis or vagina.
- **Incidence ~1 in 600,000**; very few reported cases; **significant majority female**.
- **Compatible with life only if the ureters drain ectopically** — into normally developed **Müllerian structures (female)** or the **rectum (male)**.
- **Diagnosis in survivors:** antegrade or retrograde contrast imaging; **MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) or CT (computed tomography)** for anatomic detail before surgery.
- **Treatment:** **ureterosigmoidostomy or external stoma** preserves renal function.

*Bladder hypoplasia.*
- The bladder **forms and is visible throughout pregnancy but never reaches adequate capacity** because it is inadequately filled or cannot store.

| Mechanism of under-filling | Example |
|---|---|
| Inadequate outlet resistance | Severe epispadias |
| Separation defect | Urogenital sinus anomaly |
| Abnormal renal development | Bilateral renal dysplasia or agenesis |
| Urine bypassing the bladder | Ureteral ectopia |

- **Some bladders grow once the malformation is corrected**, but **later augmentation is often required** to reach adequate capacity.

*Cloacal and bladder exstrophy.*
- Only a **bladder template** exists → suspected when **regular bladder filling is absent** on fetal ultrasound.
- **Exstrophy is distinguished from agenesis** by the **bladder template on the lower abdominal wall** and **normal amniotic fluid throughout pregnancy**.
- **Covered cloacal exstrophy** presents uniquely: **omphalocele + pelvic diastasis + genital anomalies *with* bladder filling** — the bladder may become exstrophic only close to birth.
- Despite a recognisable pattern, a **significant number are still missed prenatally**, although detection is improving; **fetal MRI** adds anatomic detail for fuller prenatal counselling.

##### Dilated Bladder and Congenital Megacystis

*Megacystis — how the term evolved.*
- **Historical view:** **bladder neck obstruction → massive bilateral vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) + thin bladder wall**; yet bladder neck surgery never changed outcome.
- **Revision:** re-examination showed **normal urethras and complete emptying on voiding cystourethrography (VCUG)** → **reflux is the cause, not the consequence**: the bladder dilates from **continuous recycling of urine between the upper tracts and the bladder**. This mechanism still applies to a subset of children with VUR.
- **Modern usage:** **megacystis = a prenatally dilated bladder**, with no cause implied.
- **Megacystis, like hydronephrosis, is a finding, not a diagnosis** — and harder to qualify, because the bladder is *designed* to be distended at times.
- Megacystis without an objective definition is **rare — well under 1 in 1000 fetuses** in a large retrospective series.

**Definitions of megacystis by gestation**

| Gestation | Method | Normal | Abnormal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **10–14 WGA** | Sagittal bladder length as a ratio of **crown–rump length (CRL)** | **<10% of CRL (≈ ≤6 mm)** | **≥13% of CRL and ≥8 mm** |
| First trimester (other series) | Longitudinal bladder length | — | **>6 mm** or **≥7 mm** |
| Second/third trimester (subjective) | Bladder cycling | Empties during observation | **Fails to empty during 45 minutes of observation** ("enlarged bladder") |
| **15–40 WGA** (formula from control fetuses) | **Sagittal length (SL, mm) = GA (weeks) − 5 (95% CI ±7)** | ≤ GA + 2 | **Enlarged: > GA + 2** (above the 95% CI); **megacystis: > GA + 12** (10 mm above the 95% CI) |

- First-trimester criteria differ only at the margin (**>6, ≥7 or ≥8 mm**); the 6–8 mm band is the grey zone, consistent with the "should not exceed 6–8 mm" ceiling above.
- **Beyond the first trimester definitions are imprecise and mostly subjective**; later-gestation studies define the bladder as **part of a disease pattern** (posterior urethral valves [PUV], PBS) rather than by size, and **cycling becomes the marker of normal function**.
- The formula's split into "enlarged" versus "megacystis" is intended to allow **more specific prenatal counselling**.

*Differential diagnosis.*
- **Obstructive — lower urinary tract obstruction (LUTO):** **PUV, urethral atresia, urethral stenosis / anterior urethral valves**.
- **Nonobstructive:** **PBS, megacystis–microcolon–intestinal hypoperistalsis syndrome (MMIHS), cloacal anomalies** — with recognised crossover between some of them.

**TABLE 42.1 Definitive Diagnoses in Fetuses With Megacystis** (From Taghavi K, Sharpe C, Stringer MD: Fetal megacystis: a systematic review. *J Pediatr Urol* 2017; pooled from 16 series, 1985–2014, >1000 fetuses)

| Definitive diagnosis | Mechanism | Pooled proportion |
|---|---|---|
| **Posterior urethral valves** | Obstructive (LUTO) | **57% — commonest** |
| Urethral atresia / stenosis | Obstructive (LUTO) | 7% |
| Prune-belly syndrome | Nonobstructive | 4% |
| MMIHS | Nonobstructive | 1% |
| Cloacal abnormality | Nonobstructive | 0.7% |
| **Undefined** | — | **36.5% (more than one-third)** |

- **Denominator:** PUV and urethral atresia/stenosis are calculated over **998**, the rest over **1067**, because one series reported LUTO without specifying which lesion.
- **What the table is for:** the pre-test probabilities behind prenatal counselling.
- **Pattern:** **PUV dominates** → megacystis shows an **8:1 male predominance**.
- **Traps:** **more than a third have no cause found**; and **megacystis in a female fetus should not be assumed to be LUTO** — the nonobstructive, female-predominant causes (MMIHS) become relatively important.

*Prognosis — three independent axes.*
- **Karyotype:** about **one in five** megacystis fetuses is **aneuploid**, mostly **trisomy 13 and 18** (around four-fifths of the abnormal karyotypes), well above the rate expected from maternal age and nuchal translucency; nearly all such pregnancies are terminated or the fetus dies before term → **urological outcome data in aneuploid megacystis are sparse**.
- **Size (euploid, first trimester):**

| Longitudinal bladder diameter | Outcome |
|---|---|
| **7–15 mm** | **Most resolve spontaneously**; the remainder prove to have obstructive uropathy |
| **>15 mm** | **None resolve; all have obstructive uropathy** |

- **Timing (national Dutch fetal-medicine database):** early megacystis = **<18 WGA**; late = **>18 WGA**; early resolution = **<23 WGA**.

| | Early presentation (<18 WGA) | Late presentation (>18 WGA) |
|---|---|---|
| Spontaneous resolution | Roughly three times as frequent as late | Uncommon |
| Size predictor | **≤12 mm maximal longitudinal diameter → most resolve in utero** | — |
| Postnatal course | **Early presentation + resolution before 23 WGA → no postnatal urological surgery** | **Late presentation + resolution after 23 WGA → most still need postnatal urological intervention** despite prenatal resolution |

- **Overall only a minority resolve**; high termination rates make the evaluable cohort selective.

*Concept —* **Megacystis prognosis runs on three independent axes — karyotype, size and timing.** The favourable end of each (euploid, ≤12–15 mm, early onset with early resolution) predicts transient physiology; the other end predicts obstruction, and **late prenatal resolution is not reassurance**.

*Megacystis–microcolon–intestinal hypoperistalsis syndrome (MMIHS).*
- **~1% of megacystis**, but important because of its **morbidity**.
- **Prenatal:** **megacystis is the most consistent sign**; **normal-to-increased amniotic fluid** (unlike LUTO); GI (gastrointestinal) signs usually not visible; **amniotic digestive enzymes are abnormal in most cases** if sampled.
- **Pathology:** **smooth-muscle dysfunction of both GI and urinary tracts**; **ACTG2 mutations** (smooth-muscle actin filament).
- **Female predominance (~70%)**. **Survival:** historically **~1 in 5**, improving to **more than half (~55%) in the 2004–2011 era**.
- **Postnatal:** abdominal distension, **bilious vomiting**, failure to pass meconium. **Bowel obstruction + enlarged bladder → MMIHS until proven otherwise.**
- Differentiation: **colon biopsy showing ganglion cells excludes Hirschsprung disease**; contrast shows **hypomotile small bowel + small colon (microcolon)**.
- **Enteric management:** ileostomy, gastrostomy, **total parenteral nutrition (TPN)**. **TPN complications (blood-borne infection, liver dysfunction) are leading causes of death**; **small-intestinal transplantation** is promising.
- **Urological management:** urological morbidity is often secondary to enteric complications → **short-term clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC) or vesicostomy** for retention → **VUR is uncommon, so early VCUG is of limited use** → once enterically stable, **urodynamics: acontractile bladder expected** → normal voiding reported in some older patients, but **most need lifelong CIC** → **lifetime urological care**, as for PUV or neurogenic bladder.

**LUTO vs MMIHS — the prenatal contrast**

| | LUTO (e.g. PUV) | MMIHS |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Outlet obstruction | Visceral myopathy (acontractile bladder) |
| Sex | **Male (8:1 overall megacystis)** | **Female (~70%)** |
| Amniotic fluid | **Oligohydramnios** if persistent | **Normal to increased** |
| VUR | Common in valves | **Uncommon** |
| Lethal problem | Renal/pulmonary | **Gut and TPN** |

*Concept —* **MMIHS is a pan-visceral myopathy**: the bladder is large because it cannot contract, not because it is obstructed — so liquor is preserved, reflux is rare, the bladder needs catheterising rather than unobstructing, and the fatal organ is the gut.

#### Bladder Duplication

- Complete or incomplete, with variable orientation. **Abrahamson classification (1961): complete duplication in the sagittal plane is commonest.**

| | Incomplete duplication | Complete duplication |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | **Two halves communicate** | **Fully separate bladders**, each with normal mucosa and full-thickness muscle, **divided by a peritoneal fold** |
| Drainage | **Single urethra** | **Own ureter + own urethra and meatus** each (sizes may differ) |
| Surgery | **May need none** if both halves drain adequately through the common urethra | Bladders **combined into one** |

- **Rarely one bladder has no urethra** → complete obstruction → **ipsilateral renal dysplasia**. Continence is variable.
- **Associations:** **external genital duplication in up to 90%**; **lower GI duplication in up to ~40%**; duplicated vaginas may connect to a separate unicornuate uterus; **each duplicated penis has its own urethra**; **VUR, renal ectopia, dysplasia** common.
- **Nonurological anomalies are more frequent in sagittal than coronal duplication:** GI malformations, spinal duplication, spina bifida, urogenital–GI fistulae.
- **Exstrophy variant:** an exstrophic bladder and urethra **plus** a closed normal intra-abdominal bladder.
- **Presentation:** newborn period (associated GI or genital malformation) or later, when **recurrent infection or incontinence** prompts work-up.
- **Work-up:** karyotype, ultrasound, **videourodynamics**, genitogram, cross-sectional imaging; VCUG and renal scintigraphy for VUR and function; the final plan is **often deferred to endoscopic and surgical exploration**.
- **Management priorities in sequence:** **renal preservation + infection prevention by relieving obstruction** → **continence** → **reconstruction of internal and external genitalia**.
  - Complete duplication: **both sphincters competent → join the distal urethras**; **one incompetent → close that bladder neck and excise its urethra**.
  - Duplicated vaginas → combined in the midline + vulvoplasty.
  - **Asymptomatic → may be left uncorrected** (successful pregnancies reported).
  - **Rarity and variety → individualised surgery in centres experienced in complex urogenital reconstruction.**

*Concept —* **Duplication has no standard operation — only a standard order of priorities**: kidneys and infection first, continence second, genital reconstruction last, each decided on the individual anatomy found at exploration.

#### Congenital Diverticula

- **Mechanism:** **herniation of bladder mucosa through a defect between smooth-muscle fibres**; **neck size = size of the muscular defect**.
- **Incidence low** (under 2% of imaged symptomatic children); **true incidence higher**, since asymptomatic diverticula go undetected.
- **Detection:** prenatal ultrasound possible; mostly found postnatally during work-up for **infection, haematuria, incontinence or obstruction**.
- **Gold standard: VCUG** — shows **accompanying VUR** and **post-void retention**. Ultrasound shows large diverticula; **renal scintigraphy** for anatomy, function and ureteral obstruction if hydroureteronephrosis.
- **Diverticula are dynamic** — may not appear on every study → **repeat imaging if clinical suspicion persists**.

*Paraureteral diverticula — by far the commonest congenital type.* **Hutch (1961)** divided them into primary and secondary:

| | **Primary (congenital)** | **Secondary (acquired)** |
|---|---|---|
| Bladder wall | **Smooth** | **Trabeculated** |
| Number | **Isolated** | **One of many** |
| On imaging | **May be intermittent** | **Always present** |
| Infravesical obstruction | **Absent** | **Present** (or neurogenic bladder) — the cause |

- **Primary:** localised mucosal herniation **through the ureteral hiatus, between the intravesical ureter and the roof of the hiatus** → most likely a **congenitally deficient bladder wall**. An isolated **Waldeyer sheath** defect has been proposed, but **unilaterality argues against it**.
- **Management:**
  - **Small, asymptomatic, incidental → conservative**.
  - **With VUR → many surgeons excise**.
  - **Females resolve spontaneously (diverticulum with VUR) more often than males** — attributed to **higher male voiding pressures** even without obstruction.
  - **A primary paraureteral diverticulum does not preclude endoscopic subureteric dextranomer/hyaluronic acid (Deflux) injection** for VUR.

*Posterolateral and multiple congenital diverticula.*
- Typical of **generalised connective-tissue disease — Ehlers–Danlos, Williams, Menkes syndromes** — occasionally non-syndromic.
- Can be **massive, often larger than the bladder itself**.
- **Histology:** not classic herniation through a defect but an **attenuated muscle wall** → laxity **without obstruction**.
- **Presentation:** **recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI)** from stasis and poor emptying; **stones**.
- **Management:** **asymptomatic → close observation** (especially if syndromic) → symptomatic → **open or laparoscopic resection** (eliminates recurrent UTI, often improves spontaneous voiding) → **recurrence or new diverticula common in syndromic children**, so weigh surgery against quality of life → alternatives: **vesicostomy or suprapubic drainage** → **postoperative urodynamics** to detect obstruction or high-pressure voiding that could hasten recurrence.

*Concept —* **The wall determines the diverticulum**: a focal defect (primary paraureteral), a globally weak wall (connective-tissue disease) or pressure from below (secondary). Recurrence follows the wall, which is why syndromic diverticula recur after excision and why the outlet must be checked afterwards.

> **KEY POINTS: CONGENITAL BLADDER ANOMALIES**
> - **Persistent megacystis with oligohydramnios → LUTO**; megacystis that **resolves before delivery may leave no postnatal sequelae**.
> - **MMIHS may show only megacystis prenatally.**
> - **Nondilated anomalies** (e.g. bladder exstrophy) are the **most severe** congenital urological malformations; **amniotic fluid is usually normal**.
> - **Bladder agenesis is compatible with life only with ectopic ureteric drainage.**
> - **Connective-tissue disorders → multiple diverticula** that may need treatment for stasis-related infection or stones.

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 1

**Key facts, figures and cut-offs**
- **Urine production from ~10 weeks**; fetal bladder seen in about half at 10 weeks, **virtually all by 13 weeks**.
- **Cloacal division weeks 4–6**; **bladder descent / urachal obliteration months 4–5**.
- **Fetal bladder wall ≤3 mm**; **first-trimester diameter ≤6–8 mm**.
- **Bladder cycles every 15–20 min** → repeat scan in same session.
- **Fetal sex unreliable <14 weeks** (use testes, not phallus).
- **Liquor = predominantly fetal urine after 16 WGA.**
- **Cord: 2 arteries, 1 vein**, no fluid-filled urachus.
- **First-trimester megacystis (10–14 WGA): normal ≤6 mm (<10% CRL); abnormal ≥8 mm (≥13% CRL)**; other series >6 or ≥7 mm.
- **Later-gestation "enlarged bladder": no emptying in 45 min.**
- **Formula (15–40 WGA): SL = GA − 5 (±7) mm; enlarged > GA + 2; megacystis > GA + 12.**
- **Megacystis causes (Table 42.1): PUV 57%, urethral atresia/stenosis 7%, PBS 4%, MMIHS 1%, cloacal 0.7%, undefined 36.5%; male:female 8:1.**
- **Aneuploidy ~1 in 5 megacystis — trisomy 13 and 18.**
- **7–15 mm → most resolve; >15 mm → none resolve, all obstructive.**
- **Early <18 WGA; early resolution <23 WGA; ≤12 mm early → most resolve.**
- **Bladder agenesis ~1 in 600,000, mostly female.**
- **MMIHS ~70% female; survival ~20% historically → ~55% (2004–2011).**
- **Duplication: genital duplication up to 90%, lower GI up to ~40%.**

**Gold standards, commonest, first-line**
- **Commonest cause of megacystis: PUV.**
- **Commonest duplication: complete, sagittal** (Abrahamson).
- **Commonest congenital diverticulum: paraureteral.**
- **Gold standard for diverticula: VCUG.**
- **Posterolateral diverticula — commonest presentation: recurrent UTI.**
- **MMIHS — most consistent prenatal sign: megacystis; expected urodynamic finding: acontractile bladder.**

**Eponyms**
- **Hutch (1961)** — primary vs secondary paraureteral diverticula.
- **Abrahamson (1961)** — duplication classification; complete sagittal commonest.
- **Waldeyer sheath** — proposed defect in primary paraureteral diverticulum; unilaterality argues against.
- **Ehlers–Danlos, Williams, Menkes** — connective-tissue syndromes with multiple/posterolateral diverticula.
- **Hirschsprung disease** — excluded in MMIHS by ganglion cells on colon biopsy.
- **Deflux (dextranomer/hyaluronic acid)** — usable despite a primary paraureteral diverticulum.

**When to operate / when not to**
- **Bladder agenesis survivor → ureterosigmoidostomy or stoma.**
- **Hypoplastic bladder → correct the cause; augmentation often later.**
- **Duplication → first relieve obstruction for renal preservation**; complete → combine bladders; incompetent side → close bladder neck, excise urethra.
- **Not:** incomplete duplication draining well; asymptomatic duplication.
- **Paraureteral diverticulum with VUR → often excised; small asymptomatic → observe.**
- **Syndromic diverticula: asymptomatic → observe; symptomatic → resect**, weighing recurrence; diversion as alternative.
- **Bladder neck surgery does not help megacystis** (historical lesson).

**Contraindications**
- None stated in this section of the source.

**Classic traps**
- **Non-visualised bladder on one scan ≠ agenesis** — repeat after 15–20 min.
- **Exstrophy vs agenesis:** exstrophy has a template on the abdominal wall and normal liquor.
- **Covered cloacal exstrophy may show a filling bladder.**
- **Megacystis is a finding, not a diagnosis**; >⅓ never get a cause.
- **Reflux can cause megacystis** (recycling), not only result from obstruction.
- **Normal or increased liquor + megacystis (especially female) → MMIHS, not LUTO.**
- **MMIHS: VUR uncommon → early VCUG unhelpful.**
- **Late-onset megacystis that resolves after 23 WGA still usually needs postnatal intervention.**
- **Female urethra is entirely from the pelvic UGS.**
- **Primary paraureteral diverticulum does not rule out Deflux.**
- **Diverticula are dynamic — a normal VCUG does not exclude one.**

### CONGENITAL BLADDER ANOMALIES (continued)

#### Urachal Anomalies

- **Symptomatic remnant → complete excision with a bladder cuff**, open or laparoscopic; simple operation, low morbidity.
- **The asymptomatic incidental remnant is the dilemma:** **urachal adenocarcinoma has not been reported in a child**, but in adults it carries **high mortality** and needs far more complex excision.

**Asymptomatic urachal remnant — two positions**

| | Excise all | Excise only if symptomatic |
|---|---|---|
| Rationale | Pre-empt adult malignant progression | Progression risk, once quantified, is low |
| Status | Earlier position | **Increasingly adopted over time** |

- In practice, **discuss excision of an incidental remnant with the family** (Key Points).

**The four urachal anomalies (Fig. 42.6)**

| Anomaly | Where the lumen persists | Typical presentation | Diagnosis | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Patent urachus** | **Whole length**, bladder to umbilicus | **Neonatal umbilical urine drainage** | Longitudinal US; retrograde fistulogram or VCUG | Complete excision with bladder cuff |
| **Umbilical–urachal sinus** | **Umbilical end only** (closed at bladder) | Continuously draining umbilical sinus | **Sinugram** (no bladder connection) | Complete excision |
| **Urachal cyst** | **Isolated segment** — no communication with either end | Infection: umbilical abscess, pain, mass; **more often in adults** | **US: cyst between anterior abdominal wall and peritoneum**; CT/MRI if extensive | Drain if infected → complete excision |
| **Vesicourachal diverticulum** | **Bladder apex only** | **Usually asymptomatic, incidental** | Incidental imaging | Only if symptomatic (narrow neck: stones, UTI) |

*Patent urachus.*
- **Cause:** non-descent of the bladder or, **more commonly, failure of the epithelial canal to obliterate**.
- **Fetal obstruction theory doubted:** patency is **often absent in severely obstructed bladders**, and **only a minority** of patent urachus cases have postnatally confirmed in-utero obstruction → **obliteration may be independent of bladder distension**. **Retubularisation, rather than primary patency**, may explain umbilical drainage — supported by umbilical urinary fistulae in **acquired obstruction later in life**.
- **Presentation:** **continuous or intermittent umbilical fluid drainage** in the neonate; enlarged or **oedematous umbilicus**; **delayed healing of the cord stump**.
- **Organisms:** ***Staphylococcus aureus***, ***Escherichia coli***, *Enterococcus*, *Citrobacter*, rarely *Proteus*.
- **Diagnosis:** **fluid-filled canal on longitudinal ultrasound** or **contrast filling on retrograde fistulogram or VCUG** (Fig. 42.7); MRI helps but depends on bladder filling.
- **Differential: patent omphalomesenteric duct** (both together is rare).
- **Infected / abscess:** **drainage under antibiotic cover → once settled, complete excision including a bladder cuff**. **Remove all anomalous tissue** — prevents recurrence, stones and the rare later adenocarcinoma.
- **Open excision sequence:** balloon catheter distends bladder → transverse or midline infraumbilical incision (in infants a **small transverse subumbilical incision** suffices because the **bladder dome is still high**) → feeding tube in the urachus for identification → **umbilical attachment incised circumferentially, umbilicus preserved** for cosmesis → rectus fascia opened longitudinally, muscles split → distended dome identified → urachus isolated on a vessel loop → **extraperitoneal dissection to the umbilicus** → umbilical portion delivered and removed → **bladder cuff with urachal insertion excised with electrocautery** → **two-layer watertight bladder closure** → catheter out next morning.
- **Laparoscopic excision:** feasible **even under 6 months**; safe at all ages. Port arrangements described:
  - **Camera in the midline between umbilicus and xiphoid** + working ports in both upper quadrants; or
  - **Camera in the left lower abdomen** + 2-mm working ports in the left lower and upper abdomen, for a better view of the **umbilical end** of the urachus.
  - Reported series: no complications, no recurrences, short operating time.

**Open vs laparoscopic urachal excision**

| | Open (extraperitoneal) | Laparoscopic (transperitoneal) |
|---|---|---|
| Access | Infraumbilical; **stays extraperitoneal** | **Intra-abdominal** |
| Strength | No peritoneal contamination | **Good view of the whole urachal course and bladder dome** |
| Risk | Larger wound | **Spillage of infected or malignant material into the peritoneum; intraperitoneal urinary leak** |

- **Robotic-assisted and single-port laparoscopic** removal have also been proposed.

*Urachal sinus (umbilical–urachal sinus).*
- **Obliterated at the bladder, open at the umbilicus** → continuously draining sinus; presents like a patent urachus.
- **Diagnosis: sinugram** — caudal urachus filled with desquamated epithelium; **no bladder connection**.
- **Differential: persistent omphalomesenteric duct** — a **Meckel diverticulum connected to the umbilicus**; **indistinguishable on sinugram** (neither shows a bladder or bowel connection).
- **Both need complete excision of all tissue**; the distinction is **histological — gastric or small-bowel mucosa = omphalomesenteric remnant**.

*Urachal cyst.*
- **No communication with bladder or umbilicus**, yet may **drain intermittently** through either.
- Commoner in the **distal (lower, juxtavesical) urachus**; **presents more often in adults than in children**.
- Contents: desquamated epithelium; **infection — *S. aureus* commonest**.
- **Infected cyst:** umbilical abscess or bladder infection; localised lower abdominal pain, voiding symptoms, **painful palpable mass**.
- **Diagnosis: ultrasound — cyst between anterior abdominal wall and peritoneum**; CT or MRI if infection is extensive.
- **Unrecognised → perforation into bladder or peritoneum** → **peritonitis, enteric fistula**.
- **Treatment: drain → complete excision** of urachal remnant.

*Vesicourachal (urachal) diverticulum.*
- Urachus **obliterates except at the bladder apex** → diverticulum of variable size.
- **Usually asymptomatic**, found incidentally.
- May enlarge with outlet obstruction but **rarely troublesome because of a wide neck that drains well**.
- **Narrow neck → stones and UTI → intervention**.

*Concept —* **The level at which the urachus stays open dictates the presentation**: open throughout drains urine, open at the umbilicus drains debris, closed at both ends forms an infected cyst, open at the bladder is a silent diverticulum. **Treatment is uniform: drain infection first, then excise completely with a bladder cuff.**

> **KEY POINTS: URACHAL ANOMALIES**
> - **Usually detected postnatally because of umbilical drainage.**
> - **Infected remnants: antibiotics, then surgical excision.**
> - **A urachal remnant can be confused with a persistent omphalomesenteric duct** on work-up.
> - **Excision of an asymptomatic incidental remnant should be discussed with the family**, because of potential malignant transformation in adulthood.

### ACQUIRED BLADDER ANOMALIES IN CHILDREN

#### Noninflammatory Acquired Conditions

##### Acquired Diverticula

- **Caused by infravesical obstruction, which is treated first** → once outlet resistance is normal, **bladder remodelling may make diverticulectomy unnecessary**.
- **Symptomatic → excise**, with **ipsilateral ureteral reimplantation if the ureter is near or within the diverticulum**; intravesical or extravesical, open or laparoscopic/robotic.
- **Secondary paraureteral diverticula (Hutch):** from **infravesical obstruction or neurogenic bladder** → raised pressure forces mucosa between muscle fibres → usually **one of many "pop-off" diverticula** across the bladder. Other causes: **muscle weakened by infection**, **muscle defect after bladder surgery**.
- **Causal chain in both paraureteral types:** diverticulum expands → **Waldeyer sheath damaged** → intravesical ureter pulled out of its anchorage → **ureterovesical junction dysfunction (VUR)** → eventually **ureteral obstruction** → even **renal dysplasia**.
- **Very large paraureteral or posterolateral diverticula** compress the **bladder neck or posterior urethra** → **vicious circle**: outlet obstruction → diverticulum fills and expands → worse obstruction → **complete urinary retention** (also seen in the congenital diverticular syndromes) → surgery.

*Concept —* **An acquired diverticulum is a symptom of outlet pressure — treat the pressure, not the pouch.** Once large enough, the diverticulum becomes an obstruction itself, first of the ureter and then of the outlet, and the causal chain reverses.

##### Bladder Haemangiomas

- Benign vascular tumours, mostly with **Klippel–Trenaunay or Sturge–Weber syndrome**.
- Typically **solitary and <3 cm**, but may be multifocal.
- Present with **gross haematuria**, with or without irritative voiding symptoms.

| Lesion | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Small** | **Biopsy + fulguration** or **neodymium:yttrium–aluminium–garnet (Nd:YAG) laser** |
| **Large, or risk of rapid blood loss** | **Partial cystectomy** |

##### Fibroepithelial Polyps

- **Rare in the bladder**; cause **haematuria and irritative voiding symptoms**; reported bladder outlet obstruction, ureteral obstruction, bladder irritation.
- **Second commonest paediatric bladder tumour after rhabdomyosarcoma** (two-decade single-centre pathology review).
- **Simple endoscopic resection is typically curative.**

#### Inflammatory Acquired Conditions

**Inflammatory and reactive bladder lesions in children — comparison**

| Condition | Sex | Cause / setting | Diagnosis | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nephrogenic adenoma** | **Female 5:1** | **Reaction to infection, stones, trauma, surgery; augmented bladders** | **Cystoscopy + biopsy** | **Transurethral resection or fulguration**; **cystoscopic surveillance (~80% recur)** |
| **Cystitis cystica** | **Almost exclusively female** | Chronic inflammation → fluid-filled cysts; **mimics tumour** | Cystoscopic appearance | **Long-term antibiotic prophylaxis** |
| **Cystitis glandularis** | — | **Metaplasia of mucus-secreting epithelium**; rarer | Biopsy | Premalignant potential **unproven** |
| **Eosinophilic cystitis** | **Male predominance** | Unclear; immunological / allergic suggested | **Biopsy essential — no typical appearance** | **TUR + corticosteroids, antihistamines, antibiotics**; observe in neonates |
| **Haemorrhagic cystitis** | — | **Cyclophosphamide / ifosfamide**, pelvic radiation; worsened by **BK virus, CMV, adenovirus** | Clinical | **Mesna prevention**; early detection, catheter avoidance, **oral pentosan polysulfate** |

##### Nephrogenic Adenoma and Cystitis Cystica

*Nephrogenic adenoma.*
- **Rare benign tumour, mostly in adults**; in children a **reactive lesion** after infection, lithiasis, trauma or surgery.
- **Female predominance (5:1)**; presents with **haematuria or irritative symptoms**.
- **Diagnosis: cystoscopy with biopsy.** **Treatment: transurethral fulguration or resection** of isolated lesions.
- **Recurrence in ~80% of children, latency ~4 years** → **routine surveillance cystoscopy**. **No malignant transformation in paediatric series.**
- **Adjunct:** after resection, **ibuprofen + trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole** has produced long-term resolution (case report).
- **Malignancy is possible:** one young adult with a post-traumatic neurogenic bladder developed nephrogenic adenoma → multiple resections → **moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma** → radical cystectomy, tumour-free.
- **Setting:** most biopsy cases have **prior bladder augmentation with recurrent stones and infection**.
- **Origin:** immunohistochemistry suggests derivation from **distal renal tubular cells**.

*Cystitis cystica and cystitis glandularis.*
- **Cystitis cystica:** **fluid-filled cystic masses** from **chronic inflammation** — can **mimic a bladder tumour**; **almost exclusively female**; **best treated with long-term antibiotic prophylaxis**.
- **Cystitis glandularis:** **rarer in children**; **metaplasia of mucus-secreting epithelium**; premalignant concern **not supported by available data**.

##### Eosinophilic Cystitis

- Sporadic paediatric case reports; **male predominance**.
- **Cause unclear**; **immunological disease and allergy** suggested.
- **Histology:** inflammatory infiltrate with **numerous eosinophils through all layers of the bladder wall**.
- **Symptoms:** dysuria, haematuria, suprapubic pain, **urinary retention**.
- **Diagnosis: cystoscopy with transurethral biopsy — the lesion has no "typical" appearance** (may present as a mass on ultrasound, Fig. 42.8).
- **Treatment:** **transurethral resection + corticosteroids, antihistamines or antibiotics** most successful across all ages (large review); combined **steroids + antihistamines + antibiotics** successful in paediatric series.
- **Neonates and young children: may be self-limiting → observation justified.**

##### Haemorrhagic Cystitis

- **Almost always related to treatment of childhood malignancy**, especially **haematological**.
- **Commonest risk factor: cyclophosphamide or ifosfamide** → **exacerbated by viral infection (BK virus, cytomegalovirus [CMV], adenovirus)** in the immunocompromised child. **Pelvic radiation** also causes significant haemorrhagic cystitis.
- **Prevention: mesna (sodium 2-mercaptoethane sulfonate)** given with urothelial-toxic agents.
- **Bladder irrigation is difficult and traumatic in children** because only small catheters fit.
- **Improved success with early detection + catheter avoidance + oral pentosan polysulfate sodium** — a promising way to avoid further trauma.

*Concept —* **Every inflammatory lesion here is a mimic of tumour whose diagnosis is histological and whose treatment is removal of the stimulus** — infection (nephrogenic adenoma, cystitis cystica), allergen or immune trigger (eosinophilic), or toxic metabolite (haemorrhagic) — so biopsy, then treat the cause, and survey what recurs.

> **KEY POINTS: ACQUIRED BLADDER ANOMALIES IN CHILDREN**
> - **Acquired diverticula can obstruct or cause stasis and recurrent UTI.**
> - **Routine cystoscopy after resection of nephrogenic adenoma — ~80% recurrence in children.**
> - **Cystitis cystica can mimic a bladder tumour; treat with long-term antibiotic prophylaxis.**
> - **Eosinophilic cystitis has no typical appearance — biopsy is required.**
> - **Cyclophosphamide → haemorrhagic cystitis; mesna for prevention; oral pentosan polysulfate shows promise.**

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 2

**Key facts, figures and cut-offs**
- **Urachal adenocarcinoma: not reported in children.**
- **Laparoscopic urachal excision feasible <6 months.**
- **Haemangioma typically solitary, <3 cm.**
- **Nephrogenic adenoma: female 5:1; ~80% recurrence; latency ~4 years.**
- **Open urachal excision: two-layer bladder closure; catheter out next morning.**

**Gold standards, commonest, first-line**
- **Symptomatic urachal remnant → complete excision with bladder cuff.**
- **Commonest organism, infected urachal cyst: *S. aureus*.**
- **Patent urachus — commonest cause: failure of canal obliteration** (rather than bladder non-descent).
- **Urachal cyst — commonest in the distal urachus; presents more in adults.**
- **Sinus diagnosis: sinugram; patent urachus: longitudinal US / fistulogram / VCUG; cyst: US.**
- **Fibroepithelial polyp — second commonest paediatric bladder tumour after rhabdomyosarcoma; endoscopic resection curative.**
- **Haemorrhagic cystitis — commonest cause: cyclophosphamide / ifosfamide; prevention mesna.**
- **Cystitis cystica — long-term antibiotic prophylaxis.**
- **Eosinophilic cystitis — biopsy; TUR + steroids, antihistamines, antibiotics.**

**Eponyms**
- **Hutch** — secondary paraureteral diverticula from infravesical obstruction / neurogenic bladder.
- **Waldeyer sheath** — damaged as paraureteral diverticulum enlarges → VUR, then obstruction.
- **Meckel diverticulum** — omphalomesenteric remnant connected to umbilicus; mimics urachal sinus.
- **Klippel–Trenaunay, Sturge–Weber** — syndromes associated with bladder haemangioma.

**When to operate / when not to**
- **Infected urachal remnant → drain + antibiotics first, excise later** (never primary excision of an abscess).
- **Symptomatic urachal remnant → excise with bladder cuff; asymptomatic → discuss; trend towards observation.**
- **Vesicourachal diverticulum → only if narrow neck with stones/UTI.**
- **Acquired diverticulum → relieve outlet obstruction first; excise only if still symptomatic**, reimplant if ureter involved.
- **Haemangioma: small → fulguration / Nd:YAG; large or bleeding → partial cystectomy.**
- **Eosinophilic cystitis in neonates → observe.**

**Contraindications**
- None stated as absolute in the source. Relative concern: **laparoscopy for an infected or possibly malignant remnant** risks intraperitoneal spillage.

**Classic traps**
- **Urachal sinus vs omphalomesenteric duct — identical on sinugram; histology (gastric/small-bowel mucosa) decides.**
- **Urachal cyst is not a disease of infancy** — presents more in adults.
- **Fetal bladder obstruction does not explain patent urachus** — retubularisation more likely.
- **Acquired diverticulum: excising it without relieving obstruction treats the wrong problem.**
- **Nephrogenic adenoma is benign but recurs** → surveillance, not discharge.
- **Cystitis cystica → antibiotics, not resection.**
- **Eosinophilic cystitis looks like anything** → must biopsy.
- **Irrigation in paediatric haemorrhagic cystitis is itself traumatic** → catheter avoidance.
