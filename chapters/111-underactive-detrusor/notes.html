Chapter 111 — Underactive Detrusor

  • Impaired emptying with age is common in both sexes — raised outlet resistance, weak contraction, or both. The weak contraction is detrusor underactivity (DUA) (International Continence Society [ICS] urodynamic term); the symptomatic condition is underactive bladder (UAB).
  • Outlet resistance dominates male therapy: benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) raises it with age and is the target of most treatment for male lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS).
  • DUA mostly lacks effective treatment other than catheterisation, preferably intermittent self-catheterisation (ISC), when the postvoid residual (PVR) is raised.
  • Prevalence in referred LUTS rises with age; cause and mechanisms are poorly understood.

Concept — Emptying is the product of contraction against resistance; medicine can lower resistance but cannot yet strengthen the detrusor, so DUA management is largely drainage.

Terminology, Definitions, and Symptoms

  • Older terms abandoned: impaired detrusor contractility, detrusor areflexia, detrusor failure.
  • ICS 2002 definition of DUA (urodynamic): "a contraction of reduced strength and/or duration, resulting in prolonged bladder emptying and/or failure to achieve complete bladder emptying within a normal time span."
    • No thresholds are specified for reduced strength, reduced duration or prolonged emptying.
  • No contraction at all → acontractile detrusor. Distinguish from an isolated failure to void during a urodynamic study (UDS) — the "bashful bladder", which is common and often matches a history of inability to void in public toilets.
  • A purely urodynamic definition is impractical (UDS is invasive, not universally available) — the reasoning behind the overactive bladder (OAB) symptom complex. But OAB is anchored on urgency (variably linked to detrusor overactivity [DO]); UAB has no pathognomonic symptom and overlaps with OAB and bladder outlet obstruction (BOO).

Epidemiology

  • LUTS are highly prevalent and increase with age; the contribution of DUA is unknown.
  • Epidemiology of LUTS (EpiLUTS) (UK, USA, Sweden; over 40s): storage LUTS commoner in females, voiding in males, postmicturition similar. Difficulty emptying is common in older community dwellers.
  • Population prevalence of DUA cannot be established: there is no simple non-invasive marker.
  • Proxies fail in males: voiding LUTS, raised PVR and low flow can all be caused by BOO.
  • In females straining may simply accompany OAB (frequency, small volumes); but voiding LUTS with low flow and raised PVR make DUA likely, because female BOO is rare (2.7–8% of those referred for UDS).
  • In referred patients, DUA is common, especially in the elderly (Table 111.1).

Key points: Terminology, Definitions, and Symptoms, and Epidemiology

  • DUA is a common clinical problem associated with a range of storage, voiding and postmicturition LUTS.
  • Its population prevalence is unknown for lack of a non-invasive marker.
  • DUA affects 9–28% of males under 50 and 48% of those over 70 undergoing UDS, and is more prevalent among the institutionalised elderly.
  • In females, DUA is found in 12–45% undergoing UDS, more so among the institutionalised elderly.
  • DUA commonly coexists with other LUTDs.

Concept — Every estimate of DUA is a referral-series, criterion-dependent figure; because BOO is common in men and rare in women, the same voiding picture points to obstruction in a man but to DUA in a woman.

Etiopathogenesis

  • DUA occurs in both sexes and diverse groups. Recognised causes include diabetes mellitus and cauda equina compression, but many patients have no obvious cause, suggesting common age-related change.
  • Age-related decline in detrusor function is likely but unproven.
    • Rodent strip data conflict; human strips often show no decline in response to stimulation or agonists.
    • Clinical urodynamic data suggest an age-related fall in Pdet, flow and maximum contraction strength with a rise in PVR — but mostly in symptomatic subjects with progressive pathology.
    • BOO in males adds connective tissue and reduces smooth muscle; the same changes in females suggest a partial contribution of ageing itself (Fig. 111.4A–C).

Myogenic Factors

  • ECM change reduces transmission of contractile force.
  • Myocyte dysfunction — ion storage and exchange, excitation–contraction coupling, calcium storage, energy generation — gives a weak contraction despite normal neural drive.
  • Elbadawi ultrastructural patterns (electron microscopy): the degeneration pattern — widespread disrupted myocytes plus axonal degeneration — is associated with DUA. The classification is disputed, though others report similar findings.
  • Cause or consequence? Whether myocyte disruption causes DUA or follows another insult is unclear.

Neurogenic Factors

Brain Circuits

  • The micturition reflex is a spino-bulbo-spinal loop: sacral parasympathetic nucleus → PMC → sacral cord; the PMC is modulated by the cortex, particularly the limbic system.
  • PMC "direct neurons" fire immediately before and during reflex contractions and are silent otherwise; many project to the lumbosacral cord.
  • Human functional neuroimaging implicates the insula, hypothalamus, PAG and PMC.
  • A lesion anywhere here — disturbing perception and integration — could cause DUA, but site and urodynamic finding do not always correlate.

Bladder Efferent Pathways

  • Interruption in the sacral cord, sacral roots or pelvic nerves → absent or reduced contraction.
  • Autonomic innervation of the bladder declines with normal ageing.

Bladder and Urethral Afferent Pathways

  • Intact sensation is critical to the efferent limb. Bladder afferents monitor volume during filling and contraction magnitude during voiding; urethral afferents sense flow and contraction.
  • Afferent impairment reduces or prematurely ends the micturition reflex → loss of voiding efficiency.
  • Sensation declines with age: higher thresholds to reach capacity in older females; reduced insular response to filling on functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) in older asymptomatic people; in ageing rats, weaker urethral flow–evoked contraction needing a larger triggering volume.
  • Tissue change and sensory decline both likely contribute in the elderly.

Smith "stiff balloon" hypothesis (afferent output is a function of wall stress):
connective-tissue deposition + smooth-muscle disruption → loss of elasticity and receptive relaxation; connective tissue also impairs sensory innervation → stiff bladder → precipitous rise in wall stress on filling → delayed first sensation, then urgency soon after → small void → wall stress collapses → afferent drive falls → contraction terminates prematurely → raised PVR.

  • Support: in DUA, strong desire at higher volume, similar contractility (Watts factor), smaller voids and higher PVR than other LUTDs.
  • Urothelium: biopsies in DUA suggest impaired urothelial signalling and sensory transduction; causality versus effect of BOO is unresolved, and animal models are limited by different innervation.

Bladder Outlet Obstruction

Rodent partial-BOO model — three stages:
obstruction (ring or ligature) → bladder distension → compensation: hypertrophy and hyperplasia, sharp weight gain then plateau, increased blood supply, near-normal function → variable interval → decompensation: falling contractility, poor emptying, reduced response to stimulation and agonists, fibrosis.

  • If obstruction is not relieved before decompensation and fibrosis, dysfunction is permanent.
  • Obstructed bladders show reduced blood flow (laser Doppler) and denervation in both humans and animals.

Ischaemia–reperfusion hypothesis:
raised intravesical pressure to overcome resistance → raised wall tension (law of Laplace) → vessel compression, ischaemia, hypoxia → reperfusion each fill–void cycle → reactive oxygen species and free intracellular calcium → proteases, phospholipases, lipid peroxidation → damage to nerves, synaptic membranes, mitochondria, sarcoplasmic reticulum → denervation and detrusor decompensation.

  • Poor translation: immature, often female animals with acute obstruction; the sequence is not seen with urethral stricture and does not explain female DUA.
  • Long-term follow-up of men with BOO (over a decade): no significant fall in Pdet@Qmax, Qmax fell by only about 1 mL/s, and few needed intervention — prolonged BOO need not decompensate.
  • Most men with acute urinary retention have preserved detrusor function. Some with chronic retention also do — typically with poor compliance and upper-tract change (high-pressure chronic retention). Why outcomes diverge is unknown; the picture is probably multifactorial.

Diabetes Mellitus

  • Diabetes-induced bladder dysfunction (DBD), classically diabetic cystopathy: emptying efficiency falls in a time-dependent way over the disease course, by combined neurogenic and myogenic mechanisms.
  • Neurogenic: autonomic neuropathy — axonal degeneration and segmental demyelination → diminished sensation. Driven by hyperglycaemia via the polyol pathway, free radicals, protein kinase C activation and advanced glycation end products.
  • Reduced nerve growth factor (NGF) (maintains sympathetic and sensory nerves): low dorsal-root-ganglion NGF accompanies raised PVR and capacity in diabetic rats.
  • Myogenic: altered intercellular connections, excitability, intracellular signalling and receptor density; animal data show both increased and decreased contractility.

Pelvic Arterial Ischemia

  • Vascular risk factors (ageing, smoking, diet, atherosclerosis, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes) may cause chronic bladder ischaemia, analogous to their role in erectile dysfunction.
  • Animal pelvic arterial insufficiency → wall ischaemia, low oxygenation → oxidative stress → ultrastructural damage and denervation → features of DUA in some studies.

Key points: Etiopathogenesis

  • Aetiopathogenesis is incompletely understood; diverse factors and mechanisms are implicated.
  • Confirmed: neurological injury/disease and diabetes mellitus. Probable, not definite: BOO, ageing, atherosclerosis.
  • These factors impair processes essential to an efficient voiding contraction.
  • Mechanisms are myogenic (myocytes or ECM) or neurogenic (central control via afferent or efferent nerves).

Concept — Every cause acts on one link of the voiding reflex loop — afferent, central, efferent or muscle — and afferent failure is the unifying theme of age-related DUA: the contraction stops early because the signal sustaining it fades.

Diagnosis

  • Invasive UDS is the only accepted way to estimate detrusor voiding function clinically.
  • No universally agreed criteria exist; current methods measure almost only contraction strength, neglecting speed and sustainability.

Detrusor Contraction Strength

  • A contraction generates pressure and flow, so DUA is usually diagnosed by low Qmax plus low Pdet@Qmax. Male ranges come from men having outlet surgery; healthy ranges are poorly characterised.
  • Limitation 1 — underestimation: the bladder outlet relation (BOR), an adaptation of the Hill equation, makes pressure and flow inversely related during voiding (high flow, low pressure and vice versa), so Pdet@Qmax is the point of lowest pressure.
  • Limitation 2 — outlet resistance ignored: with a low Pdet, low Qmax may reflect BOO; a normal Qmax may reflect a low-resistance outlet (e.g. post-prostatectomy incontinence).
  • Hence isovolumetric estimates — by calculation or by interrupting flow; many are complex or impractical.

Detrusor Contraction Speed

  • Slow contraction could cause symptoms, but speed is not in the ICS definition.
  • Detrusor shortening velocity: Vdet = (Q/2)[3/(V + Vt)/4π]0.66 (Q flow, V bladder volume, Vt non-contracting wall volume).
  • A fall in Vdet precedes a fall in WF in longitudinal studies of both sexes — a potential early marker.

Detrusor Contraction Duration

  • In the ICS definition, but normal limits are undefined and few studies measure it.
  • Paradox: in men with BPH, unobstructed poor-contractility patients had longer contractions than unobstructed normals.
  • Duration probably reflects mechanism — early reflex termination would shorten it. A standard measurement method is needed.

Bladder Sensation

  • Afferents initiate and sustain contraction; men with DUA have reduced sensation versus other LUTDs.
  • Usual method: filling sensations (first sensation, first desire, strong desire, capacity; normal thresholds exist) — subjective: reported even without filling, and vary with setting and anxiety.
  • Current perception threshold testing: more objective, but invasive and unvalidated.

Ambulatory Urodynamics

  • Failure to void on UDS: bashful bladder (poor pelvic floor relaxation + reflex detrusor inhibition) or true DUA / acontractility — history usually separates them.
  • If in doubt → ambulatory UDS: over four-fifths of apparently acontractile patients contract. Criteria are not standardised and differ from conventional UDS.

Key points: Diagnosis

  • There is no published consensus on diagnostic criteria for DUA.
  • Most criteria focus on contraction strength as determined by Pdet during UDS.
  • Several indices estimate isometric strength; most are unvalidated and not applicable to all groups.
  • Stop and occlusion tests are more reliable but may be painful and impractical.
  • The significance of contraction speed and duration is unclear.

Concept — Because pressure and flow trade off along the bladder outlet relation, no single voiding point measures strength; every index is an attempt to recover isovolumetric pressure, and each gains either practicality or validity at the expense of the other.

Management

  • Goals: symptoms and quality of life (QoL); prevent urinary tract infection (UTI), stones, reflux with upper-tract back-pressure, skin damage from overflow.
  • Nothing effectively improves detrusor function → drainage or lower outlet resistance (e.g. relax the external sphincter).

Initial Assessment

  • Routine urological evaluation: bladder diary, digital rectal examination, urinalysis, uroflowmetry, ultrasound PVR.
  • Neurological assessment: sacral dermatomes, anal tone, bulbocavernosus reflex, lower-limb reflexes; deficits → specialist referral, commonly spinal MRI (lumbar cord, cauda equina).
  • Drug history: agents reducing contractility (anticholinergic, opioid; Box 111.1) or raising outlet resistance (α-adrenoceptor agonists).
  • Faecal impaction / constipation can obstruct directly; treating it may improve emptying.

Conservative Management

  • Scheduled voiding for sensory loss; double voiding to empty better and cut frequency.
  • Bladder expression (Valsalva, Credé): only in specific neurogenic cases — DUA with an incompetent sphincter. Otherwise avoided: high vesical pressure → vesicoureteric reflux or reflux into prostate and seminal vesicles.
  • Pelvic floor physiotherapy and biofeedback: effective in dysfunctional voiding, where a non-relaxing pelvic floor and sphincter obstruct flow and reflexly inhibit the detrusor — including some "acontractile" patients who contract on ambulatory UDS.
  • ISC is the preferred drainage for problematic high PVR, given adequate cognition and dexterity: safe, effective, fewer infections than indwelling catheters.
    • Problems: urethral bleeding (about one-third), false passages, time and social restriction, psychological barriers (fear of harm or infection).
  • Avoid a long-term indwelling urethral catheter; if ISC is impossible or declined, suprapubic catheter is the best long-term option.

α-Adrenoreceptor Antagonists

  • Lower outlet resistance in neurogenic bladder and dysfunctional voiding; results and combination therapy in the drug table.

Prostanoids

  • Five endogenous prostanoids — PGE2, PGF2α, PGI2, PGD2, thromboxane A — are made in the bladder wall and released on stretch.
  • They enhance contraction to acetylcholine and adenosine triphosphate (ATP); non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs abolish isolated-bladder tone, reversed by prostanoids. Efficacy in DUA is unclear.

Future Prospects in Pharmacotherapy

  • Bladder-specific parasympathomimetic allowing dose escalation — unlikely on current knowledge; presynaptic M2 antagonists — promising.
  • Uroselective exogenous prostanoids (sparing uterus and gut) in development; oral efficacy uncertain.
  • Transient receptor potential (TRP) agonists, e.g. TRPV4 agonist GSK1016790A, increase contractility.
  • Other-organ analogues (cardiac inotropes, gut prokinetics); sensory sensitisers for afferent failure.
  • Prerequisite: better understanding of normal contraction and of DUA mechanisms.

Botulinum Toxin

  • Botulinum neurotoxin A (BoNT-A) into the urethral sphincter lowers outlet resistance in detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia.
  • Rationale in DUA: relax the sphincter to remove reflex detrusor inhibition, or to facilitate Valsalva voiding.
  • Technique: 50 U in 4 mL saline (or 100 U in 8 mL) — cystoscopic in males, periurethral in females.
  • Results: some catheter-dependent patients voided with low PVR, ISC fell; about half of idiopathic cases improved.
  • Predictors of response: better bladder sensation; poor sphincter relaxation responds best, pure DUA or DHIC about one-third.
  • Limits: short-lived; no dose studies; unlicensed — clinical trials only.

Surgery

Bladder Outlet Surgery

  • Controversial in males with non-neurogenic DUA; no high-level evidence.
  • Meta-analysis of comparative studies of transurethral surgery: DUA → smaller improvement in International Prostate Symptom Score (I-PSS) and Qmax; QoL change and PVR similar to preserved contractility.

  • Predictors of poor outcome in retention: voiding pressure <45 cm H2O, age >80, PVR >1500 mL.
  • Females: urethral dilatation lacks evidence; bladder neck resection or incision not recommended — incontinence or bladder neck stenosis.

Concept — Outlet surgery helps only where outlet resistance is the part of the problem that can be changed: the symptomatic low-PVR man has little to gain, while the catheter-dependent man may gain enough to void.

Urinary Diversion

  • Rarely needed outside neurogenic patients, since ISC and indwelling catheters are acceptable and safe.
  • Continent catheterisable stoma when urethral ISC is impossible and a suprapubic catheter is declined — typically young women with idiopathic retention refractory to SNM.
  • Incontinent diversion (ileal conduit) occasionally, for renal deterioration from intramural ureteric obstruction by a thick-walled bladder.

Regenerative Medicine

  • Animal stem cell studies (DUA model → injection into bladder or neural injury site → urodynamics): several show improved contractility.
  • Barriers: incomplete understanding of lower urinary tract structure and function, and whether injected cells can acquire functional innervation and contribute meaningfully.

Key points: Management

  • There are no effective pharmacotherapies for DUA.
  • Parasympathomimetics have questionable efficacy, potentially fatal systemic effects, and are not used routinely.
  • Intravesical electrotherapy shows promise in restoring contraction but is time- and resource-consuming.
  • SNM is effective in restoring voiding in DUA associated with urethral sphincter / pelvic floor disorders.
  • Outlet surgery has a high failure risk in men not reliant on catheters. Catheter-dependent men with DUA fare worse than those with normal detrusors but may still benefit.

Concept — Treatment works only where some part of the loop survives — intact sensation is the recurring predictor of response (bethanechol, botulinum toxin, IVE); where muscle is lost, only drainage or replacement of the muscle remains.

Conclusions

  • DUA is not uncommon and hard to treat, and has been under-researched compared with OAB and DO.
  • A symptom-based definition is difficult — wide symptom spectrum, diverse conditions, no accurate non-invasive marker.
  • Highly prevalent in both sexes presenting with LUTS; causes affect every part of the micturition reflex.
  • Preserved bladder sensation predicts better response to treatment.
  • Drugs to enhance strength, duration or sensation need mechanistic understanding first; regenerative, stem cell and gene therapy depend on the same.
