Chapter 84 — Inguinal Node Dissection
 • MacCormac (1886) described the first penectomy series; Basset (1912) first described ILND (inguinal
   lymph node dissection) for cancer, as en bloc removal of the superficial and deep packets. Daseler (1948)
   described radical ILND and the five-zone nodal anatomy.
 • The inguinal nodes receive metastases from penile, vulval and rectal cancer and melanoma.
 • Penile cancer is rare, so there is no level I evidence; practice rests on small series and retrospective data.

CURRENT REAL-WORLD PRACTICE
 • Adherence to ILND guidelines is poor in United States registries (Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End
   Results [SEER]; National Cancer Database), the Swedish national register and multicentre European–
   American series. Even patients at high risk by European Association of Urology (EAU) criteria are often not
   dissected.
 • An adequate ILND removes at least 8 nodes per side, yet few dissections reach this.
 • Few clinicians treat many cases a year, and practice varies widely by region. In intermediate-risk disease
   with impalpable nodes, a substantial minority chose surveillance. Invasive staging was split between
   dynamic sentinel node biopsy (DSNB), modified ILND, superficial ILND and video-endoscopic inguinal
   lymphadenectomy (VEIL). For node-positive disease, adjuvant chemotherapy was preferred to
   neoadjuvant, except in cN3 disease.
 • Staging is often omitted in pT1b–T3, clinically node-negative (cN0) disease. Omission is associated with
   older age, comorbidity, lower stage, Medicaid insurance and non-academic care, and independently
   predicts overall mortality.
 • Guideline-concordant nodal management is associated with better overall survival, so fear of morbidity
   should not lead to omission.

Current Indications and Guidelines
 • There is no American Urological Association (AUA) guideline. The EAU–American Society of Clinical
   Oncology (EAU-ASCO, 2023) and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN, 2023) guidelines each
   use three risk tiers, defined differently because level I data are lacking (Table 84.1).
 • Risk grouping has high false-positive and false-negative rates. Occult nodal metastasis is uncommon in
   low-risk disease and substantial in intermediate- and high-risk disease.
 • Low risk with impalpable nodes: surveillance without routine staging imaging, because occult
   metastasis is unlikely.
 • Palpable nodes or high-risk disease: fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–
   computed tomography (18FDG-PET/CT), or computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen and
   pelvis, before treatment.
 • Consensus: complete inguinal and pelvic management within 3 months of diagnosis, unless upfront
   chemotherapy is given.
 • Grading is poorly reproducible between pathologists, which limits prognostication. Cases should have
   expert genitourinary pathology review, and patients should be counselled about under- or over-staging.

BENEFITS OF INGUINAL LYMPH NODE DISSECTION
 • Penile cancer usually has a contained locoregional phase in which inguinal disease precedes systemic
   spread. In many other cancers, nodal disease signals systemic disease; here it creates a window of cure.

 • Nodal yield is the surrogate for oncological adequacy. Removing 8 or more nodes is associated with
   better survival in grade 3 or pT2–4 disease. There is no universal cut-off, but surgeons should aim for at
   least 8 nodes per side when safe, accepting that higher yield brings more complications.
Concept — In penile cancer the groin is a curable station, not a marker of systemic disease. This is why timing
(within 3 months) and completeness (at least 8 nodes per side) are oncological variables, not technical
refinements.

PATIENT SELECTION
 • Human papillomavirus (HPV)-positive tumours have better disease-specific survival on meta-analysis,
   but the nodal data conflict. Some series show less lymphatic tumour embolisation, lower clinical N stage
   and lower nodal metastasis density with HPV positivity. Others show no difference in N classification,
   disease-specific or overall survival, or upstaging from cN1 to pN2/N3 at ILND. HPV status is therefore not
   reliable for choosing ILND or surveillance.
 • About half of palpable nodes, and up to a quarter of impalpable groins, harbour metastasis, so surgery
   carries a real risk of overtreatment.
 • Nomograms predicting nodal metastasis exist, but, like HPV status, are not recommended for routine
   use, especially where they produce guideline-discordant advice.
 • The guidelines select by primary-tumour pathology: T stage, grade, lymphovascular invasion, perineural
   invasion and sarcomatoid features.
Concept — Patients are selected on primary-tumour pathology alone. HPV status and nomograms are
prognostically interesting but are not decision tools: their nodal predictive value is inconsistent, and they can
pull recommendations away from the guidelines.

PREOPERATIVE WORKUP
Physical Examination
 • Examine the groins at the first evaluation and classify as cN0 (impalpable) or cN+ (palpable). For
   palpable nodes, record number, location, laterality, and whether mobile or fixed.
 • If examination is indeterminate (obesity, previous groin surgery), obtain magnetic resonance imaging
   (MRI), CT or PET/CT. Centres with melanoma experience also use groin ultrasonography (US).
 • Infection of the primary can cause reactive nodes, but a trial of antibiotics with observation is not
   recommended, because it delays diagnosis and treatment.

Cross-Sectional and Nuclear Imaging
 • CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis detects distant metastasis in patients with suspicious nodes or a
   high-risk primary.
 • For the groin itself, imaging misses deposits below 1 cm, so it is reserved for when examination is
   unreliable (obesity, previous surgery or radiotherapy). FDG-PET is therefore unlikely to change
   management in the impalpable groin (see the modality table below).

Ultrasonography, Fine-Needle Aspiration Cytology and Dynamic Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy
 • An involved node on US shows altered architecture and is hypoechoic and heterogeneous.
 • Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC), guided by US or CT, is specific but insensitive, whatever the
   guidance. Positive → ILND; negative → DSNB or ILND, never discharge.
 • US is performed before or at DSNB to find and sample enlarged nodes, for this reason:
      ◦ A node replaced by tumour blocks lymphatic inflow, and drainage is rerouted to a neo-sentinel node.
      ◦ The blocked node takes up no radiotracer or dye, so DSNB is falsely negative.
      ◦ Adding US improves DSNB accuracy and lowers the false-negative rate.
 • Single-photon emission computed tomography–CT (SPECT-CT) in palpable groins shows frequent
   rerouting to neo-sentinel nodes, occasionally contralateral, and sometimes absent drainage.

 KEY POINTS
   • Lymph node metastasis is the strongest prognostic factor for survival in penile cancer.
   • Real-world adherence to guideline recommendations on the inguinal nodes is low.
   • Address the inguinal nodes within 3 months of diagnosis; delay worsens oncological outcomes.


SURGICAL APPROACH
Anatomy
Camper's and Scarpa's Fascia and the Arterial Supply to the Skin
 • Skin flaps are 5–7 mm thick, raised deep to Camper's fascia along the plane of Scarpa's fascia. Camper's
   fascia carries a rich capillary network, so staying beneath it minimises flap necrosis.
 • The subdermal supply runs lateral to medial along Langer's lines. An incision parallel to these lines
   preserves skin viability; one that crosses them transects the vessels.

 • Sentinel nodes lie mainly in the superomedial zone, then the central zone. Spread to the two inferior

 • The deep group has 1–3 nodes, medial to the femoral vein in the femoral canal. The node of Cloquet,

Concept — The superomedial sentinel pattern and the no-skip sequence justify every reduced template.
Tissue that is never the first station can be left behind safely when the first station is clear.
Landmarks and Templates
 • Standard radical ILND boundaries (the femoral triangle): inguinal ligament superiorly, adductor longus
   medially, sartorius laterally, and the crossing of these muscles inferiorly.
 • Positive deep nodes have been found only with positive superficial nodes, so some experts omit deep
   ILND when the superficial nodes are negative.

Relationship to Nearby Structures
 • The spermatic cord crosses medial and cephalad to the fossa ovalis, over the inguinal ligament. With
   bulky nodes, identify and medialise the cord early.
 • The femoral nerve, the largest branch of the lumbar plexus (dorsal divisions of the ventral rami of L2–
   L4), runs deep to the iliacus fascia and under the inguinal ligament just lateral to the femoral artery. It is
   motor to pectineus, quadriceps femoris and sartorius, and sensory to the anterior thigh.
 • The anterior cutaneous branches to the groin skin are commonly sacrificed, but the motor nerve must
   be preserved: never dissect lateral to the femoral artery.
 • Fig. 84.1 shows the modified field lying inside the standard field, sparing the lateral and inferior territory,
   with the superficial tributaries marking Daseler's zones. Fig. 84.3 shows the order at the fossa ovalis,
   lateral to medial: nerve, artery, vein.
Concept — The femoral artery is the lateral boundary of safety. The motor nerve lies beyond it, the sentinel
nodes lie medial to it, and the modified template stops at it.

SURGICAL RESECTION
Open Approach
Sterility
 • The groin is colonised in skin folds and hair, and wound infection or infected lymphocele severely
   impedes recovery. Shave carefully, prepare thoroughly, drape appropriately, and apply a sterile dressing
   at the start.
Incision and Dissection
  • Position supine in frog-leg (thigh abducted and externally rotated, knee flexed and supported); in obesity,
    tape the pannus to the chest. The anaesthetist may be asked to keep the patient incompletely paralysed.
 • Sequence: mark the borders → oblique incision about 2 cm below and parallel to the inguinal ligament,
   along Langer's lines, across the width of the dissection → flaps 3 cm cephalad to the ligament and
   caudally to the apex → sweep fat and areolar tissue off the external oblique aponeurosis and cord down
   to the ligament (superior boundary) → at the apex, identify and usually divide the great saphenous vein
   (spare it if metastatic disease is minimal) → incise the fascia lata over sartorius laterally and adductor
   longus medially → identify the femoral artery and vein at the apex and dissect cephalad along their
   anterior surfaces, ligating cutaneous perforators → divide the saphenous vein again at the
   saphenofemoral junction → clear the deep nodes up to the femoral canal → never expose the lateral
   surface of the femoral artery (femoral nerve, deep femoral artery).
 • Reconstruction: detach sartorius from the anterior superior iliac spine and transpose it, or roll it 180
   degrees medially, over the femoral vessels (Baronofsky manoeuvre), suturing it to the inguinal ligament
   and adjacent muscles → close the femoral canal by suturing the shelving edge of the inguinal ligament
   to Cooper's ligament, without narrowing the external iliac vein or injuring the inferior epigastric vessels
   → closed-suction drains subcutaneously → tack the flaps to the muscle to obliterate dead space →
   absorbable subcutaneous sutures or staples.

CONCOMITANT INGUINAL LYMPH NODE DISSECTION WITH PENECTOMY AND/OR
PELVIC LYMPH NODE DISSECTION
 • With palpable nodes, DSNB, excisional biopsy or ILND can accompany treatment of the primary, and a
   positive inguinal frozen section allows pelvic lymph node dissection at the same operation.
 • Staging is acceptable, but ILND must not be delayed beyond 3 months. Concurrent versus staged surgery
   is decided with the patient, weighing age, goals of care, comorbidity and surgeon judgement.

Open versus Minimally Invasive Surgery (VEIL, Robotic VEIL)
Perioperative outcomes
 • Operating time varies widely; the minimally invasive approach takes about 2 hours, roughly 15 minutes
   longer per side than open surgery. Intraoperative complications are rare in experienced hands.
 • The data are retrospective and selection-biased, because video-endoscopic and robotic VEIL (RVEIL) are
   usually reserved for non-bulky, non-fixed nodes.
Oncologic Outcomes
 • With little long-term recurrence data, nodal yield is the surrogate, and it is similar between approaches.
   In a prospective phase I study, open re-exploration after robotic ILND found robotic dissection adequate in
   almost all groins, with one conversion; positive nodes were removed robotically.

Bulky or fixed nodes (cN3)
 • cN3 nodes can invade the femoral vessels and cause catastrophic haemorrhage. The preferred
   treatment is upfront systemic therapy, then surgery.
 • Upfront resection, curative or palliative, is considered if the patient is ineligible for or intolerant of
   chemotherapy, or vascular invasion is imminent.
 • If the skin is involved, perform a wide open resection with a gracilis or vertical rectus abdominis
   myocutaneous flap over the vessels, usually with plastic surgery. Without that support, negative-pressure
   wound therapy closes the wound over about 12 weeks.
Concept — The minimally invasive approach reproduces the open template through a remote port, keeping
the same boundaries, the same apex-to-ligament sequence, and the same frozen-section gate for deep
dissection. It gains wound outcomes and gives up bulky and skin-involving disease, which remains open
surgery.

POSTOPERATIVE CARE
 • Care depends on approach, patient factors (obesity, smoking, poor healing) and surgeon preference.
   Some allow same-day walking and next-day discharge, especially after minimally invasive surgery; others
   start with bed rest when wound breakdown or poor healing is a concern.
 • Drains stay for weeks, until output falls below 30 mL per day. The authors use a 19-Fr Blake drain on a
   suction bulb, removed at below 30 mL per day after mobilisation, avoid bed rest unless the wound is at
   risk, and prescribe thigh-high compression hose for at least 1 month.

COMPLICATIONS
 • ILND is highly morbid: phlebitis, wound infection, dehiscence, flap necrosis, lymphoedema, lymphocele,
   neurapraxia and pulmonary embolism. Rates vary with patient factors, template and approach, and
   because there is no standard reporting system.
 • Wound-healing problems (infection, flap necrosis) are the commonest. Prevent them with rigorous
   asepsis, appropriate antibiotics, and never dissecting above Camper's fascia. Incisional negative-
   pressure therapy may be considered, without robust ILND data.
 • Minimally invasive ILND has the more favourable profile (table above). In robotic series, lymphocele,
   surgical-site infection, cellulitis and early or late limb oedema are similar to open, but major
   complications, flap necrosis and severe limb oedema are fewer. After robotic ILND the commonest
   complication is lymphocele, then lymphoedema and cellulitis.
 • Surgeon experience probably drives both oncological outcome and morbidity; refer patients to high-
   volume centres and surgeons.
Concept — Almost every complication of ILND traces to one of two disruptions: of the skin's blood supply
(necrosis, dehiscence, infection) or of lymphatic channels (lymphocele, lymphoedema). Each refinement in
this chapter targets one of them: flap plane, templates, saphenous sparing, sealing devices, and minimally
invasive access.

FOLLOW-UP
 • Median time to inguinal recurrence is about 1 year; in-field recurrence after complete ILND is
   exceedingly rare. Most locoregional recurrences occur within 2–3 years, and after 5 years recurrences are
   local or new primaries. This supports intensive early follow-up, reduced after 3 years.
 • Beyond 5 years, follow-up is case by case: late recurrence is rare, so visits can stop in patients who
   understand and perform self-examination. Those unlikely to self-examine may need long-term follow-up.

 KEY POINTS
   • A contained locoregional phase gives a window of cure; when indicated, immediate ILND improves
     oncological and survival outcomes compared with delayed surgery.
   • Drainage goes to either or both sides and cannot be lateralised from the lesion; it proceeds
     superficial inguinal → deep inguinal → external iliac and does not skip.
   • Morbidity can be reduced in selected patients with DSNB and minimally invasive techniques.
   • Counsel patients appropriately, and do not avoid this life-saving operation for fear of morbidity.
