Chapter 143 — Focal Therapy for Renal Tumours                                                          Introduction


 Chapter 143 — Focal Therapy for Renal Tumours
 INTRODUCTION
   • Thermal ablation (TA) use for small renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is rising in population-based data: rapid
     growth in the early 2000s, then a plateau at about one in eight treatments overall and almost one-
     third of treatments in patients aged 80–89 years.
   • RCC incidence is rising through incidental detection on cross-sectional imaging performed for
     abdominal complaints; incidental tumours are small, so more than 70% of new tumours are clinical
     stage T1, with a consequent fall in cancer-specific mortality.
   • Age at diagnosis is rising, with increasing incidence in patients over 65 years.
   • Rationale for nephron sparing: chronic kidney disease (CKD) increases all-cause mortality, particularly
     after nephrectomy, while the cancer mortality of small RCC is low → emphasis on nephron-sparing
     treatment without oncological compromise.
   • Partial nephrectomy (PN) is the gold standard for small renal tumours: cancer-specific survival (CSS)
     >95%, equivalent to radical nephrectomy, with better renal function, superior cardiac outcomes and
     improved overall survival (OS).
   • PN remains underused: in Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) data for 2019, more
     patients with localised kidney cancer still received radical nephrectomy than PN; PN is used less in the
     elderly because of concern about complications.
   • 2021 American Urological Association (AUA) guideline: prioritise PN for clinical T1a (cT1a) masses;
     consider TA for cT1a masses ≤3 cm, particularly with substantial comorbidity.
   • Ablative options: percutaneous cryoablation (CA), radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and microwave
     ablation (MWA) — extend nephron sparing to highly comorbid patients while minimising morbidity.
   • Approaches: open, laparoscopic or percutaneous; modern series are predominantly percutaneous.
   • Advantages over extirpation: no tissue dissection or renorrhaphy → faster convalescence, fewer
     complications; post-ablation renal function similar to or better than after PN.
   • Indications: small renal tumours in high-surgical-risk patients; solitary kidney; bilateral tumours;
     hereditary syndromes such as von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease, in which repeated treatments and
     new tumours are expected.
 Concept — Ablation exists because small RCC seldom kills while nephron loss and comorbidity do;
 treatment is chosen to minimise competing-risk harm, not to maximise oncological radicality.

 CRYOABLATION
 Background
   • Cryotherapy: tissue destruction by extreme cold generated from liquefied gases that cool as they
     expand (e.g. argon).
   • Historical sequence: mid-1800s, Arnott — ice and salt (–18 to –24 °C) for breast, cervical and skin
     cancers → liquefied air (–190 °C), solid carbon dioxide (–78.5 °C), liquid oxygen (–182.9 °C), then liquid
     nitrogen (–196 °C) for skin conditions → 1963, Cooper and Lee: first modern cryoprobe — pressurised
     liquid nitrogen in a three-channel probe (one inflow, two outflow) at –196 °C, opening cryotherapy to
     deep, less accessible sites.

   • Argon-based probes — the key breakthrough: the Joule–Thomson principle (rapid expansion of a
     high-pressure inert gas produces cooling) yields –185.7 °C in target tissue; argon systems reach target
     temperature faster, more efficiently and with a steeper internal thermal gradient.
   • Most commercial CA units are now argon based (Galil Medical, Endocare and Oncura systems).

 Cryotherapy Mechanism of Action
   • Probe design: an inner chamber delivers compressed argon to a porous membrane at the distal tip,
     where expansion produces rapid cooling in a closed system; temperatures reach about –160 °C during
     argon evaporation.
   • Tissue death occurs during both freezing and thawing, by four mechanisms: direct cell injury, vascular
     injury with ischaemia, apoptosis and immunomodulation.
   • Freeze phase (closest to probe): extracellular water freezes first → osmotic gradient → cellular
     dehydration and membrane distortion → intracellular ice → organelle and membrane damage.
   • Thaw phase: extracellular osmolarity falls as ice melts → water re-enters cells → cellular oedema and
     lysis.
   • Vascular injury: endothelial damage → platelet activation → thrombosis → tissue ischaemia.
   • Zonal effect: direct injury and necrosis centrally; sublethal temperatures peripherally activate
     apoptosis.
   • Immunomodulation: necrotic cells release organelles, antigens and damage-associated molecular
     patterns (DAMPs — DNA, heat shock proteins) → dendritic-cell antigen presentation → T-cell activation
     → further immune-mediated tumour killing.
   • End result: coagulative necrosis, apoptosis, immunomodulation and eventual fibrosis and scar.

 Treatment Temperature
   • Lethal threshold is cell- and tissue-specific: normal renal parenchyma dies at about –19.4 °C; cancer
     may require as low as –50 °C (more fibrous) in small-animal models → preferred target temperature
     ≤–40 °C.
   • Iceball temperature is not uniform: 0 °C at the iceball edge; temperatures consistently below –20 °C
     are reached only 3 mm inside the edge → iceball must extend at least 5–10 mm beyond the tumour
     margin.
   • Multiple cryoprobes may be required, depending on lesion size and probe type and size.
   • Heat-sink phenomenon: large adjacent vessels dissipate ice formation → colder temperatures or
     longer freezing required.

 Freeze-Thaw Cycles
   • Multiple freeze-thaw cycles enlarge the necrotic zone and improve cure in animal tumour models →
     double freeze-thaw cycle is the current recommendation.
   • Thawing is itself lethal, and may be passive or active:

 Duration of Treatment
   • Duration needed for complete cell death in humans is unknown.
   • Porcine data: 5-, 10- and 15-minute freezes all produced complete necrosis 5 mm from the probe, but
     only ≥10 minutes produced necrosis ≥10 mm beyond the probe → most contemporary series use 8–
     10-minute freeze cycles (see Technique for the alternative 10 + 6–8-minute schedule).

   KEY POINTS: CRYOABLATION
     • Argon-based systems reach treatment temperatures below –40 °C via the Joule–Thomson principle.
     • Renal CA is performed under real-time imaging, with the treatment zone about 5–10 mm beyond
       the tumour margin.
     • A double freeze-thaw cycle, each freeze about 8–10 minutes, is the standard of care.

 Concept — Cryoablation is a thermal-gradient problem: lethality exists only within the –40 °C isotherm,
 which lies well inside the visible iceball, so every technical rule — margin, multiple probes, double cycle,
 duration — exists to push that isotherm beyond the tumour edge.

 HYPERTHERMIC MECHANISMS OF TISSUE INJURY
   • Heat-based modalities: RFA, MWA, laser ablation and high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU); they
     differ in how tissue temperature is raised, and RFA and MWA are the most commonly used.
   • Direct and indirect injury occur, as with CA.

 Concept — Heat and cold share the same two-zone architecture — a central lethal zone of direct injury
 and a peripheral sublethal zone of apoptosis, ischaemia and immune response — differing only in the
 direction of the temperature gradient.

 RADIOFREQUENCY ABLATION
 Background
 Historical sequence: 1891, d'Arsonval — radiofrequency waves heat living tissue → 1928, Cushing and
 Bovie — electrocautery knife (alternating current passes through the patient to a dispersive grounding pad
 on the lower limb) → 1990, two groups independently — percutaneous radiofrequency electrodes

 (insulated shaft, exposed metal tip; ablation length set by exposed tip length, but poor circumferential
 damage limited use to lesions ≤1.5 cm) → modern generators and electrodes for larger, complex lesions.

 Mechanism of Action
   • Alternating current at 450–1200 kHz → ionic vibration → molecular friction → heat.
   • Heat is generated within the tissue by ionic agitation, not supplied by the electrode.
   • Heating depends on energy deposited and surrounding blood flow, which acts as a heat sink.

 Treatment Temperature
   • Determinants of ablation: power delivered, maximum temperature and duration; with low impedance,
     an expanding sphere of damage spreads from the probe.
   • Current delivered too rapidly → desiccation at the probe–tissue interface → impedance rise blocks
     energy transfer and halts heating → temperatures kept ≤105 °C.

Chapter 143 — Focal Therapy for Renal Tumours                                                              Microwave Ablation
   • Lethal time–temperature combinations (in vitro, human prostate cells): 45 °C for 60 min, 55 °C for 5
     min, 70 °C for 1 min; even brief exposure >70 °C is lethal to RCC in vitro.
   • Temperature-based generators target 90–105 °C; ablation is not successful unless ≥70 °C is reached
     uniformly.
   • Impedance-based protocol: start at 40–80 W → increase 10 W/min → maximum 130–200 W, until
     impedance reaches 200–500 Ω.
   • Heat sink: highly vascular targets or large adjacent vessels disperse heat into cooler blood → spared
     tumour cells and treatment failure.
   • Two cycles separated by a ≥30-second cool-down are suggested for temperature-based systems, by
     extrapolation from animal CA data.

 Intraoperative Monitoring
   • Electrode placement is visible on ultrasound (US), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed
     tomography (CT), but there is no reliable radiographic visualisation of the RFA treatment zone.
   • Contrast with CA: CA shows an expanding iceball; the RFA zone is inferred from electrode choice,
     accurate placement and impedance or temperature readings.
   • Independent thermometry: non-conducting temperature probes at the tumour periphery or deep
     margin (also usable with CA).
   • Success is judged by generator feedback, thermal probes and absence of contrast enhancement on
     axial imaging.

   KEY POINTS: RADIOFREQUENCY ABLATION
     • Radiofrequency energy produces ionic friction and agitation; >70 °C for ≥1 minute causes
       irreversible coagulative necrosis and desiccation.
     • Treatment is temperature-based or impedance-based.
     • Real-time monitoring relies on impedance and temperature, not visual or radiographic cues.

 Concept — RFA is self-limiting: heat is made in the tissue by ionic friction, so anything that raises
 impedance (charring, desiccation) or carries heat away (blood flow) stops the ablation — every equipment
 variation is a device for keeping impedance low and the zone spherical.

 MICROWAVE ABLATION
 Background
   • Microwaves: electromagnetic radiation; ablative frequencies 915 MHz or 2.45 GHz.
 Historical sequence: 1930s — tissue heating proposed, generators inadequate → 1930s–1940s —
 progressively more powerful magnetron tubes → after the Second World War, a Massachusetts Institute of
 Technology magnetron (400 W at 3000 MHz) → 1946 — Mayo Clinic animal exposure studies → late 1970s
 — antennas for local tissue destruction.

 Mechanism of Action
   • Dielectric hysteresis (rotating dipoles): polar molecules continuously realign with a field alternating
     billions of times per second → kinetic energy → heat; frequencies 915 and 2450 MHz.
   • Water is polar → high-water tissues (most tissues and tumours) heat best.
   • Three components: generator (magnetron or solid-state amplifier), coaxial cables, antenna.
   • Antennas radiate energy — no grounding pads or return electrodes.
   • Microwaves propagate through desiccated tissue and blood, so ablation is not limited by charring;
     tissue often exceeds 100 °C.
 PURPOSE OF PROBE COOLING: RFA VERSUS MWA
  RFA electrode cooling                              MWA antenna cooling
  Prevents tissue charring, which otherwise          Protects the insertion track from unwanted ablation and permits
  limits ablation                                    higher power

   • Advantages over RFA: faster heating → less susceptible to heat sink (important in the highly vascular
     kidney); active heating up to 2 cm from the antenna versus a few millimetres for RFA (the rest by
     conduction); multiple antennas can run simultaneously for larger tumours; larger zones and higher
     temperatures than equivalent RFA applicators in porcine kidney.
   • Disadvantages: energy harder to distribute; bulkier coaxial cables, more prone to heating; rapid high
     temperatures endanger adjacent sensitive structures in poorly selected tumours.

 Treatment Duration and Power
   • Current devices: 915 MHz or 2.45 GHz, 45–150 W, gas- or fluid-cooled antennas; high temperatures
     allow larger tumours to be treated.
   • Temperature control versus power control: no significant difference in ablation geometry (porcine
     kidney).
   • Time and power: increasing either enlarges the zone, but power beyond 45 W adds nothing → porcine
     optimum 45 W for 10 minutes; longer ablation time consistently gives larger zones.
   • Multiple simultaneous antennas exploit overlapping fields to enlarge the zone.
   • Settings are largely manufacturer-derived → the operator must know the zone shape and size
     produced by each time–power combination on their own system.
   • No single strategy is evidence-based; for clinical T1 masses, typically 5–15 minutes at 45–65 W;
     authors' standard: 65 W for 5 minutes (NeuWave system).

   KEY POINTS: MICROWAVE ABLATION
     • MWA heats tissue by dielectric hysteresis.
     • Tissue temperatures often exceed 100 °C without desiccation or charring limiting the zone.
     • MWA is less susceptible to heat sink than CA and RFA.
     • Zone size is directly proportional to time of energy application.

 PERCUTANEOUS ABLATION SURGICAL TECHNIQUE
 Patient Selection and the Role of Preablation and Postablation Biopsy
 Renal mass management algorithm (Fig. 143.3): initial counselling (evaluate age, comorbidity, CKD risk,
 tumour size and location; educate on tumour risk versus competing comorbidity; shared decision making)
 → surgery, nephron sparing if feasible (younger, few comorbidities, preference) or watchful waiting /
 active surveillance (significant comorbidity, preference) or renal mass biopsy → second counselling
 (review biopsy, staging, options) → surgery, surveillance or percutaneous ablation — offered only after
 biopsy has been performed and discussed.
   • 2021 AUA guideline: biopsy universally, before (preferred) or at the time of ablation, to establish
     pathology and guide surveillance; the authors biopsy all candidates beforehand.
   • Biopsy avoids unnecessary treatment and post-ablation imaging surveillance of benign masses.

 Collaboration Between Urology and Radiology
   • Successful programmes need both specialties; in a United States academic survey most centres
     performed few ablations per month, radiologists alone performed them most often, a combined
     urologist–radiologist approach was common, and urologists alone least often.
   • Urologist: pre-procedure counselling on all kidney cancer options and long-term clinical cancer
     surveillance. Radiologist: imaging before, during and after. Both may perform the procedure → a
     symbiotic collaboration.

 Procedure
 Approach and anaesthesia
  • Laparoscopic ablation has declined sharply since 2010 while percutaneous ablation has risen → the
    vast majority is now percutaneous (less morbidity and cost).
   • Conscious sedation with local anaesthesia or general endotracheal anaesthesia, outpatient or with
     23-hour observation.
   • Outpatient suitability: tumour of minimal complexity ≤3 cm, low bleeding risk (no anticoagulation, no
     post-ablation haematoma), no significant comorbidity.
   • General anaesthesia controls respiration during probe placement → better outcomes.
   • High-frequency jet ventilation: short high-flow pulses through a small-calibre tracheal catheter, about
     120 breaths/min; small tidal volumes reduce renal motion; needs a specialised ventilator.
   • Conscious sedation reserved for highly selected patients who cannot tolerate intubation.
 Positioning and guidance
 Intravenous prophylactic antibiotics → prone or modified flank position on the CT gantry (chosen by
 tumour location) → probe placement under US (or CT or MRI) guidance → non-contrast CT to confirm
 probe position.
 Protecting adjacent structures
  • Hydrodisplacement (Fig. 143.4): spinal needle into the plane between tumour and structure (bowel,
    ureter, liver, spleen, pancreas) → inject sterile saline, or 5% dextrose in water (D5W) if using RFA;
    contrast may be added for CT fluoroscopic confirmation of separation (e.g. colon displaced >1 cm).
   • Saline may be added slowly during ablation to prevent overheating; avoid excess fluid in poor cardiac
     function (absorption).
   • Pyeloperfusion for central tumours or tumours near the ureter: pre-placed ureteral access catheter
     irrigated with cool saline for RFA/MWA, warm saline for CA — always opposing the ablative
     temperature — to protect the collecting system.
   • Probe number and duration follow lesion size and manufacturer recommendations; all modalities
     require intraprocedural monitoring.
 Cryoablation
  • CA's advantage: the iceball is visible on CT or MRI and on real-time US (ice–tissue interface); on CT it is
    a distinct hypodense zone.
   • Probe tips reach –140 to –190 °C with a steep gradient to 0 °C at the iceball edge; death is reliable at –
     40 °C → iceball 5–10 mm beyond the tumour margin.
   • Two freeze-thaw cycles. No standard duration provided the intended zone is reached; commonly 10
     minutes for the first cycle and 6–8 minutes for the second — animal data show inadequate necrosis at
     5 minutes and more tissue fracture at 15 minutes, so 10 minutes is the compromise.
   • Each freeze is followed by an active (helium) or passive thaw → after the second thaw, the probe is
     gently twisted and removed only if there is no resistance.
   • Multiple probes 1–2 cm apart create overlapping iceballs and an adequate edge margin; single probes
     rarely ablate most renal masses completely.
   • Tumours >4 cm have been ablated, but haemorrhage increases with tumour size and multiprobe CA.
   • Contrast-enhanced CT after treatment assesses completeness and excludes complications.

 Radiofrequency ablation
  • Efficacy is monitored by tissue temperature or impedance — single or multitined electrodes with
    thermistors, or multiple single-shaft impedance electrodes.
   • Electrode positioned for complete coverage plus a ≥5 mm peritumoral margin (Fig. 143.6).
   • Radiation reduction: confirmatory scans limited to three or four 3-mm images centred on the lesion at
     lower tube current (70 versus 150 mA).
   • Two cycles at 105 °C, separated by a 30-second cool-down; passive temperature ≥70 °C in each
     quadrant during cool-down confirms no significant heat sink.
   • Contrast-enhanced helical CT after treatment; inadequately treated areas → reposition and repeat.
     Tract ablation on withdrawal (manufacturer dependent). Larger lesions: multiple single electrodes in
     overlapping ablations.
 Microwave ablation
  • Antennas placed tangentially under US guidance, so the tip neither points towards nor penetrates the
    collecting system; the smaller, more spherical Precision PR antenna is preferred to the older LK
    antenna.
   • Antennas are fixed by cryogenic cooling → non-contrast CT confirms position → ablation by time and
     power (e.g. 65 W for 5 minutes) → continuous US shows gas bubbles progressively covering the
     tumour (Fig. 143.7) → contrast CT: no enhancement in the zone including a ≥5 mm margin, and
     exclusion of haematoma.
   • Larger tumours: antenna repositioning. Poor renal function: unenhanced CT plus contrast-enhanced
     US.

 Recommended Radiographic Follow-Up Protocol
   • AUA: abdominal CT or MRI with and without intravenous contrast plus chest imaging within 6
     months, then at 12, 24, 36, 48 and 60 months; beyond 5 years, shared decision making dictates
     further imaging.
   • Risk-adjusted follow-up may be considered, especially for larger or high-grade tumours.
   • US is not routine after ablation unless a contrast-enhanced US protocol is in place.

   KEY POINTS: TUMOUR ABLATION TECHNIQUE AND FOLLOW-UP
     • Biopsy before the ablation session is safe, increases histological diagnosis and reduces benign
       tumour ablation.
     • Ablation must achieve an adequate margin, which depends on the modality.
     • Complete loss of enhancement on CT or MRI indicates complete destruction and success.
     • Enlargement (any modality) or enhancement in the zone → local recurrence or incomplete ablation.
     • AUA: CT or MRI with and without contrast plus chest imaging at 6, 12, 24, 36, 48 and 60 months;
       thereafter shared decision making.

 Concept — Ablation leaves the tumour in situ, so the pathological margin of surgery is replaced by an
 imaging margin: enhancement is the only surrogate of viability, which is why histology must precede
 treatment and why surveillance imaging is structured and prolonged.

     nephrectomy for masses ≤5 cm, 534 patients, median follow-up 9.3 years: only 21 metastatic
     progressions and 12 RCC deaths → too few events for definitive conclusions.
   • Implication: trials comparing small-RCC treatments need very large cohorts followed for many years.

 Local Recurrence-Free Survival
   • Meta-analyses: local recurrence-free survival (LRFS) is better after PN than TA, but the difference
     disappears once salvage re-ablation is counted.
   • Meta-analysis of TA versus PN for cT1: higher all-cause mortality with TA, but no significant difference
     in local recurrence or metastasis; a further meta-analysis found equal 5-year local recurrence.
   • Caveat: many tumours were not biopsy-proven RCC → inclusion of benign tumours biases towards
     ablation.
   • Tumour size governs success: RFA LRFS markedly worse for tumours ≥3 cm than <3 cm; LRFS and
     disease-free survival (DFS) worse for T1b (>4 cm) than T1a; CA for T1b (>4 to ≤7 cm) gave substantially
     more recurrence than PN.
   • MWA for biopsy-proven cT1b RCC: no difference from PN or radical nephrectomy in MFS, CSS or LRFS
     (versus PN); lower OS, reflecting a more comorbid cohort.
   • Only the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) offers TA for cT1b, in select non-surgical
     patients; all other guidelines reserve TA for cT1a.

 Overall Survival
   • Ablation patients are older and more comorbid → OS 75–85% at 5 years, falling to 54–64% at 10 years.

   • No prospective trial has compared ablation modalities; observational comparisons are confounded by
     selection, tumour size and location, technique, approach and experience.
   • Network meta-analysis: no modality oncologically superior; MWA had the lowest complication rate.
   • Single-centre and single-institution comparisons: similar 2-year DFS, MFS and CSS for MWA, RFA and

     long-term cT1a data (benign masses included).

     tumour characteristics.

 PERIOPERATIVE OUTCOMES
   • TA versus surgery: shorter stay, less blood loss, lower transfusion rates; in one randomised trial, MWA
     had fewer complications than surgery.
   • Meta-analytic observations: TA cohorts are older and more comorbid (lower OS, higher other-cause
     mortality, similar kidney-cancer mortality) and so at higher complication risk; percutaneous carries
     fewer complications than laparoscopic ablation; complications should be graded by Clavien–Dindo;
     major complications are uncommon.
   • Major complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥3) in ≤5% after CA, RFA or MWA for small tumours; major
     urological complications fewer than after minimally invasive or open PN, depending on the
     population.
   • No direct comparison of urological complications between CA, RFA and MWA.
   • Most events are minor and self-limiting: small asymptomatic haematomas and transient urinary
     retention, often unreported.
   • Laparoscopic ablation has more complications, a substantial share attributable to the laparoscopic
     technique itself; complications fall with centre experience.

 Pain and paraesthesia
  • Insertion-site pain or paraesthesia, historically common, has fallen: modern cryoprobes are thermally
    insulated along the shaft, reducing freezing burns.
   • RFA: active only at the distal tip; tract ablation only long enough to clear the kidney and Gerota fascia,

     the long axis of the thigh — returning energy takes the shortest arc, so this maximises dissipation area.
   • MWA: shaft cooling, no grounding pads → pain or paraesthesia rare.
   • Posterior tumours: injury to nerves on the posterior abdominal wall → self-limiting neuralgia or
     neuropraxia; avoid by positioning so the tumour falls away from the wall, or by hydrodisplacement.

     advantage over surgery.

 NEW ABLATION MODALITIES
 High-Intensity Focused Ultrasonography
   • Principle: part of an acoustic wave's energy is absorbed as heat; focusing with a shaped transducer
     raises the focal point above the lethal threshold while sparing adjacent tissue.
   • At >3500 W/cm², cavitation and microbubble formation produce very high temperatures and a
     mechanical "shock wave" effect similar to extracorporeal shock-wave lithotripsy.
   • Unique among TA technologies: entirely non-invasive and extracorporeal → minimal or no risk of
     tumour seeding, haemorrhage or urinary extravasation.
   • Technique: one transducer treats and monitors; the beam is focused on the zone under real-time
     guidance, then refocused to ablate overlapping volumes and "paint" a larger region.
   • Evidence: early trials showed feasibility, but ablate-and-resect studies found incomplete treatment in
     every specimen; a small transcutaneous series found viable tumour on early MRI in about half, though
     most with longer follow-up showed involution.
   • Reasons for failure: poor targeting from respiratory movement; acoustic interference (shadowing,
     reverberation, refraction); lack of effective intraoperative monitoring.
   • Status: outcomes have stagnated and are inferior to current ablative technologies → investigational.

 Histotripsy
   • Focused ultrasound with a mechanical (non-thermal) mechanism, under real-time imaging guidance
     — the key contrast with HIFU.
   • Investigated preclinically, especially in liver and kidney models.
   • HOPE4KIDNEY: single-arm trial of safety and technical success in primary solid renal tumours, accruing
     from 2023.

 Radiation Therapy
   • Conventional external-beam radiotherapy was historically ineffective for RCC: perceived inherent
     radioresistance, limited parenchymal tolerance, scatter damaging surrounding tissue, difficult target
     localisation.
   • Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) = stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR): a high dose
     delivered in a single or few fractions with high precision.
   • Technique: three-dimensional coordinates; real-time tracking and correction for respiratory and organ
     movement without interrupting treatment → minimal scatter, focal high dose, ablation without
     compromising overall renal function.
   • Porcine kidney (CyberKnife): 24–40 Gy → complete necrosis without collateral damage.

   • Multi-institutional retrospective cohort (localised tumours, median 5-year follow-up): SABR safe; local
     recurrence comparable to TA, but CSS lower than expected — below the near-100% 5-year CSS of
     active surveillance series — possibly reflecting a higher-risk population.
   • Limitations of that cohort: non-RCC tumours included in survival analysis; heterogeneous single- and
     multi-fraction protocols without central, time-point-standardised review; highly selected comorbid
     population with substantial non-RCC mortality → recurrence likely underestimated.
   • Unresolved questions: optimal dose fractionation (trials ongoing); imaging endpoint — SABR causes
     only subtle change, absence of growth is used, yet many untreated small masses barely grow; post-
     SABR biopsies show viable but attenuated tumour of unknown potential; need for larger prospective
     studies with long follow-up in populations comparable to surgery and TA.
   • Roles: possibly combined with TA for larger tumours; salvage after TA or PN in comorbid patients;
     modern responsiveness contradicts the radioresistant reputation.
   • Advantages: reduced morbidity; no general anaesthesia; no interruption of anticoagulation.
   • Status for small renal tumours: experimental until dose fractionation, imaging criteria and prospective
     comparison with TA or surgery are established.

 Irreversible Electroporation
   • Irreversible electroporation (IRE): novel non-thermal ablation; an electric field creates nanoscale
     membrane pores, reversible or lethally irreversible according to voltage.
   • Delivered as electrical pulses via a single bipolar or multiple monopolar electrodes → reproducible cell
     death while preserving extracellular matrix, tissue scaffolding, ductal structures and large vessels.
   • Outcomes: few studies in the past decade; safe (no major complications) but oncologically
     suboptimal, with frequent salvage for residual tumour.
   • Status: investigational.

 CONCLUSION
   • TA is a guideline-endorsed alternative for clinically localised small RCC; CA, RFA and MWA are
     standard, with no prospective evidence that any one is superior.
   • Local recurrence is higher than after surgery but usually salvaged by re-ablation; meta-analyses show
     no difference in MFS or CSS.
   • Percutaneous ablation has less morbidity than open or minimally invasive surgery with renal function
     comparable to PN.
   • Pretreatment biopsy improves informed consent and shared decision making.
   • Decision integrates tumour characteristics, comorbidity, team experience and patient perspective.
