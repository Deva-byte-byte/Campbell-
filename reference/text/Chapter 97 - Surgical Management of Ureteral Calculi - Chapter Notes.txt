Chapter 97 — Surgical Management of Ureteral Calculi
 • Lifetime risk of a ureteral stone is 12% in males and 6% in females, so every urologist manages the
   problem.
 • Obstruction distends the renal capsule, which causes the pain; the dangerous consequences are renal
   damage and infection or sepsis.
 • Depending on size, location and circumstances, spontaneous passage may be attempted first.
 • Diagnosis can be difficult because the pain mimics conditions from appendicitis to ruptured aortic
   aneurysm; imaging is the most definitive step.
 • Absence of hydronephrosis alone does not exclude a ureteral stone. Ultrasound is now argued by its
   proponents to be almost as sensitive as CT.

MEDICAL EXPULSIVE THERAPY
 • Medical expulsive therapy (MET) with α-blockers, which relax ureteral smooth muscle, has been the
   mainstay of adjunctive treatment.
 • MET remains controversial: the large prospective SUSPEND trial (Pickard et al., 2015) showed no
   difference in the need for intervention at 4 weeks and equivalent passage rates versus control.
   Publications nonetheless continue on α-blockers, calcium channel blockers and phosphodiesterase-5
   inhibitors.
 • Spontaneous passage becomes less likely with time, so intervention should generally be considered
   after 4–6 weeks.
 • Interventions from least to most invasive: shock wave lithotripsy (SWL) → ureteroscopy (URS) →
   percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) with antegrade URS.

 • History and examination identify absolute contraindications to PCNL or SWL, such as uncorrected
   coagulopathy, and any active, untreated urinary tract infection (UTI).

 • Surgeons need several techniques at their disposal to achieve the best outcome.
 • SWL copes poorly with larger stones because the patient must pass many fragments or undergo repeated
   sessions; the historical 2 cm upper limit has given way to evidence of poorer stone-free rates (SFRs)
   above 10–15 mm.
 • A plain-language shared decision aid for URS versus SWL both informs the patient about the procedures
   and informs the urologist of that patient's goals.
 • Impaction is gauged on CT or US by the thickness of the ureteral wall around the stone. Severely
   impacted stones resist SWL and are better treated endoscopically, retrograde or antegrade.
 • Both SWL and URS can treat a stone anywhere in the ureter. In the upper ureter (≤2 cm) a randomised
   trial found similar SFR at 3 months, more retreatment with SWL and similar complications; in the distal
   ureter, SFRs are similar, SWL more often needs a second session, and complications are lower with SWL.
 • Large, hard proximal stones favour PCNL with antegrade URS; in a comorbid patient unfit for PCNL,
   repeated retrograde URS may be safer.

URETEROSCOPE CHOICE
 • Three types exist — rigid, semirigid and flexible — differing in size, optics and working channels.
   Availability often dictates the choice, but anatomy, stone location and expertise matter. Scopes were
   designed as reusable; single-use flexible scopes are recent.

Semirigid Ureteroscopes
 • Rigid scopes use the Hopkins rod-lens system: contoured glass rods with intervening air spaces acting as
   lenses. The outer diameter increases toward the base, so the scope itself dilates the orifice and ureter.
 • Force applied off the scope's axis distorts the rod-lens image, first seen as a semicircular blacked-out
   area.
 • The semirigid scope (1989) replaced the rod lenses with a flexible fused-quartz fibre bundle inside a
   rigid casing, allowing flexion of up to 2 inches off axis; it rapidly superseded rigid scopes.
 • Semirigid scopes are robust and cheap, but are damaged by torque — typically at the pelvic brim, where
   the shaft can bend or break — and by laser fired too close to the tip.

Flexible Ureteroscopes
 • Flexible scopes suit upper ureteral and renal stones: if a proximal stone migrates into the kidney, the
   scope can follow and treat it.
 • Deflection up to 270° reaches even the lower pole, and the standard 3.6-French (Fr) working channel
   admits small baskets or laser fibres while preserving irrigation.
 • Fibreoptic and digital, reusable and single-use versions exist, and no single type is best for all cases.
Fibreoptic Flexible Ureteroscopes
 • The fibre bundles that carry light and image are delicate; deflection, torque and bending break fibres
   early in a scope's life, producing black spots and falling brightness until repair is needed.
 • Channel leaks show as lens fogging or water staining and demand repair, because catastrophic failure
   usually follows during reprocessing.
 • Bundle density varies by manufacturer; a honeycomb pattern limits image fidelity, worse with coarser
   bundles.
Digital Flexible Ureteroscopes
 • Introduced in the mid-2000s with charge-coupled device (CCD) or complementary metal-oxide
   semiconductor (CMOS) sensors that convert photons to electrons at the tip ("chip-on-the-tip"). Because
   the signal travels digitally, the image does not degrade over time.
 • CMOS runs cooler, uses less energy, processes faster and is cheaper than CCD, so CCD has been phased
   out.
 • No camera head and fewer cords improve ergonomics.
 • In comparison studies digital scopes gave better clarity, magnification and manoeuvrability without
   moiré, reached the whole collecting system more often, and shortened operative time with similar SFRs.
   The better view prompted speculation about seeing the earliest stages of calcium oxalate stone
   formation.

 • Fibreoptic scopes survive because the chip makes the digital tip larger — about 8.5 Fr, flatter and less
   tapered — so entering the orifice and a narrow ureter is harder. Future miniaturisation may close this gap;
   meanwhile all major manufacturers still make both fibreoptic and digital scopes.
Durability and Failure Rates of Flexible Ureteroscopes
 • Fibreoptic scopes have always needed frequent repair, and modern designs have not improved on earlier
   generations.
 • Scope-sparing technique extends the repair interval: relocating lower-pole stones to an accessible calyx,
   using small-core (200 μm) fibres, working through a ureteral access sheath (UAS) and avoiding deflection
   inside it.
 • Digital scopes were hoped to be more durable, and early single-centre series suggested so, but later
   comparison showed failure rates similar to fibreoptic scopes.
 • Keep a fibreoptic scope available: when a digital scope cannot pass a narrow UPJ or infundibulum, the
   smaller fibreoptic scope has reached the target, sparing a second procedure.
 • Overall, one repair falls due roughly every 15 procedures, at high cost per repair — a burden on health
   systems.
 • Most centres therefore run a hybrid fleet: reusable scopes routinely, single-use scopes for difficult cases
   likely to damage an instrument.
Single-Use Digital Flexible Ureteroscopes
 • Single-use digital flexible ureteroscopes (SDFUs) answer the repair problem: a new scope with
   maximum deflection every case, no reprocessing cost or staff, and a fixed, budgetable cost instead of
   unpredictable repairs.
 • Bench, cadaveric and clinical performance matches reusable scopes, so cost-effectiveness becomes the
   deciding question. The true cost of a reusable scope includes purchase, repair, cleaning, sterilisation,
   quality checks and lost opportunity when it fails; micro-costing has found similar cost per case.
 • Economics shift with infrastructure — a new centre without towers or processors faces higher capital
   cost — so each centre must do its own analysis.
 • Later SDFUs are all CMOS-based with subtle differences and few direct comparisons; manufacturers are
   prioritising single-use development.

Intrarenal Pressure Monitoring
 • Raised intrarenal pressure (IRP) drives fluid absorption, pain, fever, systemic inflammatory response
   syndrome (SIRS) and sepsis.
 • Measuring IRP has needed a separate sensor in the collecting system; the LithoVue Elite SDFU integrates
   a pressure sensor for continuous real-time monitoring, which should allow safe thresholds and
   guidelines to be defined. Temperature and fragment-size sensing are expected next.
Concept — Every ureteroscope choice trades calibre against image and durability against cost: the fibre
bundle gives the smallest tip but degrades, the chip gives the best image but a larger tip and no durability
gain, and single use removes repair cost at a price that only low-volume centres recoup.

CONSENT AND ACCESS: URETEROSCOPY
 • Consent covers: failure to access the ureter or kidney; incomplete fragmentation or clearance; further
   procedures; perforation, avulsion and sepsis; and long term, stricture, further surgery, loss of renal
   function or nephrectomy.
 • The first step is to identify the ureteral orifice and place a safety wire, which may also serve as the
   working wire.
 • Segura's dictum: the only difference between an endourological procedure and a motorcycle accident is
   the presence of a safety wire. Its routine value is debated, but in the rare case that needs it, it is decisive.

Getting to the Stone
 • The "difficult ureter" is narrow or tortuous and will not admit instruments easily. The choice is between
   endourological manoeuvres now and a stent for passive dilation with a staged return — the latter costs
   extra procedures, anaesthetics and convalescence but is among the safest options.
 • Second-look URS after pre-stenting is best done after 14 days and within 5 weeks; earlier or later
   procedures had lower SFRs.

Ureteral Access Sheaths
 • UASs suit proximal ureteral and renal stones best. For renal stones they may improve SFR and lower
   infection risk, irrigation pressure, scope wear and operative time.
 • For ureteral stones the sheath helps when there is a large burden requiring repeated scope passes,
   when dusting relies on irrigation outflow for vision, and when high laser energy creates heat that must
   be carried away.
 • Placement uses two wires: the safety wire holds access while the sheath passes over a second, stiff
   working wire under fluoroscopy. Sheaths have a hydrophilic coating and a tapered obturator; the
   obturator may be passed alone first to dilate.
 • A safety wire outside the sheath can cause resistance: either use the safety wire as the working wire, or
   use sheaths designed to keep the wire outside the lumen, where it still serves as a safety wire.
 • About half of endourologists use a UAS for ureteral stones, most commonly 12/14 Fr.
 • Sheath pressure can cause ureteral ischaemia and stricture; in animals 14/16 Fr sheaths reduced
   ureteral blood flow faster, more deeply and for longer than smaller ones. Clinically, stricture after sheath

   use is rare; earlier high stricture rates reflected larger scopes and repeated passage with fragments, the

 • Sheaths are hard to stabilise low in the ureter, so most reserve them for stones above the iliac vessels,

Guidewire Types
 • Wires range from 0.018 to 0.038 inch in diameter and 145 to 260 cm in length, in varied materials,
   stiffness and coatings.
 • Tactile feedback and judicious fluoroscopy guide passage, following ALARA (as low as reasonably
   achievable) but never at the expense of safety or the surgical goal.
 • If the wire's path is uncertain, pass an open-ended or dual-lumen catheter and perform a retrograde
   pyelogram (RPG); whether an RPG should be routine before every URS is debated.
 • Nitinol (nickel–titanium) is pseudoelastic with shape memory and resists kinking;
   polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) wires with a nitinol core are very kink-resistant. The aim is a wire stiff
   enough to carry instruments yet flexible enough to pass an obstruction.

Safety Wires
 • A safety wire delineates anatomy, prevents loss of access and allows a stent after perforation.
 • "Wireless" URS, using the flexible scope itself as the safety wire, has produced low complication rates
   without perforation or avulsion in uncomplicated cases.
 • Because complexity cannot be predicted in advance, most experts still advocate a safety wire, tailored
   to the case and the surgeon.

Obstructed Ureters
 • About one ureter in ten cannot be accessed safely despite miniaturisation. Single-stage techniques spare
   anaesthetics, but safety governs: a double-J stent and a staged procedure are preferred to risking injury.
Wire will not bypass the stone. Open-ended catheter over the wire to 1–2 mm short of the obstruction →
RPG to outline the obstruction and any contrast passing it → using contrast hydrodissection, pass a
hydrophilic floppy-tip wire at once, the catheter's stiffness directing the force → if successful, advance the
catheter past the obstruction → exchange for a stiff wire, which also straightens a tortuous ureter.
 • If the catheter abuts the stone, the wire cannot find the natural channel and may exit at 90° —
   perforation. A torque vice and angled wires or catheters help in a tortuous ureter.
 • Alternative: leave the wire at the obstruction as a safety wire, displace the stone or perform direct
   lithotripsy, then place the safety wire under direct vision once the proximal ureter is seen.

Ureteral Dilating Balloons
 • Balloons (4–10 cm) pass over a wire under fluoroscopy. There is no set inflation time; the working rule is
   pulsed fluoroscopy until the waist resolves → hold 1–2 minutes → deflate and remove, the wire
   remaining for the stent.
 • Miniaturisation has made active orifice dilation uncommon; even an early 7.5 Fr flexible scope needed
   none in about half of cases.
 • When the wire is in but the scope will not enter the orifice, persistence risks a mucosal flap, false lumen
   and perforation.
Scope will not enter the orifice.
 • Railroading: a second wire is placed through the scope into the ureter, and the scope dilates its way in
   between the two wires; best with a rigid or semirigid scope.
 • Dual-lumen catheter: dilates the ureter and places a second wire or performs an RPG through its second
   channel; the scope then passes between the wires.
 • Rotate the flexible scope, because its working channel is offset from the centre of the tip; rotation
   minimises the leading profile.
 • Use the UAS obturator as the dilator (Table 97.1), saving a disposable dilator and avoiding a dual-lumen
   catheter.

α-Blockers Before Ureteroscopy
 • The rationale is the abundance of α1 receptors in the distal ureter, which govern peristalsis and tone.
 • A meta-analysis of randomised trials found less need for intraoperative dilation, better access to the
   stone, higher stone-free status at 4 weeks and shorter operative time. α-Blockers may also ease
   postoperative and stent pain and lower the force needed to insert a 12/14 Fr sheath.
 • Without adequately powered trials, use is judicious — distal stones, or when large scopes or a sheath
   are expected. Most trials gave 1 week preoperatively.
Concept — Every access manoeuvre is a way of widening the ureter without tearing it: the hierarchy runs
from time (stent, α-blocker) through graded mechanical dilation to direct force, and safety always permits
retreating to a stent and a staged procedure.

LITHOTRIPSY TECHNIQUES
Basketing Techniques
 • A small stone may simply be grasped and removed; retrieval devices vary in shape, size, material and
   engagement ability.
Engaging from behind. Pass the basket beyond the stone → open → draw back toward the scope under
vision → agitate gently → close slowly. For a stone that drifts forward, open the basket just larger than the
stone and push it slightly forward as it closes.
 • Keep the stone 5–10 mm from the scope tip during withdrawal so the ureter is always seen and not
   caught; blind basketing is unsafe.
 • Align the stone's long axis with the ureter by partly opening and agitating the basket, using the ureteral
   walls or irrigation.
Entrapped stone. Open the basket and push the stone into a wider segment → if immovable, disassemble
the basket, leaving its shaft as a wire → reinsert the scope alongside → laser the stone (or a nitinol basket)
→ if the basket cannot be disassembled, cut it distal to the handle.
 • Dual-channel scopes allow a small fibre through the irrigation channel without disassembly; very small
   baskets (1.5 Fr Sacred Heart halo) allow laser and basket together in a single channel.
 • Assess before engaging; when in doubt, fragment before extracting, to prevent perforation and avulsion.

Electrohydraulic Lithotripsy
 • Electrohydraulic lithotripsy (EHL), described in the 1950s, discharges a spark through fluid, creating an
   expanding vapour bubble and a pressure wave that radiates in all directions — hence its danger.
 • The 3–5 Fr probe limits irrigation, must sit on or very near the stone and away from the scope tip; hard
   stones (cystine, calcium oxalate monohydrate) resist it, and ureteral trauma risk is high, so an RPG after
   treatment checks for extravasation.
 • Holmium laser proved more effective for larger stones and faster, so EHL has largely been abandoned.

Pneumatic Lithotripsy
 • Compressed air drives a metal probe against the stone like a jackhammer. The probes are semirigid, so
   they work only through rigid or semirigid scopes — never flexible ones; the usual base rate is 12 Hz, and
   flexing the probe off axis wastes energy.
 • Success is greatest below the pelvic brim and least above it, and falls with larger stones and existing
   hydroureteronephrosis.
 • The energy is not directly traumatic, but pressing the probe on a partly fragmented stone can perforate
   the ureter. The chief limitations are accessibility and proximal stone migration.
Antiretropulsion devices. Placed between stone and kidney to stop fragments migrating, they may double as
a safety wire but add cost and time. Migration matters most with rigid or semirigid URS, where it may end
the procedure or force conversion to antegrade percutaneous access or SWL. They improve SFR, but demand
has fallen with flexible URS.

Holmium:YAG Laser Settings
 • Early low-power, short-pulse (SP) holmium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet (Ho:YAG) lasers fragmented
   stones, the short pulse breaking pieces off, which were split further until they could pass or be basketed.
 • Higher-power Ho:YAG introduced dusting — low pulse energy at high frequency. Fragments can then be
   "popcorned": they whirl in a cyclone-like motion and are ground to dust as they meet the laser (Fig. 97.4).
 • Lower pulse energy produces smaller pieces and less retropulsion, so the stone is easier to target
   continuously; long-pulse (LP) and pulse-modulated Ho:YAG reduce retropulsion further. The fibre tip
   serves as a size reference for fragments.
 • High dusting settings (0.3 J × 80 Hz or 0.2 J × 120 Hz, both 24 W) heat the irrigant; for large stones
   needing prolonged lasing, a sheath for outflow and room-temperature or colder irrigant are needed, and
   in the ureter power should be capped at 10 W.

Thulium Fibre Laser Settings
 • The thulium fibre laser (TFL) can fire above 2000 Hz, but a randomised trial showed lower settings may
   be optimal: TFL was faster than Ho:YAG, equal for ureteral SFR and better for renal zero-fragment rate,
   challenging the idea that low energy with high frequency is always best.
 • Why lower settings work: power (W) is heat, so low settings widen the safety margin; lower frequency
   means more energy strikes the stone rather than water and less misfires into tissue. Start low; increase
   only as needed.
 • TFL's lower peak power and long pulse (about 50–200 μs) ablate stone into fine particles rather than
   breaking off fragments, so technique differs from SP Ho:YAG.
 • In an impacted ureteral stone, target the centre first: as space opens the sides fall into the lumen and can
   be lased away from the wall; the scope tip dislodges fragments stuck to urothelium. Increase energy in
   small steps, keeping total power ≤10 W; fragments are usually small enough that no basket is needed.
 • If visibility is poor, lower the frequency and keep power constant by raising energy.
 • In the kidney, ureteral settings are safe, or dusting settings can be used; paint the stone surface and do
   not drill — with TFL drilling melts the stone and causes flashing, although it can fragment with SP Ho:YAG.

Laser Settings for Ureteroscopy
 • Settings depend on location and desired outcome — fragment and basket, or dust and let pass — and
   both yield high-quality results.
 • A second randomised trial compared a 60 W TFL with a 120 W pulse-modulated Ho:YAG at higher
   starting settings (fragmentation 0.8 J × 8 Hz, 6.4 W; dusting 0.3 J × 80 Hz, 24 W) and found SFR, operative
   time, complications and zero-fragment rate similar, though follow-up used KUB and US rather than the
   more sensitive NCCT.
 • Criticism: the manufacturer's Ho:YAG dusting setting may not suit TFL, since at high frequency fewer
   pulses hit the stone and more energy is absorbed by fluid.

Temperature Safety When Using Lasers
 • Heat is a by-product of every laser; attention to settings and thermal relaxation time prevents delayed
   thermal injury and stricture.
 • The ureter and a calyx hold small fluid volumes that heat quickly; above 43 °C, tissue is damaged.
   Without irrigation, every setting tested on the bench exceeded 43 °C.
 • Laser type does not determine heat — power, technique and adjuncts do. Fluid temperature tracks
   power for both TFL and Ho:YAG, so the rule is the lowest effective power, with a guide of ≤10 W in the
   ureter and ≤25 W in the kidney.
 • An access sheath lowers both IRP and temperature by providing outflow, with both lasers; in an animal
   model, a 14 Fr sheath kept temperature below 44 °C where no sheath did not.
 • Low-retropulsion lasers keep the stone still, allowing continuous lasing and so more heat; how the laser
   is used matters more than where.

 • Irrigant should be room temperature or colder and run continuously. The old habit of stopping irrigation
   to limit retropulsion overheats the ureter and risks thermal injury and stricture.
Concept — Power is heat and heat is stricture: every laser recommendation — low settings, 10 W in the
ureter, 25 W in the kidney, a sheath for outflow, cool continuous irrigation — follows from keeping energy
delivered to a small fluid volume below 43 °C.

DRAINAGE AND ANTIBIOTICS AFTER URETEROSCOPY
 • Two decisions close every case — stent or not, and postoperative antibiotics or not — and the evidence
   below applies to uncomplicated URS without infection, impaction or perforation.

Antibiotics
 • Postoperative antibiotics are indicated only if the patient had urosepsis during that stone episode. A
   single perioperative intravenous dose gives the same infection rate as a postoperative course, so the
   default is one dose and nothing afterwards unless something arises during the case.
 • Higher infection risk: higher Charlson comorbidity index, older age, female sex, prolonged pre-stent
   dwell time, neurogenic bladder and high body mass index (BMI).

Ureteral Stents
 • Stents drain and dilate the ureter for passage of fragments and dust, but are a major source of
   morbidity — symptoms in about four in five patients.
 • A randomised trial (Denstedt et al., 2001) showed URS can be performed safely without a stent, and a
   Cochrane review found pain, return to hospital and secondary interventions similar with or without one.
 • Optimal stent duration is unknown; removal at 3 rather than 7 days caused more unplanned returns and
   flank pain, with no difference in hydronephrosis.
 • Removal by string equals cystoscopy for infection and outcome, is preferred by patients and is cost-
   effective.
 • Degradable stents would need no removal and may be better tolerated if biocompatible and not
   inhibiting peristalsis, which is thought to contribute to stent symptoms.
 • Access-sheath use does not affect postoperative hydronephrosis at 8 weeks.
 • Practice lags evidence: more than half of urologists stent routinely, and about three-quarters in the
   United States.

SHOCK WAVE LITHOTRIPSY FOR URETERAL CALCULI
 • SWL remains a worldwide mainstay and the only truly effective noninvasive treatment, though burst
   wave lithotripsy (BWL) may change that.
 • Modern machines treat most ureteral stones but may need more than one session; SWL can be done
   under intravenous sedation or local anaesthesia, and patients value avoiding a stent and general
   anaesthesia (GA).
 • Quality-of-life (QoL) comparisons may favour SWL, but most used generic instruments that miss the
   disease-specific impact.

Indications for Shock Wave Lithotripsy for Ureteral Calculi
 • SWL is an option for a stone anywhere in the ureter; because outcome depends on stone and patient
   factors, informed consent and shared decision-making, including patient preference, are central.
 • In acute colic with an obstructing stone, SWL within 48 hours outperforms delayed SWL; emergency
   SWL gives similar SFR in the upper, mid and distal ureter, low complication rates, and rarely needs urgent
   drainage.
 • Distal ureteral stones are treated safely and effectively, despite some centres' reluctance, with a second
   session adding clearance.

Patient Selection
 • Stone size is the most important predictor of SWL outcome, and the largest linear dimension predicts as
   well as area or volume. SFR is slightly lower than URS overall, and the gap widens in favour of URS at ≥10
   mm.
 • Location matters little once size is accounted for.
 • Pure cystine and dense, smooth, homogeneous stones (brushite, some calcium oxalate monohydrate)
   fragment poorly, although composition is often unknown before treatment.
 • Chronic impaction worsens outcome and may be the strongest predictor of all. CT suggests it by soft-
   tissue density of the ureteral wall around the stone (the "ring" sign) with severe hydronephrosis, or by a
   stone unchanged in position on earlier imaging.
 • Hounsfield units (HU) above 1000 and SSD above 10 cm predict failure for renal stones; for ureteral
   stones the evidence is mixed, and they may add nothing once size and impaction are considered.

System Factors
 • Where SWL is unavailable, distant or offered only on certain days, local URS may be preferable.

Patient Preference
 • An informed patient may choose SWL despite a predicted inferior result — a stone over 10 mm, high HU
   or some impaction — accepting a possible second session or salvage URS.
 • When rapid clearance in one treatment is critical (imminent travel, return to work or caregiving), URS
   may be preferred.

Patient Preparation
 • Routine pre-stenting before SWL is not needed: it improves neither SFR nor retreatment, though SWL
   can proceed with a stent already in place. A stent may reduce steinstrasse after large stones but does not
   change its presentation or management, and stents worsen lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS).
 • Correct a bleeding diathesis first or choose URS. If thrombotic risk allows, hold acetylsalicylic acid (ASA),
   non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and anticoagulants to achieve normal coagulation on
   the day and for 48 hours after, especially for upper ureteral stones where kidney, spleen or liver lie in
   the blast path. Continuing low-dose aspirin (81 mg) or NSAIDs has been reported for mid and distal
   stones.
 • Hypertension increases bleeding and perinephric haematoma when the kidney is in the blast path;
   untreated or severe hypertension is an absolute contraindication, and even controlled hypertension
   raises risk.
 • Pacemakers and defibrillators may need deactivation or reprogramming, particularly for upper ureteral
   stones near the cardiac conducting system.
 • Urinalysis and culture before SWL; treat any bacteriuria first. Routine prophylaxis is unnecessary, but
   antibiotics are given with indwelling stents, nephrostomies or catheters, struvite stones, recent or
   recurrent UTI, and colonised or reconstructed tracts (ileal conduit, augmentation, neobladder).

Technique of Shock Wave Lithotripsy to Maximise Outcomes
 • Good analgesia or anaesthesia keeps the patient still, and GA or intravenous sedation outperforms
   minimal anaesthesia, because stone movement of 10–15 mm significantly impairs fragmentation. Target
   during expiration, two-thirds of the respiratory cycle, and use pressure belts.
 • Position by bone: upper ureter supine; mid-ureteral stones over the sacrum or pelvis prone, so bone does
   not absorb the energy; stones below the sacroiliac joint supine.
 • Coupling is critical, especially with "dry" shock heads, because air dissipates shock waves: the gel, water
   or oil interface must be free of bubbles, as even tiny air pockets sharply reduce energy transfer.
 • Shock rate is one of the most important parameters: 60–90 shocks/min (1–1.5 Hz) outperforms 120 (2
   Hz) in randomised trials and meta-analyses, and the slower rate also protects the kidney and its
   vasculature.
 • Treat until complete fragmentation or the manufacturer's maximum shock count, with continuous or
   frequent imaging. US and fluoroscopic targeting are equally effective; longer fluoroscopy time predicts
   better outcome, reflecting better targeting.
 • Ramp energy gradually to the maximum: this improves fragmentation and reduces renal injury by
   inducing protective vasoconstriction and lowering injury markers (microalbumin, β2-microglobulin).
 • Experienced urologists and technologists, and ongoing quality assurance, give better results.

SPECIAL SITUATIONS
Pregnancy
 • Conservative management is first-line if safe — no infection, oliguria, anuria, solitary kidney or fetal
   distress — and SWL is contraindicated.
 • If conservative treatment fails, URS is safe, with high success and near-universal normal continuation of
   pregnancy; sedation, GA, spinal or epidural anaesthesia add no risk, and limited fluoroscopy with fetal
   shielding is safe.
 • Stents are safe but encrust faster in pregnancy, so they are changed every 4 weeks, which shapes the
   timing of definitive treatment. Retrograde stent is attempted before nephrostomy.

Urinary Diversion and Transplanted Kidneys
 • After diversion, reimplantation or transplantation, retrograde access is the hardest part; flexible
   cystoscopy through an ileal conduit or Indiana, Kock or Studer pouch is often very difficult or impossible.
 • SWL can be tried, allowing for altered positioning — more often prone — where the ureter crosses bone.
 • The most definitive route is antegrade and percutaneous; guidelines support percutaneous treatment of
   large proximal ureteral stones even without diversion. Antegrade flexible URS through a percutaneous
   sheath benefits from a dilated proximal ureter and lets dust wash down into the bladder or diversion.
 • In transplanted kidneys both antegrade and retrograde approaches are safe and successful.
Congenital Renal Anomalies
 • In ectopia, crossed-fused ectopia and complete or partial duplication, retrograde URS is usually feasible
   with good results; the challenge is an ectopically placed orifice, as in some duplex systems.
 • Escalation: SWL → PCNL if SWL fails or retrograde access is impossible → laparoscopic ureterolithotomy
   or pyelolithotomy as salvage.
Concept — When the retrograde route is compromised — by pregnancy's limits on radiation and SWL, or by
reconstructed anatomy — the choice shifts to whatever route is safest: stent and URS in pregnancy,
antegrade access after diversion, laparoscopy as the final salvage.

COMPLICATIONS
 • Complications are becoming rarer with smaller scopes, better vision and efficient lasers; most are minor
   (perforation) and easily managed (stent), and the major one, avulsion, is very rare and preventable.

Perforation
 • Incidence 0–4%, and falling. Causes: the scope; forcing an oversized sheath; unseen distal slippage of
   the dilating wire during sheath placement (hence fluoroscopy); laser on the wall; lithotrite or
   electrocautery, which cause full-thickness injury; stone manipulation; wires without a floppy tip; baskets
   gripping the wall; and poor visibility from bleeding or debris.
 • If a procedure is difficult and not progressing: stop, stent, stage.
Management. Stop → stent across the injury (no further lithotripsy, since fragments in the wall provoke a
granulomatous reaction and stricture) → saline irrigant to prevent electrolyte disturbance from
extravasation → drain a urinoma and give antibiotics → stent for about 4 weeks → mandatory imaging
after removal.

Submucosal Stone
 • Submucosal and "lost" stones are more common with chronic obstruction; a submucosal stone is
   lasered free and a stent placed, because of the risk of later stricture.
 • A stone fully extruded through a perforation is left where it lies — retrieval worsens injury and
   extravasation. Stop, stent, image afterwards, and tell the patient, since it may later be mistaken for a
   new ureteral stone.

Avulsion
 • The most disastrous complication, occurring in about 0.06–0.5% of cases, it results from forceful
   manipulation of a large or impacted stone. The proximal third is most at risk, having the least muscular
   support, and the injury is recognised when ureter emerges with the stone and basket.
 • Prevention: never basket blind; keep space between stone and scope; fragment stones too large to
   remove intact; release or reposition an oversized engaged stone, or disassemble the basket and
   fragment in situ; a safety wire allows a stent and abandonment at any time.

 • Management: nephrostomy and delayed repair, not immediate primary repair; drain any urinoma;
   reconstruction depends on level and remaining viable ureter. Nephrectomy is not recommended,
   because stone disease recurs and stone formers are prone to hypertension and diabetes.

Stricture
 • Incidence 3–6%, falling with better technology and technique. Risk factors: impacted or long-standing
   stones, perforation, previous ureteral surgery and pelvic radiation (poor blood supply and healing),
   though some strictures follow uncomplicated URS.
 • Avoid aggressive passage through a narrow segment, and image every patient after URS, since some
   strictures cause silent obstruction.
 • Management depends chiefly on length and location, plus time since injury, mechanism and patient
   factors.

Post-Ureteroscopy Sepsis
 • Sepsis follows about 5% of URS in meta-analysis and is serious, bringing longer stay, intensive care unit
   (ICU) admission, readmission, cost and mortality.
 • Risk factors: positive preoperative culture, preoperative stent, older age, diabetes, ischaemic heart
   disease (IHD) and longer surgery.
 • Prevention: culture and treat before surgery; keep IRP as low as possible in at-risk patients. Treatment:
   broad-spectrum antibiotics and support until urine, blood or stone cultures identify the organism.
Concept — Nearly every serious URS complication traces to force applied without vision — blind basketing,
forced sheaths, persistence in a tight ureter — so the cure is uniform: see, fragment, and when in doubt stop,
stent and stage.

FUTURE OF URETEROSCOPY IN URETERAL STONES
 • Smaller scopes, digital chips and temperature and pressure sensors are already here, and further
   miniaturisation will ease access.
 • Surgeon ergonomics and repetitive strain injury are driving robotic URS, which adds degrees of freedom
   as it did in laparoscopy.
 • Artificial intelligence (AI) may adjust laser settings from fragmentation feedback, control irrigation,
   temperature and IRP, compensate for respiration, and navigate by electromagnetic tracking integrating
   fluoroscopy, preoperative CT and sensor data.
 • BWL (Break Wave, SonoMotion) uses a handheld ultrasound device delivering low-amplitude ultrasonic
   bursts that make the stone resonate, vibrate and shatter. Preliminary data show it fragments all stone
   types in the ureter; it is expected to be an adjunct to SWL and URS, suited to certain locations and SSDs.
