DEFINITION AND IMPACT OF URINARY INCONTINENCE AND PELVIC ORGAN
PROLAPSE
Pelvic floor disorders.
 • Female pelvic floor disorders (PFDs) comprise urinary incontinence (UI), faecal incontinence (FI), pelvic
   organ prolapse (POP), sensory and emptying abnormalities of the lower urinary tract, and defecatory
   dysfunction.
 • About one woman in four has POP, UI or FI — UI the commonest (about one in six), then FI, then
   symptomatic POP.
 • Prevalence is predicted to rise because both drivers — ageing and obesity — are increasing in the
   population.
Urinary incontinence.
 • International Continence Society (ICS) definition: the complaint of involuntary loss of urine. Principal
   types: stress (SUI), urgency (UUI) and mixed (MUI); others are listed in Table 105.1.
 • Reported prevalence varies widely — about one-third of women over 60 worldwide on meta-analysis; up
   to four-fifths of frail elderly women; US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)
   cycles show a rising trend, to over half. Variation reflects lack of age standardisation, culture, willingness
   to report and inconsistent definitions.
 • Risk factors for all types: age, obesity, smoking, comorbidity and postmenopausal hormone therapy.
   Vaginal delivery → more SUI. Lower UUI with higher household income, physical activity and caesarean
   (not vaginal) delivery.
 • Pregnancy: UI rises from about one in ten in the first trimester to about one in three in the third.
 • Race: Asian American women have the lowest UI prevalence; White and Mexican-American women have
   more SUI than Black women; Black women have more UUI and detrusor overactivity (DO). Disparities in
   access to care probably distort these estimates.
 • Subtype order (current US data): isolated SUI > UUI > MUI. Earlier data ranked MUI second. SUI and MUI
   prevalence stable 2005–2018; UUI rising in women aged 60 and over.
 • Burden: direct costs in billions of dollars annually, rising as more women are treated; cumulative risk of
   SUI surgery 13.6% by age 80.
 • Functional impact: physical, social, psychological and sexual. Many women stop exercising because of UI
   → risk of diabetes and cardiovascular disease; UI impairs intimacy and leads to avoidance of intercourse.
Pelvic organ prolapse.
 • ICS definition: descent of one or more of the anterior vaginal wall, posterior vaginal wall, uterus and
   cervix, or vaginal vault (cuff after hysterectomy).
 • Commonest site: anterior wall > posterior wall > apex.
 • Anatomical vs clinical POP: about half of parous women have some degree of POP on examination, but
   the diagnosis requires symptoms attributable to it.
 • Symptoms: vaginal bulge, pelvic pressure, difficulty voiding or defecating, and splinting (hand or fingers in
   the vagina) for comfort or evacuation.
 • Vaginal bulge is the symptom most specific to POP and the one most likely to resolve after repair.

 • Symptomatic POP (asked as "Do you see or feel a bulge?") affects about 3% of US women, stable 2005–

 • Cumulative risk of POP surgery 12.6% by age 80; direct surgical costs run to millions of dollars annually.

Knowledge and care-seeking.
 • Knowledge of UI and POP is poor among community-dwelling women, more so among non-White
   women → many suffer in silence.
 • Only 18–50% of women with PFDs seek care. Barriers are multifactorial; misbeliefs call for culturally
   competent education.
 • Costs, lost productivity, reduced quality of life (QoL) and inactivity-related illness → need for trained
   providers.

Concept — PFDs are common, under-reported and defined by symptoms and bother, not anatomy: half of

DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION

General Considerations
 • Patient-reported and QoL outcomes are now central; PFDs affect social, psychological, economic and
   sexual life, so treatment success is hard to define.
 • PFDs coexist and interact — POP may disturb voiding or defecation.
 • Women delay seeking care for 1 to 5 years or more after symptoms become bothersome and rarely
   volunteer them → ask proactively; a short validated screening questionnaire opens the discussion.
 • POP and UI are distinct diagnoses: patients may expect prolapse repair to cure leakage. Relief of bulge
   drives satisfaction after POP surgery → educate on anatomy and set expectations.
 • Inequities in care for minority women operate at patient, provider–patient and system levels; social
   determinants of health and systemic racism need evaluation.
 • Identify and quantify the UI type to target treatment; consider reversible causes — DIAPPERS (Table
   105.2).
 • POP compartments: anterior (cystocele — bladder descent); posterior (rectocele — posterior wall
   protrudes); apical (uterine prolapse, vault, and enterocele — bowel at the vaginal apex).
 • Rectocele vs rectal prolapse: rectocele is posterior vaginal wall into the vagina; rectal prolapse is rectal
   mucosa out through the anus.
 • Evaluation → differential → shared decision-making; degree of bother balances risk against benefit,
   alongside the patient's goals.
 • Objective improvement does not always predict subjective improvement. Good communication
   improves satisfaction, adherence, emotional health, symptom resolution and pain control.

 • Diagnoses overlap (mixed UI; detrusor hyperactivity with impaired contractile function, DHIC). Do not
   treat asymptomatic bacteriuria.

History
 • History is the foundation; women seeking help should be believed and their symptoms validated.
 • SUI is a urethral problem, UUI a bladder problem, MUI both; MUI may be stress- or urgency-
   predominant.
 • Accuracy of history: systematic reviews show a positive SUI history predicts urodynamic SUI in about
   three-quarters, but a negative history does not exclude it → examination, voiding diary or urodynamic
   studies (UDS) if the type is unclear.

History of Present Illness
 • Parameters: type, duration, severity, frequency of episodes, volume, bother, pad use, trajectory (worse,
   better, stable); alleviating and aggravating factors; prior treatments and results; treatment goals.
 • Typing questions: leakage with laughing, coughing or activity → SUI; leakage preceded by urgency or on
   the way to the toilet → UUI; both → MUI.
 • Bother is assessed per type — in MUI it decides which component is treated first.
 • Quantity: pads per day, pad size, containment, clothing changes, volume (drops → tablespoons → whole
   bladder). Pad weight testing is used mainly in trials and academic settings.
 • Voiding habits: hesitancy, incomplete emptying, stream strength, intermittency, straining, positional
   voiding, daytime and night-time frequency; urgency and frequency are the key lower urinary tract
   symptoms (LUTS) to characterise.
 • Onset: traumatic delivery; lifelong since childhood; peri- or postmenopause; after surgery; neurological
   disease. Urinary symptoms may precede a neurological diagnosis → screen for numbness, tingling,
   tremor, balance or coordination loss, and blurred or double vision.
 • QoL impact: patients modify lifestyle to avoid leakage; use questionnaires (see Questionnaires).
 • POP — bother is the key. Stage 1 (within the vagina) may be asymptomatic; symptoms usually begin
   when POP reaches or passes the hymen, where it can be seen and felt: stage 2 (−1 to +1 cm), stage 3 (>1
   cm beyond), stage 4 (complete eversion).
 • POP is dynamic — worse later in the day and with activity.
 • POP symptoms: bulge, pressure, heaviness, "falling out". Splinting to void or defecate suggests high-grade
   POP; high-grade anterior POP can raise urethral resistance and impede voiding.
 • POP is associated with DO and overactive bladder (OAB); repair may improve OAB, but not in every
   patient → staged OAB treatment may be needed.
 • SUI coexists in about 40% of women with POP.

Compassionate Care
 • Compassion improves outcomes and lowers costs — medical outcomes, parasympathetic responses,
   well-being, self-care, sense of purpose.
 • A strong doctor–patient relationship increases medication adherence.
 • Compassion need not take time: expressions lasting under 1 minute are meaningful — scripted
   reassurance by an oncologist reduced anxiety in women with breast cancer.
 • Validate and acknowledge concerns → patient-centred care.

Past Medical and Surgical History
 • Urological: infections, stones, haematuria, trauma, procedures (childhood and adult).
 • Neurological: neurological trauma, stroke, spinal cord injury or surgery, back pain or surgery, multiple
   sclerosis, Parkinson disease, multiple system atrophy, Huntington disease, myelodysplasia.
 • Medical: diabetes, thyroid disease, dementia; prior pelvic radiation.
 • Surgical: pelvic trauma or surgery (including prior UI or POP surgery, hysterectomy) may damage sphincter
   or bladder function; rectal resection (low anterior, abdominoperineal) and sacral lesion resection can
   injure pelvic nerves → urinary, bowel, pelvic and sexual dysfunction.
 • Obstetric and gynaecological: gravidity, number and mode of delivery, hormonal status (pre-, peri-,
   postmenopause; systemic or local hormone therapy; oral contraceptives; oophorectomy).
 • Obstetric risk factors for UI and POP: prolonged second stage, large fetal head circumference, high birth
   weight, forceps or vacuum delivery.

Medications
 • Drugs alter outlet resistance, bladder contractility, urine production or cognition; stopping the culprit may
   reverse the dysfunction.

 • Pattern: every agent acts through one of four routes — outlet, detrusor, urine volume, or the patient's
   cognition and mobility.
 • Traps: α-blockers (usually thought of as helping voiding) worsen SUI in women; ACE inhibitors cause UI via
   cough; dementia drugs (cholinesterase inhibitors) and antimuscarinics act in opposite directions on the
   detrusor.

Other Contributing Factors
 • Family history of POP → higher risk of POP and of recurrence; connective-tissue genetics (collagen,
   laminins, elastic fibres) implicated. Genetics also contribute to OAB, SUI and UUI.
 • Social history: smoking, substance use, sexual activity, partner, social support, physical activity,
   occupation.
 • Screen for pelvic trauma and abuse — trauma is common; asking creates a safe, healing environment
   (trauma-informed care).

Social Determinants of Health
 • Social determinants of health (SDOH) = conditions where people live and work that affect health and
   QoL; assess them in history-taking. They drive health disparities, which are a barrier to equity and an
   economic burden.

 KEY POINTS
   • PFDs are common and rising; history and examination are central; bother guides therapy.
   • Adjuncts: UA, PVR, UDS, cystoscopy, imaging, questionnaires. Surgical and non-surgical options exist.
   • POP becomes symptomatic at or beyond the vaginal opening; screen for pelvic and sexual trauma.

Concept — the history sorts leakage into urethral (stress) versus bladder (urgency) failure and prolapse into
anatomy versus bother; it is the patient's bother, not the finding, that decides what is treated and in what
order.


PHYSICAL EXAMINATION
 • General: age, mobility and frailty inform performance status. Abdomen: habitus, incisions, hernias,
   organomegaly, bladder distension — relevant if abdominal surgery is considered.
 • Frailty predicts poor postoperative outcomes and complications in urological surgery → preoperative
   frailty assessment recommended at age ≥65.
 • Frailty is distinct from comorbidity: lack of resilience to external stressors, or decline in physiological
   reserve. No single standard definition or tool.

Neurological examination.
 • Perform in any patient with known or suspected neurological disease: speech, gait, cognition, facial
   symmetry, lower-limb and perineal/perianal sensation, lower-limb power, vaginal and pelvic floor
   strength. Mobility and cognition govern timely toilet access.
 • Bulbocavernosus reflex (BCR) tests S2–S4; present in 70% of normal women vs 100% of normal men →
   an absent BCR in a woman is not by itself pathological.
Eliciting the BCR: squeeze the clitoris (or glans) → anal and pelvic floor contraction, seen or felt on DRE;
alternatively, pull an indwelling catheter balloon against the bladder neck.
 • Anal sphincter tone (also S2–S4): ask the patient to tighten as if stopping the stream. Laxity = neurological
   deficit or poor voluntary muscle awareness.
 • Urethra: meatal stenosis, mass, tenderness, fullness, foreign body; expect postsurgical changes after anti-
   incontinence surgery or a mesh mid-urethral sling (MUS).

Trauma-Informed Care
 • Trauma-informed care = awareness of the patient's whole life situation; improves engagement and
   adherence. Recognise trauma in patients, families and staff; avoid retraumatisation; embed in policy.
 • Sexual abuse is common: about 1 in 4 women and 1 in 13 men in the USA experience child sexual abuse;
   rape-related post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) affects millions of American women.
 • Genital examination triggers flashbacks or anxiety in about half of trauma survivors.
 • Principles — safety, control and choice: patient chooses timing (now or later) and a support person; may
   self-insert or decline the speculum; clinician leaves the room while the patient undresses; privacy
   prioritised; expose only what is needed for the shortest time; discuss while the patient is dressed.

Chaperones During Physical Examinations
 • Role: comfort, safety, privacy and dignity for patient and provider; positioned to observe and assist;
   prevents misunderstandings. Supported by gowns, private undressing, sensitive draping, explanation.
 • American Medical Association (AMA) guidance — physicians should:
     ◦ Adopt and communicate a policy that patients may request a chaperone; always honour the request.
     ◦ Use an authorised member of the health-care team, bound by privacy and confidentiality.
     ◦ Use a chaperone even when a trusted companion is present.
     ◦ Offer private conversation without the chaperone; minimise sensitive history-taking during the
       chaperoned examination.
 • Patients and physicians differ on the role and choice of chaperone; offering one is respectful of privacy
   and autonomy.

 KEY POINTS
   • Systematic genitourinary examination; POP supine and standing; SUI supine or standing.
   • Baden-Walker and POP-Q most used; neurological and rectal examination when indicated.
   • Trauma-informed principles; offer a chaperone.

Concept — the examination is designed to reproduce the patient's worst state — full bladder, standing,
straining, prolapse reduced — because each omission (empty bladder, supine position, unreduced kinking
prolapse) hides the pathology the patient actually lives with.

Symptom Quantification Instruments
Bladder Diary
 • Bladder diaries (voiding or micturition diaries, frequency–volume charts) quantify UI and are both
   diagnostic and therapeutic — patients gain insight into behaviours driving symptoms. Full discussion lies
   in the online chapter and Chapter 114.

Questionnaires
 • Severity alone does not show QoL impact; POP and UI are not life-threatening but can severely impair
   QoL → assess self-reported symptoms and their impact.
 • International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire (ICIQ): modular, validated, international
   standard for clinical and research use, in over 30 languages. Modules: UI short form (UI SF), bladder diary,
   male/female LUTS (MLUTS/FLUTS) and their sexual modules, nocturia (N), OAB, vaginal symptoms (VS); in
   development: cognitively impaired adults (Cog), underactive bladder (UAB), children (CLUTS).
 • ICIQ-UI SF correlates with 1-hour and 24-hour pad tests for SUI severity, and its change correlates with
   the Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I) after sling surgery.
 • Validated questionnaires are encouraged to standardise patient-reported outcomes; choose by clinical
   question. No single universal instrument, making comparison hard — but validated tools correlate well.

Urinalysis
 • UA is part of the diagnostic evaluation of LUTS and UI: haematuria, pyuria, glucosuria or proteinuria may
   indicate a secondary cause. Abnormal dipstick → microscopy or culture → further testing or treatment.

Postvoid Residual
 • PVR = urine remaining shortly after voiding; measures emptying. US preferred over catheterisation;
   portable bladder scanners are reliable, though devices vary.
 • Recommended by the European Association of Urology (EAU), ICS and AUA/SUFU — yet evidence linking
   PVR to dysfunction is weak:
        ◦ Slow stream, straining and incomplete-emptying symptoms do not correlate with measured PVR.
        ◦ Voiding dysfunction on UDS or uroflow may coexist with normal PVR; patients with and without LUTS
          have similar PVRs.
 • A single PVR is unreliable — repeat it. A single low PVR does not exclude incomplete emptying; a single
   high PVR does not predict emptying dysfunction.
 • No agreed cut-off — thresholds range 30–200 mL. In asymptomatic women almost all PVRs are <100 mL
   and few exceed 50 mL; defining normal in asymptomatic women may set cut-offs.
 • Elevated PVR does not identify the cause but warrants further evaluation and alters counselling before
   SUI procedures or drugs that reduce detrusor contractility for UUI; it may contribute to UTI, renal
   dysfunction and hydronephrosis.

Cystoscopy
 • Not routine in the index SUI patient; consider when an anatomical abnormality is suspected — urgency,
   haematuria, irritative LUTS.

URODYNAMIC STUDIES
 • Full discussion of UDS lies in the online chapter and Chapter 107.

Radiographic Imaging
Voiding Cystourethrogram
 • Imaging is not needed in uncomplicated UI; image if renal damage or pelvic pathology is suspected.
 • Voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG): suspected vesicoureteric reflux (VUR) or urethral diverticulum;
   shows bladder contour and diverticula, bladder-neck configuration on voiding, bladder-base position on
   straining (cystocele grade), and fluoroscopically subtle leakage on cough or Valsalva.

Ultrasonography (Including Translabial Ultrasound to Visualise Mesh)
 • Renal US — non-invasive assessment of obstruction and hydronephrosis. Upper-tract imaging
   indications: chronic retention, severe POP (ureteric kinking → hydroureteronephrosis), suspected fistula,
   neurogenic detrusor dysfunction.
 • Bladder and urethral US: bladder wall thickness associates with OAB; in SUI, bladder-neck and urethral
   swing, bladder-neck funnelling, urethral position relative to the symphysis and hiatal area differ — role in
   management still unclear.
 • Translabial US for POP identifies involved structures (rectocele vs enterocele); three-dimensional
   translabial US detects levator ani defects with moderate-to-good agreement with MRI — defects
   associate with POP and recurrence. Translabial US ≠ pelvic US (uterus and adnexa).
 • Translabial US for mesh: the only modality that reliably images mesh and confirms the presence and
   location of a mesh MUS — proximity to urethra and bladder, proximal or distal position, path, contour,
   twisting, continuity after revision → plans revision or removal; helpful when mesh is impalpable or not
   seen cystoscopically. Office or theatre; distinct protocol.
 • Computed tomography (CT) and MRI are poor for mesh — seen only if infected, associated with other
   pathology, or inferred from surrounding structures.

 • MRI gives soft-tissue detail of bladder neck, urethra, pelvic floor relaxation and prolapse.

MANAGEMENT

Urinary Incontinence Treatment Overview
 • Complete evaluation = history and examination, plus exclusion of UTI, outlet obstruction, stones, foreign
   body or tumour where suspected.
 • UI arises from the bladder, the outlet or both; treatment targets the cause, individualised to patient
   goals, risk–benefit and cost–benefit, with counselling to align them.
 • Full treatment discussion lies in the online chapter and Chapters 113–118.


CONCLUSIONS
 • Goal of reconstruction: restore normal anatomy and function of vagina, bladder and surrounding
   structures to improve QoL while minimising complications.
 • Pelvic floor care is refocusing on patient experience, goals and QoL and patient-centred outcomes
   research; the patient–surgeon relationship — open communication, trust, mutual respect — is pivotal.
