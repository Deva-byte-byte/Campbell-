Chapter 18 — Fundamentals of Laparoscopic and Robotic
Urologic Surgery
 • Osler: “Diseases that harm require treatments that harm less.” Over the past 30 years, minimally
   invasive urology has become predominant.
 • Multi-institutional comparisons with open surgery show equivalent efficacy and acceptable efficiency.
   The advantages are less pain, better cosmesis, faster recovery, shorter stay and, as techniques mature,
   often lower cost.
 • The objectives of even complex open surgery on the adrenal, kidney, ureter, bladder, prostate and lymph
   nodes can now be met with less injury. Open surgery's role is steadily shrinking.

PREOPERATIVE PREPARATION

Patient Selection and Contraindications
 • Start with a history focused on previous surgery and an examination mapping every abdominal scar.
   Laboratory tests, electrocardiogram and chest radiograph follow the same criteria as any major operation
   under general anaesthesia.
 • Severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) → arterial blood gases and pulmonary function
   tests, because the CO₂ pneumoperitoneum loads the lungs. Arrhythmias must be treated beforehand:
   hypercarbia and acidosis destabilise the myocardium.

Bowel Preparation
 • No mechanical bowel preparation for retroperitoneoscopy, extraperitoneoscopy, or transperitoneal
   surgery not using bowel. Propensity-matched data show no benefit for laparoscopic nephrectomy or
   prostatectomy, and emerging data show none even for cystectomy with conduit or neobladder.

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery Protocols
 • Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS), Europe, 2001: a multimodal, multidisciplinary programme.
 • Elements: no solids for 6 hours and carbohydrate drink 2 hours before surgery (not overnight fasting);
   balanced, not liberal, fluids; early removal of drains and tubes; early mobilisation; eating and drinking on
   the day of surgery. Later additions: counselling, thromboprophylaxis, ileus prevention, normothermia.
 • Results: stay shortened by about 30%, complications halved, fewer readmissions, lower cost. Benefit in
   cystectomy is seen with open or minimally invasive approaches. Surgery–anaesthesia collaboration is
   the key.

Preparation of Blood Products
 • Group and screen suffices for low-bleeding-risk procedures: radical nephrectomy and
   nephroureterectomy (transfusion 3–12%), prostatectomy (2.5% at experienced centres).
 • Crossmatched blood for extensive procedures (partial nephrectomy, cystectomy, caval thrombectomy),
   especially early in experience. With experience, group and screen may do for partial nephrectomy
   (transfusion 6–7%).

   starting (Box 18.1), with an open laparotomy tray ready.

Patient Positioning
 • Supine: arms tucked or on sleds. Trendelenburg or lateral: tape and belts across chest and thighs.
 • Lateral: pad all bony prominences and the strap contact points. Lower leg flexed about 45°, upper leg
   straight on pillows (no sciatic stretch). Pad the knee and ankle of the lower leg. Axillary roll three to four
   fingerbreadths below the axilla (brachial plexus). Use active warming for long cases.
 • Flank interface pressure is higher in men, with BMI ≥25, with the kidney rest (the most harmful; avoid
   beyond 20–30 minutes), and with full rather than half table flexion. → Men with BMI ≥25, kidney rest up
   and full flexion are at highest risk of rhabdomyolysis. The plain table mattress beat egg-crate or gel, and
   egg-crate was at least as good as the costlier gel.
 • Pelvic surgery: Trendelenburg on split-leg positioners or Allen stirrups. Never shoulder braces (brachial
   plexus injury).

Cord Management
 • Secure cords to the drape at two points without compressing them. Keep the camera cable separate from
   diathermy and suction. Place the boom or robotic cart at the foot of the table without cable tension. On
   the robot, wrap redundant camera cord once round the camera shaft. Leave a U-shaped loop in suction
   tubing when racked.

Prophylaxis and Other Preparations
 • Pneumatic compression stockings; optional 5,000 units subcutaneous heparin preoperatively; both in
   high-risk patients (e.g., morbid obesity), continued postoperatively.
 • Foley catheter: monitors urine output, decompresses the bladder (space, fewer pelvic injuries). Secure it
   with a stabiliser and put the bag at the head of the table.
 • Nasogastric or orogastric tube for upper-abdominal working space, especially on the left, where a full
   stomach hampers spleen mobilisation and access to the kidney.

PERFORMING THE PROCEDURE

Before the Initial Incision
 • Complete Box 18.1 just before insufflation. For the robot: console, vision cart and patient cart plugged
   into different circuits; cables connected; self-test and homing complete; 3D endoscope calibrated, black/
   white-balanced, target-aligned; patient cart draped.

Achieving Transperitoneal Access and Establishing the Pneumoperitoneum
Closed Techniques
 • Veress needle: 70- or 120-mm, 14-gauge, 2-mm outer diameter. Pre-checks: blunt tip springs back; saline
   passes; at 2 L/min the pressure stays ≤2 mm Hg; no leak at the hub when the tip is occluded.
 • Grasp it at midshaft and pass it perpendicularly. Two points of resistance: fascia, then peritoneum.

 • In a large series of blind Veress placement (including previous surgery and obesity), there were no major

Confirming placement: (1) aspiration–irrigation–aspiration: 10-mL syringe with 5 mL saline; aspirate (blood
or bowel particles?) → inject freely → re-aspirate (nothing returns) → inject 2–3 mL more to clear omentum
from the tip → (2) drop test: saline in the open hub falls freely → (3) advancement test: advance 1 cm
without resistance (resistance = still preperitoneal) → only then insufflate, at up to 9 L/min to 15 mm Hg.
Free flow is confirmed by an initial pressure <8–10 mm Hg, with flow stopping at 15 mm Hg.
 • Towel clips or Allis clamps can steady the fascia, but do not lift the fascia: it widens the fascia–peritoneum
   gap without enlarging the abdominal space.

Open-Access Techniques
 • Hasson: easier and, early in experience, safer. It needs a larger incision and leaks gas more.
   Recommended when extensive adhesions are expected; it is as efficient as closed access and possibly
   safer after previous surgery.
Hasson sequence: 2-cm semicircular infra-umbilical incision → open fascia and peritoneum separately,
transversely, finger-sized → confirm entry by sight and touch → two 0 silk fascial stay sutures → insert the
cannula with the blunt tip → seat the funnel adapter and wrap the sutures round its struts → remove the
obturator → insufflate at maximum flow.
 • Hand-port access: safest for the inexperienced. Open a 6.5–7.5-cm incision, place the device, insufflate
   through it, and guide secondary trocars with the intra-abdominal hand (or place them under vision).

Retroperitoneal Access and the Retroperitoneal Space
Open (Hasson) retroperitoneal access (the commonest, most precise): 2–2.5-cm transverse incision in the
midaxillary line just below the tip of the 12th rib → S-retractors → incise the posterior lumbodorsal fascia,
split muscle → open the anterior thoracolumbar fascia snug to the index finger (limits leak) → finger
confirms the psoas behind and the Gerota-covered lower pole in front → finger-develop a space → balloon
inflated twice, cephalad then caudad, anterior to the psoas and posterior to Gerota's fascia → extra caudal
dilatation for ureteric work; extra cephalic subdiaphragmatic dilatation for adrenalectomy.

Extraperitoneal Access and the Extraperitoneal Space
Pelvic extraperitoneal access: 1.5–2-cm infra-umbilical incision → anterior rectus sheath opened vertically
1.5 cm → recti separated → finger tunnels caudally between the rectus and posterior sheath to the pubis →
puncture the transversalis fascia → digital prevesical space → balloon in the midline, then each side →
prevesical fat displaced, peritoneum reflected cephalad.
 • Caveat: early retroperitoneoscopy with a standard Hasson cannula caused heavy subcutaneous
   emphysema and hypercarbia. This was solved by the blunt balloon port: an internal balloon plus an
   external foam cuff give an airtight seal.

Hand-Assist Devices
 • GelPort and Endopath Dextrus maintain pneumoperitoneum with the hand inside. The cost falls on the
   surgeon: more pain and numbness of hand, wrist and forearm, and more fatigue, than standard
   laparoscopy.

Placement of Initial Trocar
 • After Veress insufflation, hold the trocar in the dominant hand with the middle finger along the shaft and
   insert with a twisting downward motion, the other hand at the skin as a brake. Perpendicular for mid/
   upper abdomen; 70° caudad for the pelvis. The hiss of CO₂ from the open side arm confirms entry. With
   an optical trocar, monitor entry with a 0° lens.
 • Some raise the pressure to 20 mm Hg for primary trocar insertion, to protect underlying structures. Data
   suggest it may not increase volume or ease insertion.
 • With open access, the Hasson cannula is the first trocar.

Hand-Assist Placement
 • As a secondary port: insufflate first (skin on stretch → smaller incision). Trace the device “footprint” and
   plan the other ports around it. Incision length in cm = surgeon's glove size (glove 7 → 7 cm).
 • Waterproof the glove–gown seam with a drape or Ioban. Use a brown glove on the intra-abdominal hand
   (less glare).

Secondary Trocar Placement
 • Inspect the whole abdomen first for access injury.
 • Avoid “crossing swords” (tips meet) and “rollover”. Aim for triangulation: a four-point diamond around
   the target.
 • Suturing geometry: needle drivers <55° to the horizontal, 25–45° between instruments.
 • Point each port at the target, so constant redirection does not widen the tract and cause emphysema.

 • Standard approach: under vision with a 30° lens turned 180° to view the wall. Transilluminate with lights
   dimmed to avoid wall vessels. Make a skin incision just wide enough; twisting insertion; suture-anchor
   non-retaining ports with No. 2 non-absorbable sutures.
 • Never use a metal trocar with a plastic retaining ring: stray current cannot dissipate into the wall, so it
   injures viscera out of sight (capacitive coupling).
 • Hand-assist approach: the intra-abdominal finger guides each non-bladed trocar, which is fast and safe.

Trocar Configuration and Robotic Considerations
 • Detailed configurations are in Chapter 142 (upper tract) and Chapter 161 (pelvis). The rule: minimise
   intra- and extracorporeal clashing.
 • Robot: camera 8 mm (Xi) or 12 mm (Si); arm ports 8 mm; a 12-mm port with an 8-mm reducer for the
   robotic stapler. Ports 8–10 cm apart. Put the assistant's port caudal to the arms, not between them.
   Advance robotic ports until the thick black line is below the abdominal wall.

Laparoscopic Instrumentation
Instruments for Visualization
 • Four components: laparoscope, light source, camera, monitor.
 • Laparoscopes: 2.7–10 mm; 0° or 30° (range 0–45°). The 30° lens shows more and can look round
   structures. Deflectable tip (up to 100° in four directions; needs an adept assistant). Variable-view prism
   (0–120°, standard eyepiece).
 • Rod-lens with fibreoptic light versus chip-on-tip (CCD) digital scopes, which give a better image and need
   no separate light cord.
 • Fogging: pre-warm (warm saline or thermos), antifog solution or povidone-iodine, all-in-one defogger. Dry
   the eyepiece–camera junction (not an issue with digital scopes). White-balance.
 • 3D: two-lens binocular systems give depth perception, chiefly via the da Vinci InSite system (surgeon-
   controlled, 0° or 30°).

Instrumentation for Grasping and Blunt Dissection
 • Mostly 5 mm (range 3–12 mm). Single-action (one jaw moves) versus double-action (both jaws move).
 • Tips: blunt, pointed (dolphin), straight (duckbill), curved (Maryland), angled. Atraumatic (serrated or
   smooth, e.g., 3-cm bowel forceps) versus traumatic (toothed). Handles are locking or non-locking; some
   articulate.
 • The suction tip, the heel of the hook and Kittner (“peanut”) dissectors (5 and 10 mm) give rapid blunt
   dissection. Kittners do not conduct current, so they can safely lift a line of tissue for division.

Instrumentation for Incising and Hemostasis
 • Cutting mechanisms differ. Electrocautery and laser heat cells until internal gas pressure ruptures them.
   Ultrasonic devices use a blade vibrating at 25–55 kHz over up to 100 µm. Haemostasis is always by
   denatured protein: via current or light (heat), or via mechanical vibration (ultrasonic).
 • Scissors: shorter blades; hooked (sutures), micro (spatulating the ureter), curved (dissection); replaceable
   tips keep them sharp; some articulate to 90°.

Instrumentation for Suturing and Tissue Anastomosis
 • Needle drivers: one fixed jaw; ratchet, spring or Castroviejo lock; straight or pistol grip; articulating
   drivers improve angles. The Endo Stitch (10 mm) is used less as skills and robots spread.
 • Lapra-Ty clips: 3.5-mm absorbable polydioxanone clips that act as a knot, holding up to 14 days under
   low–mid tension. They hold best on 1-0 to 3-0 polyglactin; avoid with 4-0 and with tension on 3-0
   monofilament (slippage). Uses: single or running sutures, renorrhaphy bolsters.
 • Barbed suture (V-Loc; Quill, which is bidirectional): knotless, does not slide back. Popularised for the
   vesicourethral anastomosis: meta-analysis shows shorter operative time with comparable
   complications.

Instrumentation for Specimen Entrapment and Morcellation
 • LapSac (1990; nylon with polyurethane lining): most tear- and leak-resistant, holds up to 2 kg, suited to
   morcellation, but difficult to deploy. Endocatch (10 and 15 mm): self-opening metal frame, drawstring.
   Spring-wire sacks make scooping easy for intact removal through a hand-port or a port enlarged to 5–7
   cm.
 • Manual morcellation: finger, ring forceps or Kelly clamp. Ring forceps are preferred because they are
   least likely to puncture the sack.

Robotic Instrumentation
 • EndoWrist: 7 degrees of freedom, 90° articulation. Most 8-mm instruments have a 10-use limit (record
   the “lives” left).
 • Incising and haemostasis: round-tip and Potts scissors; monopolar HotShears, hook and spatula; bipolar
   Maryland, fenestrated, curved, micro, long and force; Harmonic ACE; Vessel Sealer Extend and
   SynchroSeal (E-100 generator).
 • Graspers: ProGrasp (most used in urology), Cadière, tenaculum, Cobra and others. An Allen bolt opens

Renal Effects of the Pneumoperitoneum
 • Oliguria and anuria come from renal vein compression and direct parenchymal compression, not
   hormones or ureteric compression. Cortical flow falls with pressure; medullary flow rises up to 20 mm
   Hg, then falls.
 • Management: keep pressure ≤10 mm Hg. If needed, give furosemide, mannitol 12.5–25 g and dopamine
   2 µg/kg/min to keep urine output >100 mL/hour, rather than fluid boluses, which cause overload and
   oedema.

Effects on Mesenteric Blood Flow and Intestinal Motility
 • Splanchnic flow falls (mesenteric vessels, liver, pancreas, stomach, spleen, bowel). Rarely, mesenteric
   thrombosis develops over days.
 • Laparoscopy causes less ileus than open surgery, perhaps via hypercarbia. Gut perfusion is unchanged at
   15 mm Hg. Bacterial translocation rises with pressure in rats. There is no increase in reflux or
   regurgitation.

Acid–Base Metabolic Effects of Pneumoperitoneum
 • Respiratory acidosis comes from transperitoneal CO₂ absorption (dead space is unchanged). In normal
   patients, increase minute ventilation; in COPD it is dangerous.
 • COPD: arterial blood gases for any procedure with >1 hour of insufflation, continued in recovery.
 • CO₂ is absorbed transperitoneally through the peritoneum, and extraperitoneally through fat, connective
   tissue and disrupted lymphatics. Absorption rises over the first 30–60 minutes, then plateaus.
 • Which route absorbs more is debated. A sealed balloon-tip cannula removes any clinical difference.
 • Monitor end-tidal CO₂ and saturation; check gases in long cases and high-risk patients (airway disease,
   renal failure, heart failure, old age).
 • Rising end-tidal CO₂ → the anaesthetist raises rate and volume, and the surgeon lowers the pressure or
   desufflates.

   by CT (bowel above the fascia). Repair laparoscopically, with general surgery if strangulated.
 • Hernia prevention: blunt trocars; closure rules as tabulated above. Hand-assist midline sites herniate in
   4–7%: close with interrupted non-absorbable sutures. Transverse beats vertical midline fascial incisions;
   in robotic prostatectomy, switching to horizontal camera and extraction incisions cut hernias from 5.4% to
   0.4%.
 • DVT and pulmonary embolism (PE): no more common than after open surgery, and no randomised trials
   exist.
     ◦ American Urological Association (AUA) Best Practice Statement: pneumatic compression for all, from
       before surgery to 48–72 hours after. High risk (morbid obesity, smoking, previous DVT): add heparin
       5,000 units 2 hours before and every 12 hours after.
     ◦ In upper retroperitoneal surgery, compression alone performed as well as heparin, and heparin may
       add bleeding (non-randomised data).
 • Wound infection: rare, except at hand-assist sites (about 9%). Prevent with antisepsis, port irrigation and
   careful closure.
 • Rhabdomyolysis (about 0.4% overall, about 1% retroperitoneal): men, long renal cases, kidney rest
   throughout. It presents in recovery with severe downside hip pain and brown urine, with creatine kinase
   >5,000 units. Padding is unproven. Prevent by limiting the kidney rest to <1 hour, avoiding hypotension,
   and keeping cases under 5 hours.

Late Postoperative Complications
 • After 3 weeks, complications are rare: mainly lymphatic and incisional hernia.

 KEY POINTS: COMPLICATIONS AND TROUBLESHOOTING IN LAPAROSCOPIC AND ROBOTIC SURGERY
   • Start with low-risk, normal-habitus patients.
   • Gas embolism: abrupt end-tidal CO₂ rise, saturation fall, then end-tidal CO₂ collapse.
   • Four thermal mechanisms: direct activation, direct coupling, capacitive coupling, insulation failure.
   • Plan ports to avoid crossing swords, striking handles, rollover and arm collisions.

Concept — Complications follow the operation's chronology: entry (needle, first trocar), insufflation (gas in
the wrong place), dissection (energy and traction), exit (entrapment, bleeding) and recovery (hernia,
rhabdomyolysis, delayed bowel necrosis). Each phase has a characteristic sign, and the dangerous injuries
are the ones that present days later.

Equipment for Practicing Laparoscopic and Robotic Surgery
 • Models range from inanimate boxes to VR, live animals and cadavers. Use non-living models unless a live
   one is essential.
 • Training box: opaque top with ports, monitor, light source, camera. Commercial boxes are costly; mobile-
   phone and 3D-printed options (e.g., iTrainer) exist.
 • Set up as in theatre: camera between the instruments, 25–45° between instruments, <55° to the
   horizontal. A camera driver also trains camera skills. Practise with 3-0 suture on an SH needle.
 • Inanimate drills use paper, sponge, glove and tubing. Non-living animal tissue is realistic and cheap but
   messy. A chicken oesophagus and stomach works for vesicourethral anastomosis and pyeloplasty
   practice.

Formal Training Programs
 • Competence = technical plus non-technical skills (judgement, anticipation, economy of motion).
   Residency has been more “learning” than “teaching”. Safety, efficiency and cost pressures favour formal
   programmes.
 • Mini-residency or mini-fellowship (typically 5 days): didactics, case observation, inanimate and animate
   laboratory, then proctoring. About three-quarters were doing laparoscopic renal surgery at 3 years, and
   about 90% robotic prostatectomy after a 5-day course.

 KEY POINTS: TRAINING AND PRACTICING LAPAROSCOPIC AND ROBOTIC SURGERY
   • Skills training outside the operating room prepares the learner for safe, effective surgery.

Concept — Training moves skill acquisition off the patient: from box to VR to animal or cadaver, then
proctored practice. Each step adds realism at greater cost, and validated metrics decide progression.

CONCLUSION
 • Procedures unthinkable in 1990 are now routine. The robot extends complex reconstruction to more
   urologists and often improves precision and outcome.
 • Mastery of the basics (pneumoperitoneum physiology, access, complication management) is what makes
   this possible: Osler's “treatments that harm less.”
