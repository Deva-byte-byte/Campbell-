Chapter 3 — Indications and Interpretation of Laboratory
Testing
 • Serum, urinary and radiological studies complement examination; knowing when to order each from the
   history and findings identifies pathology.
 • Urinalysis is among the commonest laboratory studies, sometimes performed in the office. Dipsticks
   persist in small clinics and as backup; most urine now runs on semi-automated systems using the same
   reagents.

URINALYSIS
 • Fundamental, non-invasive test for almost all patients with urinary symptoms; clues to infection,
   metabolic derangement, hydration and wide-ranging disease.
 • A complete urinalysis has three parts: gross examination → biochemical analysis → microscopic analysis
   of the urinary sediment.
 • Microscopy of the sediment is indicated for: an abnormal dipstick (e.g., microscopic haematuria);
   suspected or known nephrolithiasis; acute kidney injury (AKI); the initial evaluation of chronic kidney
   disease (CKD).

Collection of Urinary Specimens
 • Wash hands → cleanse genitalia → midstream specimen into a sterile, dry container.
 • Examine within 2 hours at room temperature, else refrigerate at 2–8 °C and rewarm before examination.
   Delay causes bacterial overgrowth, pH change and disintegration of red blood cell (RBC) and white
   blood cell (WBC) casts; refrigeration causes crystal precipitation that may not redissolve.
Males
 • Midstream clean catch: retract foreskin → cleanse glans and meatus → start voiding into toilet → collect
   midstream in a wide-mouth sterile container.
Females
 • Routine: separate labia → cleanse introitus and meatus → midstream. When sterility matters, consider a
   catheterised specimen.
Neonates, Infants and Children
 • Depends on toilet training: trained children give a clean voided specimen; otherwise clean-catch void,
   adhesive sterile bag, transurethral catheterisation or suprapubic aspiration (SPA).

Gross Examination of Urine
 • Visual and physical examination: colour, turbidity and odour.

   metabolites, infection.
 • Bright red/pink → possibly active bleeding; purple/brown → old haemorrhage or retained clot; brown/
   cola → glomerular disease.

 • Purpose and trap: red or dark urine is not synonymous with blood — pigments, foods and drugs mimic
   haematuria, so colour prompts microscopy; the same agents also disturb dipstick colour development.
Turbidity
 • Commonest cause of cloudy urine: phosphaturia — benign phosphate precipitation in alkaline urine;
   intermittent, post-prandial, asymptomatic. Pyuria is the other common cause.
 • With indwelling catheters or nephrostomy tubes, smell and turbidity may influence the decision to treat
   presumed infection.
 • Rare causes: chyluria (abnormal lymphatic–urinary communication, lymph mixed with urine), lipiduria,
   hyperoxaluria, hyperuricosuria.

Odour
 • Malodour does not reliably correlate with infection, though ammonia from some urinary tract infections
   (UTIs) gives an acrid smell.
 • Sweet or fruity → ketones; maple syrup → maple syrup urine disease; musty or mousy →
   phenylketonuria.
Concept — Gross examination only generates hypotheses: every abnormal colour, turbidity or odour has a
benign mimic, so the visual finding is always confirmed chemically or microscopically.

BIOCHEMICAL EXAMINATION OF URINE (URINE DIPSTICK)
 • Reagent pads give a colorimetric change; quick, cheap. Dwell time and urine volume vary by
   manufacturer. Semi-automated analysers standardise timing and colour reading, and share every
   interference.
 • Pads: specific gravity, pH, blood, protein, glucose, ketones, urobilinogen, bilirubin, leucocyte esterase,
   nitrite.
 • Technique: fully immerse pads in fresh, uncentrifuged urine → remove quickly, drawing the edge along
   the container rim to shed excess → hold horizontally for the reaction time → compare each pad with its
   colour chart. Excess urine or vertical holding lets reagents run between pads → faulty results.
 • Outdated or exposed strips are a common cause of false results; pad discoloration before immersion
   gives them away.

Specific Gravity and Osmolality
 • Range 1.001–1.035; tracks osmolality; reflects hydration, renal function, solute load and concentrating
   ability.
 • <1.008 dilute; >1.020 concentrated; fixed at about 1.010 in acute or chronic renal insufficiency.

 • Osmolality (dissolved particles) 50–1200 mOsm/L, same influences; abnormal → test renal function.
 • Very dilute urine (below 308 mOsm) lyses RBCs, precluding reliable assessment.

pH
 • Physiological 4.5–8.0, average 5.5–6.5; methyl red + bromothymol blue read pH 5–9. <5.5 acidic; >6.5
   alkaline.
 • Urine pH mirrors serum pH by renal compensation (acidosis → acidic urine); renal tubular acidosis (RTA)
   is the exception — acidic serum, alkaline urine.

 • Diagnosis of RTA: inability to acidify urine below pH 5.5 after an acid load; low urine ammonium with
   high urine pH suggests RTA; risk of calcium phosphate stones.
 • Presumed UTI with pH >7.5 → urea-splitting organism, commonly Proteus: urease splits urea →
   ammonia → ammonium, raising pH → precipitation of magnesium ammonium phosphate (struvite) with
   calcium phosphate → rapid growth → staghorn calculi.
 • Uric acid and cystine stones form in acidic urine; urinary alkalinisation is central to therapy, with frequent
   pH monitoring to confirm adequacy.
Concept — Urine pH normally follows serum pH; its diagnostic value lies in dissociation — alkaline urine
despite acidaemia (RTA) or alkaline urine generated by bacterial chemistry (urease).

Blood/Haematuria
 • Normal: <3 erythrocytes per high-power field (HPF). A haem-positive pad means haematuria,
   haemoglobinuria or myoglobinuria — it suggests but never confirms RBCs.
 • Chemistry: peroxidase-like activity of haem oxidises a chromogen, colour proportional to haemoglobin.
 • Free haemoglobin/myoglobin → uniform field colour change; intact RBCs lyse on the pad → speckled
   dots; more intact cells, more dots. Detects as few as 5 RBC/µL; dots coalesce above about 250 RBC/µL.

Differential Diagnosis and Evaluation of Haematuria
    • First step: nephrological versus urological, glomerular versus non-glomerular — this decides further
      tests. Nephrological haematuria: often casts, almost always significant proteinuria.
    • Urological bleeding, even gross, rarely raises urinary protein to 100–300 mg/dL (2+ to 3+); proteinuria of
      this magnitude almost always means glomerular or tubulointerstitial disease → nephrology consultation.
    • Morphology: glomerular RBCs are dysmorphic; tubulointerstitial and urological RBCs are uniformly round
      — they may lose haemoglobin ("ghost cells") but the shape stays round.

IgA nephropathy (Berger disease)
  • Commonest cause of glomerular haematuria. Children and young adults, male predominance.
 • Haematuria after upper respiratory tract infection or exercise; most have no systemic symptoms; gross
   haematuria intermittent, microscopic may be constant.
 • Chronic; prognosis mostly excellent, but a minority progress to renal insufficiency.
 • Poor prognosis: older age at onset, abnormal initial renal function, persistent proteinuria, hypertension.
 • Pathology: focal or segmental mesangial proliferation; IgA, IgG and β1c-globulin deposits (mesangial IgA/
   IgG also occurs in other GNs). Role of IgA uncertain; viral trigger suspected, unproven; exercise link
   unexplained.
 • Mimics: Henoch–Schönlein purpura, SLE, bacterial endocarditis, Goodpasture syndrome.
 • RBC casts establish glomerular origin; without casts, a urological evaluation excludes a urinary tract
   source and confirms bilateral renal origin.
 • Confirmed by renal biopsy. Once established, repeat haematuria evaluations are generally not indicated.
 • No curative treatment; management is supportive (renin–angiotensin blockade), and renal function
   remains stable in most.
Concept — Glomerular bleeding announces itself by what crosses a damaged filter — dysmorphic cells, casts
and protein — and each clinical clue points to the immune process causing the damage.
Nonglomerular Haematuria
Medical/nonsurgical
 • Renal-origin causes are tubulointerstitial, renovascular or systemic. Urinalysis: circular RBCs, no RBC
   casts, frequently significant proteinuria — which separates these nephrological diseases from urological
   disease.

 • Therapeutic anticoagulation does not predispose to haematuria; urological disease is found as often as
   in controls. Never attribute haematuria to the anticoagulant — full work-up.
 • Exercise-induced: runners (>10 km); at end of run, clears rapidly with rest; renal (dysmorphic cells in
   some) or bladder (punctate haemorrhagic lesions at cystoscopy); may be the first sign of IgA
   nephropathy.
Surgical
 • Collecting-system pathology: urological tumours, stones, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), UTIs.
 • Circular RBCs, no casts; "essential" haematuria suggested by absent significant proteinuria — though
   renal disease may also lack proteinuria.
 • Asymptomatic microscopic haematuria (AMH) = ≥3 RBC/HPF on a properly collected specimen in the
   absence of an obvious benign cause (AUA/SUFU 2020). The guideline covers diagnosis, initial evaluation,
   risk stratification, follow-up and an algorithm.
Concept — Once glomerular disease is excluded, proteinuria is the discriminator between renal-parenchymal
and collecting-system bleeding: heavy bleeding from the urinary tract barely raises urinary protein.

Proteinuria
 • Normal 80–150 mg/day; concentration seldom >20 mg/dL (dilute urine can hide significant proteinuria
   below this).
 • First sign of renovascular, glomerular or tubulointerstitial disease, or overflow (multiple myeloma); also
   non-renal and physiological (strenuous exercise).
 • Normal mix: ~30% albumin, 30% globulins, 40% tissue proteins (chiefly Tamm–Horsfall); electrophoresis
   of the shifted profile points to the cause.

Glucose and Ketones
 • Diabetes screen. Filtered glucose is reabsorbed proximally up to the renal threshold ≈ serum glucose 180
   mg/dL.
 • Chemistry (double sequential reaction): glucose + glucose oxidase → gluconic acid + hydrogen peroxide →
   peroxidase oxidises chromogen → colour. Specific for glucose; no cross-reaction with other sugars.
 • Sensitivity falls with rising specific gravity and temperature.
 • Drug-induced glycosuria: sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors (gliflozins).
 • Ketones appear when carbohydrate reserves are depleted and fat is broken down: diabetic ketoacidosis,
   pregnancy, starvation, rapid weight loss. Acetoacetic acid, acetone and β-hydroxybutyric acid are
   excreted; ketones appear in urine before blood.
 • Dipstick: sodium nitroprusside + acetoacetic acid → purple; detects acetoacetate at 5–10 mg/dL but not
   acetone or β-hydroxybutyrate. Laboratory analysers quantify β-hydroxybutyrate down to 0.05 mmol/L.
 • Glucose-positive dipstick → test ketones; diabetes suggested.

LEUKOCYTE ESTERASE AND NITRITE TESTS
 • Leucocyte esterase → WBCs; nitrite → bacteriuria; both are used to screen for UTI.
 • Most accurate diagnosis of infection: sediment microscopy for pyuria + urine culture.
 • A "directed" urinalysis that reflexes to culture when abnormal reduces unnecessary cultures and avoids
   missing specimens that need one.
 • Leucocyte esterase positive, nitrite negative → consider non-infectious inflammation; obtain microscopy
   and culture before any empirical antibiotic.

Urinary Sediment
Obtaining and Preparing the Specimen
 • Clean-catch midstream; harder in women (introital leucocytes and bacteria).
 • Catheterised sample for culture when findings are equivocal for true UTI, when contamination is
   expected or hard to avoid, or when concerning results are at odds with a benign clinical picture.
 • First-voided specimen is the specimen of choice; examine within 1 hour.
Preparation: centrifuge 10–15 mL for 5 minutes at 3000 rpm → pour off supernatant → resuspend sediment
in 0.3 mL saline by tapping the tube → pipette the residual fluid onto the slide (pouring deposits excess) →
ideal volume 0.01–0.02 mL → lower the coverslip edge first so fluid rises by capillary action and air is
expelled. Dropping the coverslip flat disperses urine and traps bubbles that distort examination.
Microscopy Technique
 • Low (×100) and high (×400) power; ≥10–20 fields; vary fine focus; oil immersion seldom needed. Scan
   the whole coverslip at low power, especially the edges, where casts concentrate.

Bacteria
 • Normally absent (asymptomatic bacteriuria rises with age); in a fresh uncontaminated specimen, suggest
   UTI.
 • Each HPF views 1/20,000–1/50,000 mL, so 1 bacterium/HPF ≈ >30,000/mL and 5 bacteria/HPF ≈ 100,000
   (105)/mL — the standard UTI threshold in a clean-catch specimen.
 • That threshold applies mainly to women (frequent contamination); any bacteria in a properly collected
   male midstream specimen → culture.
 • High-power morphology: gram-negative rods — bacillary; streptococci — beaded chains; staphylococci
   — clumps.
Yeast
 • Candida albicans is commonest; oval biconcave shape mimics erythrocytes and calcium oxalate crystals
   — distinguished by budding and hyphae.
 • Seen in diabetes mellitus, chronic illness, indwelling catheters, or as contaminants in vaginal candidiasis.

 KEY POINTS
   • A complete urinalysis includes gross examination, dipstick chemical analysis and microscopic analysis of
     the urinary sediment.
   • A dipstick alone is inadequate for the diagnosis of microscopic haematuria.
   • AMH is defined as ≥3 erythrocytes/HPF on a properly collected specimen in the absence of an obvious
     benign cause.
   • Haematuria should be stratified into glomerular, non-glomerular, medical and surgical causes.
   • A dipstick positive for only leucocyte esterase or nitrite, but not both, should be confirmed with
     microscopy and urine culture.
   • A catheterised specimen should be considered when uncontaminated voided specimens are difficult to
     obtain.


Serum Laboratory Studies
 • Electrolytes, creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) define renal function in obstruction
   and medical renal disease.
 • Urgency of intervention for obstruction is determined by renal function (and subsequent imaging).
   Check electrolytes in postobstructive diuresis.
 • Presence or history of renal disease informs evaluation of haematuria, proteinuria and anatomical
   pathology.
 • Serum potassium, carbon dioxide (bicarbonate) and liver transaminases assist evaluation of malignancy or
   multi-system metabolic derangement.
 • Albumin: non-specific marker of debility and postoperative recoverability; as an acute-phase reactant it is
   artificially low early in infection or systemic inflammation.
 • Complete blood count (CBC: RBC, WBC ± differential, haemoglobin, haematocrit, platelets): perioperative,
   malignancy follow-up, infection work-up; haemoglobin assesses anaemia in ongoing haematuria.

Prostate-Specific Antigen
 • Important, controversial; interpret with digital rectal examination.
 • Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) = human kallikrein peptidase 3 (hK3), a serine protease of the kallikrein
   family, produced by prostatic luminal epithelial cells; highly specific for the prostate gland (organ, not
   cancer).
 • Sensitive for prostatic pathology → useful in BPH and prostatitis; correlates with prostate volume and
   prostatic inflammation.
 • One of the most reliable tests for following prostate cancer after treatment — radiation, radical
   prostatectomy, hormonal therapy or immunotherapy.

Urinary Markers in Bladder Cancer
 • Cytology, formerly standard screening for urothelial cell carcinoma (UCC), is no longer recommended for
   screening or initial AMH evaluation (AUA/SUFU 2020).
 • Uses: surveillance of known UCC; persistent microhaematuria after a negative initial evaluation;
   suspicious radiological abnormalities; suspected high-grade malignancy.
 • Highly sensitive for high-grade UCC; sensitivity falls markedly for low-grade; highly specific for high-
   grade disease.
 • Nuclear matrix protein 22 (NMP22), bladder tumour antigen (BTA) stat, ImmunoCyt and UroVysion
   fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) are available but not recommended for routine use or screening;
   used in selected cases, generally not at initial evaluation.

Nephrolithiasis
 • AUA medical management of kidney stones guideline (2014; validity confirmed 2019): every newly
   diagnosed renal or ureteric stone patient → screening evaluation: medical and dietary history + serum
   chemistries (basic metabolic panel, calcium; possibly intact parathyroid hormone [PTH] and uric acid) +
   urinalysis; stone analysis at least once.
 • High-risk or interested first-time stone formers → 24-hour urine on usual diet: at least volume, pH,
   calcium, oxalate, uric acid, citrate, sodium, potassium, creatinine; ± cystine screening, magnesium, sulfate,
   urea nitrogen.
 • Fast and calcium-load testing are no longer routine — subtyping hypercalciuria does not change
   treatment.

Adrenal Disorders and Lesions
 • Cushing syndrome, Conn syndrome and phaeochromocytoma meet urology via rising incidentalomas.
   Every adrenal incidentaloma is tested for function: cortisol and catecholamine excess in all; aldosterone
   in hypertensive patients.

   • Serum creatinine and GFR should be ordered when renal obstruction is suspected.
   • PSA is a very sensitive test for prostate conditions such as BPH and prostatitis and correlates most
     often with prostate volume.
   • Urine cytology is not recommended during initial evaluation and screening for AMH; it is quite specific

   • AFP, βhCG and LDH should be obtained whenever testicular malignancy is suspected.

   • The functional status of all adrenal masses should be biochemically assessed for hypercortisolism,

Uroflowmetry and Ultrasound for Postvoid Residual
 • Uroflowmetry (standing or seated): maximum flow rate (Qmax), mean flow, voided volume, curve —
   assesses bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) in suspected BPH.

Radiologic Imaging
 • Basic imaging in the office; computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and nuclear
   imaging at a radiology facility.
Ultrasonography
 • Renal ultrasonography: size, shape, hydronephrosis, cysts and masses, echogenicity, vasculature;
   increasingly performed by urologists in the office.
 • Cortical echogenicity greater than liver or spleen → medical renal disease.
 • Stone history or flank pain → ultrasonography for obstruction, weighing CT detail against convenience.
 • Renal ultrasonography alone is not sufficient for the haematuria work-up — cross-sectional imaging is
   needed.
 • Bladder ultrasonography rarely ordered diagnostically; assesses large masses, large stones and volume,
   with limited information.

 • Ureteric ultrasonography is very difficult — do not use it to diagnose ureteric stones or malignancy; it
   may detect severe hydroureter.
 • Prostate ultrasonography: mostly with biopsy; increasingly with MRI — software fusion in real time or
   cognitive fusion — for lesion targeting; also volume in BPH/obstruction.
 • Seminal vesicles and ejaculatory duct: full seminal vesicles with a dilated ejaculatory duct inform further
   testing or treatment in ejaculatory and fertility disorders.
Computed Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging
 • Cross-sectional abdominopelvic imaging, with or without contrast, for suspected upper tract and bladder
   conditions.
 • Haematuria (microscopic or gross): contrast CT, or MRI if iodinated contrast is significantly
   contraindicated.
 • Non-contrast CT is the most sensitive test and gold standard for urinary stones; stone history + flank
   pain → non-contrast CT abdomen and pelvis.
 • MRI is not useful for diagnosis or follow-up of stones; it excels in soft tissue — renal malignancy, tumour
   thrombus, adrenal pathology, cystic lesions, other abdominal, pelvic and retroperitoneal soft-tissue
   disease.
Intravenous Pyelogram and Plain Radiographs
 • Intravenous pyelogram (IVP) and plain films are largely eclipsed by CT (including low-dose protocols) and
   MRI.
 • IVP when dynamic imaging of upper tract excretion and obstruction is needed and contrast CT is
   unavailable.
 • Plain radiograph of kidneys, ureters and bladder (KUB): initial evaluation and follow-up of non-emergent
   ureteric or renal stones; more than 70% of stones are radio-opaque.
 • Sequence: non-emergent ureteric stone → initial KUB → stone visible → follow up with KUB rather than
   CT. Non-obstructing renal stones on surveillance can be followed by KUB; symptoms decide when more
   complex imaging is needed.

 KEY POINTS
   • Renal ultrasonography can provide basic screening information on hydronephrosis and medical renal
     disease but is not an adequate stand-alone study for the work-up of haematuria.
   • CT without contrast of the abdomen and pelvis is the gold standard for detecting urinary stones.
   • A KUB is a useful and easy test for the follow-up of existing non-emergent radio-opaque urinary stones.

Concept — Imaging is chosen by the question, not the organ: calcium is best seen by CT and followed by
plain film, soft tissue by MRI, and ultrasonography screens but never completes a haematuria work-up.

SUMMARY
 • Laboratory testing is critical to the initial evaluation and subsequent management of urological patients;
   understanding when to order each test enables efficient care.
 • History + examination + a properly collected urinalysis build the differential diagnosis, direct further
   laboratory tests, office procedures and imaging, and guide treatment.
