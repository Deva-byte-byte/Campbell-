Chapter 64 — Paediatric Urological Oncology: Pelvis,
Bladder, Testis and Genitalia
 • Tumours of the pelvis, bladder, gonads and genitalia are rare in children. Rhabdomyosarcoma is the
   commonest, but the differential is long and spans benign, malignant and intermediate-biology tumours.
 • Children with cancer do not present only to high-volume cancer centres, so every paediatric urologist
   needs working knowledge of these tumours and their management.

RHABDOMYOSARCOMA
 • Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is an aggressive soft-tissue sarcoma derived from rhabdomyoblasts (striated-
   muscle precursor cells). Because striated muscle is ubiquitous, it can arise almost anywhere; the
   commonest sites are the head and neck, the extremities and the genitourinary (GU) tract.
 • Five-year survival for GU-RMS exceeds 80% — below Wilms tumour (90% or better) but above RMS at
   non-GU sites (about 70%).
 • The GU sites are bladder/prostate (BP), paratesticular (PT) and vulvovaginal (VV).

A Brief History of Rhabdomyosarcoma Collaborative Research
 • The Intergroup Rhabdomyosarcoma Study Group (IRSG) formed in 1972; survival improved as a direct
   result of collaboration. Survival was below 25% before consistent multiagent chemotherapy, popularised
   by IRS-I (Intergroup Rhabdomyosarcoma Study I).
 • Overall survival (OS) for localised RMS rose from 55% (IRS-I) to 71% (IRS-IV) through better staging, risk
   stratification, improved local therapy and better supportive care.
 • Protocols (more than 5200 children): IRS-I 1972–1978 → IRS-II 1978–1984 → IRS-III 1984–1991 → IRS-IV
   Pilot (advanced disease only) 1987–1991 → IRS-IV 1991–1997 → IRS-V, the “D-series” (D9803
   intermediate risk, D9602 low risk), 1997–2005.
 • In 2000, IRSG merged with the Pediatric Oncology Group, the National Wilms Tumor Study Group and the
   Children’s Cancer Group to form the Children’s Oncology Group (COG); IRSG became the COG Soft Tissue
   Sarcoma Committee, which took over D9803 and D9602. COG is affiliated with more than 200 hospitals;
   over 90% of North American paediatric cancer patients are treated at a COG facility.
 • Europe has several groups, including single-nation groups in the UK, France and Italy.

Epidemiology
 • Commonest sarcoma of childhood and adolescence, about 3% of all paediatric cancers.
 • In the United States 400–500 children develop RMS each year; 20–30% arise in GU organs.
 • Age-adjusted incidence about 4.6 per million, stable for decades.
 • Most cases occur in the first decade, with a bimodal distribution: a major peak in the first 2 years and a
   smaller adolescent peak.
 • Survival is slightly higher in non-Hispanic White than non-Hispanic Black patients; a socioeconomic
   survival gradient was not statistically significant.

Histopathology and Molecular Characterisation
 • Four histological subtypes exist; only embryonal (ERMS) and alveolar (ARMS) typically occur in GU
   organs. Pleomorphic and spindle cell/sclerosing RMS are rare in children, particularly at GU sites.
 • Local infiltration and regional nodal spread are common; distant metastases are present in 16% at
   diagnosis (lung, bone, bone marrow).
 • The fusion is a translocation between PAX3 (chromosome 2) or PAX7 (chromosome 1) and FOXO1
   (chromosome 13), producing PAX3/7–FOXO1.

Cancer Genetics
 • The most significant recent development is the discovery of oncogenic fusion proteins from
   chromosomal translocations; PAX3/7–FOXO1 is the commonest and most clinically useful.
 • Other fusions, of uncertain clinical import (INSTRuCT biobanking should clarify):

Biomarkers
 • PAX3/7–FOXO1 fusion status is currently the most clinically meaningful biomarker.
 • Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) at diagnosis in fusion-negative intermediate-risk RMS (many BP) was
   associated with significantly worse EFS and OS in a COG analysis, independent on multivariable analysis.
   If prospectively confirmed, ctDNA may enter risk stratification.
Concept — risk assignment is moving from anatomy and histology to molecular biology: fusion status now,
ctDNA and MYOD1 next; and young age at diagnosis should prompt a search for germline predisposition.

 KEY POINTS
   • Most RMS is sporadic; some arises from predisposition syndromes such as Beckwith-Wiedemann or
     DICER1.
   • PAX3/7–FOXO1 fusion status is more clinically relevant than histology.


Presentation and Diagnosis
 • Presentation is determined by site:
      ◦ BP-RMS: haematuria, stranguria or frank urinary retention; botryoid tumour may protrude from the
        urethral meatus. A 3- to 5-year-old boy with haematuria and new-onset stranguria needs immediate
        urological and imaging assessment.
      ◦ PT-RMS: palpable scrotal mass; may be found incidentally during evaluation for trauma or torsion.
      ◦ VV-RMS: bloody vaginal discharge or a prolapsing vaginal/introital mass.

Initial Evaluation
 • Sequence: US first (new mass, haematuria, obstructive symptoms) → mass suspected → definitive CT or
   MRI, including fine-cut assessment of the site’s nodal basin:
      ◦ BP: pelvic and retroperitoneal nodes up to the renal vessels.
      ◦ PT: retroperitoneal nodes.
      ◦ VV: inguinal, pelvic and retroperitoneal nodes.
 • Chest CT is mandatory — lung is the commonest metastatic site.
 • Bone scan, bone marrow biopsy and aspirates are indicated for most RMS, but COG now recommends
   reduced staging in fusion-negative disease: non-invasive (T1), small (≤5 cm), N0 ERMS has virtually no
   chance of bone metastasis, so ERMS without nodal or lung metastases needs no bone scan or marrow
   evaluation.
 • Positron emission tomography–CT (PET-CT): role in staging and response controversial; whether it could
   supplement or replace lymph node dissection is a question of particular interest to urologists.
 • Baseline laboratory tests: full blood count, electrolytes and renal function, liver function, urinalysis.

Stage, Group and Risk Stratification
 • Three layers: pretreatment TNM (tumour, node, metastasis) stage → post-biopsy/resection surgical-
   pathological clinical group → composite risk group (low, intermediate, high) from stage, group, age and
   fusion status.
 • Stage sorts favourable versus unfavourable sites: in the GU tract, PT and VV are favourable; BP is
   unfavourable. Most PT and VV tumours are stage 1; BP cannot be less than stage 2. European
   collaboratives (EpSSG, CWS) use a similar system but factor site into risk stratification rather than stage.

 • Clinical group is driven by the completeness of surgical resection (post-biopsy residual disease). BP-RMS
   was historically treated by up-front exenteration; the shift to organ preservation means initial biopsy
   rather than resection, moving most BP-RMS from group I or II (R0 or R1 resection) to group III.
 • Result: BP-RMS is most commonly intermediate risk; PT-RMS is almost always low risk.
 • Age is an independent prognostic factor: under 1 year and over 10 years → significantly inferior survival
   → higher risk group.
 • International harmonisation (COG, INSTRuCT) is increasingly adding biological factors (fusion proteins) to
   clinical ones (age, size).

Concept — stage records where the tumour began, group records what the surgeon left behind, and risk
adds biology; organ-preserving biopsy deliberately places BP-RMS in group III and therefore intermediate
risk.

Treatment: General Principles of Rhabdomyosarcoma Treatment
Surgical principles and local control.
 • Local control = radiotherapy and/or surgery; chemotherapy is universal, treating widespread disease and
   preventing recurrence.
 • Complete resection of GU-RMS (except PT-RMS) is attempted only if organ preservation is possible; local
   failure and progression are the exceptions. Initial surgery is therefore usually biopsy only, so most BP-RMS
   starts chemotherapy with gross disease (group III, intermediate risk).
 • The clinical group, set by the extent of resection, determines whether radiotherapy is given and at what
   dose.

 KEY POINTS
   • PRE is excision before chemotherapy; DPE is excision after it. PRE improves survival in GU-RMS; DPE
     does not.
   • Excising a stable end-of-therapy mass does not improve survival.

Concept — in GU-RMS chemotherapy is the backbone and surgery serves organ preservation; the only
excision that changes outcome is the one done before chemotherapy.

Bladder/Prostate Rhabdomyosarcoma
 • Biopsy sequence: cystoscopic (preferred) → percutaneous → open transvesical (last resort: significant
   risk of tumour spill).
 • Cystoscopic biopsy is harder than in adult urothelial cancer: paediatric resectoscopes are 9 or 11.5 French
   with even smaller working elements. Technique: undermine a section of tumour with cutting current →
   remove it intact with biopsy forceps; avoid excess cautery, whose artefact delays and confounds the
   diagnosis.
 • Relieve obstruction early and effectively to protect renal function before systemic therapy. Sequence:
      ◦ Urethral catheter — effective for most; response is often rapid and the catheter can be removed
        within weeks.
      ◦ Ureteric obstruction → indwelling JJ stents; if a large pelvic mass prevents retrograde placement →
        percutaneous nephrostomy (best option).
      ◦ Suprapubic tube: possible, but with a primary bladder tumour risks seeding/spillage, as does open
        biopsy.
      ◦ Vesicostomy or ureterostomy: rarely helpful — surgery delays chemotherapy, the treatment most
        likely to relieve the obstruction.
 • Residual mass after chemotherapy and local control is usually not viable tumour: tumour cells involute
   leaving stroma, or differentiate into mature rhabdomyoblasts. Observe; resection does not improve
   survival; second-look or extirpative surgery should be discouraged.
 • By contrast, high-risk patients with viable tumour and no distant metastasis after chemoradiation may
   be cured by extirpative or exenterative surgery. Partial cystectomy suits local/regional disease after
   chemoradiation or early treatment failure if an adequate margin is possible without unduly compromising
   capacity and function.
 • Primary radical cystoprostatectomy/exenteration declined from virtually all patients in IRS-I to 13% in
   IRS-IV, with OS unchanged (78–83%).
 • Only 40% of IRS-IV patients who kept their bladder had “normal” bladder function; objective
   assessment (validated questionnaires, urodynamics) is scarce, so the true incidence is unknown. Detrusor
   overactivity and reduced functional bladder capacity are common; anticholinergics help frequency,
   urgency and incontinence (anecdotal).
 • Follow-up: baseline urodynamic study (UDS) 6 months after therapy → annual renal US for at least 10
   years; repeat UDS and imaging if urinary symptoms arise.

Paratesticular Rhabdomyosarcoma
 • Typically a mass within the tunica vaginalis but separate from the testis. Treat by radical orchidectomy via
   an inguinal approach.

 • If a scrotal approach was mistakenly used → excise the remaining cord via an inguinal incision as a PRE
   before chemotherapy. Hemiscrotectomy or scrotal radiation has not improved outcome and should be
   avoided — consider them only for a positive cord margin or direct scrotal skin invasion.
 • Tumour abutting but not invading the tunica vaginalis is not a positive margin; no further local
   treatment is needed.
 • Nerve-sparing staging ipsilateral retroperitoneal lymph node dissection (RPLND) before chemotherapy
   if age ≥10 years, or at any age with retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy (N1) on imaging. Sample at least
   7–13 nodes; the upper limit is the renal vessels.
 • Rationale (COG, SIOP-MMT, CWS, EpSSG data and international consensus): patients ≥10 years are much
   more likely to have retroperitoneal disease, and CT has a very high false-negative rate for
   retroperitoneal nodes.
 • No modality is proven superior; the authors prefer robotic RPLND (3–4 robotic ports and a 12-mm AirSeal
   assistant port) — anecdotally shorter stay, faster recovery and fewer chemotherapy delays.
Concept — PT-RMS is the one GU site where resection is always done, because organ preservation is not the
constraint there; the only question is nodal staging, and age ≥10 replaces imaging because CT misses nodal
disease.

Female Genital Rhabdomyosarcoma
 • Endoscopic biopsy plus chemotherapy controls the large majority. Lymph node biopsy is not
   recommended; PRE/DPE only if R0 resection is anticipated; hysterectomy or exenteration only for
   chemotherapy-unresponsive tumours.
 • Initial approach for all three sites: abdominopelvic MRI → bimanual examination under anaesthesia
   (EUA) → cystoscopy → vaginoscopy with endoscopic biopsy.

Perineal and Perianal Rhabdomyosarcoma
 • Very rare; alveolar histology more common, about 80% fusion-positive. Poor outcomes (5-year OS about
   one-half); larger tumours and older patients fare worse. A significant minority of survivors have faecal
   incontinence; some urinary frequency or incontinence.

Special Situations (e.g., Infants)
 • Infants have greater treatment-related morbidity and a higher probability of a predisposition syndrome.
 • Local control is the problem: the infant pelvis limits surgery, and EBRT is often withheld for fear of
   damaging the bony pelvis and hips → higher local failure rates, likely from reduced radiotherapy.
 • COG and European protocols allow deviation from protocol radiotherapy for fusion-negative children
   under 24 months; therapy for infants, especially congenital or neonatal RMS, remains controversial.

Late Effects of Rhabdomyosarcoma Treatments
 • Survivorship is a key focus of current protocols; the COG Late Effects Committee publishes a
   comprehensive guide.
 • Complications are frequent and often severe: second malignancy, renal and urinary dysfunction,
   hormonal disturbance, infertility and sexual dysfunction, bowel complications, psychological
   disturbance.

OTHER PAEDIATRIC BLADDER TUMOURS
Urothelial Carcinoma/Papillary Urothelial Neoplasms of Low Malignant Potential
Presentation, Diagnosis and Management
 • Most paediatric urothelial tumours are papillary urothelial neoplasms of low malignant potential
   (PUNLMP) or low-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma, depending on the classification used.
 • Most present with haematuria and are diagnosed on renal–bladder ultrasound (RB-US) or CT, typically as
   a papillary tumour of the bladder wall.
Treatment
 • Almost exclusively endoscopic: transurethral resection of bladder tumour (TURBT) with a 9- or 11-French
   paediatric resectoscope, or cold-cup biopsy if the stalk is narrow.
 • As in adults, fulgurate the base (± deep base biopsy). The authors instil immediate post-resection
   intravesical chemotherapy by institutional norm; there are no data for or against.
Outcomes
 • Adults: PUNLMP recurs in about a third after complete resection; some enlarge if unresected; malignant
   transformation or progression to high grade is exceedingly rare.
 • Children: similarly low transformation, moderate recurrence risk → follow-up needed. In the authors’
   experience recurrence after complete resection is rare (their single recurrence was in an adolescent with
   Lynch syndrome) → surveillance by RB-US.

Special Situations
Spina Bifida/Bladder Augmentation
 • Original hypothesis: mixing urine and faeces is carcinogenic; enterocystoplasty, by separating them,
   should avoid cancer. Aggressive cancers were then reported in reconstructed bladders, especially after
   gastrocystoplasty (survival about 50%) → proposals for routine endoscopic screening and a moratorium
   on gastrocystoplasty.

Bladder Exstrophy
 • As with spina bifida, exstrophy carries increased bladder cancer risk: adenocarcinoma, squamous cell and
   mixed tumours are reported.
 • These cancers are rare: few respondents in a national exstrophy survey reported cancer (likely under-
   counted because deaths remove respondents, but reassuring).
 • Cancer also arises in non-augmented patients; lifetime risk in augmented patients about 2%; no
   compelling evidence that augmentation changes the likelihood or severity of bladder cancer.

 KEY POINTS
   • Paediatric urothelial tumours are rare and typically low-grade/PUNLMP.
   • Recurrence is rare and should prompt suspicion of a predisposition syndrome.
   • Spina bifida and exstrophy carry a 2–4% baseline bladder cancer risk not increased by augmentation.

Concept — the cancer risk belongs to the abnormal bladder, not to the augment; and because these
tumours are aggressive and fast-growing, periodic screening cannot catch them in time.

TESTICULAR TUMOURS
 • 1–2% of paediatric solid tumours; incidence about 1 per 100,000 under 15 years. Most occur at 2–4
   years, with a second rise at puberty.
 • Benign tumours are far commoner than in adults (38–74% of paediatric testicular tumours). Germ cell
   tumours (GCTs) comprise the vast majority at all ages; the rest are stromal.
 • Teratoma is the commonest prepubertal benign tumour; yolk sac tumour (YST) the commonest
   prepubertal malignant tumour.

Presentation and Diagnosis
 • Typically a painless testicular mass; rarely found with epididymitis, torsion, hydrocele, undescended testis
   or hernia.
 • As in adults: examination, history for risk factors, scrotal US and markers — lactate dehydrogenase (LDH),
   AFP, beta-human chorionic gonadotrophin (β-hCG). Unlike adults, check hormone levels if a hormonally
   active tumour is suspected (precocious puberty).
Imaging.
 • Doppler US; it cannot reliably distinguish benign from malignant. Anechoic cystic lesions suggest
   benignity (simple cyst, cystic dysplasia, teratoma). Epidermoid cyst: heterogeneous mass with alternating
   hypoechoic and hyperechoic keratin rings (onion skin). Benign masses typically show absent flow in
   surrounding tissue from compression.
 • Malignancy suspected → CT chest, abdomen and pelvis; timing before or after resection is controversial
   (authors: before).

Special Situations
Leukaemia and lymphoma.
 • The commonest childhood malignancies to spread to the testis. With modern intensive chemotherapy,
   testicular relapse in acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) is 2% or less → biopsy, orchidectomy or
   testicular irradiation is exceedingly rarely indicated.
Disorders of sex development.
 • Data are scant despite research dating to the 19th century. The pooled risk of 12% is misleading — risk
   varies widely by diagnosis.
 • Y-chromosome material, particularly the sex-determining region Y (SRY) and gonadoblastoma on Y
   chromosome (GBY) regions, significantly raises risk; XY disorders of sex development (DSD), especially
   complete and partial gonadal dysgenesis, carry very high risk.

Testicular microlithiasis.
 • Controversial across all ages. An EAU Paediatric Urology Guidelines panel systematic review of several

Treatment
Surgery.
 • Normal preoperative markers → testis-sparing partial orchidectomy, as for GCTs. Multiple systematic
   reviews give no justification for radical orchidectomy in prepubertal boys.
 • Because scrotal US cannot reliably separate stromal from germ cell tumours, the urologist should
   proceed confidently with partial orchidectomy in a prepubertal child with normal markers.
 • Leydig, Sertoli and juvenile granulosa cell tumours behave more aggressively after puberty → radical
   orchidectomy is likely justified in Tanner 5 adolescents and young adults.
Systemic treatment.
 • Chemotherapy is rarely indicated: these tumours are rarely malignant before puberty, surgery alone
   usually suffices, and there are little to no data for any regimen.
Outcomes
 • Generally excellent: recurrence 1–7%, metastasis rarely reported in prepubertal children, versus about
   10% aggressive behaviour in adults.

 KEY POINTS
   • Partial orchidectomy is the most appropriate surgical option for testicular stromal tumours in
     prepubertal children.

Concept — puberty is the dividing line: prepubertal testicular tumours are overwhelmingly benign, so normal
markers license testis-sparing surgery for germ cell and stromal lesions alike; after puberty, adult biology and
adult radicalism return.

Sacrococcygeal Teratoma
 • Sacrococcygeal teratoma (SCT): rare pelvic GCT arising from the coccyx, incidence about 1 in 40,000;
   female predominance; many diagnosed prenatally.
 • Often managed by paediatric surgeons, but pelvic by definition and treatment affects the bladder →
   paediatric urologists must be involved.
 • Neurogenic bladder in 36–51%, commonly presenting as urinary retention; most need intervention, from
   clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC) to bladder reconstruction.
 • Altman classification: type I (predominantly external, minimal sacral involvement) is commonest; types
   II–IV (significant internal abdominopelvic component) carry much higher neurogenic bladder and bowel
   risk.
 • Proposed risk-based monitoring: annual RB-US for all until toilet training; UDS for higher Altman types,
   neurogenic signs or RB-US abnormality.

Paratesticular Tumours
 • RMS is by far the commonest paratesticular tumour. Others: epididymal cysts (common), adenomatoid
   tumour, epididymal cystadenoma (classically von Hippel–Lindau, VHL, syndrome; also sporadic),
   Müllerian cysts.
 • Testis-sparing excision is generally appropriate, but an inguinal approach is mandatory and frozen
   section must be confirmed before closure, because PT-RMS may be misdiagnosed.

OTHER FEMALE GENITOURINARY TRACT TUMOURS
 • With more fellowship-trained paediatric gynaecologists, urologists are not always primarily responsible,
   but ovarian and adnexal lesions surface as incidental findings or during symptom workup, so familiarity is
   required.

Cystic Lesions
 • Up to 98% of female newborns have small ovarian cysts on US; clinically significant newborn cysts are
   rare (about 1 in 2500 births); 2–3% of preschool and school-age girls have incidental cysts.
 • Cysts reflect maternal hormones and involute spontaneously as levels fall. Intervene only if
   indeterminate or symptomatic.
 • Prepubertal surgical indications: persistence, concern for malignancy, or any symptom including
   hormonally induced changes.
 • Adolescents: cysts usually from dysfunctional ovulation; aim is ovarian preservation; hormonal
   manipulation common; surgery for non-resolution, persistent symptoms, or concern for teratoma or
   malignancy. Simple cysts causing mass effect or discomfort → organ-sparing surgery.
 • Malignant cysts are rare; even complex cysts are often observed.

Ovarian Malignancies
 • About 1% of childhood cancers; incidence 2.6 per 100,000 females. Classified, as in the testis, into germ
   cell, stromal and epithelial; epithelial tumours are very rare before puberty.
 • Imaging: abdominal US with a full bladder → abdominopelvic MRI.

Nonrhabdomyosarcoma Vulvovaginal Tumours
 • Primary vulval YST is reported in female toddlers (usually an adult tumour); these may instead be
   SMARCB1-deficient vulvar neoplasms, a group including epithelioid sarcoma. Very rare, poor prognosis
   → excision or biopsy with regional node dissection, then aggressive chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

Urethral Prolapse
 • Frequently mistaken for RMS: a doughnut-like circumferential swelling around the urethral meatus; in
   the United States most often in prepubertal Black girls.
 • Topical steroid or oestrogen cream is frequently curative; surgical excision rarely.
 • Neither cancerous nor precancerous → once diagnosed, no further workup or monitoring.

   • Ovarian cysts are common; ovarian cancers in girls are rare.
   • Girls with Sertoli–Leydig cell tumours should be screened for DICER1 mutations.
