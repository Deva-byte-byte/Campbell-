Chapter 19 — Energy Modalities in Urology
Chapter Notes · Bailey, Forbes, Milner and Teichman

 • Energy modalities deliver five surgical effects: cutting, tissue removal (ablation), tissue destruction,
   coagulation and fragmentation.
 • Safe outcomes require informed use of each modality. The chapter's emphasis is the tissue–energy
   interaction mechanism and the safety of each.
 • Organising perspective: the universality of the wave nature of physical processes explains most energy-
   based devices in current use.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — THE PHYSICS YOU NEED BEFORE STARTING
   Boxes titled "In plain terms" are explanatory additions for understanding. They are not Campbell-Walsh-
   Wein text; examinable content is in the notes around them.
    • Energy is the capacity to do work: heat tissue, crack a stone, move something. It is measured in joules
      (J). One joule is roughly the energy needed to lift a 100-g object by one metre.
    • Power is how fast energy is delivered, measured in watts (W) = joules per second. A 100-W laser
      delivers 100 J every second. The same energy delivered faster means more power. On a lithotripsy laser,
      power = pulse energy × pulses per second: 0.5 J × 20 Hz = 10 W.
    • Intensity is power per unit area. Sunlight spread over your hand is harmless; the same sunlight
      concentrated by a magnifying glass burns paper. The power is the same, but the intensity is far greater.
    • Pressure is force per unit area, measured in pascals or bars. 1 bar is about atmospheric pressure, so a
      lithotripter shock of 1000 bar is about a thousand atmospheres, applied for a millionth of a second.
    • A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from place to place without carrying the material with it.
      In a stadium "Mexican wave", each spectator only stands and sits in place, yet the wave travels round
      the stadium.
    • Sound (acoustic, elastic) waves: the material itself is alternately squeezed and stretched, with each
      molecule pushing its neighbour. Sound therefore needs a material (water, tissue, stone) and cannot
      cross a vacuum. Ultrasound is simply sound too high-pitched to hear. Lithotripter shocks, ultrasound
      imaging, HIFU and histotripsy are all sound.
    • What "electromagnetic" means: an electric field is what makes charged objects push or pull on each
      other (static cling). A magnetic field is what turns a compass needle. A changing electric field creates a
      magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field creates an electric field, so the two keep regenerating
      each other and travel as a wave, even through empty space. That is why sunlight reaches us across a
      vacuum.
    • Radio waves, microwaves, infrared (the heat felt from a fire), visible light, ultraviolet and X-rays are
      all the same phenomenon, differing only in wavelength. Lasers (light), microwave ablation and
      radiofrequency energy are therefore cousins, and the X-rays of your fluoroscopy are the same family.
    • Frequency is how many waves pass per second (hertz, Hz). Wavelength is the length of one wave. For a
      given speed, higher frequency means shorter wavelength (speed = frequency × wavelength).
    • You already use this trade-off daily: a high-frequency linear probe (scrotum) shows fine detail but only
      superficially, and a low-frequency curvilinear probe (kidney) reaches deep but with less detail. High
      frequency = shallow; low frequency = deep runs through the whole chapter.
    • Absorption is a wave giving up its energy to the material it passes through, mostly as heat. Where
      energy is absorbed is where the surgical effect happens. Water absorbs holmium laser light strongly,

    • A pulse is a short burst of energy rather than a continuous stream; pulse duration is how long each
      burst lasts. The same energy squeezed into a shorter burst gives a far higher peak power, like a

    • Size prefixes: milli (ms) = one-thousandth; micro (μs) = one-millionth; nano (ns) = one-billionth; femto

   IN PLAIN TERMS — READING THE WAVE DESCRIPTORS
    • Picture a wave on a rope you are shaking. Amplitude is the height of the wiggle (how strong the wave
      is). Frequency is how many times per second you shake. Wavelength is the distance between two
      crests.
    • Phase is where a point sits in the cycle: crest, trough or halfway. Two waves "in phase" rise and fall
      together and reinforce each other; out of phase, they cancel.
    • Polarisation is the direction of shaking, up–down or side-to-side.
    • Longitudinal vs shear: pushing a Slinky along its length sends a compression along it (longitudinal:
      sound in water and tissue). Flicking a rope sideways sends a sideways ripple (shear). Liquids cannot
      carry shear waves; solids can. This is why shear waves inside a stone matter in lithotripsy.
    • Dispersion means different frequencies travel at different speeds in a material, so a mixed pulse
      spreads out, like runners who start together but finish strung out. A glass prism splitting white light into
      a rainbow is dispersion.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — SQUEEZE, PULL AND CAVITATION
    • A sound wave alternately squeezes (positive pressure) and pulls apart (negative pressure) the medium
      it passes through.
    • Liquids resist squeezing very well but can be torn by pulling. When the pulling phase is strong enough,
      the liquid rips open into a vapour bubble. This is cavitation, the same bubble that appears when you
      pull hard on a syringe plunger against a closed tip.
    • Boiling makes bubbles by heating; cavitation makes bubbles by pulling.
    • Cavitation matters throughout the chapter. It helps break stones, it injures tissue, and when too many
      bubbles form it blocks the energy from reaching the target.

Pressure, negative pressure and cavitation
 • Real waves are never a pure single frequency; they are sums of harmonic components.
 • For acoustics, amplitude (loudness) is pressure. It can be negative, because the fluid is elastic enough to
   hold its phase through the negative half-cycle.
 • A sufficiently intense negative phase causes cavitation, a liquid–gas phase change. Cavitation vs boiling:
   in cavitation the pressure drops; in boiling the temperature rises.
 • Spherical bubble dynamics in an incompressible fluid follow the Rayleigh–Plesset equation, derived from
   the Navier–Stokes equation.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — ONE NOTE VERSUS A HAND-CLAP
    • A single sustained flute note is narrowband: essentially one pitch. A hand-clap is broadband: a sharp
      sound containing many pitches at once. The shorter and sharper a sound, the more frequencies it
      must contain.
    • The Fourier transform is a mathematical prism. As a prism splits white light into its colours, Fourier
      analysis splits any pulse into the pure frequencies it is built from.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — WHAT A "SHOCK" IS
    • In a very loud wave the high-pressure crests travel slightly faster than the troughs. The front of the
      wave therefore steepens until it becomes an almost vertical jump in pressure. That jump is a shock,
      the same thing as a sonic boom.
    • A steep jump means an enormous pressure change in a tiny time, and that is what cracks brittle
      stones.
    • The word "shock" has nothing to do with electricity.

Shock formation
 • A shock is a discontinuous increase in pressure. It forms at high amplitude, especially at high frequency
   over long distances.
 • Mechanism: nonlinearity. Wave speed depends on amplitude, so peaks travel faster than troughs and
   the peak catches the preceding part of the wave. It forms a shock but does not "crash" like an ocean
   wave.
 • Frequency-domain signature: energy transfer from the fundamental (centre) frequency into higher
   harmonics.
 • Medical acoustic waves lie well above 20 kHz (limit of human hearing), but some contain audible
   components. What is heard is the click rate of pulse delivery, not the centre frequency.
 • Duty cycle = percentage of total time that sound is emitted.
Dispersion in EM
 • EM wave speed depends on frequency, according to the medium's dispersion relation ω(k), where ω is
   the angular frequency (2πf, radians/s) and k the wavevector.
 • Consequence: a pulse sorts by frequency. Components arrive with different delays and the pulse shape
   changes as it propagates.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — FOCUSING IS THE MAGNIFYING GLASS
    • A magnifying glass concentrates the same sunlight into a tiny spot hot enough to burn paper.
      Lithotripters and high-intensity focused ultrasound do exactly this with sound.

Chapter 19 — Energy Modalities in Urology                                                                 WAVES
    • Where the beam is wide (at the skin) the energy is spread and harmless. Where it converges (at the
      stone or target) the energy is concentrated and effective. This is how energy passes through skin and
      fat without injuring them.
    • The focal spot is about one wavelength wide and about ten wavelengths long, giving a cigar-shaped or
      rice-grain-shaped zone of effect.

Focusing and beams
 • A point source (smaller than the wavelength) radiates spherically; a lens or reflector focuses waves into a
   beam.
 • Beams decompose into spatial modes, each of which diffracts and propagates — analogous to the
   temporal Fourier decomposition.
 • Beam width = full width at half maximum (FWHM): the distance between points where amplitude falls to
   half the peak; it can also be measured on intensity.
 • The commonest laser beam is the Gaussian beam, whose amplitude varies with radius as a Gaussian
   function.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — PHOTONS
    • Light comes in tiny indivisible packets called photons.
    • The colour (wavelength) of a laser fixes the energy of each photon; its power setting fixes how many
      photons leave per second. Turning up the power sends more photons, not stronger ones.

Intensity, power and photons
 • Intensity ∝ amplitude², so it is always positive even though amplitude oscillates.
 • Power = energy per unit time. Intensity = power per unit area, the rate of energy passing through the
   beam cross-section; often averaged over on-time or area.
 • Photon energy is related to frequency by Planck's constant (h): E = hf. A more powerful laser produces
   more photons per unit time, not more energetic photons.
 • Phonons are the acoustic analogue; the term is not used in medical ultrasound.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — WHY GEL, WHY SHADOWS
    • Acoustic impedance is essentially how stiff and dense a material is to sound.
    • Sound passes easily between materials of similar impedance (water to soft tissue) but bounces back
      from very different ones (tissue to air, or tissue to bone or stone).
    • This explains four things you already know:
    • First, you apply gel, because even a thin layer of air between probe and skin reflects almost everything.
    • Second, bowel gas and ribs cast shadows on ultrasound.
    • Third, a stone appears bright with an acoustic shadow behind it.
    • Fourth, a lithotripter must be coupled to the patient with water or gel.
    • Refraction is the bending of a wave as it enters a new material at an angle, the reason a straw looks
      bent in a glass of water.

Scattering, diffraction, refraction and reflection
 • Diffraction: free-space spreading from an aperture, computed with the Fraunhofer and Kirchhoff
   relations.
 • Scattering occurs wherever the dispersion relation changes — a different state (pressure, temperature), a
   different phase, or a different material.


                                                     page 5 of 33
Chapter 19 — Energy Modalities in Urology                                                                              WAVES
 • Refraction: at oblique incidence the ray bends on entering a layer with a different refractive index (EM) or
   wave speed (elastic).
 • At an interface, part of the energy is transmitted and part reflected. Acoustic impedance = sound speed ×
   density governs the ratio.
 • Large impedance mismatch → reflection, poor transmission. Air (bowel gas) and bone reflect ultrasound.
 • Rough surface (irregularities smaller than the wavelength) → scatter in many directions; a smooth surface
   gives specular reflection.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — WHY ENERGY FADES WITH DEPTH, AND WHY WAVES PUSH
    • Every material "eats" part of a wave as it travels, either by absorbing it (turning it into heat) or by
      scattering it in other directions. Together these are attenuation.
    • The absorption distance is how deep the wave goes before most of its energy is gone. A short
      absorption distance dumps all the energy close by: intense local heating, but safe for deeper
      structures.
    • Radiation force: a wave also gives a small push to whatever it hits, like wind on a sail. For light the push
      is negligible. For sound it is large enough to be useful, and ultrasonic propulsion literally pushes
      stones.

Attenuation and absorption
 • Attenuation = scattering + absorption.
 • Absorption reduces amplitude by e−αz, where α is the absorption coefficient (1/distance) and z the
   propagation distance.
 • Absorption distance = 1/α: the depth at which amplitude or energy falls to 1/e.
 • Absorption usually increases with frequency. The acoustic absorption distance in tissue is about 25
   wavelengths.
 • A short absorption distance cuts both ways: it protects deeper structures but concentrates heat in a
   small, engineerable region.
 • Absorbers: dark objects (at the incident wavelength) absorb EM; viscous material absorbs acoustic waves.

Radiation force
 • Absorption also imparts a force along the propagation direction.
 • Radiation force = power absorbed ÷ wave speed. It is much smaller in EM, because light is far faster than
   sound.
 • It is doubled when the object is totally reflective, because the momentum is reversed rather than
   absorbed.
 • When the wavelength is much longer than the distances of interest, modal analysis replaces ray thinking.

 KEY POINTS
   • Lithotripsy shock waves are acoustic waves with abrupt pressure rises, not electrical shocks.
   • "Frequency" means either the oscillation frequency within a pulse or the pulse delivery rate; the
     latter is lower.
   • A pulse has a duration in time and a length in space, linked by the wave speed.
   • Short pulses are broadband; long pulses are narrowband.
   • EM bioeffects = absorption → heat. Acoustic bioeffects = heat, mechanical stress (solids), cavitation
     (fluids/tissue).

Concept — every modality in this chapter is a question of where the wave's energy is absorbed and in what
form it is released. Frequency sets the penetration, impedance sets what is reached, and absorption sets
whether the result is heat, stress or a bubble.

ELECTROMAGNETIC WAVES

Lasers
   IN PLAIN TERMS — HOW A LASER WORKS
    • An ordinary bulb is heated atoms giving off light at random: every colour, every direction, out of step,
      like a crowd chatting.
    • A laser energises ("pumps") atoms and then triggers them to release light in lockstep: one colour, one
      direction, in step, like a choir singing one note in unison.
    • Mirrors at each end bounce the light back and forth through the material to build it up, and one mirror
      lets a little escape as the beam.
    • Result: a pure single wavelength in a tight beam that can be sent down a thin fibre with enormous
      concentration.
    • The laser's name is the material that makes the light: Ho:YAG = holmium atoms in a YAG crystal; Tm =
      thulium; KTP/LBO = crystals that halve the wavelength to make green light.
    • The wavelength decides what absorbs the light (water, haemoglobin, pigment). That is the whole
      reason different lasers do different things.

Principle
 • LASER = light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation. A gain medium (gas, solid or liquid) sits
   inside a resonant optical cavity.
 • The medium is "pumped" by an external source: electrical current, chemical energy, a flashlamp or
   another laser.
 • Stimulated emission: pumped electrons in uniform higher-energy orbitals → an incoming photon
   stimulates release of a photon with identical energy, propagation direction and phase → cascade →
   output.

Resonator
 • Two inward-facing mirrors act as feedback elements; photons pass repeatedly through the gain medium,
   stimulating further emission, until amplitude saturates.
 • The opposed mirrors give directional, in-phase output: a collimated, monochromatic, coherent beam.
 • In practice: one total reflector (ideally 100%) and one partial reflector that lets a small fraction escape —
   the output end.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — TAPS AND DAMS
    • Continuous wave is a tap left running.
    • Q-switching is a dam: the energy is held back while it builds up and then released all at once as one
      huge, very brief flood.
    • Mode-locking produces even shorter flashes.

Diode lasers
 • Semiconductor gain medium with mirrors fabricated on the semiconductor; a doped wafer (holes and
   electrons) creates a charge bias, and the diode is pumped by electrical current.
 • Advantages: smaller, more energy-efficient, and configurable across a wide range of wavelengths and
   pulse durations.
 • Historically non-medical (barcode scanners, CD/DVD players). Clinical diodes run at 800–980 nm, mainly
   for soft tissue.
History
 • First lithotripsy laser: ruby crystal with flashlamp pump (1960). Medical lasers must be mobile,
   serviceable and robust to clinical use.
 • CO₂ laser (1964): gas laser, 10,600 nm; very highly absorbed by water → narrow penetration. Uses:
   penile and vulvar lesions (superficial squamous cell carcinoma), skin resurfacing.
 • Not usable for intracorporeal lithotripsy: 10.6-μm light suffers fibre losses, and hollow waveguides fail in
   aqueous environments.

Laser Lithotripsy

   IN PLAIN TERMS — THE TWO WAYS A LASER BREAKS A STONE
    • Photomechanical ("hammer"): an extremely short pulse (billionths to millionths of a second) dumps
      energy so fast it cannot escape as heat. Instead it explodes, creating a bubble and a shock wave that
      crack the stone mechanically. Pulsed dye and FREDDY lasers worked this way.
    • Photothermal ("blowtorch" or "drill"): a longer pulse (hundreds of microseconds: holmium and
      thulium) heats the spot it hits until the stone melts, decomposes, or its internal water flashes to steam.
      The stone is eroded into dust and small fragments.
    • Pulse duration decides which happens, and modern holmium and thulium lithotripsy is
      photothermal.
    • Why the "pulse duration" on the machine can mislead: manufacturers quote how long the electrical
      current pumping the laser lasts, not how long the light actually lasts at the stone.

Pulse duration — the key parameter
 • Lithotripsy pulses span femtoseconds to microseconds.
 • Nomenclature trap: manufacturers usually quote the electrical pulse duration of the pump current; the
   optical pulse duration (FWHM) is the physically informative value; labels such as "short / medium / long"
   differ between platforms.
First principle
 • Fragmentation begins with photon absorption. No absorption in the stone or surrounding water → no
   fragmentation.
 • The most important factor is fluence: optical energy absorbed by the stone and/or water. Ablation is
   multifactorial: photothermal plus photoacoustic/photomechanical.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — BUBBLES, PLASMA AND "STRESS CONFINEMENT"
    • Cavitation bubble: the laser tip boils a pocket of water into a vapour bubble that balloons outward and
      then collapses violently (implodes), sending out a pressure wave.


                                                            page 9 of 33
Chapter 19 — Energy Modalities in Urology                                                   ELECTROMAGNETIC WAVES
    • The punch of the collapse rises with the cube of the bubble radius: double the bubble size gives eight
      times the punch.
    • Plasma: at extreme intensity the material is torn into charged particles, a tiny spark like miniature
      lightning, whose sudden expansion hits the stone.
    • Spallation: a pressure wave travelling through a stone reflects from its far surface as a pulling wave and
      flakes material off that surface, like a chip blown off the far side of a wall that is struck.
    • Stress confinement means delivering the blow faster than the stone can relieve it. A karate chop
      breaks a board that a slow push would only bend.

Photoacoustic / photomechanical mechanisms (pulses <50 μs)
 • Sudden thermoelastic expansion, optical breakdown or plasma initiation convert laser energy into stress
   waves (at sound speed) or shock waves (faster).
 • Water-absorbed light → spherical cavitation bubble → single-locus collapse → outgoing shock wave.
   Stone-absorbed light → thermoelastic expansion within the stone → internal fracture.
 • Three generators of transient stress waves:
    1. Thermoelastic expansion → pressure wave reflects at the free surface as an inward negative (tensile)
       wave → if strong enough, spallation (disruption from rapid heating or cooling; rapid cooling gives
       irregular fragmentation).
    2. Recoil: ejected ablated material → conservation of momentum → recoil pressure wave → stress
       fracture deep to the ablation surface.
    3. Phase change, e.g. plasma, the "fourth state of matter" (an ionised cloud of free electrons); unstable
       rapid expansion and contraction → stress fractures in the stone matrix.
 • Laser shock waves arise twice per pulse: at the outset (bubble expansion or plasma formation) and at the
   end (spherical bubble collapse).
Cavitation bubble dynamics (ultrashort and short pulses, up to ~100 μs)
 • Laser-induced water vapour expands spherically at the fibre tip, then collapses in the incompressible
   surrounding water hundreds of microseconds later.
 • Collapse releases the stored potential energy instantaneously → strong pressure waves → strain on the
   crystal matrix → fragmentation.
 • Pressure-transient sequence: initial transient (bubble expansion) → end of laser pulse → bubble collapse
   → second, higher transient → fragmentation.
 • Transient amplitude ∝ (maximum bubble radius)³.
Stress confinement
 • Efficient photomechanical fragmentation requires energy delivered within a pulse shorter than the time
   for stress to propagate out of the irradiated zone → rapid pressure and heat build-up, often a shock
   wave, and dissociation of the crystalline structure.

Long Pulse Durations (≥250 μs) and Photothermal Ablation

   IN PLAIN TERMS — THE MOSES EFFECT, FIBRE ANGLE AND WHY WATER MATTERS
    • Water absorbs holmium and thulium light strongly, so a fibre held a few millimetres from the stone
      would waste its energy heating water.
    • What actually happens: the first part of each pulse boils a tunnel of steam between fibre and stone.
      Steam absorbs this light far less than liquid water, so the rest of the pulse passes through the tunnel to
      the stone, as the sea parted for Moses.
    • Practical consequences: keep the fibre close to the stone, and fire straight at it (perpendicular), not
      grazing its surface. A photothermal laser works only if its photons actually enter the stone.
    • Why long pulses give so little pressure: the bubble forms slowly and lopsidedly and collapses in several
      places at different times, so there is no single violent implosion.

 • Mid-infrared (thulium and holmium) light is highly absorbed by water. Pulses of several hundred μs →
   photothermal removal by rapid vaporisation, melting, carbonisation or chemical decomposition, confined
   to the zone of absorption.
 • Pulses >20 ms, or CW → heat conducted to surrounding tissue → coagulation and carbonisation;
   lithotripsy pulses are therefore kept well below 20 ms.
 • Moses effect: mid-infrared light is absorbed more by liquid water than by water vapour, so the vapour
   channel transmits energy to the stone ("parting the water").
 • Stone-absorbed photons cause melting, chemical decomposition or superheated interstitial water →
   localised microexplosion.
 • Most Ho:YAG, TFL and pulsed Tm:YAG lasers fragment photothermally. Pulses of hundreds of μs
   preclude stress confinement; time-resolved imaging and pressure studies show no significant
   photomechanical effect.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — THULIUM VERSUS HOLMIUM
    • The thulium fibre laser wavelength is absorbed several times more strongly by water than holmium, so
      it dumps its energy in a shorter distance. That favours fine dust.
    • Thulium delivers its energy with lower peak power (a longer, gentler pulse), so each pulse kicks the
      stone less: less retropulsion.
    • Holmium gives a harder punch per pulse: more kick, and more fragmenting than dusting.
    • Modern high-power holmium machines with pulse modulation have narrowed the gap, which is why
      the two trials reached different conclusions.

Single-wavelength principle
 • One wavelength can produce every effect, depending on pulse duration:
   1. Short pulse (<10 μs) → cavitation and shock waves.
    2. Pulse of hundreds of μs → localised photothermal ablation.
    3. Pulse >20 ms → photothermal ablation with collateral thermal damage.
 • Landmark experiment, Jansen et al., 1996 (Ho:YAG in still water, no stone, high-speed imaging): 500 ns
   (Q-switched): large spherical bubbles, single-locus collapse, strong pressure ripples; longer pulses: less
   spherical, multi-locus collapse, fewer ripples; 260 μs: pear-shaped, asymmetric collapse, no ripples.
 • Some Ho:YAG, TFL and Tm:YAG platforms deliver pulses as short as 50 μs, where both mechanisms occur
   simultaneously.
 • The laser–stone interaction mechanism depends on the dosimetry settings, not merely on the platform.

Safety Issues

   IN PLAIN TERMS — HEAT AND RETROPULSION AT THE BEDSIDE
    • Heat: laser energy that does not enter the stone heats the irrigation fluid. A calyx is a small, nearly
      closed space, so high power with poor outflow turns it into a kettle, hot enough to injure urothelium.
    • The remedies all follow from that picture: lower power, pauses, more inflow, an access sheath for
      outflow, and the fibre on the stone.
    • Retropulsion is the stone being kicked away by each pulse. The size of each kick is set by the energy
      per pulse, not by how many pulses per second.
    • This is why "dusting" uses low energy at high frequency, and "fragmenting" uses high energy at low
      frequency and accepts more kick.

Heat generation
 • High-power platforms put more energy into the collecting system per unit time; because mid-infrared is
   water-absorbed, irrigant in the ureter or calyx can reach cytotoxic temperatures.
 • Strategies to limit dangerous heat: relatively low power settings; frequent duty-cycle breaks; increased
   flow (hand irrigation devices, pressure bags, small-diameter fibres to free the working channel); ureteral
   access sheath for outflow drainage; fibre tip kept close to the stone (photons into the stone mean faster
   lithotripsy; photons into water mean more total energy is needed).

Concept — pulse duration, not wavelength, decides the mechanism. Short pulses confine stress and break
stones mechanically with high-pressure bubbles; long pulses cannot confine stress and ablate thermally

Lasers for Soft Tissue
Uses
 • Endoscopic management of urothelial carcinoma; benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH); external treatment
   of genital warts and penile carcinoma in situ.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — PENETRATION DEPTH IN SOFT TISSUE
    • Optical penetration depth is how deep light gets before it is absorbed.
    • Very shallow penetration (thulium fibre laser) concentrates all the energy in a thin layer, which
      vaporises and chars (turns black).
    • Deeper penetration (Nd:YAG) spreads gentler heating through a thick layer, which coagulates deeply
      but risks delayed sloughing of tissue.
    • Scatter is light bouncing around inside tissue like headlights in fog. It spreads the energy sideways and
      reduces how deep it goes.
    • Fluence is simply the energy delivered per unit area of tissue.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — WHY GREEN LIGHT FOR THE PROSTATE
    • An object looks red because it absorbs green light and reflects red. Blood and a vascular prostate
      therefore absorb green light avidly.
    • GreenLight energy is deposited exactly where the blood is, vaporising tissue while sealing vessels,
      which is why it can be used in patients who continue anticoagulation.
    • "Side-firing" means the fibre tip deflects the beam at an angle, so it strikes the prostate wall beside the
      fibre rather than straight ahead.

KTP/LBO side-firing ("GreenLight") laser
 • Development: 1990s 60 W → 80 W (popularised internationally; an Nd:YAG beam frequency-doubled
   through a KTP crystal → 532 nm) → 120 W and 180 W (LBO crystal instead of KTP, same 70° side-firing
   delivery). All are pulsed, solid-state lasers.
 • 532 nm (green) is preferentially absorbed by haem; green's complementary colour is red, so absorption
   is high in well-vascularised red tissue such as prostate.
 • Optical penetration 0.8 mm, but tissue effect 1–3 mm. Mechanism: photothermal vaporisation of
   intracellular water (photovaporisation).
 • Haemostasis permits photovaporisation without interrupting anticoagulation.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — PHOTODYNAMIC THERAPY
    • A light-sensitive drug is given; it sits harmlessly in tissue, concentrating in tumour, until light of the right
      colour switches it on.
    • Once activated, it produces a highly reactive form of oxygen that damages the cells around it. No heat
      and no cutting: the light acts as a trigger, not as a tool.

Photochemical interaction and photodynamic therapy
 • A third laser mechanism, after photomechanical and photothermal: light catalyses a chemical reaction in
   tissue.
 • Photodynamic therapy: photosensitising dye → accumulates in target tissue → activated by a specific
   wavelength → singlet oxygen → reacts with nearby biomolecules.
 • Used for urothelial cancer: a large tumour volume can be treated with a single intravesical light
   application.

 KEY POINTS
   • Pulse duration is the key lithotripsy parameter.
   • Ultrashort (<500 ns) → plasma. Short (>500 ns, <10 μs) → cavitation and shock waves. Long (>250 μs)
     → photothermal ablation (direct photon absorption).
   • Lasers generate heat, with risk of operative injury.
   • Retropulsion rises with pulse energy and falls with longer pulse duration.
   • Soft-tissue ablation depends on fluence in tissue and on the wavelength's scatter and absorption in
     that tissue.

Concept — in soft tissue the question is not what the laser can do but where its energy stops. Absorption and
scatter by water and haemoglobin fix the penetration depth, and penetration depth fixes the balance of
vaporisation, carbonisation and coagulation.

Electrical Energy
   IN PLAIN TERMS — ELECTRICITY FROM FIRST PRINCIPLES
    • Electric current is a flow of charged particles: electrons in wires, and ions (sodium, potassium, chloride)
      in tissue and body fluids.
    • Voltage is the push driving the flow. Resistance is how hard it is to push through.
    • With a garden hose: voltage is tap pressure, current is water flow, and resistance is how narrow or long
      the hose is.
    • When current is forced through resistance, energy is released as heat. That is how a toaster element
      glows, and it is the whole of electrosurgery: the patient's tissue is the resistance, so the tissue
      becomes the toaster element.
    • Direct current flows one way (a battery). Alternating current reverses direction many times per second:
      mains supply in India reverses 50 times per second; an electrosurgical generator reverses about
      500,000 times per second.
    • Impedance is simply the name for resistance when the current is alternating.

 • Electrosurgery is ubiquitous and its principles are often learned empirically; understanding the physics

Electrosurgery

   IN PLAIN TERMS — WHY SURGICAL CURRENT DOES NOT ELECTROCUTE THE PATIENT
     • At mains frequency (50 Hz) each push of current lasts long enough to fire nerves and muscles. That is
       why a mains shock causes spasm and cardiac arrest.
     • At about 500,000 Hz the current reverses so fast that nerve and muscle membranes cannot respond.
       The ions simply jiggle back and forth, and that jiggling becomes heat.
     • This is what makes it possible to pass a surgical current through a patient safely.
     • The obturator jerk during TURBT is the exception: the nerve lies just outside the lateral bladder wall
       and can still be stimulated. Arcing at the loop is thought to generate lower-frequency components that
       nerves can respond to.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — CURRENT DENSITY: STILETTO HEEL VERSUS ELEPHANT FOOT
     • A woman in stiletto heels can dent a wooden floor that an elephant's broad foot would not, because
       her weight is concentrated on a tiny area. Electric current behaves the same way.
     • At the small active tip the current is crowded into a tiny area, so the heat is intense.
     • At the large dispersive pad the same current is spread over a big area, so the heat is negligible.
     • If the pad peels partly off, the contact area shrinks, the pad effectively becomes a second "tip", and the
       patient is burned under the pad.

Monopolar electrosurgery
 • Circuit: radiofrequency generator → active electrode (Bovie tip) → patient's tissue → dispersive pad
   (return electrode) → generator.
 • Current is constant around the circuit; current density decides the effect: small tip area → high current
   density → local heating (the tip stays cooler than the tissue, which absorbs the heat); large pad area →
   low current density → no heating.
 • Pad must be fully adherent: partial adherence → smaller contact area → higher current density → skin
   burn. Inspect the pad site post-operatively; many units detect inadequate pad contact and block
   activation.
Cutting, coagulation and blended settings

   IN PLAIN TERMS — CUT, COAGULATE, SPRAY, BLEND
    • Cut (continuous current): heats cells so fast that the water inside them flash-boils and the cells burst.
      The result is a clean split with little charring, like a very hot knife.
    • Coagulate (short bursts at higher voltage, off most of the time): heat builds slowly and spreads during
      the pauses, so cells cook and dry out rather than burst, as egg white turns solid. This seals vessels and
      spreads deeper.
    • As the tissue dries it conducts less, so coagulation naturally limits itself.
    • Spray (fulguration): voltage high enough for sparks to jump the air gap, like small lightning. It chars a
      surface without touching it and is useful for a diffusely oozing bed.
    • Blend is a mixture: cutting with some coagulation.

Safety Issues in Electrosurgery
 • Injury is rare and largely preventable.
Surgical fire
 • Requires flammable material + electrosurgical ignition: drapes, preparation solutions, oxygen-rich
   anaesthetic gas. Ignition by inadvertent activation or direct coupling to a nearby metal instrument.
 • Electrosurgery is implicated in the majority of operating-room fires, which most often involve the head
   and upper torso, usually with supplemental oxygen.
Current leaks and the dispersive pad
 • Insulation failure lets the circuit contact metal, water, the patient or the surgeon.
 • The dispersive pad is a common injury site — technically a "dispersive electrode". Avoid bony
   prominences, scars, dense hair, implants, pacemakers, and breast or orthopaedic implants.
 • Orthopaedic prostheses are not a contraindication: place the pad away from the implant, e.g.
   contralateral to a hip prosthesis. Remove metal jewellery; if impossible, never place the pad over it.
Inadvertent activation
 • Leaning on the hand-switch or stepping on the foot pedal. Keep spatial awareness of the devices; holster
   active electrodes when not in use.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — ELECTRICITY THAT JUMPS THROUGH INSULATION
    • Alternating current can induce a current in a nearby metal object without touching it, passing through
      the insulation between them.

Chapter 19 — Energy Modalities in Urology                                                            ELECTROMAGNETIC WAVES
    • Cords wrapped together, or wrapped around metal instruments, set this up, and current can appear
      where no one expects it.

Capacitive coupling
 • A capacitor = two conductors separated by an insulator; if the charge difference exceeds the insulator's
   capacity, current jumps across.
 • Examples: monopolar and bipolar cords wrapped together; cords wrapped around metal instruments.
   Active electrode monitoring systems detect insulation failure and capacitive coupling.
Surgeon injury and personnel responsibilities
 • Surgical gloves have low electrical resistance; high voltage penetrates weakened or damaged gloves. Use
   the lowest current that achieves the effect.
 • Perioperative personnel: equipment in date and maintained; jewellery removed; checklist for implants,
   prostheses and implantable cardiac devices.

Endoscopic Use of Electrosurgery
 • Main uses: transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) and transurethral resection of bladder tumour
   (TURBT), each available in monopolar and bipolar versions.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — WHY GLYCINE, WHY SALINE
    • Monopolar resection needs the current to leave the loop, pass through the prostate and travel to the
      pad. If the irrigant were saline (full of sodium and chloride ions, a good conductor), the current would
      leak away through the fluid instead of cutting, like trying to cut under seawater. Hence a non-
      conducting fluid such as glycine.
    • The price: glycine absorbed into the circulation dilutes blood sodium (the transurethral resection
      syndrome). This is why resection time is limited, especially in vascular glands.
    • Bipolar resection deliberately uses saline, because the current only needs to pass from the loop
      through the fluid to a return electrode on the same instrument.

   nerve block; avoid overfilling the bladder; intermittent activation; bipolar resection.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — MICROWAVE, RADIOFREQUENCY, HEAT SINK AND CHEMOHYPERTHERMIA
    • Microwave ablation works like a microwave oven: the field makes water molecules flip back and forth

      toaster element does. The probe itself does not heat; the tissue does.

      That is why 150 °C is undesirable.
    • Heat sink: flowing blood carries heat away, as coolant carries heat from a car engine. A tumour next to
      a large vessel is harder to "cook". Microwave energy heats faster and wider and so is less defeated by
      this.

Microwave thermotherapy (soft tissue)
 • Microwave ablation (MWA). Mechanism: dielectric hysteresis — molecules repeatedly realign with the
   oscillating electric field → rotational and vibrational kinetic energy → heat.
 • Frequencies 915 MHz or 2.45 GHz: generator → coaxial line → antenna(e) placed directly in the tumour.
   The shaft heats, so a cooling system protects the tract.
 • Urology: alternative to radiofrequency ablation (RFA) for renal tumours. Heat sink: the highly perfused
   kidney carries heat away; MWA reaches larger zones and higher temperatures than RFA and is less
   susceptible to heat sink.
 • Early animal concerns — collecting-system absorption and inconsistent zones — but safety and primary
   efficacy are comparable to RFA.
Radiofrequency in urology
 • AC at 375–900 kHz; the probe sits at the centre of the lesion. The probe does not generate heat; Joule
   heating arises in the tissue from ionic excitation and conducts outward spherically.
 • Target 60 °C for reliable cell death; at 150 °C, carbonisation creates high impedance and non-uniform
   heating. Temperature probes monitor the time–temperature plan.
 • Small renal masses: RFA is less common than cryoablation in the United States, more common in

 • Chemohyperthermia for non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) — device-assisted intravesical
   therapy like EMDA. Intravesical mitomycin is given while the bladder wall is heated to 42 °C: increased
   cell permeability → more drug absorption; cancer cells are more heat-sensitive; heat-shock protein
   release may potentiate mitomycin.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — HOW A LITHOTRIPTER MAKES ITS BANG
    • A transducer converts one form of energy into another. A loudspeaker turns electricity into sound; a
      microphone turns sound into electricity; an ultrasound probe does both.
    • Electrohydraulic: a spark in water, like an underwater firecracker.
    • Electromagnetic: a coil pushes a metal membrane, like a very powerful loudspeaker cone.
    • Pneumatic: one part strikes another, a miniature jackhammer.
    • Piezoelectric: special crystals that change thickness when a voltage is applied (and make a voltage
      when squeezed, the spark in a gas lighter). Many crystals together push the water.
    • Focusing with a curved reflector or lens works like a satellite dish gathering signal to one point.

Piezoceramic details
 • Most efficient at the frequency whose half-wavelength fits the element's thickness; a matching network
   matches transducer and source impedance.
 • Primary limitation: lower frequency → thicker element → higher voltage and larger lateral dimensions →
   larger source; if thickness approaches width or length, parasitic vibration modes reduce efficiency; excess
   voltage cracks the element.
 • Waveguide works like a reverse trumpet: it concentrates energy from horn to mouthpiece but filters
   broadband input to a few primary tones.
 • Typical focus: <1 cm transverse, a few cm axial. Treating a larger region at millimetre-scale distance with
   one beam requires frequencies <1 MHz.
 • Phased arrays: multi-element ceramics allow electronic beam steering without moving the source, but
   are more complex and expensive; most sources are single-element.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — HEAT VERSUS BUBBLES
    • Higher frequency: the tissue absorbs more of the wave, so there is more heating (and shallower reach).
    • Lower frequency: each pulling (negative-pressure) phase lasts longer, giving bubbles more time to
      grow, so there is more cavitation.
    • This single asymmetry explains why heating devices and bubble-making devices choose different
      frequencies.
    • "Threshold effect": cavitation does not build up gradually. Below a certain negative pressure nothing
      happens; above it, bubbles form.
    • The twinkling artefact you see behind stones on Doppler comes from tiny bubbles on the stone
      surface.

Acoustic interaction with tissue
 • Mechanisms: heating, cavitation (bubble activity), mechanical stress, radiation force, and possibly
   undetermined non-thermal processes.
 • Heating: absorption along the path → local heat; focusing raises amplitude, so bioeffects concentrate at
   the focus by design. Example: transrectal focusing into the prostate necrotises tumour while sparing the
   rectal wall; the focus can be moved to successive targets and necrosed tissue is resorbed. Heat can also
   seal vessels (ischaemic kill) or permit bloodless resection. Propagation and absorption are calculable, so
   injury is predictable and targetable.
 • Frequency trade-off: too high a frequency → absorbed before the focus. Nonlinear shocks forming only
   near the focus carry high-frequency components → dramatically increased local heating.
 • Boiling histotripsy: shock-enhanced heating → ~100-μm focus boils within milliseconds → millimetre
   vapour bubble → field shakes surrounding tissue → mechanical shredding to submicron particles →
   tissue flows into the bubble and atomises. Mechanical liquefaction, not thermal necrosis.
 • Cavitation and gas-body activation are driven by the negative pressure amplitude: cavitation lowers
   pressure; boiling raises temperature. Therapeutic negative pressures (MPa order) exceed 10×
   atmospheric (0.1 MPa); the tensile stress tears the medium into a rapidly expanding bubble.
 • Nucleation sites: interfaces, particles (stone surfaces, debris), pre-existing gas. Microbubbles on stones
   cause the twinkling artefact; lung and bowel gas make these organs vulnerable. No SWL within 24 hours
   of microbubble ultrasound contrast.
 • Cavitation injury: the bubble or cloud grows during one negative phase and stretches and tears tissue;
   cloud-cavitation histotripsy liquefies tissue. Near solid surfaces, collapse re-radiates shock waves and
   fluid jets through the collapsing bubble strike and crack the surface.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — WHY STONES CRACK AND KIDNEYS SURVIVE
    • Stones are brittle, like chalk or glass: they cannot stretch, so a strong pull cracks them.




                                                         page 25 of 33
Chapter 19 — Energy Modalities in Urology                                                              ELASTIC WAVES
    • Tissue is elastic, like rubber: it stretches and springs back. Bend a stick of chalk and it snaps; bend a
      rubber tube and it recovers.
    • How the stone breaks: the shock travels faster through the stone than through the fluid around it.
      Waves converge at the far side of the stone and pull it apart there, so the far end cracks off first. Each
      further shock extends the cracks, as bending a paper clip repeatedly finally snaps it (fatigue).
    • The beam must be wider than the stone, because part of the effect comes from the wave running
      along the stone's outer surface.

Physics of acoustic lithotripsy
 • Focused shocks fracture the stone; repeated shocks progressively comminute it into passable fragments
   — thousands of shocks over ~45–60 min.
 • Primary advantage: non-invasive transcutaneous delivery; in the United States usually performed in a
   surgical suite under sedation or general anaesthesia.
 • Lowest morbidity and complication rate of the three lithotripsy techniques (SWL, ureteroscopy,
   percutaneous nephrolithotomy), but SWL causes renal trauma, varying with device, kidney size and
   protocol. Unpredictable effect; incomplete breakage → repeat procedures.
 • Poor fragmentation predictors: hard compositions (calcium oxalate monohydrate, cystine, brushite);
   Hounsfield density >1000.
 • Fracture mechanism (beam must be larger than the stone): shock enters the stone (faster, higher sound
   speed) → fluid wave along the surface generates shear waves, reinforced by the Mach wave → angled
   shear waves converge on the axis at the distal end → incident wave reflects and inverts at the distal face
   (spallation) → all tensile stresses meet → maximal tensile stress distally → first fracture where focused
   shear and reflected longitudinal waves meet → cracks grow over successive shocks → distal end cleaves
   off.
 • Cavitation creates surface pits into which internal cracks grow by dynamic fatigue; surface stress is
   smaller and localised but grinds fragments smaller. Once fragments approach the pulse length, internal
   stresses no longer focus and surface cavitation continues grinding. Too much cavitation shields the
   stone.
 • Rate and shielding: faster delivery → larger cavitation cloud (bubbles lack time to dissolve; each growth–
   collapse cycle transfers gas inward, increasing the void fraction) → the cloud shields the stone. Hence 2
   Hz is less effective and more injurious than 1 Hz or 0.5 Hz.

 • Hard targets: SWL, SWT, BWL, intracorporeal lithotripsy, ultrasonic propulsion. Soft targets: calcifications

   IN PLAIN TERMS — WHY SLOWER SHOCK WAVE LITHOTRIPSY WORKS BETTER
    • Each shock leaves behind a cloud of tiny bubbles that needs about a second to dissolve.
    • Fire too fast and the next shock hits that bubble cloud, which absorbs and scatters it as fog blocks
      headlights. Less energy reaches the stone (poorer fragmentation), and the bubbles injure the kidney.
    • Hence 60 shocks per minute or slower beats 120 per minute.
    • Rectified diffusion: a bubble takes in more gas while it is large than it loses while it is small, so it grows
      a little with every cycle.

Shock wave lithotripsy
 • A primary stone modality for over 40 years. Machine = shock source + table + fluoroscopy.
 • Coupling: water bath (original) → shallow bath in the table → water-filled balloon with gel on skin
   (most current). Air bubbles at the coupling interface reduce efficiency disproportionately: a small
   fractional obstruction at the aperture substantially reduces peak focal pressure.
 • Pulse: peak positive 30–120 MPa (300–1200 bar) with a steep shock front; positive phase ~1 μs; negative
   phase ~5 μs; peak negative ~10 MPa; single cycle, broadband; centre frequency ~300 kHz (below
   diagnostic ultrasound); pulse energy ~3 J; focal width millimetres to centimetres, length centimetres; focal
   intensity >10,000 W/cm².
 • ~2000 shocks at 0.5–3 Hz (30–180/min).
 • Why stones break but tissue survives: stones are brittle and crack under tension and shear (as brick fails
   in an earthquake); tissue is ductile, like building steel; energy is concentrated at the focus, where the
   stone lies.
 • Cavitation bubble life cycle: high-amplitude, long negative phase tears water (and nucleates at tissue
   interfaces) → bubble grows by inertia long after the 5-μs pulse, to ~1 mm → water rushes in → collapse
   smaller than original size → energy concentrates → gas/vapour halts collapse → rebound shock wave →
   near a stone, asymmetric collapse drives a micro-jet into the stone → several rebound cycles → bubble
   ends larger than it began (rectified diffusion: more gas enters while large and at low pressure than leaves
   while small and compressed).
 • A 1-μm bubble grows to ~30 μm after one pulse and needs ~1 s to dissolve. A next pulse before
   dissolution re-excites bubbles → proliferating cavitation cluster → tissue injury and shielding. Slower
   rate → bubbles dissolve → each pulse reaches the stone.
 • Soft-tissue shock wave therapy (SWT): soft, fibrous or calcified tissue, e.g. Peyronie disease; presumed
   cavitation or strain → minor injury → more pliable repair. Pneumatic and ballistic sources are marketed
   for superficial soft tissue on the rationale of inflammation-driven healing (analogous to laser
   dermatology); less well studied. Shock waves can also liquefy tissue like histotripsy, intentionally or
   otherwise.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — BURST WAVE LITHOTRIPSY
    • Instead of one huge bang, burst wave lithotripsy sends a short hum of many gentler oscillations. The
      stone vibrates, and the echoes inside it add up to break it.

Burst wave lithotripsy
 • A small, diagnostic-ultrasound-sized system for clinic or emergency department use without
   anaesthesia; handheld or support-assisted probe; ultrasound targeting and real-time monitoring.
 • Piezoceramic, narrowband, 5–100 cycles; peak pressure 5–8 MPa; energy per pulse about equal to SWL
   because the burst is longer; 200–1000 kHz, probes chosen for depth and frequency; beam widths similar
   to SWL.
 • Less cavitation → higher rate (≥10 Hz) → more energy over shorter time: bubbles stay small, take in less
   gas and dissolve in ~1/16 s, so the rate can be ≥16× SWL without shielding.
 • Operators watch for cavitation (a dynamic white cloud on ultrasound) and pause if seen. Lower
   pressures plus cloud avoidance plausibly explain tolerability without sedation.
 • Coupling by gel is essential: loss of coupling = loss of image = loss of feedback and efficiency.
 • Mechanism: beam wider than the stone (≈1 cm at the stone); energy enters the lateral and proximal
   surfaces; multiple internal reflections of many cycles superimpose → >5× amplification of the applied
   negative pressure; tensile stress similar to SWL at lower applied pressure, at many points over a longer
   time.
 • Fragments are uniform and scale with wavelength (inversely with frequency); higher-frequency BWL
   grinds residual fragments to submillimetre size.

High-intensity focused ultrasound
 • Diagnostic power has negligible heating; high intensity → progressive absorption → thermal necrosis.
 • Frequency similar to imaging of the same target; focused by a bowl-shaped ceramic or lens; narrowband
   pulses of ~1 s. Lesion ≈ 1 wavelength wide × 10 wavelengths long ≈ a grain of rice. Intensity 1500–
   10,000 W/cm².
 • Higher frequency = more heating at the focus, but more path loss. Nonlinear shocks → heating within
   milliseconds.
 • Risks and limits: necrosis along the beam path; bone or gas in the path increases local heating; necrosed
   and viable tissue are not distinguishable on standard ultrasound; many exposures are needed for a
   sizeable region → gaps; alternative strategy: cauterise the feeding vessels.
 • Prostate temperatures exceed 100 °C within seconds, depending on the absorption coefficient, duration
   and magnitude.
 • HIFU also acts mechanically: negative pressure → interstitial bubbles → collapse → cellular damage
   compounding the thermal effect.
 • Advantages: extracorporeal; small focal volume; co-located imaging and treatment probes allow real-
   time monitoring. Approved for BPH and prostate cancer (transrectal, short focal distance); intra-operative
   renal devices have existed.
Histotripsy
 • Short but still narrowband pulses at higher intensity than HIFU (>10,000 W/cm²). Aim: cavitation within
   one or very few bursts → mechanical liquefaction, not thermal necrosis. First observed with
   lithotripters.
 • Result: mechanical shredding to submicron fragments; subsequent pulses shear tissue around the
   bubbles and even atomise it.
 • Being developed for transcutaneous prostate and kidney cancer; prostate challenges: acoustic window
   and fibrous tissue resisting liquefaction; kidney trials under way.
 • Advantage: the bubble zone is echogenic, visible in real time. Risks: off-target bubbles, oversized
   bubbles → shielding and cavitation injury, detectable on imaging.

Image guidance and operator safety
 • SWL is usually fluoroscopy-guided, sometimes ultrasound-guided; most other urological ultrasound
   therapies are ultrasound-guided, often from the same handheld probe; magnetic resonance imaging
   (MRI) monitoring is common elsewhere. All probes need liquid or gel coupling.
 • Ultrasound is non-ionising: the only operator risk is at the focus (transcutaneous) or the tip of ballistic

 KEY POINTS
   • Gel-coupled skin sources focus energy for a localised internal bioeffect.
   • Acoustic devices do not transmit through air; they require gel or fluid.
   • Too high a pulse rate → cavitation cloud → blocked energy delivery.
   • Acoustic therapies break stones or ablate tissue. Ablation is thermal (absorption) or mechanical
     (cavitation).
   • Cavitation amplifies tissue strain and concentrates energy. Cavitation is a threshold effect: bioeffects
     occur above a threshold pressure for a given frequency, not below.
   • Fragmentation is primarily crack growth from wave-induced stress.
   • Acoustic waves are non-ionising; repeated safe exposures do not accumulate into harm. Correctly
     operated, properly working devices are safe for operators.

Concept — acoustic therapy is the management of the negative pressure phase. Tension cracks brittle
stones and spares ductile tissue, but the same tension nucleates bubbles that either destroy tissue
deliberately (histotripsy) or shield the stone and injure the kidney (SWL at high rates). Frequency is the lever:
raise it for heat, lower it for cavitation.

Direct Contact Energies
   IN PLAIN TERMS — STEAM AND WATER JETS
    • Water vapour thermal therapy (Rezum): steam scalds more severely than boiling water at the same
      temperature, because steam releases a large amount of extra heat (latent heat) as it condenses back
      into water.
    • Injected steam therefore delivers heat quickly and evenly into prostate tissue without charring, and it
      stays within the zone it was injected into.
    • Aquablation is a robot-guided pressure washer: a high-speed saline jet cuts tissue mechanically, with
      no heat.

Fluid-based ablation modalities
 • Water vapour thermal therapy (WVTT) — Rezum (Boston Scientific), for BPH. Radiofrequency energy
   stores thermal energy in water by converting it to vapour, injected transurethrally into the transition
   zone; it releases latent heat on condensing and spreads rapidly and evenly by convection.
 • Hardware: generator + transurethral device; 18-gauge needle with regularly spaced holes; delivery
   pressure slightly above interstitial pressure for uniform delivery.
 • Vapour 103 °C; tissue ≥70 °C; 9-s injection → spherical zone of cell death. No significant thermal
   gradient; wet thermal therapy → no char or carbonisation.
 • Vapour follows the lowest-pressure path → stays within the zonal compartment, without extension into
   the peripheral zone on MRI. Multiple regularly spaced injections treat the obstructing transition zone in
   one session; MRI confirms prostate volume reduction.

   IN PLAIN TERMS — HOW A CRYOPROBE GETS COLD AND WHY FREEZING KILLS
    • Joule–Thomson effect: gas escaping from high pressure into low pressure cools sharply, the reason an
      aerosol can grows cold as you spray it. High-pressure argon expanding inside the probe tip takes it
      below −100 °C.
    • Freezing kills cells in two ways. Ice crystals form and pierce membranes; on thawing, water rushes
      back into the damaged cells and they swell and burst.
    • Two freeze–thaw cycles repeat both insults, which is why two are used.

Cryotherapy
 • For small renal masses (principally percutaneous), also applied to focal prostate cancer therapy.
 • Freeze–thaw injury (slow freezing): extracellular ice → extracellular hypertonicity → osmotic cellular
   dehydration → intracellular ice → membrane disruption → thaw: extracellular ice melts first → water
   rushes in → swelling and bursting. Rapid freezing skips dehydration; other steps appear analogous.
 • Delayed death: apoptosis; microenvironmental ischaemia and inflammatory cytokine release.
 • Probe cooling: the Joule–Thomson principle — high-pressure argon expands in the probe → below −100
   °C → continuous heat extraction.
 • Visible ice ball; ultrasound monitoring allows adjustment of time and temperature. Target <−40 °C; two
   freeze–thaw cycles with passive thaw.
