CHRONIC KIDNEY DISEASE EPIDEMIOLOGY
 • Chronic kidney disease (CKD), among the commonest chronic illnesses, affects about 14% of United
   States (US) adults, defined by reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), albuminuria, or both
   (Table 91.1).
 • Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) defines six eGFR categories, cross-classified by
   albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), with substantial prognostic implications for treatment and survival.
 • G5 (eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m²) is end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), also termed end-stage renal disease
   (ESRD), requiring dialysis or transplantation.
 • Morbidity and mortality rise with CKD stage, driven mainly by cardiovascular disease (Table 91.2).
 • ESRD incidence is falling slightly but consistently (Fig. 91.1), yet demand far exceeds supply: about
   40,000 patients join the waiting list yearly against about 27,000 transplants.

 • Permanent AV access is created well in advance of anticipated dialysis.
 • Long-term dialysis complications: cardiovascular disease (ventricular hypertrophy, coronary artery
   disease (CAD)), hyperphosphataemia, altered bone metabolism, progressive vascular calcification,
   calciphylaxis — and these increase post-transplant morbidity and mortality.
 • Transplantation is the optimal therapy for ESRD — significantly better than dialysis, though inferior to
   the general population — but modality is individualised after weighing surgical and immunosuppression
   risk.
Concept — Every year on dialysis accrues cardiovascular and mineral–bone damage that the eventual
transplant inherits; the modality that shortens dialysis exposure therefore wins, and pre-emptive
transplantation is the logical extreme of that principle.

Kidney Transplantation Epidemiology
 • Transplant numbers rise yearly (about 27,000 in 2023), driven by deceased donation; living donation is
   stable (Fig. 91.2) but fell with COVID-19 in 2020, recovering only partially by 2023.
 • Over 139,000 candidates were listed in the US (2020 report of the Organ Procurement and
   Transplantation Network (OPTN) and Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR)).
 • Allocation revisions improved equity of deceased-donor access once listed; disparities in access to the
   list persist, mainly socioeconomic.

 • A successful transplant may triple life expectancy compared with listed patients who remain on dialysis.
 • First successful kidney transplant: identical twins, 1954. Immunosuppression has since brought 1-year
   graft survival close to 100%; long-term graft survival and organ shortage remain unsolved.
 • Quality of life improves, and transplantation is cost-effective: after year one, annual care costs (drugs,
   tests, clinic visits) are significantly below dialysis.

Recipient Evaluation for Transplantation
 • Comprehensive evaluation: ESRD carries cardiac, vascular and diabetic comorbidity and higher infection
   and malignancy risk.
 • Transplantation wins long-term but carries a higher risk of death in the first 3–6 months, depending on
   recipient and donor factors (Fig. 91.3).
Fig. 91.3 — Landmark: Wolfe et al., N Engl J Med 1999. Adjusted relative risk of death in first deceased-donor
transplant recipients versus wait-listed dialysis patients.
 • Relative risk 2.84 immediately after transplant → risk equal at 106 days → cumulative survival equal at
   244 days → long-term relative risk 0.32.
Timing of evaluation and listing
 • Timing follows the pace of decline, symptoms and donor availability; ideally evaluate before dialysis.
 • Deceased-donor listing permitted at eGFR ≤20 mL/min/1.73 m².
 • Pre-emptive transplantation is preferable when a living donor is available or deceased-donor
   transplantation is accessible.
 • Elderly patients may gain from a faster elderly-donor kidney over waiting for a standard kidney.
Aetiology and recurrence
 • Hypertension and diabetes are the commonest adult causes; control them and modify lifestyle,
   including weight loss, before transplant to prevent recurrence in the graft.

Cardiac evaluation
 • CAD is highly prevalent in ESRD and the major cause of morbidity and mortality; symptomatic patients
   are evaluated per current guidelines.
 • High-risk asymptomatic candidates (diabetes, family history, smoking, prior CAD, poor functional reserve,
   long dialysis) → echocardiography, exercise or pharmacological stress testing, selectively coronary
   angiography.
 • Optimal screening is unclear; studies challenge angiography in asymptomatic patients. Programmes
   follow local algorithms tied to waiting times and retest long waiters.
Vascular evaluation
 • Peripheral arterial disease raises risk of limb complications, cardiovascular events and death; assess
   peripheral and cerebral vessels and anastomotic targets.
 • Heavy iliac calcification may preclude a safe anastomosis.
 • Claudication may worsen after transplant — counsel. Amputation is not a contraindication but marks
   higher risk.
 • Tests: lower-limb and carotid Doppler; non-contrast computed tomography (CT) for calcification in long-
   standing diabetes, smokers and the elderly.
 • Non-healing foot ulcers and osteomyelitis → not a candidate until healed.
 • Prior stents or bypasses → define safe anastomotic targets with vascular surgery.
 • ADPKD with a family history of intracranial bleeding → evaluate for cerebral aneurysm and manage
   before transplant.
Wound healing, obesity and frailty
 • Manage before transplant: obesity, malnutrition, prior abdominal surgery, hernias, tobacco.
 • Obesity is a relative contraindication — more wound complications, delayed graft function (DGF), acute
   rejection, possibly death. Body mass index (BMI) cut-offs are centre- and comorbidity-specific; pre-
   transplant weight interventions are proposed.
 • Frailty predicts mortality and morbidity; assess and optimise, though strategies are unclear.
Haematological and psychosocial
 • Screen for haematological malignancy and bleeding or thrombotic disorders, including liver disease and
   anticoagulants, for perioperative planning.
 • Psychosocial assessment for all. Unstable psychiatric disorder, non-adherence or substance abuse must
   be treated and stable, with a support network, first.

Infection
 • Infection risk is higher (diabetes, immunosuppressants). Treat bacterial infection first; dialysis catheter
   infection may cause sepsis or peritonitis and need catheter removal. Drug-resistant organism surveillance
   per local practice.
 • Mandatory screening: tuberculosis (TB), hepatitis B and C, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) →
   therapeutic, prophylactic or suppressive treatment. Active TB is a contraindication until adequately
   treated.
 • Serology for cytomegalovirus (CMV), herpes simplex virus (HSV) and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) defines
   reactivation risk and directs post-transplant chemoprophylaxis.
 • Vaccinate before transplant: responses are better, and live vaccines cannot be used after transplant.
 • Chronic hepatitis → assess fibrosis, screen for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), suppress virus.
   Decompensated cirrhosis or severe portal hypertension → consider simultaneous liver–kidney
   transplant.
 • Hepatitis C virus (HCV): direct-acting antivirals give near-universal sustained virological response,
   allowing HCV-viraemic donor kidneys into HCV-negative recipients with treatment — expanding the
   pool.
 • Hepatitis B virus (HBV): vaccinate non-immune candidates; positive serology → assess viraemia and liver
   damage, HCC surveillance, reactivation monitoring, continued antivirals.

Additional Surgical Considerations
 • Anatomical evaluation defines approach, site, targets and vessel integrity — especially after previous
   transplant, surgery or radiation.
 • Prolonged femoral dialysis access (catheter or fistula) → exclude venous obstruction from stenosis or
   thrombosis. A functioning lower-limb fistula causes venous hypertension and poor graft function.
 • ADPKD: chronic infection, cyst rupture, pain, haematuria, tumours and mass effect (early satiety, weight
   loss); nephrectomy may be needed to make room — before or simultaneously with transplant, weighing
   risks and benefits.
 • Other indications for pre-transplant native nephrectomy: chronically infected kidneys, recurrent urinary
   tract infection (UTI) with reflux, significant proteinuria.
 • Heart, lung or liver candidates with renal dysfunction: add a kidney only if disease is advanced or
   recovery unlikely; an OPTN safety net prioritises later kidney allocation if function does not recover.

ABO and Human Leukocyte Antigen
 • ABO compatibility is standard: type O universal donor, type AB universal recipient.
 • Non-A1 donors (for example A2) can donate to B and O recipients if the recipient's anti-A titre is
   acceptable per centre policy.
 • Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) compatibility means a negative crossmatch between donor
   lymphocytes and recipient serum.
 • ABO- or HLA-incompatible transplant without preconditioning → accelerated rejection, thrombosis,
   graft loss. ABO-incompatible transplant is possible with more intensive immunosuppression and rigorous
   monitoring.
 • The major histocompatibility complex (MHC), known as HLA, consists of cell-surface glycoproteins.

Kidney Donation
 • Living or deceased; deceased donation is donation after brain death (DBD) or donation after circulatory
   death (DCD).
 • Before 1980 all deceased donation was DCD. The Uniform Determination of Death Act, 1980 defined
   death as irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function or of all functions of the entire
   brain, including the brainstem.

Deceased Donation
 • National Organ Transplant Act, 1984 (NOTA) established the OPTN, which coordinates transplant centres
   and organ procurement organisations (OPOs); the SRTR provides analytical support for monitoring and
   policy.
 • Donor data generate a match run on the national list; allocation policy revised 2014, amended 2021.
 • Ranking: waiting time (from dialysis start or pre-emptive listing); biological criteria (HLA matching,
   sensitisation); longevity matching by Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI) and Estimated Post-Transplant
   Survival (EPTS); priority for centres within 250 nautical miles of the donor hospital.
 • Donor evaluation: diagnosis and cause of death, neurological and cardiorespiratory status, medical and
   social history.
 • Donor contraindications: active metastatic cancer, haematological malignancy, history of melanoma,
   untreatable viral illness.
 • Adequately treated bacterial infection, including bacterial meningitis, is acceptable; viral meningitis or
   encephalitis needs careful assessment of transmission risk.
 • Laboratory: ABO and HLA typing; screening for HIV, HBV, HCV, EBV, CMV, syphilis and toxoplasma by
   serology and nucleic acid testing (NAT).
 • Paediatric donors under 15 kg → en bloc, with graft survival comparable to adult transplants.

Kidney Cold Preservation and Machine Perfusion
 • Immediate cooling to 4 °C sharply reduces metabolism and damage; preservation fluid maintains
   intracellular homeostasis, limiting anaerobic glycolysis and acidosis; reperfusion free radicals compound
   the injury.
 • Common fluids: University of Wisconsin (UW) solution and histidine–tryptophan–ketoglutarate (HTK)
   solution.
 • Pulsatile (hypothermic machine) perfusion extends preservation time and reduces DGF, with no
   difference in long-term graft survival.
 • Normothermic perfusion: limited clinical experience.
Concept — Preservation buys time by slowing metabolism, not stopping it; every measure here targets
ischaemia–reperfusion injury, which shows clinically as DGF — hence machine perfusion improves DGF but
not long-term survival.

Living Kidney Donation
 • Advantages: significantly better graft and patient survival; recipient optimised for a planned operation;
   pre-emptive transplant possible; negligible risk of donor-transmitted infection or malignancy.
 • Multidisciplinary team: independent donor advocate, social worker, physician, surgeon, coordinators.
 • Exclusions: concern for kidney disease, impaired function, malignancy history, chronic or active infection,
   cardiovascular disease, diabetes, significant obesity.
Evaluation sequence: self-administered questionnaire (medical, social, family history) → physical and
psychosocial evaluation, blood group and HLA typing → renal function by at least two methods, including a
measured technique → comprehensive metabolic panel including glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), lipid and
coagulation profiles → serology per OPTN, endemic infections, age-appropriate cancer screening → anatomy
by CT angiography (CTA) or magnetic resonance angiography, split function if needed → cardiac evaluation
in selected cases.
 • Urological evaluation: stone disease, genitourinary malignancy, recurrent UTI, previous urological
   surgery, bladder outflow symptoms. Metabolic stone work-up if stone history; bilateral stones generally
   exclude.
 • Counselling: healthy lifestyle, ideal weight, regular care — stricter in younger donors.

Living Donor Nephrectomy
 • Donor safety is the primary goal at every stage.
 • Laparoscopic donor nephrectomy first described by Ratner, 1995; now the preferred approach. Two-
   thirds are hand-assisted laparoscopic; in the US fewer than 2% are open.
 • Minimally invasive nephrectomy is the procedure of choice: faster recovery, less pain, higher
   acceptance, better cosmesis.
 • Open nephrectomy is reserved for complex anatomy or previous surgery and is more often needed on
   the right.
 • Donor-specific technique: minimal handling of the kidney; careful vascular dissection for adequate length
   without compromising donor safety; preserve periureteric tissue to avoid ureteric ischaemia; minimise
   warm ischaemia — divide ureter and vessels and extract expediently.

Kidney Paired Donation
 • Incompatibility arises from ABO mismatch or donor-specific antibodies (DSA) from sensitising events
   (transfusion, pregnancy, previous transplant).
 • Incompatible pairs are matched with other pairs through exchange databases (National Kidney Registry,
   Alliance for Paired Donation, United Network for Organ Sharing), maximising transplant chances.
Concept — Living donation is an operation on a healthy person for another's benefit, so every decision —
selection, counselling figures, surgical approach — is judged first by donor harm and only second by graft
quality.


KIDNEY TRANSPLANTATION SURGICAL PROCEDURE

Preparation for the Transplantation
 • On admission, re-review suitability, dialysis need and interval health changes.
 • Counsel on DGF, primary non-function, arterial and venous thrombosis, bleeding, urinary complications,
   immunosuppression side effects, and the rare transmission of donor-derived infection or malignancy.
 • Antibiotic prophylaxis covers skin flora and urinary contaminants or colonisation, guided by previous
   positive cultures.
 • Safety check including ABO verification before induction of anaesthesia.
 • Central venous catheter (CVC) for drugs and sampling; multiple previous dialysis lines with possible
   stenosis → pre-operative interventional radiology placement.
 • Foley catheter after induction, accessible intraoperatively for bladder distension during the ureteric
   anastomosis.

Multiple renal arteries
 • Incidence 18–43%, bilateral in 15%. A meta-analysis found more complications and DGF but comparable
   long-term graft and patient survival.

   artery into the patch nearer the main artery or into the main artery itself (Figs. 91.6, 91.7).
 • The lower pole artery must be preserved — it supplies the ureter. Upper pole branches ≤2 mm may be
   ligated if reconstruction is complex.

Choice of Site
 • Factors: iliac artery quality, iliac vein patency, failed graft or scar, stoma, space in ADPKD, groin dialysis

 • Right iliac fossa is the traditional choice: the common iliac vein can be mobilised by securing internal iliac

 • Contralateral placement is suggested: the renal pelvis and ureter then lie anterior, easing future urinary
   reconstruction.
 • Retroperitoneal placement is ideal.

Ureteric anastomosis
 • Commonest technique: extravesical ureteroneocystostomy (Lich, 1961); choice depends on ureteric
   vascularity and length and on recipient bladder quality.
Lich–Gregoir sequence: fill bladder via catheter → dissect perivesical fat, clear detrusor → myotomy along
the intended ureteric course → open mucosa at the distal end of the myotomy → trim ureter to length and
spatulate → mucosal anastomosis with fine (6-0/5-0) absorbable suture (polyglyconate or polydioxanone),
with or without a stent → close muscle over the mucosal suture → short submucosal tunnel.
Fig. 91.9 — Lich–Gregoir (Konnak et al., 1975): anterolateral seromuscular incision down to bulging mucosa;

prevents proximal migration of the ureter in the tunnel; seromuscular layer closed loosely over the ureter.
 • Alternatives: ureteroureterostomy, pyeloureterostomy, pyelovesicostomy, anastomosis to ileal conduit or
   pouch.
 • Double ureters → conjoined (Wallace) or individual anastomosis.
 • Document the stent and a retrieval plan: forgotten stents cause calcification, graft dysfunction, even loss.
 • Drain: surgeon's preference, by extent of dissection and lymphatic control; closed system, removed early.
Concept — The operation is ordered to protect the graft's two vulnerabilities, ischaemia and the ureteric
blood supply: the vein goes first to shorten arterial ischaemia, the lower pole artery and periureteric tissue
are untouchable, and contralateral placement keeps the ureter reachable for later repair.

Paediatric En Bloc Kidney Transplantation
 • Donors under 15 kg: both kidneys transplanted en bloc with the aorta and IVC as vascular conduits;
   ureters anastomosed conjoined or individually (Fig. 91.10).
 • Ideal recipient: adult, BMI <30, low immunological risk, low recurrence risk, no significant vascular
   disease.
 • Early vascular thrombosis and graft loss 10–15%, yet long-term graft survival equals living-donor
   transplantation.

Surgical Complications
 • Early complications: haematuria, urine leak, haematoma and haemorrhage, lymphocele, renal vein and
   artery thrombosis. Urological complications are covered in Chapter 92.
 • Bleeding (brisk sanguineous drain output, falling haematocrit, graft swelling; risk raised by renal failure,
   antiplatelets, anticoagulants) → transfusion and immediate exploration. Large haematoma causes pain
   and pressure on renal vein and bladder. Ultrasound and CT help, especially if obese.

Intraoperative prevention
 • Clamp and anastomose clear of atheroma — clamp injury causes intimal dissection, stenosis, limb-
   threatening ischaemia. Trial the final position to avoid stretch or kinking.
 • Renal artery usually to external or common iliac. Avoid the internal iliac artery if the contralateral one
   is occluded or already used → gluteal claudication and erectile dysfunction.
 • Short right renal vein → extend with donor IVC, or mobilise the recipient iliac vein by dividing the internal
   iliac vein.
 • Previous deep vein thrombosis (DVT), leg swelling or groin dialysis line → image iliac vein patency.
   Ipsilateral functioning groin AV fistula may need ligation to avoid graft venous hypertension.
 • Resect venous valve cusps at the anastomosis — inclusion in the suture line causes outflow obstruction.
 • On reperfusion, a flaccid soft kidney or a tense engorged kidney → evaluate inflow and outflow,
   anastomoses, twisting or stretching of the pedicle; intraoperative ultrasound; occasionally take-down and

Lymphocele
 • Perinephric pseudocyst of variable incidence.
 • Risk factors: extensive dissection, retransplantation, obesity, anticoagulation, mechanistic target of
   rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors.
 • Mostly asymptomatic; large ones compress iliac vein (leg oedema), renal vein, ureter (hydronephrosis),
   bladder (frequency). Ultrasound shows it; contrast CT maps anatomy pre-intervention; fine-needle
   aspiration distinguishes urinoma, seroma, abscess.
 • Treat only if symptomatic: aspiration with or without sclerotherapy, percutaneous drain, laparoscopic or
   open fenestration into the peritoneum. Infected lymphocele needs adequate percutaneous or open
   drainage.
 • Success is highest with laparoscopic fenestration, then open surgery; sclerotherapy and drain placement
   are intermediate; aspiration alone is lowest.
 • Prevention: meticulous ligation of perivascular and hilar lymphatics; a drain placed at transplant also
   reduces incidence.
Transplant nephrectomy
 • Most grafts failing within the first year need nephrectomy. Indications: graft intolerance syndrome (graft
   swelling, haematuria, graft pain, non-specific inflammatory symptoms), infection, tumour.
