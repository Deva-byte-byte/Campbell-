Chapter 60 — Surgical Management of Differences of
Sexual Differentiation and Cloacal and Anorectal
Malformations
 • Surgery for disorders of sex development (DSDs), genital atypia and cloacal anomalies is among the most
   complex problems in paediatric urology and among the most debated topics in medicine. Most children
   with a DSD have some genital atypia.
 • Terminology is itself contested: most patients with congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) and their
   parents dislike both "DSD" and "intersex".
 • The birth of a child with a DSD is highly stressful. The team must explain the diagnosis and every
   controversy and option.
 • The historical default was early "normalising" surgery. Parents found "doing nothing" stressful and
   viewed surgery as obvious rather than a decision. Against this, genital proportions vary widely in normal
   children and adults. Families should also hear about the child's possible long-term psychosocial
   experience.
 • Non-negotiable principles: present all pros and cons of surgery and of no surgery; no parent or child is
   ever talked into surgery; the team supports the family whichever path is chosen; families have access to
   support groups (CARES Foundation, Accord Alliance, Magic Foundation, InterACT).
 • The controversy is not the technique but the timing, and who decides (parents or child). The authors are
   neither for nor against surgery; they advocate a multidisciplinary family-centred team. The chapter
   describes the surgical options at any age and assumes family and team agree on the plan.
 • Minimum team: patient and family, paediatric urologist or surgeon, paediatric endocrinologist, geneticist,
   neonatologist, psychologist or psychiatrist, and ethicist. Ideally also patients who have and have not
   elected surgery, access to all advocacy groups, and clergy if desired.
Concept — The dispute in DSD surgery concerns consent and timing, not operative technique. The
multidisciplinary team, unbiased counselling and equal support for either decision are therefore the
framework within which every technique in the chapter sits.

CLASSIFICATION OF UROGENITAL SINUS AND CLOACAL ANOMALIES
 • A urogenital sinus is a persistent communication of the vagina with the urinary tract. It can lie anywhere
   from meatus to bladder but is most often in the mid to distal urethra; the two structures exit the
   perineum as one common channel.

Concept — Whatever the label or external appearance, true urethral length (bladder neck to confluence) is

Evaluation
Congenital adrenal hyperplasia
 • Urogenital sinus is most often seen in DSD states, chiefly CAH; the nonclassic mild form affects up to 1 in
   500.
 • CAH is the commonest cause of atypical genitalia in newborns: half of all cases and 90% of those with a
   46,XX karyotype.
 • CAH is a group of autosomal recessive enzyme defects. The commonest causing genital atypia is 21-
   hydroxylase deficiency, about 1 in 15,000–16,000. The gene is CYP21A2 on chromosome 6p, accounting
   for 90–95% of CAH; virilisation corresponds to the mutation type.
 • CAH is a spectrum disorder, from mild to life-altering. The first priority is accurate diagnosis, with the
   karyotype within 48 hours.
 • Salt wasting affects 67–75% through aldosterone deficiency, so fluid and electrolytes must be monitored.
     ◦ Classic findings: hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, high urinary sodium, low serum and urinary
       aldosterone, raised plasma renin activity.
     ◦ Consequences: dehydration, poor feeding and hypotension, progressing to vascular collapse, shock
       and death (adrenal crisis).
 • Work-up: rapid karyotype on cultured peripheral leucocytes, serum electrolytes and CAH hormone levels;
   results shared with the team and family.
 • Gender is increasingly understood as fluid rather than simply binary. The child is not named until
   evaluation is complete and the family has met the team.
Cloacal anomalies
 • Far more complex and multisystem, but rare: about 1 in 20,000–50,000.
 • The most challenging anorectal malformations (ARMs), making up about 5–13.6% of ARMs.
 • External genitalia vary widely; a prominent phallus-like structure may prompt an initial DSD evaluation.
Concept — In the newborn with a urogenital sinus, the first threat is metabolic, not anatomical: diagnose
CAH and anticipate adrenal crisis before any thought of reconstruction.

History and Physical Examination
History
 • Antenatal ultrasonography identifies many cases: fluid-filled pelvic structures (vagina and bladder) with
   indeterminate genitalia. Newborn CAH screening has run in all 50 US states since 2009 and in many
   regions worldwide.
 • History and examination often reach the diagnosis. Ask about:
     ◦ maternal medication in pregnancy, especially androgens;
     ◦ family history of early infant death or fluid and electrolyte disturbance, which suggests CAH;
     ◦ siblings with genital atypia or gender dysphoria;
     ◦ relatives with abnormal puberty.
General and abdominal examination
 • Assess overall health before the genitalia; abnormal facies suggest other syndromes.
 • Hypertension points to CAH from 11β-hydroxylase deficiency, so blood pressure must be recorded.
   Dehydration also suggests CAH.
 • A suprapubic mass may be a distended bladder, hydrometrocolpos, or both. Hydrometrocolpos is
   frequently the initial sign and often the only early finding in pure urogenital sinus.
Hydrocolpos in cloaca
 • Abdominal distension may be severe, combining hydrometrocolpos, bladder and bowel distension.
   Hydrocolpos was common in early series, although its frequency varied widely, and is usually
   accompanied by uterovaginal duplication.
 • It is more frequent with a common channel over 3 cm, vaginal duplication, posterior cloaca, and a
   rectocloacal rather than rectovaginal communication.
 • Mechanism: urine flows preferentially through the sinus into the vagina(s) on voiding, and the vagina
   drains poorly. Maternal oestrogen drives cervical mucus secretion, adding to the distension.
 • Urine entering the rectum can cause significant fluid and electrolyte disturbance.
 • Severe pelvic distension can cause oedematous, cyanotic legs, respiratory distress and acidosis.
Spine
 • Spinal cord anomalies are associated with urogenital sinus and are very common with cloaca.
 • Cutaneous markers are a sacral dimple, hair patch or abnormal pigmentation. More often the clue is
   bony: an abnormal buttock crease or flat buttocks from sacral agenesis.
Genital examination
 • Record clitoral size, the consistency of the erectile bodies, and any curvature.
 • Huffman clitoral index = glans width × clitoral length: normal below 3.5 mm²; above 10 mm² is of
   concern. A term female clitoris is about 4 mm long and 3.3 mm wide.
 • Persistent isolated clitoral hypertrophy in preterm infants is not a DSD.
 • Meatus of the sinus: anywhere from a near-normal introital position to the tip of a well-formed glans.
 • Gonads: record number, position and consistency. If both gonads are descended, a 46,XX karyotype is
   extremely unlikely.
 • Labioscrotal folds: note their relation to the clitoris and rectum and the degree of fusion.
   Hyperpigmentation of the folds and areolae in CAH reflects raised melanocyte-stimulating hormone.
 • Anus: usually normally sited, but anterior displacement is not uncommon and bridges to cloaca. The
   pure urogenital sinus aligns more with cloaca than with the DSD urogenital sinus.

 • Posterior cloaca: the sinus opens into the anus (type A) or just anterior to it (type B).
 • A gentle rectal examination may identify a palpable cervix.
Examination of the persistent cloaca
 • There is a single perineal orifice, because the rectum also enters the common channel.
 • External appearance ranges from near-normal female to complete genital transposition or a blank, "doll-
   like" perineum, with vaginal-type and urethral-type appearances (Fig. 60.5).
 • An enlarged phallus-like structure may be present. The opening may exit through an apparently normal
   introitus or at the tip of the phallus-like structure, and a secondary accessory channel at the tip has been
   reported.

Radiographic and Endoscopic Evaluation
Urogenital Sinus Abnormalities
 • Define before surgery: common-sinus length, confluence site and its distance from the bladder neck,
   vaginal size and number, whether a cervix is present, and bladder and urethral anatomy, radiographically
   or endoscopically.
 • The critical determinant of the operation is the vagina's position relative to the bladder neck, far more
   than common-channel length. The same concept produced the new cloacal algorithm (Wood et al., 2017).
 • The confluence lies on a continuum from bladder to near-normal perineal position (Fig. 60.6). It is not a
   simple choice between "high" (proximal, suprasphincteric) and "low" (distal, infrasphincteric).
 • Ganesan concept: in CAH the urethrovaginal confluence is constant, with a normal proximal urethra.
   Bladder neck to confluence is generally 1.5–2 cm in CAH infants. What lengthens with virilisation is the
   vagina-to-meatus distance, so a "high" confluence in CAH is a product of virilisation.
 • The illusion of height: a short common channel looks low and a long one looks high. In the virilised sinus
   this is reinforced by a male-like external sphincter, a verumontanum-like confluence just proximal to it,
   and a masculinised bony pelvis.
 • The Rink classification records exact common-channel length and bladder-neck-to-vagina distance, with
   confluence level, clitoral size and external appearance.
 • Ultrasonography is mandatory: kidneys, ovaries, uterus, and any bladder or vaginal distension. Enlarged,
   cerebriform adrenals indicate CAH.
 • Genitography fills bladder, urethra, vagina and sinus with contrast (Figs. 60.7–60.8).
   Foley balloon occluding the perineal meatus → contrast injected → catheter into the bladder as for a
   voiding cystourethrogram → images on voiding (some vaginas fill only then).
   A cervical impression at the vaginal dome indicates normal female internal organs.
 • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is of limited value in CAH but very helpful in pure urogenital sinus,
   other DSDs and cloaca.
 • In CAH, endoscopy at reconstruction nearly always defines the anatomy, so genitography is less useful:
   in the authors' series it was often inaccurate, added nothing beyond endoscopy and did not change the
   type or timing of surgery.
 • Endoscopy is the most helpful study for surgical planning. In CAH it is done at reconstruction. It is a
   separate early procedure for gender assignment in other DSDs, complex pure urogenital sinus, or when
   genitography fails to show the vagina.
 • If the vagina is not identified, look for multiple punctate openings in the proximal urethra; pass a ureteral
   catheter into the opening and study the vagina fluoroscopically. Record vaginal position, size and number
   (Fig. 60.9: the urethra is superior and the vagina inferior).
 • In severe masculinisation there may be a male-like external sphincter with the vagina entering just
   proximal to it in a verumontanum-like structure, and occasionally a completely masculinised urethra.
 • Gonadal biopsy or inspection of the internal genitalia is rarely needed; it is now mostly laparoscopic.
     ◦ Perform it only if the result would change the gender of rearing.
     ◦ Use a deep longitudinal incision, because the ovarian component of an ovotestis may completely
       surround the testicular component or lie at the poles.
 • All children with a DSD undergo chromosomal, endocrine and genetic evaluation (Chapter 59).

Cloacal Anomalies
Prenatal diagnosis
 • Persistent cloaca is increasingly diagnosed prenatally, as early as 19 weeks.
 • Diagnostic combination: transient fetal ascites + bilobed or trilobed pelvic cysts + bilateral hydronephrosis
   + reduced amniotic fluid.
 • Mechanism of the ascites: vaginal outlet obstruction → urine refluxes through the uterus → out of the
   fallopian tubes into the peritoneum.
Postnatal imaging
 • Begin with a plain abdominal film and abdominal ultrasonography. A pelvic mass may be obvious on a
   kidneys, ureters and bladder film.
       ◦ Retrograde flow of urine and meconium produces the classic linear calcifications or calcified
         meconium.
       ◦ Granular calcification along the rectum reflects urine in the rectum.
 • Pelvic ultrasonography is of limited use for Müllerian anatomy.
 • Abdominal ultrasonography shows renal anatomy and hydronephrosis, which is common. It usually
   results from hydrocolpos compressing the bladder neck, sometimes from ureteric compression or
   primary obstructive megaureter. Renal anomalies (dysplasia, fusion, ectopia, pelviureteric junction
   obstruction, duplication) are reported in 25–83%.
Cloacogram and endoscopy
 • Unlike CAH, genitography and endoscopy are mandatory in cloaca. Most now use a computed
   tomography (CT) or MRI cloacogram combined with endoscopy.
   General anaesthesia → endoscopy → balloon catheters inflated in bladder, vagina(s) and rectum →
   contrast through these and through a mucous-fistula catheter at CT or MRI → three-dimensional (3D)
   reconstruction.
 • No modality is perfect: some hold endoscopy best, others 3D reconstructed imaging.
 • Targets: rectal and vaginal confluences; urethral length and its communication with the cloaca; vaginal
   anatomy; position of the true rectum.
 • The urinary communication is usually urethral, but in a substantial minority there is virtually no urethra
   and it lies at bladder-neck level.
Vaginal and uterine anatomy
 • Cloaca impairs Müllerian duct fusion. Single and duplicated vaginas are about equally common, the
   vagina is absent in a minority, and in modern series most have vaginal and uterine duplication (Fig.
   60.10: duplicated fluid-filled vaginas behind the decompressed bladder).

 • Preoperative definition succeeds best with vaginoscopy and worst with pelvic ultrasonography;
   cloacograms sometimes disagree with operative findings.
 • Duplication patterns: usually side by side with a single opening into the cloaca, sometimes separate
   openings. The vaginas may differ in size, one may enter the sidewall of the other, and a cervix usually tops
   each vagina. Vaginal entry lies on a spectrum from bladder to near the perineum.
 • The uterus usually mirrors the vagina (two vaginas, two uteri). The vagina may be absent with the uterus
   present, or the uterus hypoplastic. Bicornuate, hypoplastic or didelphic uterus is found in about a third.
Rectal confluence and common channel
 • Rectal confluence is most often at the level of the vaginal confluence. It may be broad or a long narrow
   fistula, and may enter the vagina or bladder without reaching the cloaca. The commonest site is within
   the septum of a duplicated vagina. The communication is usually vaginal, less often cloacal.
 • The true rectum may lie well above the fistula; locating it matters as much as locating the fistula.
 • Peña's categorisation by common-channel length (under or over 3 cm) is now almost universal.
 • Channel type: narrow and urethra-like, or large, redundant and vagina-like; roughly half each.

Associated-system imaging and early endoscopy
 • Echocardiography always. MRI is necessary for the lumbosacral spine and for pelvic anatomy and
   musculature.
 • Spine:
     ◦ sacral anomalies are common, and MRI detects spinal cord anomalies in up to 43%;
     ◦ about one-third have a tethered cord, yet skin stigmata are absent in most of them;
     ◦ MRI also defines the level of rectal atresia and sphincter development.
 • Early separate endoscopy is often needed to decompress the vagina and bladder and define the anatomy.
     ◦ The vagina is easy to see, but bladder entry can be very difficult or impossible in a neonate because
       the distended vagina compresses the bladder anteriorly. Empty the vagina first.
     ◦ The rectal fistula can be hard to find. Irrigate mucus and faeces through the scope and the mucous
       fistula; a small catheter through the mucous fistula localises the rectal confluence.
     ◦ Document the urethral length to each confluence and the common-channel length, which predicts
       faecal continence.
Concept — Cloacal imaging is organised around three measurements: common-channel length, true
urethral length and the levels of the rectal and vaginal confluences. The reconstruction is chosen from
these, and the spine and kidneys set the prognosis.

SURGICAL RECONSTRUCTION OF DISORDERS OF SEX DEVELOPMENT AND
UROGENITAL SINUS
Initial Management, Timing, and Principles
The case for and against early surgery
 • Most children with a urogenital sinus have genital atypia and are first assessed by the multidisciplinary
   DSD team; the family must know all the controversies.
 • Historical rationale for early "normalising" surgery: positive psychosocial adjustment; relief of parental
   distress (limited evidence); the belief that raising a child with atypia is very difficult; parental stress at
   "doing nothing". Surgery was advised at 2–6 months, when tissues are most pliable and psychological
   trauma minimal.
 • Core problem: assigning gender when the individual's eventual gender identity is unknown.
 • Opposition: InterACT, Human Rights Watch, the United Nations, three former US Surgeons General and
   some physician groups question early surgery. Most support a moratorium until the patient can express a
   wish, at an age not yet defined.
     ◦ Informed consent is impossible in an infant, and many regard genital surgery as the patient's right.
     ◦ The ethics of parental decision-making remain unresolved.
     ◦ The infant is the passive centre of the team yet ultimately has the most important voice.
     ◦ Data on the impact of raising a child with atypia are lacking.
 • Counselling standard: present pros and cons without bias. Observation with psychological and peer
   support must be an option, and the same support is owed to families who choose early surgery.
What surgery can and cannot achieve
 • Excellent cosmesis is achievable, but sexual identity is multifactorial and the brain is the dominant organ
   in sexual orientation.
 • Little is known of adult adjustment after surgery, or of the path an operated infant would have chosen as
   an adult.
 • Outcomes of infant surgery are not known for about 20 years, so published results may describe
   abandoned techniques.
 • Genital surgery does not "cure" a DSD. Females with CAH are less satisfied with their genitalia than
   controls, whether operated or not. Women with unoperated clitoromegaly from CAH report no net
   benefit from it and harm across social, romantic and emotional domains.
Counselling and parental decision-making
 • There is no absolutely correct answer. Never withhold information; secrecy has no place; counselling is
   unbiased and transparent.
 • At the authors' institution, all parents and patients meet a clinical psychologist before surgery, and
   families are directed to all major advocacy groups whatever their stance.
 • Attitudes to early surgery, especially clitoral surgery, have shifted.
 • Parental priorities: the largest concerns were not surgical but the life-threatening and developmental
   effects of CAH and medication side effects. The child's non-involvement in the decision ranked among the
   least prioritised.
 • Decisional regret after genital surgery is mild to moderate in a minority of parents and severe in none. It
   is lower than after hypospadias repair, adenotonsillectomy or paediatric cancer treatment, and no parent
   preferred delayed surgery.

 • Hereafter the chapter assumes all parties agree that surgery is warranted. The techniques apply from

Anatomy to be corrected
 • Clitoral hypertrophy; fusion and anterior displacement of the labia majora; absent labia minora; a
   common urogenital sinus. Fig. 60.11 shows moderate virilisation with incomplete or complete labioscrotal
   fusion, and severe virilisation.
 • Normal clitoral, vaginal and labial size varies considerably.
 • Three historical steps: clitoroplasty → labioplasty → vaginoplasty.
Timing of clitoroplasty
No consensus in the mid-1900s → about 1 year → first few months (1980s) → 1–3 weeks (1990s).
 • Parents now less often choose surgery for minimal hypertrophy.
 • Current indication: severe hypertrophy only (Prader III–IV), the basis of recent support for early surgery
   (see consensus table).
 • Satisfaction with clitoral function is higher the younger the age at surgery.
 • Clitoral surgery, whether recession or resection, is the most controversial component of the whole
   debate.
Timing of vaginoplasty
 • The vagina has no function in childhood, and some base timing partly on confluence level.
 • Low (distal) confluence: simultaneous clitoroplasty, vaginoplasty and labioplasty is the historical standard.
   High confluence: see table below.
 • US practice: in a national paediatric hospital database, genital surgery was done at a mean of about 10
   months. Vaginal and perineal surgery predominated; clitoral surgery was done in about half.
 • Patient and parent views: most CAH patients and parents support the parents' right to decide and
   oppose moratoria. They are satisfied with early surgery, and those operated later generally wish it had
   been earlier.
 • There is no universal agreement on vaginoplasty timing and no adequate data comparing early with
   late surgery.

The glans
 • Glans reduction is controversial. If attempted, any excision is ventral, near the midline, like hypospadias
   glans wings, because the glans is innervated by perforating branches at the dorsal glans–corporal
   junction.
 • Do not excise glanular epithelium, since the sensory neuropeptides lie just beneath it. No data show that
   a large glans or clitoris harms sexual function.
 • The glans is secured to the coronal stumps; a glans sewn to the pubis lies abnormally high and prominent.
Flap vaginoplasty
Omega flap incised with its underlying fat → sinus exposed → posterior sinus and vagina dissected off the
rectum (the most difficult step) → stay sutures in the meatus → posterior sinus wall opened in the midline
into the posterior vaginal wall → continued until normal-calibre vagina is reached (the distal third is usually
narrowed) → interrupted sutures from flap to split posterior vaginal wall.
 • The flap must be long enough for a tension-free anastomosis, wide enough for a normal-calibre introitus
   without compromising the perineal-body blood supply, and not redundant, which would form an
   obstructing introital mound.
Labioplasty
Clitoral skin unfurled and divided in the midline, stopping well short of the base → base combined with
preputial skin as the clitoral hood → split skin brought down as labia minora, sutured to the ventral plate
and lateral vaginal wall → Y-incisions around the labia majora → labia majora mobilised and fixed inferiorly

 • Inferior placement often gives an M-shaped hood; plicating the dermis restores an inverted U. The end
   point is an introitus lying between the labia minora and majora, not an isolated perineal hole.

and always open back to normal-calibre vagina. Most historical failures broke one of these.

Total and Partial Urogenital Mobilization
 • Peña (1997): total urogenital mobilisation (TUM) was devised to complete the sinus component of
   cloacal repair. The entire sinus is dissected circumferentially and moved towards the perineum. Peña
   reported operative time cut by about 70%, superior cosmesis, and less fistula, vaginal stenosis and
   acquired atresia, because vaginal separation is usually unnecessary.
 • TUM has since been applied to urogenital sinus, female exstrophy and penile agenesis.
 • Uses of the mobilised sinus:
     ◦ amputated, with vagina and urethra sewn flush;
     ◦ mucosa-lined vestibule;
     ◦ Passerini-type flap for the anterior vaginal wall in a pull-through;
     ◦ replacement of the posterior skin flap, either split laterally and rotated posteriorly, or halved
       longitudinally with both halves rotated inferiorly.
 • The authors incorporate mobilisation into nearly every urogenital sinus repair. A mid-level confluence
   reaches the perineum without vaginal separation; the highest still need a pull-through, which is much
   easier after mobilisation.
 • TUM is not a vaginoplasty type in its own right but enables one, and a flap or pull-through may still be
   needed. The vagina is rarely sewn flush, because a circumferential anastomosis risks stenosis.
Technique
Endoscopy (confluence level; Fogarty in vagina, Foley in bladder) → sponge in rectum → incisions marked,

Results of Urogenital Sinus and Disorders of Sex Development Surgery
Limitations of the evidence
 • The evidence base is weak: limited, nearly always retrospective, small single-institution series, often with
   outdated or unreported techniques. One review judged the data too poor to interpret meaningfully.
 • Endpoints are inadequate. Success is usually an open vagina with a feminised appearance; it should
   include erotic sensation, lubrication, sexual satisfaction, orgasm and painless intercourse, which are only
   beginning to be studied.
 • The psychological effects of early surgery without consent, versus raising a child with atypia, are largely
   unexplored.
 • Cosmetic and early functional results are uniformly favourable; long-term outcomes are the concern.
   Studies rarely match procedure to severity, and none report endocrine control.

Clitoroplasty outcomes
 • Technique has improved greatly on the basis of neuroanatomy, but there are no long-term results for
    current techniques.
 • Nerve conduction: pudendal evoked potentials show modern clitoroplasty preserves conduction in the
   dorsal bundle, and sensation was intact in early series.
 • Conduction is not sensation: absent sensation and orgasm can coexist with normal evoked potentials.
   Genitosensory testing has shown markedly altered hot, cold and vibratory sensation, while a small
   recent series found average sensation. Sensation alone may poorly reflect clitoral normality.
 • Cosmesis: unsatisfactory in nearly half in one series; normal in a majority in another, with the rest too
   large, too small or absent. No devascularisation has been seen after modern clitoroplasty.
 • Satisfaction:
     ◦ Many operated women had multiple clitoral operations, and a fifth were dissatisfied, citing reduced
       sensitivity; yet half of the non-operated thought the clitoris too large.
     ◦ Orgasm did not differ between operated and non-operated women.
     ◦ Sensitivity is affected in nearly all after clitoroplasty, but partial resection sparing more nerve function
       does no worse than no surgery.
 • Physician–patient discrepancy: in a European multicentre study, physicians rated results positively almost
   universally, while a minority of patients were (very) unsatisfied. Appearance and function correlated
   poorly. For clitoral size, about half were highly satisfied, a third indifferent, a minority dissatisfied.
 • Whether initial virilisation affects sensitivity is unknown.
Urogenital mobilisation outcomes
 • Results are very early. TUM and PUM are technically easier with superior cosmesis, but functional
   superiority is unproven. Stress incontinence or sphincteric denervation remains possible, although most
   report continence unaltered. Use with caution until long-term data exist.

 • Quality of life and health-related quality of life are normal in most, with mental health equal to or better
   than population norms. A minority score lower because of distressing memories of too-late surgery, of
   the treatment itself, or poor sexual function. Relationships begin later than in the general population, and

SURGICAL RECONSTRUCTION FOR CLOACAL MALFORMATIONS
Initial Management, Timing, and Principles
 • Stabilise first. The infant is often ill, with abdominal distension that may compromise respiration.
 • Historical approach: decompression, then rectal pull-through, with genitourinary reconstruction later or
   never; vaginal surgery was sometimes deferred to puberty for the benefit of oestrogen.
 • Majority view: piecemeal repair is a disservice and absolutely contraindicated. Rectum, vagina and
   urethra should be repaired in a single stage. This gives the best exposure for rectovaginal separation,
   untouched tissue, and no need to remobilise the rectum.
 • Hendren's rule: rectal pull-through should never be a separate procedure. Many of his referrals had had
   a previous pull-through, and definitive repair then needed repeat rectal mobilisation. Complete neonatal
   repair was also proposed, then doubted by its proponent.
 • Levitt and Peña's three neonatal pitfalls: failure to recognise and manage hydrocolpos; colostomy or
   vesicostomy problems; clinical misdiagnosis.

 • After decompression, voiding may continue into the vagina(s) and/or rectum. This causes rapid
   distension, poor urinary drainage, persistent hydronephrosis, urinary tract infection (UTI) and
   hyperchloraemic acidosis.

Repair of Obstructive Urinary and Other Pathology
 • This is the third stage. Structural renal abnormalities, some obstructive, occur in 60–83% of persistent
   cloaca. With a common channel over 3 cm, urological defects are present in nearly all.
 • Do not defer obstructive lesions while awaiting definitive repair; treat them promptly. Chronic kidney
   disease (CKD) occurs in 75% and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in 17% of persistent cloaca.
 • Bladder function: urodynamics are abnormal in 90% before and after repair. Patterns vary: overactivity,
   atonia, detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia, elevated leak point pressure.
 • Vesicoureteral reflux is common but managed medically if UTI is prevented. Other systems (cardiac,
   spinal cord) are fully evaluated, and some are repaired in this phase.

Definitive Repair of Cloacal Malformations
 • Usually at 6–12 months, in a well-nourished, thriving child; some operate at 1 month in healthy infants.
 • Never before the urinary tract has been evaluated, hydrocolpos excluded, and associated anomalies
   defined. A separate prior endoscopy helps.
 • Bowel preparation: irrigate the distal colon through the mucous fistula and distal limb, plus a standard
   GoLYTELY preparation.
Operative Technique: Cloaca
 • Some advise rectal pull-through only in infancy with later urogenital repair, but most repair both
   together; that technique is described here.
 • Prerequisites: common-channel length, urethral length, vaginal anatomy and site of the rectal fistula.

Rectal separation
Midline incision from the coccygeal tip to the posterior cloacal orifice → sphincter structures marked with

 • Rectum and vagina share a common wall: it is better to enter the vagina than the rectum or urethra. An
   abdominal approach is more likely with a common channel over 3 cm.
Classic vaginal separation (common channel over 3 cm or urethra under 1.5 cm)

   Side-by-side duplication is most common, and its septum is incised with electrocautery at the initial

Urethra, perineum and rectum
Common-channel openings closed in two to three absorbable layers to form the urethra → adjacent fat flap
over the repair (fistula prevention is critical) → a large, vagina-like cloaca is opened entirely and tailored over
the Foley → vagina secured to the perineum, rotated to avoid overlapping suture lines if injured → Foley left
for 2 weeks → perineal body reconstructed → rectum pulled through, widened rectum tailored → muscles
meet behind the rectum → rectum centred in the sphincter mass and sutured to skin.
 • Aftercare: rectal dilation from 2–3 weeks; colostomy closure at about 3 months if healed.
 • Laparoscopic assistance gives low fistula dissection, accurate rectal placement and an excellent pelvic
   view. Single-incision surgery is as effective as three-port, with better cosmesis.
 • After catheter removal: monitor voiding closely, because poor emptying from a neuropathic component
   occurs in almost one-third. Some leave a suprapubic tube until normal voiding is documented. Start CIC
   at any doubt and continue until bladder dynamics are normal; omission risks UTI, hydronephrosis and
   renal loss.

 • Peña's TUM is widely accepted within the algorithm above: easier and quicker, with less blood loss. The
   whole sinus is mobilised through 360 degrees after rectal separation, as in urogenital sinus TUM.
 • Separating a vagina that still falls short after TUM was once practised but is no longer recommended
   because of ischaemia, which again stresses accurate preoperative measurement.
 • Continence to date is no different from standard repair; long-term data are awaited.

 KEY POINTS: CLOACAL RECONSTRUCTION
   • Four steps: (1) gastrointestinal decompression (colostomy); (2) urinary decompression (CIC,
     vesicostomy, vaginostomy); (3) correction of associated organ-system anomalies; (4) cloacal repair
     (PSARVUP).
   • TUM is now commonly used after rectal separation, but only when the common channel is under 3 cm
     and the urethra over 1.5 cm.
   • Results depend largely on whether the spinal cord is normal. CIC and a bowel programme are
     frequently needed for urinary and faecal continence.

Concept — Common-channel length and urethral length decide whether the urogenital complex can be
moved en bloc (TUM) or must be taken apart (separation). Mobilising a channel whose urethra is too short
drags the bladder neck below the continence mechanism.

Results of Cloacal Surgery
 • Repair is exceedingly challenging: the anatomy is complex and individual, and the surgeon must be
   patient, imaginative and gentle. Nonetheless, most patients lead productive lives; the pioneers were
   Hendren and Peña.
 • Endpoints: classically urinary continence, faecal continence and sexual capability. Future reporting should
   add erotic sensitivity, fertility, painless intercourse, orgasm and quality of life.
Urinary outcome
 • Early results were dismal, with most patients incontinent.
 • A high proportion have a neuropathic component, far more often when the common channel exceeds
   3.5 cm.
 • High outlet resistance without neurological abnormality: most ARM patients had preoperative leak point
   pressure above 40 cm H₂O, many with a normal spinal MRI.
 • Among spinal abnormalities, a short spinal cord has the greatest impact on continence.

Renal outcome
 • CKD 75%, ESRD 17%. Hydronephrosis and reflux are frequent, and most CKD is stage I–II. Other series
   report renal insufficiency in half, and an elevated or borderline creatinine in nearly half.
 • Aggressive diagnosis and modern bladder management (CIC, diversion, augmentation) can preserve
   renal function even with CKD.
 • Identify and treat renal impairment and obstruction at presentation. Lifelong yearly urological
   evaluation, with prompt treatment of obstruction or UTI and assessment of bladder dynamics.
Faecal outcome
 • Faecal continence is directly related to neurological status. Hendren's good prognostic signs: good
   perineal raphe, well-defined anal dimple, normal spine and MRI, and brisk muscle reflex.
 • Most have voluntary bowel movements, but few never soil. The incontinent are kept clean by a bowel
   management programme. The Malone antegrade continence enema (MACE), via appendicocaecostomy,
   is used when bowel management fails.
 • Long-term series report total incontinence in about a third, constipation in half and weekly soiling in a
   third; a few choose faecal diversion. Continence improves, and constipation lessens, into adolescence and
   adulthood.
 • More than half have major long-term defects of urinary and anorectal function.
Gynaecological and sexual outcome
 • Complications of vaginal and perineal reconstruction: introital stenosis, retained vaginal septum,
   persistent rectovestibular fistula, persistent urogenital sinus, and inadequate perineal body, generally
   from an anteriorly placed rectum at the initial PSARVUP. About half of patients referred after repair
   elsewhere need redo surgery.
 • Late vaginal stenosis and obstructed menstruation: 36–41%. The vagina is adequate for intercourse in
   under half, and revision vaginoplasty is often needed. Normal menstruation occurs in about a third long
   term, and over a third need treatment for obstructive haematometra. Large cystic uterine or adnexal
   collections needing surgery also occur without obstruction.
 • Most reconstructed patients appear capable of normal sexual life. Pregnancy occurs spontaneously and
   with in vitro fertilisation, with an increased risk of preterm labour; caesarean section is preferred.
 • Gynaecological evaluation is needed at birth and again approaching puberty; uterovaginal anomalies are
   found in 53–67%.

SUMMARY
 • Urogenital sinus and cloacal anomalies can be exceedingly complex.
 • In DSD, prompt but careful evaluation by an expert multidisciplinary team is mandatory, with family
   support and education, openness and respect for child and family, respect for cultural practices, and
   confidentiality.
 • At any age, reconstruction demands great care of tissues, meticulous attention to detail and a lifelong
   commitment by the surgeon, supported by a dedicated team.
 • The severe end of the spectrum should be treated only in centres of great expertise and experience.
