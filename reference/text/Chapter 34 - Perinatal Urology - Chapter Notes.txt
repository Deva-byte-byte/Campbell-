Chapter 34 — Perinatal Urology
 • Urinary tract dilation (UTD) is the second commonest prenatal anomaly after cardiac defects, found in
   1–3% of pregnancies.
 • Most UTD is detected in the second trimester → parents can be counselled well before delivery.
 • Most causes never need surgery — transient dilation, ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) pattern of dilation,
   vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) — but a subset is at real risk of urological disease.
 • Hence the need for explicit thresholds for prophylactic antibiotics, lower urinary tract imaging and more
   invasive upper tract imaging.
 • Scope: grading systems, prenatal consultation and intervention, imaging modalities and indications,
   differential diagnosis and outcomes, postnatal risk stratification, and perinatal urological emergencies.
 • Videos 34.1–34.3. Prenatal UTD of the fetal kidney; measurement of the amniotic fluid index (AFI); fetal
   ultrasound of a multicystic dysplastic kidney (MCDK).

PRENATAL IMAGING

Maternal Fetal Ultrasound
 • Metanephric kidney appears at day 28 of gestation; urine production begins in earnest at 14 weeks and
   can be seen on prenatal ultrasound (US).
 • American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG): first screening US at 18–20 weeks. In the
   United States it is done at about 20 weeks' gestational age (GA). Low-risk pregnancies average two scans;
   high-risk pregnancies average four. Exact timing is left to the physician.
 • Referral to maternal–fetal medicine: there are no set criteria, but increasing UTD and/or an abnormal
   AFI should prompt it.
 • AFI = sum of the four largest pockets of amniotic fluid (Fig. 34.1). Normal 8–18 cm; oligohydramnios <5–
   6 cm; polyhydramnios 20–24 cm and above.
 • US detects dilation well (sensitivity up to about 90%), but scan numbers and costs keep rising without
   evidence that prenatal detection improves outcome.
 • How well prenatal US predicts the postnatal diagnosis depends on the condition:
      ◦ Severity of prenatal UTD rises linearly with UPJ obstruction, but not with VUR or duplex anomalies.
      ◦ Bladder/cloacal exstrophy and neural tube defects are diagnosed correctly in most cases. Disorders
        causing genital ambiguity are diagnosed much less consistently.
 • Main benefit of prenatal detection: dialogue with the family on differential, intervention, postnatal
   imaging and natural history.

Fetal Magnetic Resonance Imaging
 • Fetal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has a limited role in UTD. It is indicated when specific
   anatomical detail is needed, usually in rare conditions: cloacal anomaly, urogenital sinus, cloacal/bladder
   exstrophy, and duplication anomalies causing bladder outlet obstruction (Fig. 34.2, fetal MRI of a cloacal
   anomaly).
 • MRI gives better anatomical detail than US, which can be limited by amniotic fluid volume, maternal
   habitus, fetal position and the pelvic bones.

 KEY POINTS
   • First maternal screening US at about 20 weeks; low-risk pregnancies average two scans, high-risk four.

   • UTD is the second commonest prenatal finding and occurs in 1–3% of pregnancies.
   • US does not reliably predict severity for all conditions. It correlates best with obstructive lesions (UPJ

   • Prenatal detection lets the family meet a specialist before delivery and learn the expected course.


Concept — Prenatal US is a sensitive detector of dilation but a poor predictor of diagnosis. Its value is in
triage and counselling, not in proving benefit to outcome.

GRADING SYSTEMS
 • There is no consensus on the best grading system. A multispecialty survey (obstetricians, paediatricians,
   paediatric urologists, paediatric radiologists) found anterior–posterior diameter (APD) used mainly
   prenatally, and Society for Fetal Urology (SFU) or subjective (mild/moderate/severe) grading used
   mainly postnatally.
 • The three main systems are the anterior–posterior renal pelvic diameter (APRPD), SFU and UTD systems.
   They are objective, subjective, or a combination of both.

Anterior-Posterior Renal Pelvic Diameter
 • Most common prenatal system among maternal–fetal specialists. It is strictly objective and simple.
 • Technique. Diameter of the renal pelvis on a transverse image at the mid-kidney, using the renal hilum as
   the landmark.
 • Drawback: operator dependence. The classic error is measuring in the sagittal instead of the transverse
   plane.
 • Physiological APD: <4 mm before 28 weeks; <7 mm at ≥28 weeks.
 • APD correlates positively with GA, probably because physiological urine output rises in the third
   trimester.
 • Severity of prenatal dilation rises linearly with obstructive lesions (UPJ obstruction, PUV) but relates
   inconsistently to VUR (Table 34.2).
 • Several centres report an APD threshold that predicts UPJ surgery.
 • Great Ormond Street randomised trial. Patients with third-trimester APD ≥15 mm and differential renal
   function (DRF) >40% were randomised to pyeloplasty or observation.
      ◦ Surgery → function preserved and dilation reduced in almost all.
      ◦ Observation → about half improved and a third were stable. A minority lost function and needed
        surgery; their APD was 20–40 mm.
      ◦ There are no long-term data, so only short-term conclusions can be drawn.
 • Threshold trade-off: low cut-off → sensitive but over-tests; high cut-off → specific but misses disease
   and risks function.

Society for Fetal Urology Grading System
 • Subjective five-point system (grades 0–4) based on pelvicalyceal dilation plus integrity of the renal
   parenchyma. It is applied only postnatally (APRPD is used prenatally).
 • Figure 34.3 — SFU grades (modified from Timberlake and Herndon, 2013):
      ◦ Grade 1 — urine barely splits the renal sinus.
      ◦ Grade 2 — full pelvis; major calyces dilated.
      ◦ Grade 3 — minor calyces uniformly dilated; parenchyma spared.
      ◦ Grade 4 — parenchymal compromise (thinning).
 • Weakness: intra- and inter-rater variability from subjectivity. Computer-enhanced visual learning
   teaches the system well and reduces that variability.
 • SFU grade predicts renal function and the need for surgery:
      ◦ Grade inversely correlates with preserved DRF (>40%). All SFU 3 kidneys retained function; about
        70% of SFU 4 kidneys did. Some use this to limit renal scintigraphy.
      ◦ Early surgery → mostly SFU 4; continued observation → mostly SFU 3. In delayed surgery grade
        correlated less well (still mostly SFU 4).
 • Limits: not used by other specialties; ignores ureter and bladder.

Urinary Tract Dilation Grading System
 • Developed by a multidisciplinary consensus (obstetrics, radiology, urology) to give one system across
   specialties and across the prenatal/postnatal divide. The term "UTD" was chosen to include the whole
   urinary tract.
 • It standardises terminology, US technique and data reporting.
      ◦ Prenatal APD: fetal spine at 6 or 12 o'clock.
      ◦ Postnatal APD: transverse plane at the mid-kidney (hilar vessels); maximal pelvic diameter within
        the confines of the renal cortex. Serial studies must use the same lie (supine vs prone).
 • A six-parameter template combines APD (objective) and SFU (subjective) (Fig. 34.4).
 • Postnatal normal: APD <10 mm with no calyceal or ureteral dilation = transient, clinically insignificant
   pelvic dilation. It was set to curb postnatal over-testing and parallels the prenatal norms, giving a stepwise
   rise in non-pathological APD: <4 → <7 → <10 mm (Fig. 34.5).

Other Applications of Ultrasound
 • Medullary pyramidal thickness (PT): an objective measure with excellent inter-rater reliability, versus
   only fair reliability for SFU grading.
 • PT ≤3 mm predicts the need for pyeloplasty for UPJ obstruction.

 • Other pyeloplasty risk factors:
      ◦ PT <3 mm after day 3 of life.
      ◦ PT falling between the first and second postnatal US.
      ◦ Low PT ratio (hydronephrotic vs normal contralateral kidney).
 • A model using APD, PT and renal length relative to the contralateral kidney predicted pyeloplasty in
   unilateral hydronephrosis as well as a model that included SFU grade.
 • PT <3 mm → higher risk of reduced relative function on renal scan.

 KEY POINTS
   • The frequency of each grade of UTD is inversely related to its severity, while the chance of urological
     disease rises with severity.
   • APD shows a linear relationship with obstructive lesions (UPJ obstruction, PUV).
   • Third-trimester APD of 15 mm has reasonable positive predictive value for UPJ surgery.
   • SFU: five-point grading on calyceal dilation and parenchymal integrity.
   • UTD combines APD and SFU, adds the lower urinary tract, and provides standardised reporting and
     risk stratification.
   • PT may reliably identify kidneys at risk from UPJ obstruction.


Concept — Each system trades objectivity against completeness. APD is reproducible but sees only the
pelvis, and SFU sees the parenchyma but is subjective. UTD exists to give obstetricians and urologists one
language across the birth divide.

PRENATAL MULTIDISCIPLINARY CONSULTATIONS
 • Conditions that may need immediate or urgent postnatal intervention are best served by a
   multidisciplinary prenatal consultation, with an agreed plan and agreed responsibilities:
      ◦ cloacal/bladder exstrophy;
      ◦ cloacal/urogenital sinus malformation;
      ◦ autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease (ARPKD);
      ◦ bladder outlet obstruction: PUV, prune-belly syndrome (PBS), urethral atresia;
      ◦ myelomeningocele/neural tube defect spectrum.

Parental Counseling for Fetal Intervention
 • Team for lower urinary tract obstruction (LUTO): maternal–fetal obstetrician, paediatric urologist,
   paediatric nephrologist and neonatologist.
 • Three statements can be made reliably to parents:
      ◦ Fetal intervention improves perinatal survival when indicated, but carries a complication rate of over
        40% to mother and fetus combined.
      ◦ Selective treatment does not reliably reduce the long-term need for renal replacement. That burden
        may be large and lifelong.
      ◦ Each case needs an individual risk–benefit analysis with shared decision-making in a multidisciplinary
        setting.

Parental Counseling for Prenatal Surgery for Myelomeningocele Closure
 • Team: maternal–fetal obstetrician, paediatric neurosurgeon, paediatric urologist and neonatologist.
 • Work-up: complete fetal survey including echocardiogram, plus karyotype.
 • Parents must understand that fetal closure is experimental. It reduces ventriculoperitoneal (VP)
   shunting and improves cognitive/motor function, but has no meaningful effect on bladder function.
 • ACOG / Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine recommendations (2017).
      ◦ Open maternal–fetal repair improves important paediatric outcomes at the cost of maternal and
        fetal procedural risk.
      ◦ Women who meet the criteria for in-utero repair should be counselled non-directively about all
        options, including open fetal surgery.
      ◦ Interested candidates should be referred to a fetal therapy centre with the expertise, team and
        intensive care facilities.

 KEY POINTS
   • Prenatal intervention for LUTO improves perinatal survival but does not reliably preserve long-term
     renal function.
   • Counselling on fetal myelomeningocele closure belongs at a centre with fetal surgery expertise and a
     multidisciplinary team.
   • Fetal myelomeningocele surgery improves motor/cognitive skills and reduces VP shunting, with no
     clear bladder benefit.


Concept — Fetal surgery buys what it directly treats: lungs in LUTO and hindbrain/neurological outcome in
myelomeningocele. Organs damaged before the operation (kidneys, bladder) are not rescued, so
counselling must separate survival from renal and bladder outcome.

Prenatal Management
Prenatal Intervention for Lower Urinary Tract Obstruction
 • Male fetus + severe bilateral UTD (bladder, ureters, kidneys) + reduced amniotic fluid → evaluate for
   LUTO. LUTO is a spectrum, so each case is individualised. Counselling weighs fetal benefit against the risk
   to both fetus and mother.
 • Amniotic fluid changes from placental origin to fetal urine at about 16 weeks. Restoring fluid aims to
   allow pulmonary development, and possibly renal development.
 • Oligohydramnios presenting after 27 weeks → favourable pulmonary outcome (observational cohort).
   So intervention is reserved for oligohydramnios developing very early in the second trimester.
 • A recent series challenges this: vesicoamniotic shunt (VAS) placement before 16 weeks gave better
   pulmonary and renal function than later placement.
 • Need for intervention is rare, about 1 in 60,000.
 • Systematic reviews: intervention is feasible, but evidence is insufficient that it improves long-term renal
   function. Survival improves, partly because fetuses without restored fluid die in utero or as neonates.
 • Options: early delivery, fetal shunting, fetal surgery (cystoscopy), serial amnioinfusion. Early delivery is
   favoured more by non-US physicians when bladder outlet obstruction is found in the early third trimester,
   lungs are mature and fluid is falling.
 • Setting: operating room, maternal epidural. Fetal anaesthesia = narcotic and/or paralytic injected into
   the umbilical vein under US guidance. Access is percutaneous for both VAS and fetal cystoscopy.
 • Complications (early series: 44% overall):
      ◦ incomplete drainage (commonest);
      ◦ premature labour;

      ◦ urinary ascites;
      ◦ chorioamnionitis;
      ◦ case reports of bowel herniation and direct fetal injury (Fig. 34.8: bowel herniation and umbilical
        strangulation after VAS).
 • Commonest technique: percutaneous VAS (Fig. 34.9); Rodeck shunts lie flush on the abdominal wall to
   limit migration, unlike the Harrison shunt.
 • Advantage of fetal cystoscopy: direct view of the posterior urethra → diagnosis → directed treatment.
 • History (1982). First clinical series of fetal intervention for bladder outlet obstruction. Both fetuses treated
   by open fetal surgery died of neonatal renal failure. Of those with equivocal function, three were shunted
   and two survived and did well.
 • Long-term VAS outcome (Philadelphia).
      ◦ Fetal urine profile predicted early neonatal survival.
      ◦ Diagnoses: PUV, urethral atresia and PBS.
      ◦ More than half had renal insufficiency; a third needed renal replacement.
      ◦ Bladder function was judged adequate (a third needed clean intermittent catheterisation [CIC]; most
        voided spontaneously), but no urodynamics were done.
      ◦ Quality of life was similar to the normal population and better than in other chronic diseases.
 • PLUTO (Percutaneous shunting in Lower Urinary Tract Obstruction) — landmark multicentre randomised
   trial.
      ◦ Population: fetal bladder outlet obstruction where the benefit of intervention was uncertain.
      ◦ Stopped early for poor recruitment: 31 randomised (16 VAS, 15 conservative).
      ◦ Perinatal death was higher with conservative management, but there was no meaningful effect on
        renal function (Fig. 34.10).
      ◦ Complications 7/16 (44%): premature rupture of membranes (3) and non-functioning shunts (4).
 • Figure 34.10 — PLUTO flow (Morris et al., Lancet 2013).
      ◦ VAS arm (16): 3 terminations/1 intrauterine death → 12 live births → 4 neonatal deaths (pulmonary
        hypoplasia) → 8 alive at 28 days → 7 alive at 2 years (2 normal function, 5 moderate impairment).
      ◦ Conservative arm (15): 2 terminations/1 intrauterine death → 12 live births → 8 neonatal deaths

Fetal Cystoscopy
 • Same aims as VAS: decompress, restore amniotic fluid for lung development, possibly help the kidneys.
 • Advantages:
      ◦ direct visualisation gives a fetal diagnosis of the cause of LUTO;
      ◦ directed treatment (laser valve ablation) restores bladder cycling;
      ◦ no dependence on a device staying functional through the pregnancy.
 • Complications: as for VAS, plus urethrocutaneous and rectourethral fistula (up to 10%). The cause is
   antegrade access at an anatomical disadvantage plus laser energy.

Vesicoamniotic Shunt Placement Versus Fetal Cystoscopy
 • Multicentre retrospective comparison of VAS or cystoscopy with laser ablation versus expectant
   management. Strict inclusion: diagnosis at 16–26 weeks, severe LUTO, no other anomaly (including
   normal echocardiogram), favourable urine electrolytes, normal male karyotype.
 • All LUTO: both VAS and fetal cystoscopy improved survival versus observation.
 • Postnatally confirmed PUV: both improved 6-month survival, but only fetal cystoscopy improved renal
   function.
 • Newer shunts (Somatex) have a favourable safety profile, with good 2-year survival. Placement even
   before 14 weeks caused no early membrane rupture, miscarriage or maternal complication.
 • Ruano severity staging, endorsed by the SFU (Fig. 34.11), is indexed to perinatal and renal outcome to
   select candidates for intervention. Stage III had more interventions, more deaths within 24 h, higher
   overall mortality and more renal replacement.

Serial Amnioinfusion
 • Serial amnioinfusion is an emerging way to promote lung development in bladder outlet obstruction and
   in congenital bilateral renal agenesis (CoBRA).
 • In CoBRA there is no urine and no bladder filling, so shunting or cystoscopy cannot work. Case reports
   are promising.
 • RAFT (Renal Anhydramnios Fetal Therapy) trial: prospective multi-institutional trial of serial
   amnioinfusion for anhydramnios, currently under way.
 • No reliable prenatal predictor of postnatal renal function exists. Fetal urine sodium, chloride, osmolality
   and β2-microglobulin may predict the need for renal replacement but are not validated.
 • European Reference Network for Rare Kidney Diseases consensus (2022): amniotic fluid volume,
   parenchymal echogenicity and fetal urine biomarkers should not be used as accurate predictors of
   postnatal renal function.

 KEY POINTS
   • Predictors of poor outcome: oligohydramnios before 27 weeks, renal cortical cysts, increased
     echogenicity.
   • Prenatal LUTO intervention improves perinatal survival, not long-term renal function.
   • Fetal cystoscopy can both diagnose and treat LUTO.

Concept — Everything in fetal LUTO management follows from one fact: after 16 weeks amniotic fluid is fetal
urine, and without fluid the lungs do not develop. Intervention reliably treats the lung problem. The kidney
problem is often established dysplasia before the needle goes in.

POSTNATAL EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT
 • Postnatal UTD management has shifted to an evidence-based, risk-stratified model. Imaging and
   prophylactic antibiotics are tailored to the risk of urinary tract infection (UTI), renal deterioration or
   surgery. Historically they were driven by the chance of detecting VUR (up to about a quarter of patients).

Imaging
Renal Ultrasound/Resolution of Urinary Tract Dilation
 • First postnatal US after 48 hours. Newborns are relatively intravascularly depleted: third-spaced fluid is
   mobilised over the first 24–48 h, so an earlier scan under-reads dilation. Some delay the scan up to 2
   weeks without harm.
 • Follow the consensus standardisation of technique and reporting.
 • Three postnatal risk levels, P1–P3, set by APD plus the other five parameters (Fig. 34.12, below). Calyceal
   or ureteral dilation may be isolated in P2; parenchymal cysts or thinning make P3.
 • SFU Hydronephrosis Registry (prospective, multicentre): SFU 1 → 100% resolved by about 2.5 years; SFU
   2 (P1) → about 90–95% resolved by 4 years. Another prospective registry found SFU and UTD grades
   equally predictive of resolution.

   parenchymal thinning makes a kidney P3 at any APD.

Voiding Cystourethrogram
 • Dilation correlates poorly with VUR (present in up to 31%); up to 25% with VUR have a normal first US.
 • American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) standardised voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG):
      ◦ ALARA ("as low as reasonably achievable") radiation;
      ◦ non-balloon catheter;
      ◦ bladder cycling for reliability;
      ◦ images: kidney–ureter–bladder (KUB) scout, filling and voiding phases.
 • Target VCUG at UTI risk, not VUR detection: females, uncircumcised males, high-grade dilation, ureteral
   dilation, bladder abnormality; shared decision with the family.
 • Outpatient VCUG is acceptable unless bladder outlet obstruction is suspected: thick-walled bladder
   (≥0.5 cm), distended bladder, bilateral hydroureteronephrosis.
 • Active surveillance of known VUR is safe in stable patients. The historical benefit of finding
   asymptomatic VUR is unproven.

Contrast-Enhanced Voiding Urosonography
 • Contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography (CeVUS): microbubbles instilled by urethral catheter; VUR
   detected on US. No radiation.
 • Possibly more sensitive than VCUG for VUR (limited data).
 • Poor view of the bladder and urethra → a formal VCUG is still needed when anatomical detail matters
   (e.g. the posterior urethra).

Renal Scintigraphy
 • Measures differential function, cortical defects and drainage. Its weakness is lack of anatomical detail.
 • Technetium-99m mercaptoacetyltriglycine (99mTc-MAG3) is preferred over
   diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA) for obstruction or impaired function because of better
   extraction.
      ◦ About 50% of MAG3 is extracted by the proximal tubule per pass and secreted into the collecting
        system.
      ◦ Uptake is linear with renal blood flow, so it gives relative function against the contralateral kidney.
 • "Well-tempered" diuretic renogram (Conway; SFU / Society of Nuclear Medicine) standardises
   hydration, bladder drainage, tracer dose, diuretic dose and timing, and data acquisition and
   interpretation.

 • Radiation is generally low, but dose rises linearly with degree of obstruction.
 • MAG3 findings that raise consideration of surgery in an asymptomatic infant (Fig. 34.13):
      ◦ DRF <40%;
      ◦ T½ >20 minutes;
      ◦ significant retention on delayed upright imaging.

      ◦ False-positive "obstruction" from dehydration, or from furosemide given too early into a capacious

      ◦ Invasive: intravenous line plus urethral catheter.
      ◦ Region-of-interest placement changes the drainage curve, especially when pelvis and ureter are both
        dilated.
 • MAG3's role in postnatal UTD is undefined; given its pitfalls and US's ability to predict DRF >40%, many
   use it selectively, relying on serial renal ultrasound (RUS).
 • 99mTc-dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA): bound to the proximal tubule with minimal secretion → a
   cortical agent with limited role in UTD. Uses:
      ◦ cortical defects (dysplasia in the absence of infection) in VUR;
      ◦ confirming absent function in suspected MCDK;
      ◦ polar function in duplex systems.

 • Advantages of magnetic resonance urography (MRU): no radiation; best anatomical detail; also gives

 • Drawbacks: cost, possible need for sedation or anaesthesia, limited availability.
 • Reserved for operative planning that needs anatomy and/or function: ectopic ureter, megaureter, lower-
   pole crossing vessels causing UPJ obstruction.

MANAGEMENT RECOMMENDATIONS FOR POSTNATAL URINARY TRACT DILATION

P1 Urinary Tract Dilation (Low Risk)
 • Most postnatal UTD is P1 and transient. Two prospective registries (SFU Hydronephrosis Registry,
   McMaster) inform UTI risk, prophylactic antibiotic (PA) value and resolution.
 • P1 (SFU 1–2) resolves in most children by 24–48 months → second postnatal US at 3–6 months.
 • Both registries agree: no PA and no VCUG for P1 (SFU 1–2), as defined by the 2014 consensus statement.

P2 Urinary Tract Dilation (Intermediate Risk)
 • Evidence is weakest for P2. Surveyed paediatric urologists varied on PA (by sex and circumcision status).
   Most would order a VCUG, and about half a renal scan.
 • PA and VCUG are at the physician's discretion, except ureteral dilation >7 mm → PA recommended,
   because of the large UTI risk reduction shown in the Hydronephrosis Registry.
 • VCUG can usually be done as an outpatient, combined with the RUS at the first postnatal visit.
 • Counselling covers: expected UTI rate, chance of finding VUR, treatment of VUR, natural history.

P3 (High Risk) Urinary Tract Dilation
 • P3 carries the highest risk of UTI, renal deterioration and surgery. PA, lower urinary tract imaging and
   renal scintigraphy are recommended, and most SFU survey respondents support this.
 • Dissenting view: reflux prevalence is raised across all UTD grades, so VCUG should depend on features
   suggesting high-grade reflux (ureteral dilation) or LUTO, not on P grade alone.
 • Second postnatal US at about 1 month, combined with the VCUG. Suspected LUTO may justify imaging
   even for less severe upper tract dilation (e.g. bilateral ureteral dilation).
 • Persistent P3 → MAG3 (combined with VCUG if not yet done).
 • PA without VUR → continue until UPJ surgery or age 1 year, because most UTIs occur in the first year.

 • The protocols above apply to isolated unilateral UTD.

Urinary Tract Infection
 • UTI occurs in 8–22% of infants with prenatal UTD.
 • Degree of kidney dilation predicts UTI. A meta-analysis and several cohorts show a stepwise rise in UTI
   with grade, and high-grade dilation clearly exceeds low-grade.
 • Other predictors in single cohorts: high-grade VUR, ureterovesical junction (UVJ) obstruction, ureteral
   dilation >11 mm.
 • SFU registry: about 90% of UTIs occurred in the first year (Fig. 34.14); risk factors were female sex, high-
   grade dilation and intact foreskin. McMaster registry added ureteral dilation, VUR and no prophylaxis
   (see registry comparison below).
 • Agreed risk factors: female sex, high-grade dilation (P3, SFU 4), uncircumcised status. Still debated:
   ureteral dilation, VUR, PA. Confounders are non-uniform VUR screening and case mix.
 • Circumcision protects males with prenatal hydronephrosis (PHN): number needed to treat (NNT) = 10 to
   prevent one UTI. A meta-analysis found protection regardless of cause (odds ratio about 0.3). The AAP
   supports circumcision of newborn males with urological anomalies.
 • Figure 34.14 — UTI-free survival by sex, SFU registry (Zee et al., 2016). Girls' UTI-free survival falls steeply
   in the first year and keeps falling to 30 months. Boys' survival stays above 95% after about 6 months. The
   difference is significant (P <0.001).

Prophylactic Antibiotics
 • The role of PA in prenatal UTD is not clearly defined. Historically nearly everyone with dilation received
   PA and a VCUG. The reason was the link with VUR, present in up to 25% even when postnatal dilation
   had resolved.
 • Evidence for benefit:
      ◦ a systematic review found PA reduced UTI in high-grade but not low-grade UTD;
      ◦ a retrospective study found a fivefold reduction in UTI with PA in the ureteral-dilation subset;
      ◦ SFU registry: PA protective when ureteral dilation >7 mm;
      ◦ the McMaster registry showed significant risk reduction.

 • Evidence against:
      ◦ two SFU registry reports found no UTI reduction overall;
      ◦ a systematic review of 11 studies (>3,900 patients) found no reduction in pooled UTI rates with PA in
        non-randomised studies (about 8% without vs 10% with).
 • Why evidence is weak: UTI is uncommon (8–19%) → too few events; populations vary in VUR screening
   and PA use.
 • Paradigm shift: from blanket PA to PA only where a meaningful risk reduction exists. Candidates are:
      ◦ higher-grade UTD;
      ◦ uncircumcised males;
      ◦ females;
      ◦ selectively, VUR and/or ureteral dilation >7 mm.
   Most paediatric urologists support this approach.

Ureteropelvic Junction Obstruction Pattern of Dilation
 • UPJ obstruction is the commonest surgical cause of prenatal UTD. Its incidence rises linearly with
   prenatal dilation. The discussion here is limited to P2/P3 (SFU 3/4, moderate/severe).
 • APD thresholds that predict surgery:
      ◦ 15 mm APD → sensitivity about 75%, specificity about 80% for eventual pyeloplasty. Operated patients
        had a mean APD of about 22 mm vs 12 mm in those observed, and 15 mm was the optimal ROC cut-
        off.
      ◦ Second trimester 9.5 mm; third trimester 15 mm (SFU registry data).
      ◦ Postnatal APD 20 mm predicts preservation of renal function in early neonatal UTD.
 • Meta-analysis of the 15 mm threshold: sensitivity about 0.8, specificity about 0.8, odds ratio about 13 for
   surgery. Postnatal APD predicts surgery better than prenatal APD.

Recommendations for Ureteropelvic Junction Obstruction Pattern of Dilation
 • Most are followed without surgery.
 • P2 or P3 → early neonatal US.
 • PA may be considered for P3/SFU 4 (UTI risk).
 • Lower tract imaging is recommended to exclude VUR and confirm a normal posterior urethra in males.
 • Renal scintigraphy is discretionary, but should be used for persistent P3, which correlates strongly with

      ◦ Option 2: serial US to determine the fate of the obstruction.

      ◦ falling DRF on serial renograms;
      ◦ increased tracer retention on delayed upright MAG3 images;
      ◦ symptoms: stone, pyelonephritis, haematuria.

Vesicoureteral Reflux
 • VUR is found in up to 31% of screened prenatal UTD. Prenatal UTD grade correlates poorly with VUR
   severity. Screening was historically justified by the high yield.
 • There are two clinical phenotypes of VUR (Fig. 34.16: bilateral grade V VUR in P1 [SFU 2] UTD).
 • Early prospective multicentre series of prenatally detected VUR.
      ◦ Mostly males, and mostly bilateral.
      ◦ Ureteral dilation was uncommon, and a quarter had a normal first postnatal US.
      ◦ About half of uncircumcised males developed UTI.
      ◦ High-grade units resolved earlier than expected (mean about 1 year in boys, 2 years in girls).
 • However, pooled data for the 2010 American Urological Association (AUA) VUR guideline showed no
   higher resolution rate for prenatally detected VUR (overall resolution about 50%).
 • Whether prenatal VUR predicts UTI is disputed:
      ◦ McMaster registry (mostly high-grade UTD and high-grade VUR, two-thirds on PA): VUR increased
        febrile UTI. SFU registry (mostly low-grade UTD; VUR in about a fifth screened): no association. Both
        cohorts were mostly male; the difference reflects case mix (registry comparison above).

LUTO (PUV, PBS, URETHRAL STENOSIS/ATRESIA)
 • Prenatal signs of LUTO (Fig. 34.18):
      ◦ oligohydramnios;
      ◦ bladder distension with no cycling;
      ◦ ureteral and renal dilation, sometimes severe, with cystic change.
 • Imaging alone cannot separate PUV, urethral stenosis/atresia and PBS.
 • A distended bladder compressing the thorax plus oligohydramnios → may need immediate pulmonary
   resuscitation. Highest risk: oligohydramnios before 27 weeks.
 • Signs of a system under tension ("pop-offs"):
      ◦ urinary ascites (e.g. via a patent urachus);
      ◦ perirenal urinoma (Fig. 34.19: urinoma and calyceal rupture on antegrade nephrostogram);
      ◦ VUR;
      ◦ renal dysplasia.
 • Pop-offs plus the "keyhole" sign (dilated bladder and posterior urethra, Fig. 34.20) favour PUV or
   urethral atresia. However, only fetal cystoscopy establishes the cause reliably.
 • Window for intervention: 18–24 weeks, after extensive counselling about benefit against the high
   complication rate.
 • At delivery the urology team should be ready to decompress the bladder.
      ◦ PBS vs PUV on examination: PBS has absent abdominal wall musculature and bilateral impalpable
        testes.
      ◦ Insert a 5 Fr feeding tube and confirm on KUB film. It can curl in the dilated posterior urethra and still
        drain urine.
      ◦ Digital rectal pressure helps guide the tube over a high bladder neck.
      ◦ Avoid balloon catheters: the balloon can fall into the posterior urethra, obstruct or injure it, and
        impair drainage of the ureteric orifices.
      ◦ If a catheter cannot pass → bedside US-guided percutaneous suprapubic catheter, using local
        anaesthetic and an angiocatheter by Seldinger technique.

Posterior Urethral Valves
 • Commonest cause of LUTO: 1.4–2.2 per 10,000 births.
 • Diagnosed by examination and lower tract imaging. VCUG shows a dilated posterior urethra (Fig. 34.21).
 • Prenatal diagnosis allows early neonatal treatment to preserve renal function (Chapter 45).

Prune-Belly Syndrome/Megalourethra
 • PBS triad: megacystis and upper tract dilation + absent/deficient rectus abdominis musculature +
   bilateral impalpable testes (Chapter 44).
 • It may coexist with urethral atresia (→ oligohydramnios, with outcome similar to PUV) and with
   congenital megalourethra.

Urethral Stenosis/Atresia
 • Presents with oligohydramnios, severe bilateral UTD and bladder distension.
 • Postnatally a catheter will not pass. Cystoscopy shows a narrow penile urethra with a membrane at the
   membranous/prostatic urethra.
 • Initial vesicostomy → then progressive augmented dilation of the anterior urethra (PADUA procedure).

Congenital Megacystis
 • Massive reflux form: laterally displaced ureteric orifices → urine cycles between bladder and ureters → a
   large, thin-walled bladder. Correcting the reflux restores bladder dynamics.
 • Megacystis–microcolon–intestinal hypoperistalsis syndrome (MMIHS):
       ◦ the bladder is distended but not obstructed; VUR is rare;
       ◦ prognosis was poor, but survival has improved with parenteral nutrition and transplantation;
       ◦ most need vesicostomy or intermittent catheterisation;
       ◦ PA only for recurrent UTI.

Recommendations for LUTO
 • Baseline renal/bladder US, repeated after a period of decompression.
 • No immediate VCUG in a neonate with renal insufficiency who needs decompression: replacing the
   catheter may be difficult.
 • Length of decompression is guided by the serum creatinine nadir. Creatinine may rise at first from
   intravascular depletion or under-resuscitation.
 • Warn the neonatal team about the concentrating defect, which is worsened by post-obstructive diuresis.
 • Oliguria or anuria after decompression can occur because the hypertrophied bladder wall resists
   drainage from tortuous dilated upper tracts (Fig. 34.22: thick-walled bladder with distal ureteral dilation).
   Antimuscarinics mitigate this.
 • Repeat US as the baseline at maximal decompression → then VCUG to guide surgery (Chapter 45).

KIDNEY ANOMALIES

Multicystic Dysplastic Kidney
 • Most prenatal cystic disease needs no surgery and spares overall renal function.
 • Prenatally, MCDK can be mistaken for P3 UTD → review postnatal imaging critically. Whether MCDK is
   very early severe obstruction is debated.
 • Classic appearance: multiple non-communicating cysts of varying size with no large central cyst.
   Reniform variants exist (Fig. 34.23).
 • Associated findings: VUR in about 20% and contralateral UPJ obstruction.
 • Postnatal RUS confirms the diagnosis.
 • VCUG and DMSA were historically routine but are now challenged:
      ◦ VUR is found in under a fifth, is rarely surgical, and unprophylaxed VUR has not caused febrile UTI;
      ◦ US diagnosis matched scintigraphy in 100% when the bladder was normal on US.
 • Wilms tumour: a systematic review of over 1,000 children found no association between MCDK and
   Wilms tumour. Routine nephrectomy has therefore been abandoned.
      ◦ Serial US confirms involution and compensatory contralateral hypertrophy.
      ◦ A cost analysis favoured early nephrectomy, but only by assuming long-term serial imaging.

Recommendations for Multicystic Dysplastic Kidney
 • Early postnatal renal/bladder US to confirm.
 • No PA: UTI risk is low.
 • VCUG and DMSA are recommended but not evidence based; omitting them is acceptable. Long-term
   prospective data are needed.
 • Functional solitary kidney → monitor for hyperfiltration injury through puberty: blood pressure,
   proteinuria, and consider yearly US. Serial imaging is not evidence based but ensures follow-up of a
   group at risk of adult renal disease.
 • The MCDK should involute. A persistent cystic kidney with a solid component → consider removal.

Duplication Anomalies Ureterocele/Ectopic Ureter
 • Usually found prenatally because the upper-pole moiety is obstructed (Meyer–Weigert rule).
 • Suspect duplication when ureteral dilation exceeds pelvic dilation (Fig. 34.24). The dysplastic upper pole
   may be cystic.
 • A cystic mass in the bladder is better seen on postnatal bladder US, which separates ureterocele from
   ectopic ureter. This is harder when the ureterocele opening is ectopic in the bladder neck.
 • In girls a ureterocele can cause LUTO with oligohydramnios (Fig. 34.25: ball-valving ureterocele at
   cystoscopy).
 • Ureterocele disproportion: a large ureterocele with minimal upper tract dilation (rare).
 • VUR is common and also follows Meyer–Weigert. The upper pole inserts distal and medial (obstruction/
   ectopia); the lower pole inserts cephalad and lateral (short tunnel → reflux) (Fig. 34.26: ureterocele filling
   defect; "drooping lily" of lower-pole VUR).
 • Most duplication anomalies occur in females and are bilateral.
 • Ureterocele vs ectopic ureter on US (Fig. 34.27): a ureterocele is a thin-walled cyst inside the bladder.
   An ectopic ureter may indent the bladder but shows two distinct walls, with a muscle plane between
   ureter and bladder.

 • Initial management may be ureterocele puncture, but this is not mandatory.

      ◦ Upper tract approach: nephron-sparing or ablative, increasingly laparoscopic; most ectopic ureters

      ◦ Lower tract approach: common-sheath reimplantation of both moieties, for those at most risk of UTI

 • Presence and grade of VUR predict the need for lower tract surgery (Mayo series):
      ◦ no VUR → upper-pole approach alone in all;
      ◦ low-grade VUR → 40% needed further surgery for UTI;
      ◦ high-grade VUR → ureterocele excision in 96%.

Recommendations for Duplex Anomalies
 • Examine the introitus for a prolapsed ureterocele (Fig. 34.28).
 • Early postnatal US with attention to the bladder.
 • PA from birth.
 • Characterise: VUR, ureterocele size, bladder outlet obstruction.
 • Renal scintigraphy for upper-pole function.
 • VUR grade and upper-moiety function dictate treatment:
      ◦ Girl + ureterocele + high-grade lower-pole VUR + functioning upper pole → initial incision. She is
        likely to need lower tract surgery anyway because of UTI risk.
      ◦ Boy + no VUR + dysplastic upper pole → upper-pole approach, preserving or ablating.
Concept — The Meyer–Weigert rule generates the whole duplex picture: upper pole → caudal insertion →
obstruction/ectopia; lower pole → cranial-lateral insertion → reflux. Treatment follows from which moiety
has the problem. Lower-pole VUR pulls treatment to the bladder; its absence lets the upper tract be treated
alone.

Cloacal Malformation/Urogenital Sinus/Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia
 • Prenatal bladder plus vaginal distension → bladder outlet obstruction from vaginal trapping of urine.
 • Differential diagnosis:
      ◦ cloacal malformation — expect distal colonic obstruction visible prenatally;
      ◦ urogenital sinus;
      ◦ congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) — often a family history (autosomal recessive).
 • Prenatal counselling is fundamental.

Cloacal Malformation
 • Persistent cloaca: about 1 in 50,000 births; females only. It results from arrested development of the
   urorectal septum and urogenital sinus, leaving rectum, vagina and urinary tract in one common channel.
 • Prenatal findings:
      ◦ oligohydramnios, bilateral UTD and urinary ascites (as in male LUTO);
      ◦ a large pelvic cystic mass;
      ◦ calcified meconium on MRI from rectal obstruction;
      ◦ dilated vagina/uterus, which may be bifid (uterus didelphys) (Fig. 34.30: 30-week fetus with
        duplicated vagina, distended bladder and urinary ascites).
 • Urinary ascites arises by retrograde flow through the uterus and fallopian tubes into the peritoneum.
   Oligohydramnios varies with the degree of gastrointestinal obstruction.
 • Polyhydramnios → consider an associated tracheo-oesophageal fistula.
 • Delivery should be elective, with neonatal, paediatric surgical and paediatric urological teams present.
 • Prenatal accuracy varies (rarity) → high suspicion; one series needed fetoscopy or fetal MRI to diagnose.

UROGENITAL SINUS/CONGENITAL ADRENAL HYPERPLASIA
 • CAH is a rare autosomal recessive disorder causing a spectrum of genital ambiguity in females.
 • Commonest form: 21-hydroxylase deficiency; the gene is on the short arm of chromosome 6.
      ◦ Classic form: salt-wasting (75%) or simple virilising (25%).
      ◦ Non-classic form: milder course.
 • Incidence about 1 in 15,000–16,000 live births. Only 1 in 8 at-risk pregnancies produces an affected
   infant with ambiguity: 1 in 4 is affected (recessive) × 1 in 2 is female, since males are not ambiguous.
 • Degree of urogenital sinus and virilisation varies, so CAH can be confused with cloacal or urogenital sinus
   malformation.
 • Urogenital sinus malformation can also trap urine in the vagina and cause LUTO, but without
   gastrointestinal obstruction. Normal external genitalia favour urogenital sinus over CAH.

Prenatal Treatment of Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia
 • Prenatal identification and treatment of CAH is controversial. Because only 1 in 8 at-risk pregnancies
   yields an affected girl, most fetuses exposed to prenatal treatment would not benefit.

Postnatal Treatment for Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia
 • First concern: electrolyte imbalance. Alongside stabilisation, confirm the diagnosis with serum 17-
   hydroxyprogesterone and genetic testing.
 • If vaginal retention of urine causes LUTO, bladder decompression is a priority. Urinary and/or vaginal
   diversion may be needed (Chapters 59–60).

Outcomes for Fetal Myelomeningocele Closure
 • Prenatal alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) plus key US findings diagnose neural tube defects accurately. Amniotic
   fluid glial fibrillary acidic protein (AF-GFAP) is a possible additional marker. Early diagnosis allows referral
   to a fetal surgery centre.
 • Multicentre randomised trial (2011), prenatal vs postnatal closure.
      ◦ Prenatal closure before 26 weeks significantly reduced VP shunting and appeared to improve
        cognitive and motor function.
      ◦ Main complications: preterm labour and uterine dehiscence.
      ◦ Urology: no improvement in urodynamics, CIC need or need for urinary reconstruction.

Upper Urinary Tract Obstruction
 • Urinary abdominal distension is usually obstructive and anticipated prenatally.
 • The UPJ is the commonest site; others are the UVJ and the insertion of an ectopic ureter.
 • Percutaneous drainage may be needed for respiratory compromise or feeding intolerance (Fig. 34.31:
   massive upper-pole obstruction with ureterocele; mass effect relieved by ureterocele decompression).

 KEY POINTS
   • Most prenatal UTD is transient (resolution 41–88%).
   • Third-trimester APD of 15 mm predicts the need for surgery.
   • VUR in up to 31% of prenatal UTD, commonest in males with bilateral high-grade disease.
   • MCDK does not raise Wilms tumour risk and can usually be observed.
   • Lower-pole VUR in duplex systems influences how the obstructed upper pole is treated.
   • Megaureter usually resolves. Operate for recurrent UTI, reduced function or increasing UTD, using
     temporising diversion under 1 year.
   • Fetal myelomeningocele closure helps motor/cognitive outcome and VP shunt need but not bladder
     outcomes or reconstruction need.

outlet obstruction, and urine escaping through the tubes causes ascites. The companion findings pick the
diagnosis: gut obstruction means cloaca, virilisation means CAH, and normal genitalia mean urogenital sinus.

PERINATAL URGENCIES/EMERGENCIES

Neonatal Urologic Emergencies
 • Causes: genitourinary anomalies, birth trauma, vascular events; evaluate the whole newborn.

Abdominal Distention
 • Causes: cystic lesion, solid mass, urinary ascites (obstruction or extravasation); examine the whole
   abdomen.

Lower Urinary Tract Obstruction
Urinary Ascites
 • Urine causes up to 30% of neonatal ascites. Features:
      ◦ respiratory distress;
      ◦ oliguria/anuria;
      ◦ hyponatraemia and hyperkalaemia;
      ◦ raised creatinine.
 • PUV is the commonest cause of urinary ascites. Others: bladder rupture, cloacal malformation,
   persistent urogenital sinus, urethral atresia.
 • Suspected bladder perforation:
      ◦ catheterise, and follow urine output and electrolytes;
      ◦ confirm by biochemical analysis of peritoneal fluid plus VCUG;
      ◦ often unexplained if there is no outlet obstruction;
      ◦ most spontaneous perforations heal conservatively;
      ◦ iatrogenic rupture from umbilical catheters needs prompt recognition and repair.
 • Urogenital sinus or cloaca: urine pools in the vagina, flows retrograde through the uterus and fallopian
   tubes into the peritoneum. VCUG demonstrates it.
 • Percutaneous drainage if the distension compromises breathing.

Urinary Retention (>24 Hours After Birth)
 • Mostly males with LUTO: PUV, urethral atresia, rarely ureterocele. In females, suspect a urogenital sinus
   anomaly with vaginal distension; ureterocele can also cause retention.
 • Relieve the obstruction as well as working up the cause.
 • Catheterise.
      ◦ PUV with a high bladder neck → finger in the rectum to deflect the catheter over the neck.
      ◦ Females: aim for the bladder, but draining the vagina may suffice and may allow voiding.
 • If catheterisation fails → percutaneous drainage by Seldinger technique: 16-gauge angiocatheter → 3.5
   Fr feeding tube; 14-gauge → 5 Fr feeding tube.
 • Some urogenital sinus anomalies: intermittent vaginal drainage does not drain the bladder →
   vesicostomy and/or vaginostomy until definitive repair (Fig. 34.32: persistent bladder and vaginal dilation
   despite catheterisation, relieved by vaginostomy/vesicostomy, then normal cycling after vaginoplasty).

Solid or Cystic Renal Masses
 • Four categories of abdominal mass: renal cystic, pelvic cystic, solid, non-genitourinary.
 • Renal cystic: MCDK, ARPKD, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), cystic nephroma.
      ◦ MCDK = multiloculated mass with little or no parenchyma.
