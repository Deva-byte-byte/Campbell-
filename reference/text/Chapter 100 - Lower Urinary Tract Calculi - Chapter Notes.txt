Chapter 100 — Lower Urinary Tract Calculi
HISTORY
 • Hippocrates' oath reserved "cutting for stone" to specialists — the earliest statement of urological
   subspecialisation.
 • Ancient disease in Greek, Egyptian and Indus Valley civilisations; the earliest known lower tract stone is
   from the Egyptian grave of a 16-year-old male, c. 4800 BCE.
 • Three classical routes to the bladder stone: perineal, suprapubic, transurethral.
 • "Lithotomy" was coined by Ammonius, c. 276 BCE; Celsus, some 300 years later, recorded it and
   performed the first perineal urethrotomy in the lithotomy position — the methodus Celsiana.
 • Apparatus minor versus major: minor used no instruments, the stone cut down upon through a perineal
   incision beside the anus; major (Marianus Sanctus, Italy) used many.
 • Archaeological bladder stone in a middle-aged man from a British excavation was attributed to bladder
   outlet obstruction (BOO).
 • Jean Civiale developed lithotrity (transurethral stone crushing); his student Sir Henry Thompson
   operated on King Leopold I.
 • Paradigm shift: vesical calculi have steadily declined in the West over 50 years but persist in developing
   nations, particularly in children.
Concept — Bladder stone has shifted from a paediatric nutritional disease to an adult disease of outlet
obstruction and foreign material wherever diet has changed; the endemic paediatric form survives where it
has not.

ETIOPATHOGENESIS OF BLADDER CALCULI
Epidemiology and classification
 • Commonest lower tract stone: about 5% of all urinary stone disease and 1.5% of urological admissions in
   the West.
 • Non-endemic areas: adults, secondary to another disease. Endemic areas: children, without associated
   anomaly, driven by diet and socioeconomic conditions.
 • Classification: migrant, primary idiopathic (no predisposing cause) and secondary (predisposing cause
   present).

 • What it tests: that "bladder stone" is two diseases — one of the urine, one of the bladder. Trap: looking
   for an outlet problem in the endemic child.
Primary stone — mechanism
 • Decreased urine output + altered urine pH + metabolic abnormality on a background of animal-protein
   deficiency.
 • Cereal-based, low-phosphate diet → hypophosphaturia → precipitation of calcium oxalate and
   ammonium acid urate; an oxalate-rich diet adds hyperoxaluria.
Secondary stone — causes
 • Origin: de novo, or migrant from the upper tract and retained. The frequent absence of a calcium oxalate
   nucleus argues against upper tract origin for most.
 • Significant postvoid residual (PVR): BOO from stricture or benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), neurogenic
   bladder, urinary diversion, bladder neck contracture.
 • Foreign bodies: migrated intrauterine devices, pessaries and diaphragms, eroded clips. Uncommon:
   eroded cerclage wire, unrecognised anorectal impalement, eroded silk sutures from the dorsal vein
   complex, migrated brachytherapy seeds, hair introduced during clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC).
 • Nonabsorbable suture migrates to the bladder; erosion with stone after Stamey procedure, open
   colposuspension and prosthetic retropubic sling.
 • Renal transplantation: stones form on both absorbable and nonabsorbable sutures at
   ureteroneocystostomy.
Catheters and nucleation
 • Chronic indwelling catheters: stone incidence 0.7–2.2%; retained fragments of burst Foley balloons act
   as a nidus.
 • Heterogeneous nucleation around a foreign body, from supersaturation due to infection or obstruction, is
   the mechanism; homogeneous nucleation is rarely responsible.
Neurogenic bladder
 • Spinal cord injury (SCI) carries the highest risk; risk factors: complete lesion, Klebsiella urinary tract
   infection (UTI), White race.
 • Hypothesis: chronic mucosal injury and inflammation from catheter or stone → glycosaminoglycan (GAG)
   layer deficiency → stone.
 • Large series: struvite predominant; uric acid stones only in males.
Drugs
 • Triamterene (potassium-sparing diuretic acting on distal tubular sodium reabsorption) forms stones by
   precipitation of drug and metabolites, reported with BOO and high residuals. Indinavir (protease
   inhibitor) is also lithogenic.
Concept — Primary bladder stone is a disease of the urine — diet-driven supersaturation in a normal bladder;
secondary stone is a disease of the bladder — stasis, a foreign nidus and infection. This is why the first rarely
recurs and the second recurs unless its cause is removed.

PRESENTATION OF BLADDER STONES
 • Rarely asymptomatic. Commonest presentation: terminal haematuria.

MANAGEMENT OF BLADDER STONES
 • Options: medical management, extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL), transurethral lithotripsy,
   percutaneous cystolithotomy and cystolitholapaxy, open surgery.
 • Deciding factors: stone size and composition, patient age and build, coexistent stones elsewhere,
   concomitant BOO, available expertise and equipment.

Medical Management
 • Chemodissolution alone is slow and incompletely effective — now an adjunct in select cases, most
   effective for encrustation on long-term catheters (treatment and prophylaxis).

Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy
 • Indicated where endoscopy threatens a device — artificial urinary sphincter, penile prosthesis — and for
   neobladder stones and medically high-risk patients.
 • Poor choice in continent reservoirs: fragments often need endoscopic removal.
 • Protocol: prone (avoids coccygeal and rectal artefact) → three-way Foley → fill to 150 mL to localise →
   treat, with intermittent irrigation aiding localisation → drain.
 • Outcome determinants: PVR, composition, size.

Endourologic Approach to Bladder Stone
 • Miniaturised equipment with efficient energy sources means the majority of bladder stones are now
   treated endourologically.
 • Terms: cystolithotomy — intact removal; cystolithotripsy — fragmentation with an energy source;
   cystolitholapaxy — mechanical breakage.

Open Surgery for Bladder Stones
 • Prolonged catheterisation and hospital stay; catheterless, drainless two-layer cystolithotomy is feasible in
   children.
 • Indicated where transurethral or percutaneous access is contraindicated: small-capacity bladder,
   urethral stricture.
Concept — Modality is dictated less by the stone than by the route to it: an adequate urethra permits
transurethral laser; a compromised urethra or an implanted device diverts to percutaneous access or ESWL; a
small bladder or no safe route leaves open surgery.

LOWER TRACT CALCULI IN SPECIAL SITUATIONS
Bladder Outlet Obstruction With Bladder Lithiasis
 • BOO causes incomplete emptying and non-evacuation of fragments, present in 45–79% of patients with
   vesical calculi.
 • Bladder stone secondary to BOO is an absolute indication for BPH surgery.
 • Infection also contributes — over 30% of bladder stones are infected, usually by urea-splitting organisms
   — so not every secondary stone requires outlet surgery. Evidence conflicts: a urodynamic study before
   and after stone treatment found BOO in only about half; another series found BOO the cause in the great
   majority.
Figure 100.2 — Bladder calculus, one patient
 • Plain radiograph; ultrasonography supine and lateral — the stone shifts with position; cystoscopic view.
Concept — Obstruction and infection are two independent routes to the secondary bladder stone; outlet
surgery is mandatory when the stone is due to BOO, so the examinable point is proving that BOO, not
assuming it from the stone.

Bladder Calculi in Urinary Diversions and Augmented Bladder
 • Upper and lower tract stones occur after augmentation or diversion; incidence varies with the operation.
   Three aetiological groups: infection, metabolic, anatomical.
 • Metabolic: ileum or colon → hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis → hypercalciuria (reduced renal tubular
   calcium reabsorption); loss of ileum → enteric hyperoxaluria and diarrhoea.
 • Infection: stones mainly struvite; urease producers — Pseudomonas, Proteus, Klebsiella — alkalinise
   urine and promote crystallisation. Mucus aids biofilm and impairs antibiotic penetration.
 • Stomach reduces stone incidence, but haematuria–dysuria syndrome and long-term proton pump
   inhibitor (PPI) need discourage its use.
 • Stasis: stomal stenosis (conduit) or poor emptying (continent diversion). Absorbable sutures significantly
   reduced stones.
 • Know diversion type and anatomy preoperatively: it determines modality, guides percutaneous puncture
   and avoids bowel and vascular pedicle injury.

suture.

Bladder Calculi in Patients With Spinal Cord Injury
 • Neurogenic bladder from SCI or myelomeningocele raises risk; determinants: level and severity of lesion,
   previous stones, persistent infection, drainage method.
 • Risk peaks 3 months after injury; 15–30% form a stone within 10 years; risk quadruples after the first
   stone. Alkaline urine with hypocitraturia contributes.

 • CIC has lower stone incidence than an indwelling catheter; suprapubic and urethral indwelling catheters

 • Avoid indwelling Foley (encrustation, nephrolithiasis); if unavoidable, weekly changes dramatically

Concept — SCI stone risk follows the two phases of injury — metabolic early, infective late — and the
drainage method is the modifiable lever: CIC beats indwelling catheterisation, but suprapubic offers no
advantage over urethral.

Bladder Calculi After Renal Transplantation
 • Post-transplant urolithiasis 1–1.8%; renal stones commoner, but bladder calculi quite common.
 • Causes: persistent Proteus infection, outflow obstruction, retained suture; calcineurin inhibitors and
   steroids contribute. Absorbable suture has reduced incidence.
 • First investigation: ultrasonography; computed tomography (CT) to exclude renal or ureteric calculi.
 • Treatment as routine — cystolitholapaxy, laser, ESWL; removal of all exposed intraluminal suture is
   paramount.
Concept — The transplant bladder stone is a suture-and-infection stone; the modality is routine, and clearing
exposed suture is the step that prevents recurrence.

Treatment of Vesical Calculus in Children
 • Options as in adults: ESWL, open cystolithotomy (OC), percutaneous cystolithotripsy (PCCL), transurethral
   cystolithotripsy (CL); energy: pneumatic, holmium laser, thulium fibre laser.

URETHRAL CALCULI
 • Rare: 0.3–1% of all stones; an obstructing urethral calculus is very rare. A rarity in the West, not
   uncommon in endemic Asia and the Middle East; declining overall.
 • Males; bimodal age: early childhood and fourth to fifth decades. Short female urethra and higher peak
   flow in the young are protective.

Pathogenesis and Composition of Urethral Calculi
 • Commonest site: posterior (prostatic) urethra; less commonly pendulous.
 • Vast majority migratory: stricture, meatal stenosis or BPH arrests a stone that would otherwise pass.
 • Upper tract, not bladder, is often the source: composition mostly calcium oxalate, as upper tract stones;
   concomitant stones commoner in the upper tract than the bladder; in endemic areas bladder calculi fell
   with dietary change while urethral calculi did not.
Primary Urethral Calculi
 • Form at their final site, secondary to obstruction or stasis in a diverticulum; urethral stricture is the
   leading cause. Organisms: Escherichia coli, Proteus, enterococci.
 • Hair-bearing grafts (stricture or hypospadias repair): hair is a nidus; stones adhere to hair ball and
   mucosa. Epilation reduces but does not eliminate risk — follicles persist.
 • Foreign bodies: brachytherapy seeds migrating into the prostatic urethra; cryoablation necrosis with
   persistent inflammation.

Clinical Presentation and Evaluation
 • Commonest presentation: acute painful urinary retention from sudden impaction. If still voiding: weak,
   interrupted or splayed stream, gross haematuria, dysuria.
 • Pain localises the stone: posterior → perineal or rectal; pendulous → penile tip.
 • Posterior stones are commoner (up to 88%) than anterior.
 • Examination: posterior — often normal bar a mildly tender prostate; bulbar or penile — palpable hard
   mass; female — hard mass in the anterior vaginal wall; large diverticula palpable with multiple perineal
   stones.
 • Delay → larger stones and urethrocutaneous or urethrorectal fistula, especially with reduced sensation
   (infants, SCI).
 • About 98% radio-opaque (older literature 40%) → plain radiograph.
 • Retrograde urethrogram (RUG): filling defect; historical mainstay, now reserved for inconclusive
   ultrasonography or suspected stricture.
 • Transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS): hyperechoic with posterior acoustic shadowing; a 10 MHz linear
   probe along the dorsal penis screens the whole urethra.
 • Image the upper tract (ultrasonography or CT) for coexistent stones.

Treatment
 • Determinants: location and distance from either meatus, stone characteristics, whether it can be pushed
   back, associated urethral abnormality.

Unusual Lower Tract Urolithiasis
Preputial Calculi
 • Adults with severe phimosis, poor hygiene, low socioeconomic status.
 • Mechanism: smegma inspissation, stasis with salt precipitation, or both — a vicious cycle of scarring and
   worsening phimosis. Also foreign bodies, suture, extruded bladder calculi.
 • Presentation: LUTS, difficulty voiding commonest; dysuria, haematuria, foul urine, palpable stones;
   occasionally retention; tight phimosis. Lymphadenopathy → plain radiography ± CT.
 • Treatment: circumcision or dorsal slit; excised skin to histopathology. Stones multiple, smooth, rounded,
   struvite.
Concept — Phimosis turns the preputial sac into a stagnant, infected reservoir; removing the prepuce
removes the reservoir, and the excised skin always goes for histology.
Prostatic Calculi
 • Association with LUTS recognised in the early 20th century; TRUS detects them increasingly; autopsy
   shows calcification in almost all asymptomatic adult men.
 • Prostatic urethral calculi are migratory — manage as urethral calculi. True prostatic calculi lie within the
   gland.
TRUE PROSTATIC CALCULI — ENDOGENOUS VERSUS EXOGENOUS
 Primary / endogenous                                              Secondary / exogenous
 Within the acini; more numerous, smaller                          Reflux of urine; fewer, larger

 • Composition: calcium phosphate (majority), then calcium carbonate phosphate, calcium oxalate, mixed.
 • Commoner in histologically proven BPH; no correlation with adenocarcinoma.
 • A prostatitis symptom → 3.2 times more likely to harbour a significant calculus; size, not number,
   correlates with symptoms.
 • Chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS): calculi common, with more inflammation, more gram-negative
   culture positivity (E. coli, Klebsiella) and more white cells in expressed prostatic secretions; other work
   finds no conclusive link.
 • TRUS is the main modality. Harada classification: type A — discrete, small, evenly distributed echoes;
   type B — coarse, larger, focal echoes.
 • Plain radiograph detects 14%; role of CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) unclear.
 • Calculi met at transurethral resection of prostate (TURP) → remove with the loop: greater symptom
   relief.
Concept — Locate first: a prostatic urethral stone is a urethral stone and is managed as one; a true prostatic
calculus is a marker of glandular inflammation or reflux, not of cancer, and is removed when met at TURP.
Seminal Vesicle Calculi
 • Seminal vesicles (SVs): paired glands posterior to the bladder; calculi uncommon.
 • Aetiologies: haematospermia, gonorrhoea, prostatitis, vesiculitis, tuberculosis, UTI. Symptoms: none, or
   haematospermia, perineal or testicular pain, painful ejaculation, low volume.
 • Composition: calcium phosphate, struvite, protein matrix.
 • Diagnosis: vasoseminal vesiculography, MRI (T2 hypointense, no diffusion restriction), CT, TRUS — most
   accurate.

 KEY POINTS
   • Primary bladder calculi: children under 10, peak 2–4 years, far commoner in males.
   • Bladder stones: commonest presentation haematuria with LUTS; children — abdominal discomfort,
     dysuria, frequency, haematuria.
   • Options: medical, ESWL, transurethral, percutaneous cystolithotomy or cystolitholapaxy, open.
     Percutaneous: safe, effective, spares the urethra.
   • Urethral calculi: 0.3–1% of stones; usually present with acute painful retention.
   • Prostatitis symptom: 3.2 times more likely to harbour a prostatic calculus; plain radiograph shows
     prostatic calculi in 14%.
   • Preputial calculi: severe phimosis, poor hygiene, low socioeconomic status.
   • Seminal vesicle calculi: uncommon; consider in unusual LUTS.
