Chapter 56 — Hypospadias
 • Distal hypospadias is corrected successfully in about 80%; the specialty's effort must now go into
   reducing complications in proximal hypospadias, where each additional procedure adds further risk.
 • Longer urethroplasty = more demanding: a long neourethra of uniform calibre is hard to build, and the
   neourethra may be inherently narrowed and unable to expand to propel the urine bolus.
 • Poiseuille's law: resistance to flow is proportional to tube length and inversely proportional to the radius
   to the fourth power. Small changes in calibre therefore have a large effect, and the longer the tube, the
   greater the stricture risk.
 • Failure of the neourethra to expand → raised resistance → fistula and/or diverticulum.
 • The glans is non-distensible (dense spongiosum). Even with uniform calibre, the fixed distal urethra
   causes turbulence and high proximal pressure → diverticulum.
 • The neourethra is a skin-lined tube without spongiosum, an independent risk for progressive distension.
 • Tissue dysplasia, the severity of curvature and skin shortage may impair healing, perfusion and growth
   with age. Each surgeon's numbers are falling, so multi-institutional collaboration is essential.

 KEY POINTS
   • Proximal hypospadias has a higher complication rate than distal.
   • The trend is towards staged repair for severe hypospadias: curvature is corrected at stage one,
     urethroplasty at stage two. This gives supple ventral tissue for tubularisation and avoids a
     urethroplasty laid over a corporal graft in one stage.
   • 25–75% need additional procedures after a planned two-stage repair.

Concept — Proximal repairs fail for physical reasons: a long, rigid, spongiosum-free tube ending in a fixed
glans. Length and stiffness raise resistance, and raised resistance produces fistulae, diverticula and strictures.

Postoperative Management
 • A careless postoperative course can ruin a good repair, yet quality evidence for specific regimens is
   lacking.
 • First 48 hours: urinary diversion plus a compressive dressing, which reduces stress on the neourethra,
   tamponades bleeding and limits swelling. The authors leave the dressing 72 hours, then apply petroleum
   jelly.
 • A soft, pliable urethral stent in complicated repairs reduces meatal stenosis and fistula. A suprapubic
   tube is used in older children and redo cases.
 • Diversion lasts 3–21 days, by complexity, urethroplasty length and preference. Longer stenting reduced
   complications in one study but is not universally accepted, and must be weighed against discomfort and
   UTI risk, which rises with each day. Short distal repairs have been done without a stent with no change in
   outcome.
 • Bladder spasm → high-pressure voiding around the stent → stress on the repair, so give oxybutynin until
   the stent is removed.
 • Long, stiff stents press on the ventral repair and risk glans dehiscence. A Foley catheter must be fixed in
   the midline to the anterior abdominal wall. Tape often slips and pulls the catheter laterally or ventrally,
   so the authors have abandoned Foley catheters because of glans dehiscence.
 • Analgesia: paracetamol is near-universal, and about half of urologists add non-steroidal anti-
   inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Ibuprofen and ketorolac do not increase bleeding. Regular paracetamol
   plus ibuprofen minimises opioid use, tapering after 48–72 hours.

Complications of Hypospadias Repair
 • Duckett described “hypospadiology” as a humbling blend of art and science. The tissues are delicate and
   healing depends on fragile neovascularisation, threatened by oedema, infection and variable healing.
 • Complications have lacked standard definitions, which makes reporting inconsistent. The standardised
   set is in Table 56.1.

Urethrocutaneous Fistula
 • The commonest complication: just under 10% at short-term follow-up. It occurs most often at the
   original meatus or the coronal margin, from pinpoint to large.
 • Causes: ischaemia, oedema, infection, haematoma and especially distal obstruction (meatal stenosis or
   stricture → high pressure and turbulence). Technical causes: overlapping suture lines, inadequately
   inverted epithelium, poorly absorbable sutures. Risk rises with severity.
 • It is noticed as multiple streams, mostly within 2 years, or at toilet training.
 • Small early fistulae rarely close spontaneously. Repair at least 6–12 months after the primary operation,
   once tissues soften.
Assessment under anaesthesia: bougie à boule calibration for patency and irregular calibre → urethroscopy
if irregular → “stress test”: catheter in the urethra, compress proximally and distally, inject saline or
methylene blue to reveal hidden tracts.

Glans Dehiscence
 • Causes: catheter tension, poor technique, glans closure under tension, vascular compromise. The meatus
   retreats to a coronal or subcoronal position, in effect a breakdown of the repair.
 • Reoperate only for voiding symptoms. If repair is needed, an interposition inlay graft, as a staged step or
   in a single stage, avoids glans tension.

Meatal Stenosis
 • Definitions vary. Risk: urethral closure carried too far distally, or a tight glans closure. Longer stenting
   may help (weak evidence).
 • The postoperative meatus should calibrate ≥8 Fr, increasing with age. Clinically relevant stenosis means
   stranguria, deflected stream, poor flow, retention and a “laser-beam” stream.
 • Escalation:
     ◦ Sound dilatation ± topical betamethasone in some cases.
     ◦ Routine postoperative dilatation is unnecessary, but early dilatation may reveal subclinical stenosis.
     ◦ Refractory to dilatation → dorsal midline meatotomy (rarely), otherwise redo urethroplasty.
 • Meatal retrusion: excessive distal tension, or after glans dehiscence. It results from poor planning: an
   immobile or poorly vascularised urethra retracts. Revise if the deflected stream prevents standing
   voiding.

Urethral Stricture
 • Rates depend on technique and urethroplasty length. Sites: native–neourethral junction, along the
   neourethra (especially at the coronal margin with a narrow plate), or within the glans. It presents with a
   weak stream, stranguria, retention or UTI.
 • Causes: technique, ischaemia, trauma, infection. Characterise by cystoscopy (length, calibre, site).
 • Short, mildly symptomatic strictures → dilatation or endoscopic incision, rarely durable. Internal
   urethrotomy succeeds poorly and can worsen scarring. Even strictures <1 cm with intermittent
   catheterisation seldom keep normal flow. Results may be better for short anastomotic strictures treated
   at 3 months, but the authors rarely see success.
 • Refractory or extensive strictures → revision urethroplasty. Expect tissue loss, so be ready to harvest
   local flap or buccal mucosa; this is often multistage.
 • Hair-bearing skin in the urethra after multistage or complex repairs, combined with narrowing, becomes
   a nidus for stones and UTI. Options: laser epilation; endoscopic stone removal. When stones, hair and
   UTI coexist → resect and replace with buccal graft or local skin.
 • Recurrent UTI → look for diverticulum or stricture. An enlarged prostatic utricle causing stasis → excise
   or fulgurate.

Urethral Diverticulum
 • It presents with a weak stream, post-void dribbling, UTI, rarely haematuria, ballooning of the shaft, or
   the need to “milk” the urethra.
 • Commoner after preputial flap, two-stage and proximal repairs: there is no spongiosum, and a rigid
   glanular neourethra raises penile urethral pressure.
 • Small saccular diverticulum → excise and reduce to a uniform calibre. Extensive diverticulum (more
   common) → excise redundant tissue, close the urethra and add multilayer reinforcement.
 • The redundant wall is well vascularised and can be reused as a flap to repair coexisting fistula or distal
   stricture. Extraurethral plication avoids entering the urethra.

Balanitis Xerotica Obliterans
 • Balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO), or lichen sclerosus, is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis. It occurs
   with phimosis in uncircumcised boys, with meatal stenosis, or with progressive urethral narrowing after
   repair, appearing pale and white.

 • Meatal disease only → topical betamethasone or clobetasol, or systemic tacrolimus. These usually fail.
 • Definitive: resect all involved tissue, send it for histology, and replace with inner prepuce or buccal
   mucosa in a staged repair. BXO can invade buccal mucosa.
 • Recurrence up to 40% on medium-term follow-up, so monitor closely.
BXO sequence: urethral “cutback” to healthy tissue → topical steroids → excise persistent disease → place a
buccal graft and confirm take without recurrence → urethral closure.

Recurrent Penile Curvature
 • It damages urinary and sexual function and may worsen at puberty through differential dorsal–ventral
   corporal growth.
 • Causes: curvature underestimated or incompletely corrected, graft contraction after corporotomy,
   ventral skin traction, or a preserved tethering plate.
 • Assess by complete degloving with artificial erection. A tourniquet can hide basal curvature, so
   compress the corpora against the pubis instead.
Staged correction (counsel the family that several procedures are likely): stage 1 identifies the cause (watch
for corporal disproportion after a previous dorsal plication). If a corporal graft is used, cover it with healthy
dartos as a bed for buccal graft → stage 2: ventral buccal graft unless skin allows tension-free cover → stage
3: Cecil modification if shaft skin is deficient → stage 4: tension-free skin closure.
Figure legend, Fig. 56.38 — A 14-year-old after infant single-stage repair with dorsal plication presented
with marked ventral curvature. Correcting it left a long urethral gap, which needed a buccal inlay and then
final closure.

Skin Complications
 • Buried penis with shortening from concealment and poor skin fixation is prevented by degloving plus
   anchoring sutures from Buck's fascia to the dermis at the penopubic and penoscrotal junction.
 • Suture sinus tracts come from thin ventral skin, full-thickness sutures, or sutures tied too tight
   (ischaemia).
 • Penoscrotal transposition or webbing after proximal repair → scrotoplasty to prevent concealment.
Concept — Almost every urethroplasty complication traces back to distal obstruction, ischaemia or tension.
That is why fistula and diverticulum repair start by excluding distal narrowing, and why every repair adds
vascularised barrier layers.

HYPOSPADIAS OUTCOMES
 • The literature is weak: retrospective, small, technically varied, with poorly defined outcomes, no
   independent assessment and much loss to follow-up.
 • Largest meta-analysis of TIP repair (Pfistermuller et al., 2015; 49 studies, 4,675 boys, mean follow-up 16
   months): complications 10.6%, reoperation 4.5%; fistula 5.7% > meatal stenosis 3.6% > stricture 1.3%.
   An extra dartos layer improved outcomes; proximal and redo cases did significantly worse.
 • TIP vs Mathieu (meta-analysis): similar overall complications and fistula rates, but meatal stenosis much
   commoner after TIP.
 • Recurrent UTI after repair is uncommon (about 2%). Imaging finds distal narrowing, VUR, diverticulum or
   an enlarged utricle. TIP → raised post-void residual; OIF → diverticulum or fistula. Image anatomically
   any child with recurrent UTI.

 • Distal hypospadias: success 83–95%. Complications in large single-centre series are higher with longer
   follow-up and a more proximal meatus. After TD repair, fistula and glans dehiscence occur early (first
   year) but stricture late.
 • Proximal hypospadias: complications 23–68%; fistula is again commonest (3–45%), then glans
   dehiscence and meatal stenosis. Diverticula are commoner after Byars flap or onlay than after two-stage
   graft repair (STAG or Bracka).
 • Recurrent curvature after two-stage repair is low (0–10%). Two-stage repair with corporoplasty gives
   greater penile length and better cosmesis. Penile length is the chief concern of adults repaired in infancy.
 • Staged tubularised autograft (STAG): complications <25%, mainly glans dehiscence or fistula; recurrent
   curvature low. Extra layers and better blood supply reduce complications.
 • In a four-way comparison (TIP, preputial island onlay, island flap tube, two-stage Bracka), the approach did
   not change complications. Dorsal plication plus proximal urethral mobilisation corrects most curvature
   and avoids laying a skin graft over a corporal graft.
 • Byars flap two-stage repair: high complications (30–70%), mainly fistula then glans dehiscence.
   Diverticulum results from no spongiosum plus no fixation of the flap to the ventral shaft.
 • In one centre, single-stage repairs had more complications than planned two-stage repairs (not
   significant). Two-stage approaches that close the distal urethra and correct curvature at stage one report
   favourable results.
 • Penile perception scores were better with staged repair, which gave a longer penis, with similar
   complications and urinary complaints across four techniques.
 • No direct comparison identifies the ideal approach to proximal hypospadias with severe curvature, and
   long-term data are lacking.

Outcome Assessment
 • Fistulae are obvious; early stricture is subtle. Watch the stream in clinic or on family video. Office
   calibration is difficult, so examine under anaesthesia if concerned.
 • Uroflow helps, but normal values after repair are poorly defined, it cannot be done before toilet training,
   and flow improves with age.
 • Only about half of complications appear in the first year; about three-quarters are found by 3 years.
   Fistulae appear early and strictures late.
 • Authors' standard: follow through puberty, especially proximal cases. Review after toilet training
   (stream), and at puberty (velocity, curvature, sexual function).

Patient-Reported Outcomes
 • Surgeon-judged success may differ from patient and parent perception. Parental decisional regret
   follows complications, hesitancy about surgery and a wish for circumcision. Better education reduces
   conflict.
 • Decision aids (an online “hypospadias hub” of video and text): families engaged repeatedly, felt confident,
   and had less decisional regret.

 • What it tests: HOPE scores appearance on a 1–10 scale per item (higher = more normal), judged against
   reference photographs (Fig. 56.41). The pattern: torsion and curvature share the same 30° steps, and 0–
   30° counts as normal, matching Table 56.1's >30° complication threshold. The trap: HOPE is cosmetic
   only, with no voiding or sexual domain.
 • Hypospadias-specific health-related quality of life (HRQoL) framework (scoping review) defines five
   domains: penile appearance, sexual health, psychological and behavioural function, social function,
   voiding function. These are the basis for a future patient-reported outcome measure (PROM), because
   current tools are insufficient (Fig. 56.42).
 • Mild, undiagnosed distal hypospadias (no curvature) in adults: minimal sexual, fertility or voiding
   problems. Many men are unaware of it, adapt to spraying, are sexually active and father children. More
   proximal defects with curvature report worse sexual and functional outcomes.
 • After surgery:
     ◦ Older studies reported more behavioural and school problems (worse with severity) and later, less
       frequent sexual activity.
     ◦ A contemporary controlled series found equal fertility, sexual satisfaction and age at first intercourse
       overall, but worse satisfaction, fertility and sensation in proximal cases.
     ◦ Adolescents after proximal repair worry about penile growth more than meatal position and are more
       hesitant about sexual contact.
 • Overall: no major psychopathology, but less satisfaction with appearance, sexual inhibition, more
   negative genital appraisal, anxiety and lower self-esteem. Earlier surgery may help. Lay observers see
   distal repairs as normal and proximal repairs as only slightly different.

     late, so follow proximal cases into puberty.
   • Risk factors: proximal meatus, glans width <14 mm, no barrier flap, previous hypospadias surgery.
   • Fistula is commonest: primary closure if tissue is supple; redo urethroplasty and glans closure if glans
     tissue is thin.
   • Meatotomy or skin flap for stenosis usually fails with BXO; excise all BXO and stage with buccal graft.
     Failure is up to 40%, so monitor.

Concept — Hypospadias outcomes are time-dependent and observer-dependent. Complications keep
appearing for years, and surgeons, patients and parents judge success differently. Only follow-up through
puberty with patient-reported measures captures the true result.

Hypospadias Reoperations
 • Simple problems (fistula, skin redundancy) can be fixed in one stage. Each unplanned operation degrades
   tissue, scarring the penis, disrupting the blood supply and shrinking options, so complication rates rise
   with each redo.
 • Devine (1973): after failure, surgeons tend to attempt “small operations”, which make matters worse.
   What is needed is “extensive resection of scarring, major shifts of tissues and meticulous
   reconstruction.”
 • Assessment: history of voiding, skin and erectile problems; previous operative notes (tunica vaginalis
   flaps, grafts, urethroplasty type, curvature correction). Have a low threshold for cystoscopy, and always
   perform artificial erection, because missed curvature has severe long-term consequences.
 • Blood supply of previously operated flaps is unpredictable, so add layers (tunica vaginalis).
 • Tubularisation redo (Mathieu, TD, redo TIP) has reasonable success. An inlay graft after incising the plate
   improves redo outcomes, but take care with tissue that has already failed.
 • Focal problem (fistula, diverticulum) without curvature → local or remote skin flaps, as onlay or
   multilayer cover, especially from redundant non-hair-bearing shaft skin.
 • Split-onlay skin (SOS) flap: a pedicled skin flap of about 1 cm, from anywhere redundant (shaft or
   circumcision line), split into an onlay for the urethra plus a second piece for barrier or skin cover, or de-
   epithelialised as an extra layer.

 • Staged buccal repair beats single-stage tubularisation (better graft take). A dorsal inlay is better than an
   onlay or complete buccal tube in redo cases with poor blood supply (Fig. 56.31).
Buccal graft technique: harvest 10% larger than the bed → leave the donor site to heal by secondary
intention (closure adds pain, little benefit) → combine sources for long defects → fenestrate with an 18- or
21-gauge needle (less haematoma) and keep in saline → incise the recipient bed ventrally from the urethra
through the glans to the planned meatus, preserving dartos (the blood supply) → suture along its axis
without narrowing it → quilting sutures against haematoma → wait ≥6 months (up to 12) before
urethroplasty, checking take and contraction → topical steroid for contraction (from 1 month) → family
“graft massage” from about 2 weeks (vitamin E oil, silicone gel or petroleum jelly).
 • Results after completion: 30–75% success; meatal stenosis is the commonest failure, then stricture and
   breakdown.
 • Cecil–Culp (Cecil 1946; Culp 1951): urethroplasty → midline scrotal incision → recess the repaired penis
   into the scrotal dartos bed, where it parasitises the scrotal blood supply → release at a second stage
   (Cecil: 6–8 weeks).
 • It fell from favour with single-stage repair and has returned for salvage. The concerns are hair-bearing
   scrotal skin and multiple procedures.
 • Modification: delay release to 9–12 months. This transfers more vascularised layers, usually avoids using
   scrotal skin, and stretches deficient shaft skin for tension-free closure.

 KEY POINTS
   • Lip mucosa within the glans: thinner, fewer complications.
   • Buccal mucosa is the graft of choice in complex reconstruction (properties as tabulated).
   • Cecil modification vascularises skin after multiple repairs.

Concept — Reoperation is a problem of blood supply, not technique. Each step (staging, vascular layers,
buccal grafts on dartos, scrotal parasitisation, tissue expansion, nitroglycerin, hyperbaric oxygen) brings
vascularised, non-hair-bearing tissue to a bed that previous surgery has impoverished.

FUTURE
 • Progress depends on honest reporting, standardised pre-, intra- and postoperative assessment and
   definitions, and multi-institutional collaboration to power studies of complication sources.

 • Coaching and teamwork, as in athletics, can refine technique for rare and difficult cases, particularly
   proximal hypospadias.
 • Priorities: lower complication rates in severe hypospadias, follow-up into adulthood, and long-term data
   to set expectations.
 • Open questions: the ideal meatal position; what curvature is acceptable; how much surgery patients will
   accept. One patient left with a coronal meatus but a straight penis and normal voiding is satisfied (Fig.
   56.45). Others are long-term psychological effects and how best to educate families.
 • Technology: peri-operative videography and crowd-sourced assessment; AI and machine learning on
   recorded procedures; genomic, proteomic and microbiome risk profiling; a central registry linking
   anatomy, technique, outcomes and PROs; tissue banking; and perhaps printed replacement tissue.
