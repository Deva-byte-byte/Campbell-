Chapter 139 — Surgical Management of Upper Urinary
Tract Urothelial Tumours
DIAGNOSIS

Diagnostic Endoscopic Evaluation and Biopsy
 • Suspicion usually arises on imaging: a soft-tissue mass involving the collecting system of the renal pelvis
   or ureter raises the possibility of upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC).
 • Endoscopy with biopsy and selective cytology is the most accurate means of diagnosis. Obvious cases
   can be identified by good-quality computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
   urography combined with cystoscopy and positive urinary cytology.
 • Diagnostic accuracy rises from about 75% with excretory or retrograde urography alone to 85–90%
   when ureteroscopy is added. Endoscopic assessment with biopsy is therefore the gold standard for
   diagnosis, as an adjunct to contrast-enhanced cross-sectional imaging.
 • Diagnostic endoscopy carries oncologic risk: bladder tumour recurrence, pyelovenous and
   pyelolymphatic migration of cancer cells, and other routes of dissemination. Special care is required (see
   Ureteroscopy below).
 • Diagnostic versus therapeutic endoscopy: diagnostic endoscopy is a brief, low-impact procedure confined
   to visual characterisation of involved sites and tissue sampling (biopsy and cytology); therapeutic
   endoscopy has treatment intent. The two may be combined in one sitting for small, easily accessible
   tumours.
 • Percutaneous antegrade pyelo-ureteroscopy is used when retrograde ureteroscopy is not feasible, for
   example after urinary diversion.
 • Image-guided percutaneous needle sampling (CT- or ultrasound-guided) is the alternative when tissue is
   needed but endoscopy is considered less safe.
 • Endoscopy also shows areas not seen on imaging and informs treatment planning. The American
   Urological Association (AUA) / Society of Urologic Oncology (SUO) guideline on non-metastatic UTUC
   derives its risk groups from endoscopic and imaging features, which makes a standardised diagnostic
   assessment essential.
 • Preoperative ureteroscopy has clear value when the diagnosis is uncertain or kidney-sparing
   management is being considered.
 • Ureteroscopic biopsies are small and shallow, so their correlation with pathological stage is weak. Stage
   is best estimated by combining radiographic findings, the endoscopic appearance of the tumour, biopsy
   grade and cytology.
Concept — Because the biopsy cannot stage the tumour, UTUC is staged by inference: grade, appearance,
imaging and cytology together stand in for depth of invasion, and every later risk-group decision rests on that
composite.

TREATMENT ALGORITHM
 • Radical nephroureterectomy (RNU) with bladder cuff resection is the standard therapy, provided the
   contralateral kidney is normal and there is no surgical contraindication.
 • Risk-adapted strategies from the AUA/SUO and the European Association of Urology (EAU) classify
   patients by tumour features to refine surgery, including kidney-sparing management (Box 139.1).

 • Low-risk disease → consider kidney-sparing treatment irrespective of the contralateral kidney,
   preserving renal function without compromising oncologic outcome.
 • Kidney-sparing may also be considered in selected higher-risk (imperative) situations: (1) serious renal
   insufficiency, (2) solitary kidney, (3) bilateral tumours, (4) poor surgical candidate. The treatment must not
   significantly compromise oncologic outcome; cancer-control risk is weighed against renal preservation
   through shared decision-making.
 • Lower-ureteric disease, particularly with unfavourable features → segmental resection rather than
   endoscopic management as definitive treatment.

SURGICAL MANAGEMENT
 • Options mirror those for lower tract (bladder and urethral) cancer: extirpative, endoscopic, topical, and
   systemic chemotherapy and immunotherapy (neoadjuvant and adjuvant).
 • The evidence base was historically thin because UTUC is uncommon and heterogeneous; landmark trials
   and well-curated consortium data now define best practice, and better endoscopic optics and instruments
   allow less invasive diagnosis and treatment.
 • The least invasive treatment is preferred only if it does not compromise oncologic control.
 • UTUC is unforgiving of surgical indiscretion: it seeds and implants after tumour spillage or extravasation
   and is not amenable to salvage by other modalities, so every approach must avoid spillage.

Radical Nephroureterectomy
Indications
 • RNU with bladder cuff excision and lymph node dissection is recommended for non-metastatic, high-
   risk tumours of the renal pelvis and proximal ureter.
 • High-risk disease: high-grade cancer on biopsy or cytology, or clinical features of invasive or locally
   advanced cancer such as radiographic obstruction or parenchymal invasion (Box 139.1).
 • RNU also retains a role in low-risk, low-grade cancers with unfavourable features (obstruction,
   multifocality, large volume) or when endoscopic management is unsuccessful or infeasible.

Techniques
Open radical nephroureterectomy
 • Multiquadrant access is required to the kidney, perinephric fat, ipsilateral adrenal, retroperitoneal nodes,
   ureter and bladder, through a single incision or separate incisions.
 • Positioning: supine or modified flank. The genitalia are included in the field so that the bladder catheter
   can be placed and accessed intraoperatively. After prior pelvic surgery, perineal access may be needed to
   manipulate the vagina or rectum for identification.
 • Midline incision gives the best exposure of retroperitoneal nodes and bladder but limits the upper pole,
   particularly on the left and in obese patients.
 • Flank, subcostal, modified Makuuchi or thoracoabdominal incisions can be extended toward the pelvis;
   unless a midline incision is used, a second Gibson, lower midline or Pfannenstiel incision is needed for
   the bladder cuff (Fig. 139.1).
 • Sequence — kidney first, cuff last, so the collecting system stays intact until the end: incise white line of
   Toldt → mobilise colon to expose Gerota's fascia → clip or ligate the ureter early (limits migration of
   tumour cells or fragments to the bladder during renal dissection) → control the hilum before excessive
   manipulation → secure and divide renal artery and vein (suture ligature, ties, clips, or endovascular
   stapler) → mobilise the entire kidney outside Gerota's fascia, dividing hepatorenal attachments on the
   right and the splenorenal ligament on the left.
 • Side-specific hazards: right — duodenum reflected medially to expose the hilum; left — pancreatic tail,
   spleen and fourth (ascending) part of the duodenum.
 • Routine adrenalectomy is unnecessary; it does not aid oncologic control unless involvement is suspected
   on imaging or at operation.
Management of the distal ureter and bladder cuff
 • Complete removal of the distal ureter and bladder cuff is oncologically superior to incomplete
   resection. Surveillance of a residual stump is clinically impractical and local recurrence is high, so the
   entire distal ureter, including the intramural portion and the ureteric orifice, must be removed.

 • A retained intramural stump carries local recurrence in 30–75%. Non-definitive (incomplete) distal
   management carries higher local and distant recurrence and inferior disease-specific and overall survival.
 • After prior urinary diversion into bowel, the ureteroenteric anastomosis is excised with a bowel cuff;
   recurrence is substantial when it is left.
 • The kidney may stay in continuity with the distal ureter, or, because its bulk hinders manipulation, the
   ureter may be divided in a controlled manner between ties, clips or stapler at a grossly tumour-free level.
   Do not seal the ureter with a thermal vessel-sealing device alone — durable sealing of the ureter is off-
   label and unestablished.

   collecting system should be avoided:

Laparoscopic and Robotic-Assisted Radical Nephroureterectomy
Indications
 • Indications are the same as for open RNU. Possible exceptions: large bulky tumours involving adjacent
   structures, and cases in which extended lymph node dissection is considered after chemotherapy.
 • Approaches: transperitoneal, retroperitoneal, hand-assisted and robotic-assisted.
 • Retrospective non-randomised series show oncologic survival generally equivalent to open surgery in
   experienced centres; less invasive procedures are better tolerated with fewer major complications, but
   may carry more bladder recurrence and are less favoured for advanced (T3/T4) disease.
 • Two components: nephrectomy with proximal ureterectomy, then excision of the distal ureter with intact
   specimen extraction. Tissue handling must avoid spillage.
 • The extraction incision is placed to serve both extraction and distal ureteric dissection. Because an
   extraction incision is needed anyway, techniques designed to avoid a second incision lose their utility
   when the distal ureter is managed open.

Technique
Transperitoneal laparoscopic nephroureterectomy — kidney down to mid-ureter
 • Positioning: kidney and proximal ureter before the cuff; the patient is placed supine with the ipsilateral
   hip and shoulder rotated about 20–30° (the illustrated set-up, Fig. 139.4, uses a modified lateral decubitus
   with the flank rotated up 15°), secured at chest, waist and lower limb so that table rotation moves the
   patient between flank (nephrectomy) and modified supine (open distal ureterectomy). An alternative is
   slight outward rotation at the waist to flatten the lower abdomen.
 • Genitalia and ipsilateral flank prepared and draped; Foley catheter placed before insufflation.
 • Ports: three or four (Fig. 139.5). Camera at the umbilicus throughout; a 30° lens helps. Upper midline and
   lateral ports for the kidney and proximal half of the ureter; lower midline and lateral ports for the distal
   ureter; a 3- or 5-mm subxiphoid port for liver retraction on the right. In larger abdominal girth, shift the
   ports laterally (Fig. 139.6). A hand port, if used, is sited to serve the distal ureter and open cuff.
 • Choice of extraction incision (Fig. 139.5): a low abdominal (midline or Pfannenstiel) incision if
   laparoscopic dissection has reached below the iliac vessels; a Gibson-type incision if the more proximal
   ureter needs exposure.
 • Colon: incise along the line of Toldt from the iliac vessels to the hepatic flexure (right) or splenic flexure
   (left); mobilise the colon medially by releasing the renocolic ligaments while leaving the lateral
   attachments of Gerota's fascia, so the kidney does not fall onto the hilum; mobilise the colonic
   mesentery medial to the great vessels.
Proximal dissection for nephroureterectomy
 • Identify the proximal ureter near the lower pole and dissect medially toward the renal vessels, avoiding
   overmanipulation and keeping generous perihilar fat.

 • Early ureteric ligation (clip or tie) is recommended, as in open surgery.
 • Take a wide margin around the renal pelvis and hilum, because lymphatic spread is a common route of
   progression — especially when advanced disease is suspected.
 • Expose the vessels as medially as possible; ligate and divide the artery, then the vein, with a vascular-load
   stapler or multiple clips (Fig. 139.7); free the kidney outside Gerota's fascia; the adrenal is not removed
   routinely.
 • Ureteric blood supply: anteromedial in the proximal third, medial in the middle third, lateral in the distal
   third.
 • The lower ureter may require port-hopping or additional ports; in bulky ureteric disease include a wide
   margin. Continue as far as feasible and complete the remainder through the lower abdominal extraction
   incision.
 • Specimen in a retrieval bag; inspect the bed for bleeding and lymphostasis. For open distal ureterectomy,
   rotate the table and make a Gibson, Pfannenstiel or low midline incision. Close the fascia at ports of 10
   mm and larger.
Laparoscopic dissection of the distal ureter and bladder cuff
 • The open principles apply: complete resection of the orifice and avoidance of urine and tumour spillage.
 • Sequence: Trendelenburg position (bowel out of the pelvis) → extra ports as needed → extend the
   peritoneal incision from the iliac vessels into the perivesical space → free the ureter below the iliac
   vessels, following it medial to the umbilical ligament (Fig. 139.8; nodal tissue may be stripped off the
   vessels and left en bloc with the ureter) → divide the vas deferens (male) or round ligament with medial
   mobilisation of ovary and tube (female) → trace the ureter between bladder and medial umbilical
   ligament, under the superior vesical artery, to the detrusor → clip or tie the ureter at the detrusor (Fig.
   139.9) → divide the lateral bladder pedicle to rotate the bladder medially and expose the whole
   intramural ureter → partial filling aids visualisation → free the ureter extravesically by dissecting the
   detrusor down to the urothelium around the tunnel → empty the bladder and confirm catheter function
   → open the bladder and complete excision under direct vision of the orifice.
 • Alternative in a complete extravesical approach: flexible cystoscopy confirms complete ureterectomy and
   patency of the contralateral orifice.
 • Multilayer absorbable closure as in open surgery; perivesical drain after watertight closure is confirmed by
   irrigation.
Robot-assisted laparoscopic nephroureterectomy
 • A feasible alternative to open and pure laparoscopic surgery; meta-analytic data suggest comparable
   outcomes with shorter length of stay for minimally invasive procedures.
 • Newer systems (longer instruments, greater range of motion) allow the distal ureterectomy without re-
   docking or repositioning.

Outcomes and Comparative Data
 • First laparoscopic nephroureterectomy: Clayman, 1991.
 • Published series vary by approach (transperitoneal versus retroperitoneal) and by distal management
   (open removal, transurethral resection, totally laparoscopic); no approach has a clear-cut advantage in
   morbidity, cosmesis or return to activity.
 • The only randomised trial of laparoscopic (LNU) versus open (ONU) nephroureterectomy was early and
   underpowered: no significant difference in 5-year cancer-specific or metastasis-free survival; blood loss
   and time to discharge significantly lower with LNU.

Lymphadenectomy
 • Regional nodal metastasis is characteristic of urothelial carcinoma, and UTUC is no exception.
   Lymphadenectomy at extirpation provides accurate staging and demonstrable therapeutic benefit; the
   open questions are extent of dissection and risk-adapted selection.
 • Templates are based on the anatomical site of the primary, but overlap is substantial because tumour
   location and retroperitoneal drainage vary; wide regional templates are therefore advocated.
 • Classic templates: renal pelvis, proximal or mid-ureter → ipsilateral hilar nodes plus para-aortic (left) or
   paracaval (right) nodes; lower ureter → pelvic nodes.
 • Extended templates derive from node-mapping data and reflect right-to-left lymphatic drainage (Fig.

Results of Nephroureterectomy
 • RNU is the gold standard for oncologic control of non-metastatic disease, based on large retrospective
   series; complete resection gives durable local control and cure when disease is truly localised.
 • Bladder recurrence is common and treatable, most likely from drop-down implantation (supported by
   genomic evidence). Outcome reporting must therefore separate bladder recurrence from extra-
   urothelial recurrence as an end point.
 • Surgical outcomes have been stable over three decades: in a large multi-institutional review with
   minimal neoadjuvant use, disease-free and cancer-specific survival did not change by stage and grade.
   Organ-confined disease (≤pT2, N0/Nx) had consistently good 5-year cancer-specific survival; non-organ-
   confined disease (≥pT3 and/or N+) less than half of that — the baseline for surgery alone, underlining
   stage-dependent lethality and the need for adjunctive therapy.
 • Loss of renal function is the key cost; in poor renal function, the curative value of RNU is balanced against
   the morbidity of dialysis.
 • Kidney-sparing versus RNU: a meta-analysis of retrospective comparative studies found comparable
   overall and cancer-specific survival after adjustment, but about three times as many reinterventions with
   endoscopic management, reflecting upper-tract recurrence. Selection bias is strong; the cumulative
   burden must be explained to patients. High kidney-preservation rates are achievable in well-selected
   cases with careful technique.
Concept — RNU buys certainty of local control at the price of nephrons; every kidney-sparing strategy trades
that certainty for renal function and repays it in reinterventions and surveillance.

Segmental Ureteral Resection
 • Segmental ureterectomy here means mainly distal ureterectomy with reimplantation. True proximal
   segmental resection with uretero-ureterostomy is rarely indicated; total ureterectomy may require ileal
   ureter or autotransplantation.
 • Comparative data are retrospective with marked selection bias; data quality is poor.
 • Resection for compromised renal function must not sacrifice oncologic efficacy to avoid dialysis.
 • Ureteroscopy of the entire upper tract above the tumour is critical to exclude multifocality.
 • High-grade disease → many surgeons add ipsilateral pelvic lymph node dissection.
 • Advantages: preserves renal function and yields a pathological specimen that informs the need for, and
   extent of, adjuvant therapy.

Ureteroureterostomy
 • Indications: tumours of the proximal or mid-ureter not removable endoscopically, or high-grade or
   invasive tumours when the renal unit must be preserved. A clear margin with enough well-vascularised
   ureter for a tension-free anastomosis is paramount and is the main limiting challenge. The procedure is
   rare and narrowly indicated: a small tumour, no multifocality elsewhere, not endoscopically manageable.

 • Technique: full or modified flank position → flank incision from the tip of the 12th rib → extraperitoneal
   approach; ureter identified, mobilised and slung in vessel loops → tumour palpated; ureter ligated 1–2
   cm above and below the tumour (location verifiable on preoperative imaging; Fig. 139.12) → excision;

 • Success depends on preserving ureteric blood supply and a tension-free anastomosis; after a long
   excision, mobilise and descend the kidney for length.
 • Laparoscopic or robotic: flank position, trocars as for pyeloplasty or nephrectomy; strict oncologic
   principles to avoid tumour violation and obtain negative margins.

Results
 • Historical poles: RNU for all UTUC, versus segmental ureterectomy only for low-grade, non-invasive distal
   ureteric tumours.
 • Ureteric cancers occur most often in the distal ureter, then mid-ureter, then proximal ureter.
 • In properly selected patients, segmental resection gives oncologic outcomes similar to RNU; outcome
   correlates with stage and grade regardless of the extent of surgery (Table 139.1).
 • Series bottom lines: after adjustment, segmental ureterectomy and RNU gave similar outcomes (mostly
   non-muscle-invasive disease, long follow-up); recurrences after organ-sparing surgery occurred in invasive
   disease; low-grade non-invasive tumours treated segmentally had minimal recurrence and no cancer
   deaths; T3 disease had very poor cancer-specific survival; a Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results
   (SEER) population analysis showed no difference in cancer-specific mortality, segmental versus RNU,
   adjusted for stage; a single-institution comparison found no difference in bladder, local or distant
   recurrence or survival.
 • Ipsilateral recurrence after conservative treatment of ureteric tumours is 33–55%, mostly distal to the
   original lesion but also proximal. Risk is lifelong because late recurrences occur, so follow-up is lifelong.
   These figures come from an era of limited ureteroscopy; thorough ureteroscopic evaluation and selection
   probably improve results.
 • Segmental ureterectomy → low-grade, non-muscle-invasive disease of the proximal or mid-ureter not
   completely ablatable endoscopically (size or multiplicity).
 • Distal ureterectomy with neocystostomy → low-grade, low-stage, or selected high-grade locally invasive
   distal tumours when renal preservation is necessary. With high-grade disease, consider

Management of Positive Upper Tract Urinary Cytology and Carcinoma in Situ
Evaluation
 • An unequivocal positive voided cytology usually indicates urothelial carcinoma, most often from the
   bladder; extravesical sources include the upper tracts and, in males, the prostatic urethra.
 • Diagnosis is difficult: imaging of the upper tracts is limited, upper-tract endoscopy is more complex than
   cystoscopy, and minute specimens hamper histological diagnosis and staging.
 • Algorithm (Fig. 139.24): repeat cytology to confirm → upper-tract imaging (usually CT urography) and
   complete bladder evaluation — bladder biopsies with or without enhanced visualisation, and resection of
   any tumour → bladder positive: treat the bladder (intravesical therapy and/or resection) and follow
   voided cytology → cytology still positive despite a negative bladder evaluation, or after adequate bladder
   treatment (after a "6 + 1" bacillus Calmette–Guérin [BCG] course) → extravesical evaluation, with repeat
   bladder evaluation at the same time.
 • Extravesical evaluation: selective cytology from each upper tract, avoiding contamination from bladder or
   urethra; retrograde pyelograms if the upper tracts were not evaluated or visualised on CT urography;
   resection (biopsy) of a representative prostatic urethral specimen in males; ureteroscopy now, or once the
   positive cytology is lateralised.

Expectant Management
 • Little evidence; UTUC was not considered suitable for surveillance because of progression risk (especially
   high-risk disease), flank pain, chronic haematuria and obstruction.
 • Untreated UTUC has markedly worse overall survival than treated disease, so surveillance suits only
   patients with very short life expectancy.
 • AUA/SUO: watchful waiting or surveillance is an option for selected patients with significant
   comorbidity, competing mortality risks, or significant risk of end-stage renal disease with intervention —
   after a well-informed shared discussion with patient and family.

After organ-sparing therapy
 • Any procedure short of extirpation carries higher local recurrence (established ipsilateral risk); topical

Perioperative Systemic Therapy
 • Systemic strategies parallel those for bladder urothelial cancer. Prospective trials show benefit of

Treatment of Lymph Node–Positive and Metastatic Disease
Chemotherapy
 • UTUC-specific data are limited; randomised comparisons are not feasible because the disease is rare, so
   response data are extrapolated from urothelial cancer, mostly without stratification by site. In one
   institutional series, recurrence-free survival did not improve over two decades.
 • Postoperative renal decline in elderly patients compromises chemotherapy after RNU — another reason
   for neoadjuvant treatment of high-risk tumours.
 • Regional nodal metastases → chemotherapy first as primary therapy; withhold surgery until a good
   (ideally complete) radiographic response; two further cycles after maximal response (usually six in total);
   then consolidative surgery including lymphadenectomy — the bladder paradigm.

Molecular Alterations and Future of Genomic-Driven Therapy
 • UTUC and bladder cancer are phenotypically similar but genomically somewhat dissimilar.
 • MSI is high in UTUC, relevant now that pembrolizumab is approved for any solid tumour with high MSI.

 High-grade UTUC, relative to high-grade bladder cancer
 More: FGFR3 (trend), HRAS, CDKN2B mutations. Fewer: TP53 and RB1 mutations. Shared: frequent chromatin-
 modifier mutations

 • Comprehensive UTUC genomics: FGFR3 the most frequently mutated gene, in both grades but more in
   low grade; then KMT2D, PIK3CA and TP53. Hypermutated tumours with deficient mismatch-repair
   proteins link to Lynch syndrome. APOBEC and CpG are the commonest mutational signatures. RNA
   sequencing defines four putative subtypes, one with high CTLA-4, PD-1 and PD-L1 expression.
 • Erdafitinib, a pan-fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) inhibitor, is the first targeted agent for
   metastatic urothelial cancer; phase II data in FGFR-altered tumours show meaningful response and high
   disease control with intermittent or continuous dosing.
 • Summary: UTUC is chemosensitive, but standard regimens are toxic and responses not sustained;
   baseline chronic kidney disease, worsened by RNU, is unique to this population; checkpoint inhibitors are
   improving outcomes in treatment-naive and platinum-refractory disease, and genomic-driven therapy is
   expanding.
Concept — UTUC is the FGFR3-rich, MSI-high, cisplatin-poor relative of bladder cancer, and each trait opens a
different therapeutic door: FGFR inhibition, checkpoint blockade, and neoadjuvant timing of platinum.

 • Limited role outside palliation: proximity of bowel to the upper tract prevents the dose needed for

FOLLOW-UP

General Principles
 • Multifocal recurrence and metastatic spread (more with dysplastic lesions) complicate follow-up.
   Evaluate the bladder, the ipsilateral tract (if spared), the contralateral tract, and extra-urinary sites. The
   schedule depends on time since surgery, approach (kidney-sparing versus radical) and risk.
 • AUA/SUO schedules are stratified by approach and by risk (low versus high) or pathology (<pT2N0/x
   versus ≥pT2).
 • Every visit: history, examination, basic metabolic panel and office cystoscopy (high bladder recurrence
   after both conservative treatment and RNU). After kidney-sparing: upper-tract endoscopy for ipsilateral
   recurrence.
 • Urine cytology helps detect upper-tract recurrence, especially of high-grade tumours.
 • Contrast CT or MRI of abdomen and pelvis with excretory phase (risk of synchronous or metachronous
   bilateral disease; metastatic staging). After RNU, renal impairment may preclude contrast → retrograde
   pyelography if iodinated contrast or gadolinium cannot be given or the urographic phase is non-
   diagnostic. Filling defects usually need ureteroscopy. Chest imaging for metastatic staging.
 • Nephrology referral for diminished renal function after RNU or other intervention; encourage smoking
   cessation, exercise and healthy diet.

   • Assess suspected UTUC with history, imaging, endoscopy, biopsy and cytology to inform risk-stratified
     surgical management.
   • Lynch syndrome is present in 8–12% of newly diagnosed UTUC; evaluate all patients by family history
     and tissue biomarkers (mismatch-repair immunohistochemistry) or germline testing as standard of
     care.

   • Predictors of advanced stage to document: high-grade biopsy or cytology, multifocality, obstruction,
     tissue invasion, sessile endoscopic appearance.
   • Endoscopic management suits low-grade, low-risk tumours, with careful technique to minimise
     adverse events.
   • Topical treatment for highly selected patients: BCG in high-grade clinically non-invasive cancer;
     mitomycin gel in low-grade tumours not amenable to endoscopy — after counselling on serious
     adverse events.
   • Conservatively managed patients must understand frequent recurrence, repeat procedures, long-
     term follow-up, and the progression that would change strategy.
   • RNU with bladder cuff is the most definitive treatment for non-metastatic UTUC — for high-grade,
     high-risk cancers, or bulky or progressive unfavourable low-grade cancers of the proximal ureter and
     renal pelvis.
   • Distal ureterectomy with bladder cuff and reimplantation for lower-ureteric tumours, particularly
     with high-grade or invasive features.
   • Template lymph node dissection during extirpative surgery for high-grade UTUC.
   • Oncologic principles in open or minimally invasive surgery: attempted R0 resection, watertight repair,
     no uncontrolled urinary spillage.
   • Single-dose adjuvant topical therapy after endoscopic and extirpative surgery.
   • Neoadjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy for all eligible high-grade, high-risk patients with
     borderline renal function before RNU.
   • pT2–T4 and/or N1 after surgery → refer for adjuvant treatment: platinum chemotherapy, checkpoint
     inhibitors, or biomarker-directed trials.
