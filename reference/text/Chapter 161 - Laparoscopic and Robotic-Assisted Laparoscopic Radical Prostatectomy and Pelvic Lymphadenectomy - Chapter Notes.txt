EVOLUTION OF MINIMALLY INVASIVE LAPAROSCOPIC PROSTATECTOMY
 • Anatomic foundations (late 1970s–early 1980s): cadaveric studies defined the dorsal venous complex
   (DVC), the neurovascular bundle (NVB; Walsh and Donker, 1982) and the striated urethral sphincter →
   anatomic, nerve-sparing open radical prostatectomy with markedly reduced morbidity; it remained the
   standard for localised disease for over two decades.
 • First laparoscopic radical prostatectomy (LRP): Schuessler, 1997 → very long operating times and
   prolonged stay; cure appeared comparable to open surgery but no advantage could be shown, so LRP
   was not adopted.
 • Revival (2000): two French centres published a stepwise, reproducible and teachable technique →
   acceptable operating times and positive margin rates; worldwide uptake followed, but laparoscopic
   suturing of the vesicourethral anastomosis remained the skill barrier.
 • Robotic assistance was adopted largely to overcome that barrier. The da Vinci Surgical System became
   dominant in urology through:
     ◦ wristed (EndoWrist) instruments reproducing the dexterity of the human wrist;
     ◦ 10× magnified, three-dimensional stereo-endoscopic view, versus the two-dimensional view of
       conventional laparoscopy.

Concept — The robot did not change the operation; it removed the laparoscopic suturing barrier, which is
why RALRP spread where LRP had stalled.

PATIENT SELECTION
Indications and Contraindications
 • Indications identical to open surgery: clinically localised cancer — cT1–T2, or cT3 still amenable to
   extirpation with a wide resection.
 • Staging imaging is risk-stratified (2022 American Urological Association/American Society for Radiation
   Oncology [AUA/ASTRO] Clinically Localized Prostate Cancer guideline):
     ◦ High risk → cross-sectional pelvic imaging (computed tomography [CT] or magnetic resonance imaging
       [MRI]) and bone scan: obtain.
     ◦ Unfavourable intermediate risk → consider the same.
     ◦ Prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography (PSMA PET)-CT → consider in
       high risk for metastasis with negative conventional imaging.

 • What the table tests: only two things make the operation impossible — bleeding that cannot be

   a surgeon's experience.
 • Prior laparoscopic extraperitoneal mesh herniorrhaphy → prefer transperitoneal; dense retropubic

Morbid obesity.
 • Risks: compression nerve injury (extra padding) and respiratory compromise in steep Trendelenburg
   (close communication with anaesthesia once pneumoperitoneum is established).
 • Technical constraints: limited working space, trocar size and instrument length.
 • Modifications: extra-long (16 cm) trocars; trocars 1–2 cm more cephalad (better angle to the apex, less
   collision with the pubic symphysis); divide the urachus more cephalad (removes hanging fat); lower
   pneumoperitoneum to 10 mm Hg with deeper trocar insertion to reach the apex and anastomosis.
 • Bottom line: experienced surgeons achieve adequate operative, functional and oncologic outcomes.
Large prostate (>80 g) and median lobe.
 • Challenges: gland fills the pelvis → little working space and poor manoeuvrability; median lobe
   complicates posterior bladder neck division near the ureteric orifices and may need extensive bladder
   neck reconstruction; wider DVC, wider and more vascular pedicles; NVB displaced posteriorly.
 • Review preoperative imaging (transrectal ultrasound, CT or MRI) to anticipate a large gland, median lobe
   or bladder stones.
 • Outcome: longer operating time and more blood loss, but long-term continence comparable to smaller
   glands.
Prior TURP.
 • Identify the ureteric orifices before the posterior bladder neck dissection; the prostate–bladder neck
   plane is often distorted.

 • Oncologic and functional outcomes may be worse than in primary cases.
Salvage surgery (after radiation, brachytherapy, cryotherapy or high-intensity focused ultrasound [HIFU]).
 • Feasible in selected patients, but periprostatic fibrosis obliterates the prostate–rectum plane → higher
   risk of rectal injury.
 • Requires full bowel preparation and counselling on rectal injury, possible colonic diversion, and higher
   rates of impotence and incontinence than primary surgery.

Use of Preoperative Multiparametric Magnetic Resonance Imaging
 • Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) for local staging: high specificity (0.88), low sensitivity (0.61).
 • Reader dependent — a dedicated reader improves prediction of adverse pathology.
 • Added to clinical risk tools → better prediction of extracapsular extension (ECE) → sharper counselling
   on the balance of cancer control versus function.
 • Surgical planning: guides the extent of NVB preservation; capsular effacement by a suspicious lesion →
   tailored wider resection or intraoperative frozen section → fewer positive margins, even in high risk.
 • Not proven necessary in every patient, but review it when available.
Concept — Selection turns on difficulty, not eligibility: indications are the same as open surgery, and nearly
every adverse feature is a relative contraindication that experience overcomes.

PREOPERATIVE PREPARATION
Patient Preparation
 • No routine formal bowel preparation; magnesium citrate plus a Fleet enema is optional to empty the
   rectum for visibility and in case of rectal injury.
 • Prophylactic antibiotic before incision, covering enteric gram-negative bacilli (e.g. cefazolin); positive
   preoperative urine culture → targeted treatment.
 • Low-dose aspirin may be continued; other antithrombotics are withheld for the appropriate interval.

Informed Consent
 • Specific to minimally invasive surgery: conversion to open surgery; general anaesthesia is mandatory
   (cannot be done under regional anaesthesia).
 • Shared with open surgery: bleeding, transfusion, infection, impotence, incontinence, incisional hernia,
   lymphocele, injury to ureter, rectum, bladder or small bowel.
 • The surgeon discloses personal experience and gives an individualised forecast of cancer control and
   function.
 • Baseline validated questionnaires: Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM) and International Prostate
   Symptom Score (IPSS).

Operating Room Personnel
 • Whole team trained in the equipment, setup and steps: scrub technician, circulating nurse, assistant(s).
 • One skilled assistant usually suffices; for RALRP the assistant must know basic laparoscopy and robot
   setup and troubleshooting.
 • Anaesthetist familiar with prolonged pneumoperitoneum is vital.
 • Room layout (Fig. 161.1): RALRP — separate surgeon console, one bedside assistant; LRP — surgeon at
   the table, first assistant, optional second assistant and optional AESOP robotic camera holder.

Patient Positioning
Sequence: general endotracheal anaesthesia → arms tucked and padded (egg-crate; protects median and
ulnar nerves; Fig. 161.2) → sequential compression devices on → legs in low lithotomy or split-leg table (Xi
side-docking: modified supine, "frog-leg") → chest strapped with tape and egg-crate against sliding → check
chest compliance with anaesthesia → orogastric tube and urethral catheter.
 • Avoid fixed shoulder rests → shoulder and brachial plexus compression in steep Trendelenburg.
 • Hip flexion: older platforms needed slight flexion to dock; Xi does not. Exaggerated flexion → femoral
   neuropraxia.
 • Take a history of prior injuries and pad hips, shoulders, knees and calves.

Anaesthesia Considerations
 • Effects span musculoskeletal, intravascular, acid–base, ocular, respiratory and neurological systems →
   work with a consistent anaesthesia team, especially early.
 • Secure oximetry, blood pressure cuff and intravenous access before final positioning — the arms are
   inaccessible afterwards.
 • Carbon dioxide (CO2) pneumoperitoneum: initial vagal response, oliguria, hypercarbia → uncorrected,
   systemic acidosis. Response: anaesthetist raises minute and tidal volumes; surgeon lowers insufflation
   pressure. Worse with the long operating times of early experience.
 • Fluid management: urine output is unreliable (oliguria; bladder open) and excess urine obscures the field
   → typically 1.5–2 L crystalloid, mostly after the anastomosis; further fluid matched to blood and
   insensible losses.
 • Steep Trendelenburg: facial, orbital, pulmonary and laryngeal oedema; raised intraocular pressure (no
   apparent long-term consequence in healthy patients); risk of corneal oedema and abrasion → lubricate
   and protect eyes. Marked facial or orbital oedema → suspect laryngeal oedema → consider delaying
   extubation.
Concept — Almost every preparation step answers one of two stresses — prolonged CO2
pneumoperitoneum and steep head-down tilt with inaccessible limbs — so access, padding and
communication must be settled before the patient is tipped.

 KEY POINTS: PATIENT SELECTION, INSTRUMENTATION AND PREOPERATIVE PREPARATION
   • Avoid complex anatomy early in the learning curve (large gland, median lobe, morbid obesity, prior
     pelvic surgery, post-radiation, post-TURP) — none is an absolute contraindication.
   • mpMRI informs ECE risk and so the balance of cancer control against NVB preservation.
   • A skilled bedside assistant trained in the robot improves efficiency.
   • Surgeon and anaesthetist anticipate hypercarbia and acidosis, corneal oedema, raised intraocular
     pressure and neurapraxia.


INSTRUMENTATION
 • Depends on approach and, for RALRP, on a three- versus four-arm robot.
 • LRP camera control: AESOP 3000 robotic arm (hand, voice or foot control) or a human assistant.
 • RALRP (S, Si, Xi): four arms, one being the stereo-endoscope. Start with a 0° lens; left arm grasper
   (Maryland bipolar or plasma kinetic dissector), right arm curved monopolar scissors, fourth arm ProGrasp
   (large atraumatic retractor). The surgeon toggles control between any two of the three working arms.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE
Robotic-Assisted Versus Pure Laparoscopic Approach
 • Principles of dissection are the same for LRP and RALRP; the description centres on RALRP using the
   multiport platform.
 • da Vinci = master–slave system with three components: patient-side cart, surgeon console, video cart.
 • Bedside assistant and scrub technician: dock and undock; the assistant gives suction–irrigation,
   retraction, passes sutures and changes instruments.
 • Surgeon console: three-dimensional, 10×, high-definition view; full control of the camera and three
   instrument arms; finger-loop master controls replicate hand and wrist movements in real time.
da Vinci SP (single port).
 • All instruments through one 2.5-cm cannula; can be seated in an access system (e.g. GelPOINT) so
   instruments "float" outside the skin and articulate almost at once → larger working range.
 • Learning curve even for experts: 7 degrees of freedom achieved by a different mechanism from Xi.
 • Limited bedside assistance (compact trocar) → solutions: remotely operated suction–irrigation (ROSI)
   controlled by surgeon foot pedal, or an extra assistant trocar ("SP + 1").
 • Better dexterity and vision than laparoscopy with fewer incisions; same dissection principles.
 • Expert laparoscopists may find the robot unnecessary; for most surgeons it eases the anastomosis and
   the critical angles for NVB preservation.

Abdominal Access, Insufflation, and Trocar Placement
 • Access: Veress needle at the base of the umbilicus or open Hasson technique; consider the site and
   nature of prior surgery. Left upper quadrant is often safe with midline scars. The authors enter with a
   visual obturator after insufflation.
 • Adhesions: an offset working laparoscope through one trocar clears space for further trocars; the robot
   then clears the rest.
 • Insufflation 12–15 mm Hg; steep Trendelenburg; secondary trocars under vision.
 • RALRP configuration (Fig. 161.3A): camera trocar (12 mm for S/Si; 8 mm for Xi) just below or above the
   umbilicus, 15–17 cm from the pubic symphysis; three 8-mm robotic trocars; assistant 12-mm and 5-mm
   trocars on the patient's right.
 • Camera: surgeon moves it by foot pedal plus arm movements. 0° throughout for most; some switch to
   30° down for the bladder neck, NVBs and apex, especially in obesity, large glands, prior TURP or a narrow
   pelvis. Xi flips 30° up/down from the console.
 • LRP configuration (Fig. 161.3B): surgeon on the patient's left using the two pararectus trocars; one or
   two assistants use the lateral trocars; camera held by assistant or AESOP.

Extraperitoneal Approach
Sequence: 1.5-cm infraumbilical incision → through anterior rectus sheath → finger dissection anterior to
the posterior rectus sheath and peritoneum → trocar-mounted balloon dilator advanced to the pubis in the
midline → about 500 mL of air under 0° vision develops the space of Retzius (Fig. 161.4) → secondary trocars
→ proceed as for the anterior approach.

Developing the Space of Retzius
Sequence: inspect pelvis (Fig. 161.5) → sharply release sigmoid adhesions to the left pelvic side wall →
divide the urachus high and incise peritoneum just lateral to the medial umbilical ligaments (cauterise
obliterated umbilical vessels) → prevesical fatty alveolar tissue confirms the plane → posterior-cephalad
traction on the urachus; blunt and sharp dissection of the avascular retropubic space (Fig. 161.6) → lateral
bladder mobilisation down to where the medial umbilical ligament crosses the vas (tension-free
anastomosis later) → defat the anterior prostate; bipolar coagulation of superficial DVC branches.
 • Stay within the pelvic brim → avoids epigastric vessels (anterior) and iliac vessels (lateral).
 • Landmarks now visible (Fig. 161.7): anterior bladder and prostate, puboprostatic ligaments, endopelvic
   fascia, pubis.
 • Endopelvic fascia and puboprostatic ligaments divided sharply; levator fibres preserved and swept
   bluntly off the prostate to expose the deep DVC and urethra at the apex. Avoid electrocautery (pelvic
   floor, external sphincter, NVBs).
 • Accessory pudendal arteries run longitudinally on the anteromedial prostate; preserve them — in some
   men they are the dominant arterial supply to the corpora cavernosa.

Ligation of the Deep Dorsal Venous Complex
 • Pneumoperitoneum tamponades venous bleeding → the profuse DVC haemorrhage of open surgery is
   less evident, even if the complex is entered.
 • ProGrasp (or the LRP assistant) gives fixed cephalad retraction of prostate and bladder to expose DVC and
   apex.

Transection sequence: anterior bladder neck divided horizontally in the midline until the catheter appears
(not too lateral — bladder pedicle bleeding) → deflate balloon; catheter tip lifted anteriorly with external

 • Prior TURP: margin distorted; ureteric orifices often very close to the posterior margin.
 • No bladder neck sparing after TURP or with a median lobe; a 30° down lens helps; when in doubt,
   divide more proximally → avoids a positive bladder neck margin.

Dissection of the Seminal Vesicles and Vasa Deferentia
 • Anterior approach: vesicles and vasa identified in the gap between the divided bladder neck and prostate
   and freed from the NVBs; hemoclips, not electrocautery (Fig. 161.10).
 • Retrovesical approach: first step, deep in the cul-de-sac; vasa dissected lateral → medial to the
   ejaculatory ducts; vesicles lie lateral and slightly posterior to the distal vasa, freed from the NVB with
   hemoclips, antegrade from tip to base, without thermal energy (Fig. 161.11).
 • Retrovesical is especially useful with a median lobe.

Development of the Plane Between the Prostate and Rectum
 • Purpose: avoid rectal injury; define the medial border of the pedicles and NVBs. Antegrade development
   is unfamiliar to open surgeons but adapts readily.
 • Technique (Fig. 161.12): anterior traction on vasa and vesicles → 2–3-cm transverse incision in the
   posterior layer of Denonvilliers fascia about 0.5 cm below the vesicle bases → blunt dissection toward
   the apex without electrocautery.
 • Denonvilliers fascia = inferior extension of the peritoneal cul-de-sac between prostate and rectum.

Antegrade release sequence: incise levator fascia sharply on the anteromedial midprostate (Fig. 161.15) →
enter the interfascial plane → blunt dissection along the shiny white prostatic fascia, sweeping the NVB
posterolaterally → a visible lateral NVB groove forms (Fig. 161.16) → hemoclips placed on the pedicle using
the groove, without entrapping nerves → divide pedicle.
 • Releasing the NVB before mobilising the specimen helps avoid traction injury; wristed robotic
   instruments and motion scaling aid this. For LRP, fine-tipped (0.8-mm) dissectors have been described.
 • High anterior versus standard release: uncertain whether high release preserves extra nerves or simply
   gives a handle to protect the true cavernous nerves at 5 and 7 o'clock without touching them.
 • Consensus: minimise, ideally avoid, thermal energy near the NVB — cavernous fibres are highly heat
   sensitive (animal and human evidence).
 • Haemostasis: NVB bleeding is usually minimal; small arteries or venous sinuses → fine absorbable
   sutures with small, site-specific bites.
 • Very large glands (>100 g): exposure by assistant and fourth arm; consider a 30° down lens.

Apical Dissection
 • The apex is the commonest site of positive margins, and its dissection governs continence and potency.
 • By now antegrade dissection has freed the base, lateral and posterior prostate; DVC and urethra are
   divided last.
 • Do not enter the anterior prostate when dividing the DVC (iatrogenic positive margin). If the DVC stitch is
   too proximal, remove it, divide with a wider margin, then re-suture. Raise insufflation pressure transiently
   to limit bleeding.
 • Then inspect the apex–urethra junction (Fig. 161.17): preserve maximal urethral length but recognise a
   protruding anterior or posterior prostatic lip.
 • Leaving a small rim of urethra on the apex lowers apical positive margins without harming continence.
 • Sharp division, minimal electrocautery → protects the external striated sphincter and NVBs.

Intraoperative Inspection of Prostate
 • Before entrapment, inspect the whole specimen surface; excise extra tissue at any site of a close margin
   — rarely needed with experience.

Pelvic Lymphadenectomy
 • Performed after the prostate is freed and before entrapment — bladder mobilisation already exposes the
   obturator fossa and iliac vessels (detail under Minimally Invasive Pelvic Lymph Node Dissection).

Entrapment of Specimens
 • Prostate and nodes placed in a 10-mm entrapment sack passed through the 12-mm assistant trocar; left
   in the abdomen until the end.

Bladder Neck Reconstruction
 • Usually unnecessary: the slightly larger bladder opening "parachutes" onto the urethra during the
   running anastomosis.
 • Needed with a large gland, median lobe or prior TURP (bladder neck much larger than urethra) →
   tennis-racquet closure, posterior or anterior, with absorbable suture.
 • Ureteric orifices then lie at or near the posterior margin → identify them first (intravenous methylene
   blue or indigo carmine if needed).

Posterior Support of the Vesicourethral Anastomosis
 • Prostatectomy divides Denonvilliers fascia and its confluence with the posterior rhabdosphincter.
 • Rocco stitch (posterior reconstruction): reported earlier return of continence; other studies found no
   benefit. Proposed mechanisms: restored posterior support, better urethral coaptation, less anastomotic
   tension, longer functional sphincter.
 • Agreed minimum benefit: shortens the bladder neck–urethra gap → tension-free anastomosis.
 • Technique (modified Rocco stitch, Fig. 161.18): remnant Denonvilliers fascia and posterior detrusor/
   bladder neck → posterior rhabdosphincter beneath the urethra, running 2-0 Monocryl, before the
   anastomosis.
 • Anterior resuspension of the anastomosis and distal bladder neck to the arcus tendineus restores
   anterior support and the vesicourethral angle.

Vesicourethral Anastomosis
 • Hardest step of LRP; the robot's wristed instruments greatly ease it.
 • Van Velthoven running technique: spreads tension across many points. Classically two sutures tied end
   to end (each 6–8 inches); now more often double-armed barbed suture.
 • No mucosal eversion sutures needed (unlike historic open surgery) — the running closure gives mucosa-
   to-mucosa apposition.
Sequence (Fig. 161.19): start posteriorly at 6 o'clock → preplace 2–3 throws each side through urethra and
bladder → cinch by lifting each arm anteriorly (assistant or ProGrasp holds tension on one arm) → run both
arms anteriorly → pass final catheter under vision before closing → irrigate to test for leak → interrupted
sutures if leaking.
 • Avoid incorporating the NVBs in urethral bites.

Delivery of the Specimens and Exiting the Abdomen
Sequence: inspect for bleeding at low pressure (<10 mm Hg) → check nearby bowel for instrument injury →
close 12-mm fascial sites (e.g. Carter-Thomason device; 5- and 8-mm sites need no fascial closure) → move
sack string to the umbilical camera port → desufflate → extract specimen intact via extended periumbilical
incision → close anterior rectus fascia → subcuticular absorbable skin closure.

Technical Modifications to Optimise Return of Urinary Control
 • Anterior compartment structures — endopelvic fascia, detrusor apron, pubovesical and puboprostatic
   ligaments, DVC with overlying visceral endopelvic fascia — stabilise prostate, urethra and bladder and are
   thought to be part of the continence mechanism.
 • Goal: preserve rather than reconstruct them → two techniques: Retzius-sparing (RS) and hood.

Retzius-Sparing Technique
 • Bocciardi approach, 2010: entirely posterior, through the rectovesical pouch (of Douglas); no dissection
   in the anterior compartment (Fig. 161.20).

 • Evidence (Cochrane systematic review of single-centre randomised trials): earlier continence with RS at

 • Erectile function at 12 months: no difference (single-centre randomised trial).

"Hood" Technique
 • Tewari group, 2021: keeps a familiar anterior approach while preserving the anterior continence
   structures.
 • Four differences from the standard anterior approach:
     ◦ preservation of the median umbilical ligaments;
     ◦ preservation of the endopelvic fascia–puboprostatic ligament complex;
     ◦ complete antegrade control of pedicles and release of NVBs, posteriorly base to apex, then laterally;
     ◦ antero-apical dissection preserving the detrusor apron, separated from the anterior fibromuscular

 • Result: a "hood" of detrusor apron, arcus tendineus, puboprostatic ligaments and DVC over the
   membranous urethra and external sphincter (Fig. 161.21).
 • Early continence promising in a small single-institution cohort; needs randomised trials.

POSTOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT
Enhanced Recovery After Robotic Prostatectomy
 • Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS): multimodal, patient-centred — education, nutrition, analgesia,
   early mobilisation; increasingly standard after RALRP and a driver of same-day discharge.

Pain Management
Sequence: preoperative education on expected pain → infiltrate all incisions with long-acting local
anaesthetic (bupivacaine) → ketorolac 15–30 mg intravenously near the end (unless non-steroidal anti-
inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs] contraindicated) → regular paracetamol (acetaminophen) + NSAID for 24–48
h, then as needed → opioids only for inadequate control, short-acting and brief.
 • Non-pharmacological: early ambulation, relaxation techniques.

Diet
 • Mild ileus occasionally, resolving in 24–48 h; opioids worsen it.
 • Oral fluids and diet within 4–8 h; counsel on constipation — minimise opioids, use stool softeners.

Deep Venous Thrombosis Prophylaxis
 • Pneumatic compression from before incision, continued postoperatively; subcutaneous heparin 1–2 h
   before surgery may be given until ambulant. Thromboembolism is very uncommon.

Same Day Discharge
 • Same-day discharge (SDD) is feasible and safe in selected patients, experienced surgeons, high-volume
   centres; enabled by ERAS.
 • Success improved by less peritoneal irritation: ultra-low pneumoperitoneum (as low as 6 mm Hg) or the
   extraperitoneal approach.
 • Needs counselling and a structured follow-up plan; benefits — quicker return to activity, lower cost.

Pelvic Drain
 • Not routinely indicated after a watertight running anastomosis. If used: through an 8-mm robotic port
   site (no extra stab); drains unexpected urine or lymph; usually removed day 1–2 if the patient is still in
   hospital.

PERIOPERATIVE OUTCOMES
 • Early single-institution reports suggested superior potency and continence with RALRP — not borne out
   by controlled data.
 • Landmark trial — Yaxley et al., 2016 (randomised, one open surgeon vs one robotic surgeon):
   equivalent continence, potency and early oncologic outcomes at 3 months; RALRP favoured for blood
   loss (mean about 1400 mL open vs 300 mL robotic) and length of stay.
 • Scandinavian surgeon-level comparisons: outcomes depend chiefly on the individual surgeon's skill and
   experience; better with high-volume surgeons at high-volume centres.
 • Barrier to uptake: cost — capital outlay and limited-use instruments.
 • Real advantages of RALRP: minimally invasive recovery, faster return to activity, at least equivalent
   oncologic and functional results, and a wider pool of surgical candidates (prior mesh repair, older
   comorbid men, prior abdominal or pelvic surgery) who might otherwise be steered to external-beam
   radiotherapy.
Concept — The surgeon, not the platform, is the dominant variable: randomised data show parity on cancer
and function, so the robot's case rests on recovery and reach.

ONCOLOGIC OUTCOMES
 • Measures: positive surgical margin (PSM) rate and biochemical recurrence (BCR) — prostate-specific
   antigen (PSA) rising above 0.2 ng/mL after becoming undetectable.
 • Meta-analysis: no difference in PSM between radical retropubic prostatectomy (RRP) and RALRP; mean
   RALRP PSM about 15% overall, about 9% in pathologically localised disease; 7-year BCR-free survival
   about 80%.
 • Cancer-specific survival needs 15–20 years of follow-up → not yet defined for RALRP.
 • PSM as surrogate of surgical quality: lower in high-volume centres; varies between surgeons even in
   centres of excellence.
 • Tumour factors drive margins, independent of technique: Gleason ≥7, perineural invasion, higher stage.
 • Trap — do not raise PSM: obesity/high body mass index, large prostate, prior abdominal surgery.
 • Training: early BCR higher for open surgeons adopting RALRP than for fellowship-trained robotic
   surgeons; BCR risk stays stable once the learning curve is passed.
Concept — Margins measure the tumour first and the surgeon second; the approach itself contributes
nothing once volume and training are accounted for.

COMPLICATIONS
Complications Related to Patient Positioning
 • History of musculoskeletal problems → patient-specific padding.
 • Protect cervical, axillary, brachial and radial nerves; pad arms and hands; care of the brachial plexus if
   arms extended.
 • No shoulder rests; Yellowfins stirrups protect the legs.
 • Trendelenburg tilt 22–25°; prolonged tilt → ocular oedema, occasionally cerebral irritation.
 • Excessive hip flexion → femoral nerve injury; not needed with Xi.

Bleeding and Transfusion
 • Transfusion 1–2%; blood loss dramatically lower than open (pneumoperitoneum compresses veins;
   vision identifies bleeders). In the Yaxley trial, 1400 mL open vs 300 mL robotic.

Visceral Injury
 • Rectal, sigmoid and small bowel injuries: rare but serious → intraoperative general surgical
   consultation.
 • Recognised rectal injury, minimal/no spillage → primary repair without colostomy: multilayer closure +
   omental or peritoneal flap interposed between rectum and anastomosis.
 • Unrecognised rectal injury → rectovesical fistula → may need colostomy and later fistula repair.
 • Small bowel injury: often outside the camera's view (instrument exchanges by the assistant) or during
   adhesiolysis; unrecognised → delayed acute abdomen and peritonitis → immediate repair and drainage.

Thromboembolic Complications
 • AUA Best Practice Statement: routine intermittent pneumatic compression for laparoscopic and robotic
   urological surgery; pharmacological prophylaxis only with multiple risk factors (obesity, advanced age,
   malignancy, immobility, prior deep vein thrombosis [DVT]).
 • Incidence as low as 0.5% — faster mobilisation, and Trendelenburg reduces lower-limb venous stasis

Anastomotic Complications
Urine Leak
 • Can cause significant ileus. No drain → interventional radiology percutaneous drain; prolonged
   catheterisation; cystogram to confirm resolution.
 • In skilled hands rare, small, self-limiting, no long-term effect on continence; major disruption may need
   reoperation and worsens continence.

Bladder Neck Stricture
 • Open RRP: up to 16%; RALRP: <2% — attributed to precise mucosa-to-mucosa apposition.
 • Management ladder: dilation or transurethral incision + catheter → rarely, for recalcitrant strictures,
   posterior urethroplasty with anastomotic revision.

Equipment Malfunction
 • Critical robotic failure about 0.4% (<0.5%) → cancellation before starting, or conversion to laparoscopic
   or open; commonest failing parts: robotic arms and optical system.

SALVAGE ROBOTIC-ASSISTED RADICAL PROSTATECTOMY
 • Indication: local failure after external-beam radiotherapy (EBRT), brachytherapy, HIFU or cryotherapy.
 • Historically avoided (open) because of incontinence, ED, bladder neck stenosis and rectal injury — rectal
   injury above 15% at some centres.
 • Robotic vision allows precise dissection through fibrosis; salvage RALRP is feasible and safe in small
   series but with worse function than primary RALRP.
 • Work-up: biopsy-proven local recurrence + imaging, preferably PSMA PET, to exclude metastases.
 • High-volume centres, experienced robotic surgeons.

Salvage Prostatectomy Technique
 • After EBRT: pelvic side wall and Retzius harder to develop; desmoplastic reaction around the iliac vessels.
   Planes usually preserved after brachytherapy, cryotherapy or HIFU.
 • Anterior: incise endopelvic fascia to outline the prostate; initial DVC suture often unnecessary (apex hard
   to define; little back-bleeding after radiation).
 • Bladder neck: find by exposing the lateral prostate and the "pinch" manoeuvre; neck may be pale and
   thickened.
 • Complete seminal vesicle excision — invasion is commoner in recurrent disease.
 • Posterior plane is the danger zone: rectal injury occurs posterior to Denonvilliers fascia → full
   mechanical bowel preparation; patient antegrade dissection of the perirectal fat plane, sharp and blunt.
   Brachytherapy seeds often met here (or anywhere).
Rectal check and repair sequence: inspect rectum after removal → air bubble test for a suspected occult
injury → if no gross spillage and tissue adequate: secure two-layer closure + omentum or regional flap
interposed → any doubt: intraoperative colorectal consultation, consider diverting colostomy.
 • Anastomosis: same running technique, but higher leak and later stricture risk (poor vascularity) →
   catheter 2–3 weeks; cystogram strongly advised before removal.
 • Continence: only 33–80% regain reasonable continence.
 • Potency: nerve sparing rarely feasible → usually impotent; many already have ED from prior treatment →
   counsel realistically.

 • Trap: transperitoneal access is not protective — fluid can still loculate.
 • Mechanism: the bladder adheres to and walls off the lymphadenectomy fossa → peritoneal
   interposition flap keeps a window open to the peritoneum → significant reduction in clinically significant
   lymphocele (externally validated).
 • Asymptomatic → no treatment.
 • Presentation: classically ipsilateral DVT (external iliac vein compression), but more often new or worse
   irritative voiding symptoms and pelvic pressure (bladder compression); may become infected.
 • Management ladder: percutaneous drain → recurrence: sclerosant or laparoscopic fenestration and
   marsupialisation.

FUTURE OF ROBOTIC PROSTATECTOMY
 • Expiry of key first-generation da Vinci patents (2019) → new console platforms: Medtronic Hugo, CMR
   Surgical Versius, Johnson & Johnson Ottava and others.
 • Differentiators: modularity, flexible vs rigid instruments, multi- vs single-port, haptic feedback.
   Competition may lower cost and widen global access.
 • Artificial intelligence (AI): pattern recognition in large datasets, real-time decision support, performance
   assessment and outcome prediction, individualised training feedback, eventually semi-autonomous or
   autonomous functions.
 • AI hurdles: volume of high-quality surgical data; data sharing, privacy and ethics; safe response to
   unpredictable intraoperative events.
 • Training: build new platforms into curricula; graduated basic and procedure-specific simulation (console,
   troubleshooting, foot-pedal coordination) before live surgery, modelled on aviation; virtual and
   augmented reality promising but resource-intensive and hard to validate.

SUMMARY
 • RALRP is the operation of choice for localised and, increasingly, locally advanced disease; open
   prostatectomy is now less common in the United States. LRP persists where robots are cost-prohibitive.
 • 23-hour and same-day discharge → faster recovery than open surgery; robotic surgery has widened
   surgical candidacy and shown promise in the salvage setting.
 • Cost remains the main constraint; new vendors may lower it. The future is tied to AI (with data, privacy
   and ethical challenges) and to simulation and virtual-reality training.
Concept — The chapter's argument is equivalence plus access: RALRP matches open surgery on cancer and
function, wins on recovery, and its future depends on cost and training rather than on further proof of
efficacy.
