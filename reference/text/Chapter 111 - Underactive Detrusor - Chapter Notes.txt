Chapter 111 — Underactive Detrusor
 • Impaired emptying with age is common in both sexes — raised outlet resistance, weak contraction, or
   both. The weak contraction is detrusor underactivity (DUA) (International Continence Society [ICS]
   urodynamic term); the symptomatic condition is underactive bladder (UAB).
 • Outlet resistance dominates male therapy: benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) raises it with age and is the
   target of most treatment for male lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS).
 • DUA mostly lacks effective treatment other than catheterisation, preferably intermittent self-
   catheterisation (ISC), when the postvoid residual (PVR) is raised.
 • Prevalence in referred LUTS rises with age; cause and mechanisms are poorly understood.
Concept — Emptying is the product of contraction against resistance; medicine can lower resistance but
cannot yet strengthen the detrusor, so DUA management is largely drainage.

TERMINOLOGY, DEFINITIONS, AND SYMPTOMS
 • Older terms abandoned: impaired detrusor contractility, detrusor areflexia, detrusor failure.
 • ICS 2002 definition of DUA (urodynamic): "a contraction of reduced strength and/or duration, resulting in
   prolonged bladder emptying and/or failure to achieve complete bladder emptying within a normal time
   span."
     ◦ No thresholds are specified for reduced strength, reduced duration or prolonged emptying.
 • No contraction at all → acontractile detrusor. Distinguish from an isolated failure to void during a
   urodynamic study (UDS) — the "bashful bladder", which is common and often matches a history of
   inability to void in public toilets.
 • A purely urodynamic definition is impractical (UDS is invasive, not universally available) — the reasoning
   behind the overactive bladder (OAB) symptom complex. But OAB is anchored on urgency (variably linked
   to detrusor overactivity [DO]); UAB has no pathognomonic symptom and overlaps with OAB and bladder
   outlet obstruction (BOO).

EPIDEMIOLOGY
 • LUTS are highly prevalent and increase with age; the contribution of DUA is unknown.
 • Epidemiology of LUTS (EpiLUTS) (UK, USA, Sweden; over 40s): storage LUTS commoner in females, voiding
   in males, postmicturition similar. Difficulty emptying is common in older community dwellers.
 • Population prevalence of DUA cannot be established: there is no simple non-invasive marker.
 • Proxies fail in males: voiding LUTS, raised PVR and low flow can all be caused by BOO.
 • In females straining may simply accompany OAB (frequency, small volumes); but voiding LUTS with low
   flow and raised PVR make DUA likely, because female BOO is rare (2.7–8% of those referred for UDS).
 • In referred patients, DUA is common, especially in the elderly (Table 111.1).

 KEY POINTS: TERMINOLOGY, DEFINITIONS, AND SYMPTOMS, AND EPIDEMIOLOGY
   • DUA is a common clinical problem associated with a range of storage, voiding and postmicturition
     LUTS.
   • Its population prevalence is unknown for lack of a non-invasive marker.
   • DUA affects 9–28% of males under 50 and 48% of those over 70 undergoing UDS, and is more
     prevalent among the institutionalised elderly.
   • In females, DUA is found in 12–45% undergoing UDS, more so among the institutionalised elderly.
   • DUA commonly coexists with other LUTDs.

Concept — Every estimate of DUA is a referral-series, criterion-dependent figure; because BOO is common in
men and rare in women, the same voiding picture points to obstruction in a man but to DUA in a woman.

ETIOPATHOGENESIS
 • DUA occurs in both sexes and diverse groups. Recognised causes include diabetes mellitus and cauda
   equina compression, but many patients have no obvious cause, suggesting common age-related change.
 • Age-related decline in detrusor function is likely but unproven.
     ◦ Rodent strip data conflict; human strips often show no decline in response to stimulation or agonists.
     ◦ Clinical urodynamic data suggest an age-related fall in Pdet, flow and maximum contraction strength
       with a rise in PVR — but mostly in symptomatic subjects with progressive pathology.
     ◦ BOO in males adds connective tissue and reduces smooth muscle; the same changes in females
       suggest a partial contribution of ageing itself (Fig. 111.4A–C).

Myogenic Factors
 • ECM change reduces transmission of contractile force.
 • Myocyte dysfunction — ion storage and exchange, excitation–contraction coupling, calcium storage,
   energy generation — gives a weak contraction despite normal neural drive.
 • Elbadawi ultrastructural patterns (electron microscopy): the degeneration pattern — widespread
   disrupted myocytes plus axonal degeneration — is associated with DUA. The classification is disputed,
   though others report similar findings.
 • Cause or consequence? Whether myocyte disruption causes DUA or follows another insult is unclear.

Neurogenic Factors
Brain Circuits
 • The micturition reflex is a spino-bulbo-spinal loop: sacral parasympathetic nucleus → PMC → sacral cord;
   the PMC is modulated by the cortex, particularly the limbic system.
 • PMC "direct neurons" fire immediately before and during reflex contractions and are silent otherwise;
   many project to the lumbosacral cord.
 • Human functional neuroimaging implicates the insula, hypothalamus, PAG and PMC.
 • A lesion anywhere here — disturbing perception and integration — could cause DUA, but site and
   urodynamic finding do not always correlate.

Bladder Efferent Pathways
 • Interruption in the sacral cord, sacral roots or pelvic nerves → absent or reduced contraction.
 • Autonomic innervation of the bladder declines with normal ageing.

Bladder and Urethral Afferent Pathways
 • Intact sensation is critical to the efferent limb. Bladder afferents monitor volume during filling and
   contraction magnitude during voiding; urethral afferents sense flow and contraction.
 • Afferent impairment reduces or prematurely ends the micturition reflex → loss of voiding efficiency.
 • Sensation declines with age: higher thresholds to reach capacity in older females; reduced insular
   response to filling on functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) in older asymptomatic people; in
   ageing rats, weaker urethral flow–evoked contraction needing a larger triggering volume.
 • Tissue change and sensory decline both likely contribute in the elderly.

Smith "stiff balloon" hypothesis (afferent output is a function of wall stress):

connective tissue also impairs sensory innervation → stiff bladder → precipitous rise in wall stress on filling

 • Support: in DUA, strong desire at higher volume, similar contractility (Watts factor), smaller voids and
   higher PVR than other LUTDs.
 • Urothelium: biopsies in DUA suggest impaired urothelial signalling and sensory transduction; causality
   versus effect of BOO is unresolved, and animal models are limited by different innervation.

Bladder Outlet Obstruction
Rodent partial-BOO model — three stages:
obstruction (ring or ligature) → bladder distension → compensation: hypertrophy and hyperplasia, sharp
weight gain then plateau, increased blood supply, near-normal function → variable interval →
decompensation: falling contractility, poor emptying, reduced response to stimulation and agonists, fibrosis.
 • If obstruction is not relieved before decompensation and fibrosis, dysfunction is permanent.
 • Obstructed bladders show reduced blood flow (laser Doppler) and denervation in both humans and
   animals.
Ischaemia–reperfusion hypothesis:
raised intravesical pressure to overcome resistance → raised wall tension (law of Laplace) → vessel
compression, ischaemia, hypoxia → reperfusion each fill–void cycle → reactive oxygen species and free

 • Poor translation: immature, often female animals with acute obstruction; the sequence is not seen with

 • Long-term follow-up of men with BOO (over a decade): no significant fall in Pdet@Qmax, Qmax fell by

 • Most men with acute urinary retention have preserved detrusor function. Some with chronic retention
   also do — typically with poor compliance and upper-tract change (high-pressure chronic retention). Why
   outcomes diverge is unknown; the picture is probably multifactorial.

Diabetes Mellitus
 • Diabetes-induced bladder dysfunction (DBD), classically diabetic cystopathy: emptying efficiency falls in
   a time-dependent way over the disease course, by combined neurogenic and myogenic mechanisms.
 • Neurogenic: autonomic neuropathy — axonal degeneration and segmental demyelination → diminished
   sensation. Driven by hyperglycaemia via the polyol pathway, free radicals, protein kinase C activation
   and advanced glycation end products.
 • Reduced nerve growth factor (NGF) (maintains sympathetic and sensory nerves): low dorsal-root-
   ganglion NGF accompanies raised PVR and capacity in diabetic rats.
 • Myogenic: altered intercellular connections, excitability, intracellular signalling and receptor density;
   animal data show both increased and decreased contractility.

Pelvic Arterial Ischemia
 • Vascular risk factors (ageing, smoking, diet, atherosclerosis, metabolic syndrome, hypertension,
   hyperlipidaemia, diabetes) may cause chronic bladder ischaemia, analogous to their role in erectile
   dysfunction.
 • Animal pelvic arterial insufficiency → wall ischaemia, low oxygenation → oxidative stress →
   ultrastructural damage and denervation → features of DUA in some studies.

 KEY POINTS: ETIOPATHOGENESIS
   • Aetiopathogenesis is incompletely understood; diverse factors and mechanisms are implicated.
   • Confirmed: neurological injury/disease and diabetes mellitus. Probable, not definite: BOO, ageing,
     atherosclerosis.
   • These factors impair processes essential to an efficient voiding contraction.
   • Mechanisms are myogenic (myocytes or ECM) or neurogenic (central control via afferent or efferent
     nerves).

Concept — Every cause acts on one link of the voiding reflex loop — afferent, central, efferent or muscle —
and afferent failure is the unifying theme of age-related DUA: the contraction stops early because the signal
sustaining it fades.

DIAGNOSIS
 • Invasive UDS is the only accepted way to estimate detrusor voiding function clinically.
 • No universally agreed criteria exist; current methods measure almost only contraction strength,
   neglecting speed and sustainability.

Detrusor Contraction Strength
 • A contraction generates pressure and flow, so DUA is usually diagnosed by low Qmax plus low
   Pdet@Qmax. Male ranges come from men having outlet surgery; healthy ranges are poorly characterised.
 • Limitation 1 — underestimation: the bladder outlet relation (BOR), an adaptation of the Hill equation,
   makes pressure and flow inversely related during voiding (high flow, low pressure and vice versa), so
   Pdet@Qmax is the point of lowest pressure.
 • Limitation 2 — outlet resistance ignored: with a low Pdet, low Qmax may reflect BOO; a normal Qmax
   may reflect a low-resistance outlet (e.g. post-prostatectomy incontinence).
 • Hence isovolumetric estimates — by calculation or by interrupting flow; many are complex or impractical.

Detrusor Contraction Speed
 • Slow contraction could cause symptoms, but speed is not in the ICS definition.
 • Detrusor shortening velocity: Vdet = (Q/2)[3/(V + Vt)/4π]0.66 (Q flow, V bladder volume, Vt non-
   contracting wall volume).
 • A fall in Vdet precedes a fall in WF in longitudinal studies of both sexes — a potential early marker.

Detrusor Contraction Duration
 • In the ICS definition, but normal limits are undefined and few studies measure it.
 • Paradox: in men with BPH, unobstructed poor-contractility patients had longer contractions than
   unobstructed normals.
 • Duration probably reflects mechanism — early reflex termination would shorten it. A standard
   measurement method is needed.

Bladder Sensation
 • Afferents initiate and sustain contraction; men with DUA have reduced sensation versus other LUTDs.
 • Usual method: filling sensations (first sensation, first desire, strong desire, capacity; normal thresholds
   exist) — subjective: reported even without filling, and vary with setting and anxiety.
 • Current perception threshold testing: more objective, but invasive and unvalidated.

Ambulatory Urodynamics
 • Failure to void on UDS: bashful bladder (poor pelvic floor relaxation + reflex detrusor inhibition) or true
   DUA / acontractility — history usually separates them.
 • If in doubt → ambulatory UDS: over four-fifths of apparently acontractile patients contract. Criteria are
   not standardised and differ from conventional UDS.

 KEY POINTS: DIAGNOSIS
   • There is no published consensus on diagnostic criteria for DUA.
   • Most criteria focus on contraction strength as determined by Pdet during UDS.
   • Several indices estimate isometric strength; most are unvalidated and not applicable to all groups.
   • Stop and occlusion tests are more reliable but may be painful and impractical.

MANAGEMENT
 • Goals: symptoms and quality of life (QoL); prevent urinary tract infection (UTI), stones, reflux with upper-
   tract back-pressure, skin damage from overflow.
 • Nothing effectively improves detrusor function → drainage or lower outlet resistance (e.g. relax the
   external sphincter).

Initial Assessment
 • Routine urological evaluation: bladder diary, digital rectal examination, urinalysis, uroflowmetry,
   ultrasound PVR.
 • Neurological assessment: sacral dermatomes, anal tone, bulbocavernosus reflex, lower-limb reflexes;
   deficits → specialist referral, commonly spinal MRI (lumbar cord, cauda equina).
 • Drug history: agents reducing contractility (anticholinergic, opioid; Box 111.1) or raising outlet resistance
   (α-adrenoceptor agonists).
 • Faecal impaction / constipation can obstruct directly; treating it may improve emptying.

Conservative Management
 • Scheduled voiding for sensory loss; double voiding to empty better and cut frequency.
 • Bladder expression (Valsalva, Credé): only in specific neurogenic cases — DUA with an incompetent
   sphincter. Otherwise avoided: high vesical pressure → vesicoureteric reflux or reflux into prostate and
   seminal vesicles.

 • Lower outlet resistance in neurogenic bladder and dysfunctional voiding; results and combination therapy

Prostanoids
 • Five endogenous prostanoids — PGE2, PGF2α, PGI2, PGD2, thromboxane A — are made in the bladder

Future Prospects in Pharmacotherapy
 • Bladder-specific parasympathomimetic allowing dose escalation — unlikely on current knowledge;
   presynaptic M2 antagonists — promising.

 • Uroselective exogenous prostanoids (sparing uterus and gut) in development; oral efficacy uncertain.
 • Transient receptor potential (TRP) agonists, e.g. TRPV4 agonist GSK1016790A, increase contractility.
 • Other-organ analogues (cardiac inotropes, gut prokinetics); sensory sensitisers for afferent failure.
 • Prerequisite: better understanding of normal contraction and of DUA mechanisms.

Botulinum Toxin
 • Botulinum neurotoxin A (BoNT-A) into the urethral sphincter lowers outlet resistance in detrusor–
   sphincter dyssynergia.
 • Rationale in DUA: relax the sphincter to remove reflex detrusor inhibition, or to facilitate Valsalva
   voiding.
 • Technique: 50 U in 4 mL saline (or 100 U in 8 mL) — cystoscopic in males, periurethral in females.
 • Results: some catheter-dependent patients voided with low PVR, ISC fell; about half of idiopathic cases
   improved.
 • Predictors of response: better bladder sensation; poor sphincter relaxation responds best, pure DUA or
   DHIC about one-third.
 • Limits: short-lived; no dose studies; unlicensed — clinical trials only.

Bladder Outlet Surgery
 • Controversial in males with non-neurogenic DUA; no high-level evidence.

 KEY POINTS: MANAGEMENT
   • There are no effective pharmacotherapies for DUA.
   • Parasympathomimetics have questionable efficacy, potentially fatal systemic effects, and are not used
     routinely.
   • Intravesical electrotherapy shows promise in restoring contraction but is time- and resource-
     consuming.
   • SNM is effective in restoring voiding in DUA associated with urethral sphincter / pelvic floor disorders.
   • Outlet surgery has a high failure risk in men not reliant on catheters. Catheter-dependent men with
     DUA fare worse than those with normal detrusors but may still benefit.

Concept — Treatment works only where some part of the loop survives — intact sensation is the recurring
predictor of response (bethanechol, botulinum toxin, IVE); where muscle is lost, only drainage or
replacement of the muscle remains.

CONCLUSIONS
 • DUA is not uncommon and hard to treat, and has been under-researched compared with OAB and DO.
 • A symptom-based definition is difficult — wide symptom spectrum, diverse conditions, no accurate non-
   invasive marker.
 • Highly prevalent in both sexes presenting with LUTS; causes affect every part of the micturition reflex.
 • Preserved bladder sensation predicts better response to treatment.
 • Drugs to enhance strength, duration or sensation need mechanistic understanding first; regenerative,
   stem cell and gene therapy depend on the same.
