Chapter 61 — Urologic Considerations in Pediatric Renal
Transplantation
 • Children who need kidney replacement therapy (KRT) are more likely than adults to have a congenital
   anomaly of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), including obstructive uropathy.
 • The urologist's task is to make sure the condition that destroyed the native kidneys does not destroy
   the graft. Urology should be part of the multidisciplinary team before end-stage kidney disease (ESKD).
 • Five therapeutic goals: (1) unimpeded drainage from kidney to lower tract · (2) a capacious, low-pressure
   reservoir giving socially acceptable continence · (3) volitional emptying · (4) no infection · (5) the fewest
   operations.
 • Avoid a reactive approach (referral only once the graft is in jeopardy); optimise the lower tract early.
 • Chronic rejection is unavoidable, but every added insult accelerates it.
Concept — The graft is only as safe as the bladder it drains into. Paediatric transplant urology is the work of
correcting, before transplantation, the lower-tract hostility that caused the original renal failure.

 • Screen every child for urological problems, although the yield is low when there is a clear nephrological
   cause.
 • Basic screen: detailed history + renal ultrasound + uroflow with postvoid residual (PVR) if toilet-trained.
   This rules out most abnormalities.
 • Routine voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) is not necessary. Consider it with a history of neuropathic
   bladder, obstructive uropathy, VUR or previous lower-tract surgery, and with recurrent febrile urinary
   tract infection (UTI), unexplained worsening hydroureteronephrosis or refractory voiding problems.

Focused Assessment
 • A known urological abnormality → targeted assessment directed by the condition and by the state of the
   bladder and kidneys.
 • VCUG shows changes with filling and emptying, the urethra, and the presence and grade of reflux.
   Abnormal bladder dynamics on any test → urodynamics.
 • Indications for urodynamics: neuropathic bladder; outlet obstruction from PUVs or a large ureterocele;
   and selected refractory voiding dysfunction, high-grade hydronephrosis or recurrent UTI.

 • Urodynamics measure capacity, compliance, detrusor activity, sensation, emptying and sphincter
   function, to define graft-threatening dynamics and guide therapy.
 • Therapies that follow: drugs (see table below), clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC), intradetrusor
   botulinum toxin, reconstruction.
 • Treatment interacts with emptying: an antimuscarinic for poor compliance can impair emptying and so
   create the need for CIC. A sensate or difficult urethra → continent catheterisable stoma.
 • Urodynamics are serial, not one-off: repeat them to track response and change over time.

 KEY POINTS: UROLOGIC EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT
   • The five goals (drainage, low-pressure storage, volitional emptying, no infection, fewest procedures).
   • Most children needing KRT have uropathy; assessment is directed by the condition.
   • Urodynamics (capacity, compliance, emptying, sphincter) for neuropathic bladder, PUVs, refractory
     dysfunction, high-grade hydronephrosis or recurrent UTI.

Concept — Assessment is history-driven and stepwise: a negative history needs only ultrasound and residual,
and every positive finding funnels towards one question, whether the bladder can store at low pressure and
empty.

Detrusor Overactivity, Low Capacity, and Poor Compliance
 • The commonest early bladder abnormalities in ESKD are low capacity, detrusor overactivity and poor
   compliance. This is the typical initial picture with PUVs.
 • Bladder dynamics evolve. The valve bladder moves from overactivity and low capacity → better capacity
   and less contractility → insufficient contractility and poor emptying (“myogenic failure”).

 • Overactivity and poor compliance are the most dangerous patterns, because high pressure reaches the
   kidney even without reflux. Leaving them untreated puts the graft at risk.
 • Target: storage up to expected capacity for age at detrusor pressure <40 cm H₂O (during filling or at leak),
   plus social continence.
 • Medical treatment first (antimuscarinics); augmentation is second line. Medical treatment often brings in
   CIC. Some children delay CIC with double voiding or Valsalva emptying, and α-blockers can aid emptying.
 • Never assume volitional emptying is effective: prove it with repeated low PVRs and uroflowmetry.
 • Oxybutynin is titrated upwards because of its side effects, watching catheterised volumes, incontinence
   and hydronephrosis.
 • Adherence to pretransplant CIC is a test of adherence to the transplant regimen. A family that cannot
   manage CIC should be assessed for their ability to manage post-transplant care.

Capacity
 • The trend away from augmentation may have gone too far. Augment a small, non-compliant, high-
   pressure bladder when maximal medical treatment and CIC still give pressures that are safe only briefly
   or regularly exceed 40 cm H₂O.
 • There is no compelling evidence that augmentation increases complications during or after transplant.
 • Choice of segment depends on anatomy, previous surgery and whether a continent catheterisable stoma
   is wanted.

Infections
 • Graft pyelonephritis in an immunocompromised host causes major morbidity and can lead to graft loss.
 • The usual cause is urinary stasis: inadequate emptying, persistent obstruction or VUR. UTIs will not
   improve after transplant, so they are dealt with beforehand: double or triple voiding, or CIC.
 • Distinguish true UTI from colonisation, which is almost universal with CIC.
 • Persistent UTI despite good emptying and CIC technique → an anatomical abnormality or chronic
   colonisation.
 • Colonisation may respond to long-term antibiotics, intravesical antimicrobials and regular irrigation.
 • Anatomical causes such as diverticula or ureteric stumps → targeted imaging and surgery.

 • Enterocystoplasty complications accumulate over a long life, and immunosuppression worsens

 KEY POINTS: PRETRANSPLANT PREPARATION
   • Commonest abnormality: low-capacity, high-pressure, poorly compliant bladder.
   • Augmentation does not clearly raise transplant risk; recurrent pyelonephritis does (graft loss).
   • Pretransplant CIC and bladder cycling both treat the bladder and test the family.

Concept — Preparation follows one hierarchy: drugs → CIC → cycling → augmentation. Each step is tested
before the next, because the bladder changes over time and augmentation is irreversible. The PUV and
neuropathic bladders sit on opposite trajectories, so the same urodynamic numbers argue for delay in one
and for early surgery in the other.

Cutaneous Stomas
 • A defunctionalised bladder may sit behind an ileal or colon conduit, vesicostomy, pyelostomy or
   ureterostomy. The donor ureter can drain into these.
 • Contemporary preference is definitive bladder optimisation before transplant. A long-term stoma or
   transplant ureterostomy is reserved for severe neurodevelopmental impairment or inability to tolerate
   CIC.
 • A functioning ileal loop can become the augmentation patch during refunctionalisation, even in an anuric
   child. If augmentation proves unnecessary and the child still makes urine, reimplant the native kidney(s)
   into the bladder and discard the loop.

Reconstruction Strategies
 • Factors to weigh: dialysis type, likely graft site, timing relative to KRT, need for bowel surgery, and the
   native kidneys.

Dialysis Issues
 • On peritoneal dialysis (PD): intraperitoneal surgery usually forces a temporary or permanent switch to
   haemodialysis. Surgery can sometimes be done at PD catheter insertion, but bowel surgery raises the
   risk of catheter infection and removal. Protect the peritoneum, because PD is often the best temporary
   KRT in children.
 • Approaching KRT: dialysis before reconstruction improves metabolic state (healing, tolerance of bowel
   reabsorption), but extensive surgery may lose the peritoneum for PD.

 • On haemodialysis: risks are line infection with postoperative sepsis and arteriovenous fistula injury from
   low flow or pressure.

Graft Placement
 • Very small child, possibly intraperitoneal graft → move mesenteric pedicles away from the midline. Such
   children rarely have earlier reconstruction, and most grafts fit extraperitoneally.
 • A previous psoas hitch or extensive ipsilateral reimplant dissection makes ipsilateral iliac graft placement
   difficult.
 • In difficult cases, a detailed operative report with a diagram is of great value to later surgeons.

Timing
 • Major reconstruction is best done before transplant. Allow 6–12 weeks to heal after enterocystoplasty or
   continent diversion.
 • Unilateral native nephrectomy at transplant is reasonable, especially on the graft side.
 • Avoid concurrent procedures in infants and small children because of prolonged anaesthesia and
   haemodynamic instability. Prophylactic anticoagulation against graft thrombosis also raises bleeding risk.

Enterocystoplasty
 • It is feasible on dialysis or near ESKD, much as in any child. The segment and strategy are chosen on
   functional anatomy: stoma need, native bladder available, bladder neck and continence, and bowel
   function.
 • An unsalvageable bladder needing continent diversion should not wait for transplant. Exception: a child
   transplanted in the first 2 years of life, before continence is expected.
 • Metabolic problems are not an absolute contraindication. Urine reabsorption may bring dialysis forward
   in advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD). In less marginal cases, better bladder dynamics and fewer
   infections may delay ESKD.

Native Nephrectomy
 • This is a multidisciplinary, controversial decision. In general, leave the native kidneys.

Limiting the Risk of Infection
 • Prevent infection proactively, not only after systemic illness. Targets: high-grade reflux, persistent
   hydronephrosis with or without reflux, and CIC-dependent children (colonised and UTI-prone).
 • A non-dilated, non-refluxing native kidney is unlikely to be the source of infection.
 • Circumcision in boys at risk of ESKD, especially with valves, should be discussed early. It may slow
   progression to CKD and reduce post-transplant UTI.

Combining Nephrectomy and Transplantation
 • Generally avoided to limit morbidity and anaesthetic time. It is mostly done to make room for an adult
   graft in a small child or beside very large kidneys. The best case is a single ipsilateral nephrectomy
   through the transplant incision.

 KEY POINTS: PRETRANSPLANT RECONSTRUCTION
   • Spontaneous emptying decides the need for a catheterisable stoma.
   • Augment for capacity <75%, pressure >40 cm H₂O, CIC every ≤3 hours, despite maximal medical
     therapy.
   • PD → intraperitoneal surgery needs temporary haemodialysis; reconstruct well before transplant.
   • Native nephrectomy indications as in the table above.

Concept — Every reconstructive decision is judged by what it costs the future transplant: the peritoneum for
PD, the iliac fossa for the graft, the ureter for salvage, the native kidney for fluid and hormones. Preserve by
default, and remove only for a named indication.

Ureteral Stenting
 • Routine stenting is debated, and there is no conclusive evidence for it in children.
 • Stent when: the reimplant is difficult (especially into an abnormal bladder), or the graft ureter looks
   traumatised or devascularised.
 • In adult series with deceased-donor grafts, stenting reduced ureteric complications at the cost of more
   infection.
 • Standard: a short double-J removed at 4–6 weeks by cystoscopy, timed with removal of a tunnelled line
   or PD catheter.
 • Early removal at 5–7 days (stent tied to the urethral catheter tip, or an externalised stent or splint) may
   reduce stent complications and infection.

 KEY POINTS: TRANSPLANT URETERONEOCYSTOSTOMY
   • Extravesical non-refluxing reimplant preferred; ureteroureterostomy effective.
   • No data for routine stenting; early removal is safe and may cut infection.


COMPLICATIONS
 • Vascular and rejection complications are covered in Chapter 92. Urological complications are uncommon
   and rarely cost the graft.
 • Thrombosis is one of the commonest causes of early graft loss in children, so some centres anticoagulate
   early, which demands careful haemostasis.
 • Pre-existing obstructive or reflux uropathy increases all urological complications. The link between PUVs
   and graft dysfunction is inconsistent across series. Apply special vigilance and a lower threshold to
   intervene.

Infection
 • Early infections relate to the catheter, stent and instrumentation. Late infection is a relatively frequent,
   delayed complication that reflects bladder function and the original uropathy in an immunosuppressed
   host.
 • High-grade or worsening hydronephrosis → pyelonephritis and functional loss, so evaluate promptly.
   Routine assessment covers emptying, hydronephrosis and selective VCUG.
 • On CIC with no correctable cause → prophylactic antibiotics and bladder irrigation, plus proactive bladder
   management.
 • Graft VUR with recurrent febrile UTI → surgical correction. Reflux without infection and with normal
   bladder function → observe.

Hydronephrosis and Obstruction
 • Hydronephrosis is frequent after transplant and signals obstruction in up to 8%. The graft is unusually
   sensitive to obstruction, and the grade of hydronephrosis does not track the degree of impairment.
 • Rising creatinine and cystatin C often herald obstruction. More than half of obstructions occur within
   100 days, so long-term ultrasound surveillance is needed.
 • Normal pre-transplant bladder → this pattern is ureteric obstruction until proven otherwise. A known or
   suspected bladder problem → evaluate and treat both. Obstruction is commoner with bladder
   abnormality, especially PUVs.
 • Isolated VUR is unlikely to cause progressive hydronephrosis and falling function.

Bladder Dysfunction
 • It raises infection risk and can impair drainage, which harms the graft. It is hard to separate from ureteric
   obstruction, so it needs sequential diagnostic drainage:
      ◦ Catheter for 1–2 weeks → creatinine falls and hydronephrosis improves → bladder is the cause.
      ◦ If not → stent plus catheter and recheck.
 • Treatment: behavioural and medical measures and a new or intensified CIC programme. Augmentation
   rarely, and only after maximal medical treatment. A continent stoma if urethral CIC is difficult.

Stones
 • Uncommon (rates in the table above); risk is higher with a history of stones.
 • With proper management, stones do not increase graft loss but are linked to more UTIs.
 • Bladder stones reported in the past were often on retained suture material.
 • Donor-derived stones: bench flexible ureteroscopy can clear small stones during cold preparation. Adult

 • Any standard modality may be used, but choose the one most likely to succeed in a single intervention,

Concept — Post-transplant urological complications sort by time: leaks early, obstruction within about 100
days, infection late. Nearly all are amplified by the original bladder. That is why every diagnostic pathway
begins with a catheter, which separates bladder from ureter, before anything more invasive.

SUMMARY
 • Success rests on a clear pretransplant bladder strategy, urologist involvement in every case, diligent
   surveillance before damage is irreversible, and multidisciplinary collaboration.
