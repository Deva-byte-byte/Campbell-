Chapter 119 — Genitourinary Complications of Mesh and
Their Repair
     • Mesh is used for male and female stress urinary incontinence (SUI) and female pelvic organ prolapse
       (POP).
     • Its use is among the most controversial subjects in female pelvic medicine and reconstructive surgery,
       the dispute turning on whether the harms of mesh outweigh its benefits.
     • Harms fall into three groups: mesh-related pain; exposure, extrusion or erosion; and damage to adjacent
       structures — nerves, muscles, organs, vasculature — at implantation.
     • The surgical obligation is twofold: selection and counselling before implantation, and diagnosis and
       treatment of complications afterwards.

MESH AND ITS PROPERTIES
Why the pelvic floor is not the abdominal wall
     • Polypropylene has been in surgical use over 50 years; its success against hernia recurrence drove its
       migration into other compartments.
     • The pelvic floor differs from the abdominal wall in three ways that predispose to complications: proximity
       to bladder, urethra, vagina, perineum and rectum; substantial physiological stretch; abundant local
       microbiota.
     • Adverse outcomes: infection, chronic pain, dyspareunia, exposure, shrinkage, perforation into
       surrounding structures, lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS).
     • Advantages over native tissue, cadaveric tissue and xenograft: no morbid harvest, no human or animal
       material and therefore no disease transmission, moderate cost, versatility, durability.

     • The classification is single-variable: pore size alone assigns the type, and integration, infection risk and
       extrusion follow from it.
     • Within type I the contrast is configuration, not polymer: woven polypropylene (Marlex) is among the
       stiffest materials, wrinkles and extrudes readily, whereas knitted polypropylene is softer and more
       compliant, its loose knit giving larger interstices (≈1500 µm against ≈600 µm) and greater elasticity.

     • Mersilene derives bidirectional elasticity from interlinks at each fibre junction yet still extrudes:

Pore size and wound healing
 • The governing principle is a size mismatch between bacteria and host immune cells: bacteria ≈1 µm,
   macrophages and lymphocytes ≈50 µm.
 • Small pores therefore admit bacteria while excluding macrophages and lymphocytes, seeding infection
   where the immune system cannot reach it.
 • Large pores — type I polypropylene, ≈1500 µm — permit fibrocollagenous ingrowth, integrating the
   mesh into local tissue and conferring strength and support.
 • Attempts to improve tissue acceptance by surface modification rather than pore geometry have failed:
   bovine collagen injection (ProteGen) and novel welding (Uratape/ObTape) both produced unacceptable
   rates of mesh exposure and fistula formation.
Long-term biological consequences
 • The cancer hypothesis is disproven. Chronic foreign-body response was held to raise malignancy risk;
   population-based follow-up shows no excess of pelvic or any other cancer.
 • No association with autoimmune disease on long-term follow-up.
 • Histopathology after vaginal mesh placement: moderate vaginal fibrosis in more than half and giant cell
   reaction in almost all, mild inflammation predominating overall.
 • Counterintuitively, extrusion or pain is associated with less inflammation than voiding dysfunction —
   inflammatory intensity does not track the presenting symptom.
Concept — pore size is the single variable from which all mesh behaviour is generated: it decides whether
host immune cells can follow bacteria into the mesh, and whether fibrocollagenous tissue can grow through
it. Every complication profile in the Amid classification is downstream of that one dimension, and every
attempt to buy biocompatibility through surface chemistry rather than pore geometry has failed.
