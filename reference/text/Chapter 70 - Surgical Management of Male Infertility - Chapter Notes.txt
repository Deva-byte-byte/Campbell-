 Chapter 70 — Surgical Management of Male Infertility
   • American Urological Association (AUA) / American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) joint
     guidelines (2021) set evidence-based standards for the male partner of the infertile couple: diagnostic
     testicular biopsy, varicocelectomy, reconstruction for obstructive azoospermia (OA) and indication-
     based sperm retrieval.
   • In vitro fertilisation (IVF) with intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) treats even the most severe
     male factor — unreconstructable obstruction and nonobstructive azoospermia (NOA) — but is costly
     and burdens the female partner: ovarian hyperstimulation, multiple gestation, complications of oocyte
     retrieval.
   • ICSI bypasses all natural biological barriers: realistic concern of transmitting genetic abnormalities, and
     an association with increased birth defects.
   • Specific male treatment — microsurgical reconstruction for OA, varicocelectomy for impaired testicular
     function — in properly selected patients remains the safest and most cost-effective management.
   • Treating the man can upgrade the couple's pathway: donor sperm → IVF/ICSI with testicular
     microdissection → IVF/ICSI with ejaculated sperm or intrauterine insemination (IUI) → natural
     conception.
   • Microsurgical testicular sperm extraction (microTESE) makes retrieval, fertilisation, pregnancy and live
     birth feasible in NOA; retrieval now spans testis, epididymis and seminal vesicle, percutaneous or open.
   • The operating microscope identifies individual seminiferous tubules likely to contain sperm — higher
     TESE success with less morbidity; microfluidics is under investigation to improve yield and efficiency.
   • Varicocele causes progressive, duration-dependent testicular injury. Microsurgical varicocelectomy,
     once reserved for oligospermia, has induced spermatogenesis and returned sperm to the ejaculate in
     some men with NOA.
   • Varicocele also impairs Leydig cell function (lower serum testosterone than age-matched controls);
     repair halts and partly reverses the decline → emerging concept of early repair to prevent future
     infertility and androgen deficiency.
 Concept — Safer microsurgery has moved the urologist from salvaging residual testicular function to
 preventing its loss — and correcting the male factor can move a couple down the ladder of assisted
 reproduction.

 SURGICAL ANATOMY
   • The scrotum is uniquely accessible to examination, imaging and surgery; scrotal examination is a lost
     art — no substitute for history and physical examination.
   • Success rests on selecting the right operation for the correct indication, which requires anatomy
     (Chapter 66) and physiology (Chapter 67).

 Testicular Blood Supply
   • Three sources: testicular (internal spermatic) artery from the aorta — the main supply; deferential
     (vasal) artery from the internal iliac (hypogastric) artery or its superior vesical branch; cremasteric
     (external spermatic) artery from the inferior epigastric artery, chiefly to the tunica vaginalis with some
     testicular branches.
   • Testicular artery diameter exceeds deferential plus cremasteric combined.

   • Iatrogenic risk: inguinal hernia repair, orchidopexy, vasectomy. After inadvertent testicular artery
     ligation collaterals may perfuse, yet atrophy, chronic pain and/or azoospermia may follow in adults
     and children.
   • After vasectomy the vasal artery is presumed compromised — the testicular artery must be preserved
     in any later operation, e.g. varicocelectomy.

 Epididymal Blood Supply
   • Superior and medial epididymal arteries from the testicular artery; cauda (inferior pole) from the
     vasal (deferential) artery.
   • The superior and inferior supplies interconnect extensively: after vasectomy the testicular contribution
     is more than adequate.
   • For vasoepididymostomy or vasovasostomy the epididymis can be dissected off the testis up to the
     caput with the inferior and medial epididymal arteries ligated — safe provided the superior
     epididymal artery is intact; this yields an additional 4–6 cm of length.

 Blood Supply of the Vas Deferens
   • Abdominal (seminal vesicle) end: deferential artery. Testicular end: deferential artery plus inferior
     epididymal interconnections; the two freely anastomose.
   • After vasectomy the testicular end is supplied entirely from testicular/epididymal branches; the
     abdominal end entirely from the deferential artery.
   • The vas receives no supply from cremaster or other cord vessels → if transected or obstructed at two
     sites with vasal vessels interrupted at both, the intervening segment fibroses.
   • Hence never two simultaneous vasovasostomies on one vas; excise the intervening segment and
     perform a single tension-free anastomosis.

 Anatomy of the Excurrent Ducts
    Rete testis (intratesticular, beneath tunica albuginea at the cranial pole, near arterial entry) → 7–11
    efferent ducts → convolute and freely anastomose to form the caput → coalesce at distal caput into a
    single tubule from caput–corpus junction → vas → urethra
   • Injury or ligation distal to the caput obstructs the entire system on that side; a single stitch through a
     corpus or cauda tubule causes complete obstruction.
   • Iatrogenic epididymal injury: hydrocelectomy and spermatocelectomy (long-standing large/tense
     hydrocele splays the epididymis), post-infection operations, orchidopexy for torsion.

 Ejaculatory Ducts
   • Left and right ducts enter the prostatic urethra at the level of the utricle; bilateral ejaculatory duct
     obstruction (EDO) → azoospermia.
   • Causes: congenital (midline cyst), acquired (genitourinary infection), iatrogenic (urethral
     instrumentation).
   • Semen: azoospermia or severe oligospermia, low volume, acidic pH, negative/equivocal/low
     fructose.
   • Transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) shows dilated seminal vesicles and/or ejaculatory ducts; sperm on
     seminal vesicle aspiration in an azoospermic man confirms EDO/atresia.
   • Transurethral resection of the ejaculatory ducts (TURED) relieves obstruction but is not benign: it
     destroys the valve-like mechanism that prevents urine reflux → chemical/bacterial epididymitis.
     Seminal vesiculoscopy is gaining traction to limit this.

   KEY POINTS
    • The testicular artery is the main blood supply to the testis.
     • If the epididymis is injured or ligated distal to the caput, the entire system on that side is obstructed.
     • Suspect bilateral EDO in an azoospermic man with low-volume ejaculate and dilated seminal vesicles
       on imaging.

 Concept — Two anatomical facts generate most of the surgical rules: a single dominant testicular artery
 that must be preserved once the vasal artery is lost, and a single epididymal tubule distal to the caput in
 which one stitch or puncture is enough to obstruct.

 TESTIS BIOPSY

 Indications
   • Indicated in the azoospermic man with normal testicular size and consistency, palpable vasa and
     normal follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) — distinguishes OA from NOA (AUA/ASRM guideline).
   • Cryopreserve at biopsy → avoids a repeat retrieval at the time of IVF.

Chapter 70 — Surgical Management of Male Infertility                                                  TESTIS BIOPSY
   • Biopsy unnecessary: congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens (CBAVD) with normal FSH
     (spermatogenesis always present) before definitive retrieval; highly positive serum antisperm
     antibody with a history of iatrogenic vasal (hernia) or epididymal (hydrocelectomy/
     spermatocelectomy) injury — obstruction almost certain.
   • Diagnostic biopsy is usually bilateral irrespective of size discrepancy.
   • Because ICSI needs only a single sperm, biopsy is diagnostic and potentially therapeutic; even men
     with markedly elevated FSH and small soft testes often harbour rare mature sperm.
   • NOA testes are heterogeneous and testicular sperm motility can be induced → examine fresh unfixed
     tissue for tailed, possibly motile sperm, and multiple samples if initially negative; an andrology
     laboratory must be available to process and cryopreserve.
   • Biopsy alone is rarely indicated — usually planned as TESE.
   • Frozen retrieved sperm do not compromise IVF/ICSI, except in spinal cord injury (SCI), where a fresh
     testicular sperm–ICSI cycle may be better.

 Open Testis Biopsy
   • Open biopsy with TESE is the gold standard — optimal tissue for diagnosis and sperm retrieval.
   • General, spinal or local anaesthesia; local with spermatic cord block gives pain relief, office
     convenience and cost-effectiveness. Loupes or microscope show vessels on the tunica albuginea.
   • Choose the larger testis; with unilateral varicocele or prior varicocelectomy use the contralateral testis.
    Assistant stretches scrotal skin over anterior testis, epididymis posterior → (local) 3–4 cm wheal + cord
    block 5–10 mL → 2 cm transverse incision in skin folds (or 3–4 cm median raphe for bilateral, with two
    separate dartos openings) → skin and dartos, haemostasis → curved haemostat / Jacobson forceps to
    tunica vaginalis, open → Barraquer eyelid retractor → enlarge and deliver testis if anatomy distorted,
    epididymis not palpable posteriorly or tunica albuginea unclear → tunica edges held; wound dry before
    incising albuginea → two 5-0 Vicryl stay sutures parallel to surface vessels → 3–4 mm incision with 15-
    degree microknife → bipolar to crossing vessels → pea-sized sample excised with iris scissors
   • Minimal handling (no forceps) for fixed tissue; fixatives: Bouin's, Zenker's or collidine-buffered
     glutaraldehyde. Never formalin — it distorts testicular histology.
   • Touch prep: blot cut surface on slide + drop of saline, lactated Ringer's or human tubal fluid (HTF) +
     coverslip → high power ± phase contrast shows tailed sperm and motility. Negative → wet squash
     prep (specimen crushed under coverslip). Negative → close and biopsy contralateral testis.
   • Microscope at 10–25× allows selective sampling of larger tubules more likely to contain sperm.
   • Sperm found → slide and extra tissue to andrology for cryopreservation; nonabsorbable suture marks
     the site of spermatogenesis for future TESE.
   • Closure: tunica vaginalis continuous 5-0 Vicryl (limits scarring for future surgery) → dartos running 4-0
     Vicryl → skin 4-0 Monocryl horizontal mattress → bacitracin, fluffs, snug support. No antibiotics.
   • Tips: loupes/microscope and Barraquer retractor; avoid transecting albugineal vessels and keep
     specimen blood-free; preplaced parallel sutures give upward traction and easy closure; close tunica
     vaginalis to prevent albuginea–tissue scarring before repeat biopsy.

 Percutaneous Testis Biopsy
   • 14-gauge prostate biopsy gun — a blind procedure with risk to epididymis or testicular artery; not
     after surgery that has scarred the anatomy. Cord block needed under local.
   • Therapeutically, percutaneous biopsy or aspiration is most useful for fresh retrieval in OA with
     normal spermatogenesis.

 Percutaneous Testicular Aspiration
   • 23-gauge needle or angiocatheter sheath: less invasive and painful than percutaneous biopsy, but few
     tubules with poorly preserved architecture.
   • Flow cytometry distinguishes haploid from diploid cells (late spermatogenesis), but direct wet
     examination for sperm and motility is the practical test; 3–4 aspirations until sperm found.
   • In OA, aspirated sperm serve ICSI when epididymal retrieval fails; fine-needle aspiration yields far
     fewer sperm than microTESE in NOA.

 examination, an on-site laboratory, cryopreservation and a marked site all follow from that.

 Complications of Vasography
   • Stricture: repeated percutaneous sharp-needle attempts, imprecise vasotomy closure, non-water-
     soluble contrast (never use).
   • Injury to the vasal blood supply: vasovasostomy proximal to an injured vasography site → ischaemia,
     necrosis, obstruction of the intervening segment.
   • Haematoma: meticulous bipolar haemostasis in the perivasal sheath.
   • Sperm granuloma: leaky closure → granuloma → stricture/obstruction; closure technique identical to
     vasovasostomy.

 Transrectal Vasography and Seminal Vesiculography
   • Markedly dilated seminal vesicles and/or midline Müllerian duct cyst in OA → TRUS-guided
     aspiration then indigo carmine with contrast; same bowel preparation and antibiotics as TRUS
     prostate biopsy.
   • Sperm in aspirate → at least one vas and epididymis patent → 0.5 mL indigo carmine + 1.5 mL 50%
     water-soluble contrast → resectable lesion → TURED; blue efflux from ducts or unroofed cyst confirms
     adequate resection.
   • Obviates open scrotal vasography for transrectally accessible lesions; TUR can proceed without
     violating the scrotum; aspirated sperm frozen in case surgery fails.

   • No sperm → secondary epididymal obstruction. Simultaneous TURED + vasoepididymostomy is rarely
     successful → EDO with bilateral epididymal obstruction is best managed by sperm retrieval and

 Summary
   • Vasography only if biopsy confirms spermatogenesis consistent with OA.
   • Only at the time of planned reconstruction.
   • Vasal fluid always sampled first to allow cryopreservation.
   • Indigo carmine, not methylene blue, to confirm patency.
   • Formal x-ray vasography only to locate obstruction proximal to the internal inguinal ring.
   • Dilated seminal vesicles or midline cyst → transrectal aspiration with contrast and indigo carmine;
     cryopreserve motile sperm.

   KEY POINTS
    • Perform vasography only at the time of planned reconstruction.

 Concept — Vasography interrogates the downstream tract, so it is justified only once sperm production
 upstream is proven and only when the answer can be acted on in the same sitting; the vasal fluid sample,
 taken first, often answers the question on its own.

 VASOVASOSTOMY
   • About 500,000 vasectomies a year in the United States, stable; divorce rate 40–50%; a small
     proportion ultimately seek reversal and a larger minority express interest in future fertility.
   • Iatrogenic vasal injury, usually from hernia repair, causes OA in a proportion of azoospermic men.

 Preoperative Evaluation
   • Document adequate spermatogenesis: prior natural fertility before vasectomy usually suffices; FSH if
     significant medical change, bilateral small testes or advanced paternal age.

 Anaesthesia
   • General anaesthesia preferred — small movements are magnified by the microscope. Regional or local

     free vas toward internal ring; free testicular end to vasoepididymal junction.
   • Too far for one anastomosis and contralateral testis lacks sperm or is not easily reconstructed → single
     crossed vasovasostomy to create one good system; otherwise aspirate vasal/epididymal sperm into
     micropipettes and cryopreserve.
   • Never simultaneous vasovasostomies at two sites → devascularisation, fibrosis and necrosis of the
     intervening segment.

 Varicocelectomy and Vasovasostomy
   • Proper varicocelectomy ligates all spermatic veins, leaving vasal veins as the only venous return; after
     vasectomy and reversal vasal veins and vasal artery are likely compromised → must preserve the
     testicular artery and some venous return.
   • Microscopic varicocelectomy preserves the artery; deliberate preservation of small cremasteric or
     perivasal veins maintains drainage.
   • In one series, leaving cremasteric and periarterial veins intact gave a clearly higher recurrence than
     standard microsurgical varicocelectomy in non-vasectomised men → these veins drive a significant
     share of recurrences. Another series combined both without preserving them: low recurrence, no
     atrophy.
   • If combined: microscope and testicular artery preservation mandatory. Alternative, especially with a
     young female partner: reconstruct first, assess semen, then varicocelectomy ≥6 months later once
     venous and arterial channels cross the anastomosis — two-stage approach without atrophy or
     recurrence.

 Crossed Vasovasostomy
   • Indications: unilateral inguinal vasal obstruction with contralateral atrophic testis → join healthy
     testis to contralateral unobstructed vas; inguinal vas or ejaculatory duct obstruction/aplasia on one
     side with epididymal obstruction on the other.
   • Principle: one anastomosis with high probability of success beats two operations with low probability
     (e.g. unilateral vasoepididymostomy plus contralateral TURED).
    Vas of atrophic testis transected at straight–convoluted junction; patency confirmed by saline or indigo
    carmine vasogram → contralateral vas dissected toward inguinal obstruction, clamped and transected as
    high as possible with right-angle clamp → testicular end crossed through capacious opening in scrotal
    septum → vasovasostomy as standard
   • Much easier than inguinal vasovasostomy, which requires finding both ends in dense inguinal scar.

 Intra-Abdominal Robotic Vasovasostomy
   • For vas transected above the inguinal ligament, most often during hernia repair in children or adults;
     first reports 2014, robotic and laparoscopic.
   • Insufflation → identify and mobilise vas → modified Prentiss rerouting for length; single-layer 8-0 or
     two-layer 9-0/10-0 anastomosis.
   • Restrictions as for abdominal surgery: no vigorous exercise or lifting >10 lb for 6 weeks; semen
     analysis after 6 weeks.

 Transposition of the Testis
   • Critically short vas → testicular transposition for tension-free crossed anastomosis; the spermatic cord
     is always longer than the vas, so the testis crosses the septum comfortably.

 Wound Closure
   • Drain rarely needed; Penrose from dependent scrotum if extensive dissection, diffuse dartos oozing or
     anticoagulation — placed with care not to disturb anastomosis; patient removes it next morning.
   • Dartos running 3-0 Vicryl incorporating septum; skin running horizontal mattress 5-0 Monocryl —
     midline raphe scar virtually invisible.

   • Fluffs and scrotal support; perioperative antibiotics only; 2-week long-acting non-steroidal anti-
     inflammatory drug (NSAID) such as meloxicam, paracetamol, 3-day opioid supply.
   • Scrotal support 4 weeks. Ejaculation after 2 weeks; intercourse after 4 weeks.
   • Semen analysis at 6 weeks and at 6 months if no conception. Poor motility at 6 weeks → 4-week
     prednisone course, repeat analysis, repeatable once or twice.
   • Azoospermia at 6 months after vasovasostomy → redo vasovasostomy offered.

 Postoperative Complications
   • Most common: haematoma — rare, small, walnut-sized, perivasal; none needed drainage; resolve in
     6–12 weeks. Wound infection did not occur.
   • Anastomotic sperm granuloma — usually a harbinger of eventual obstruction.
   • Late stricture and obstruction are disappointingly common: progressive loss of motility followed by
     falling counts signals stricture.
   • Late obstruction reduced by nylon instead of Prolene, the microdot system, cutting back to healthy
     tissue, blood supply preservation and scrotal support until pregnancy.
   • Cryopreserve semen as soon as motile sperm appear in the ejaculate.

 Long-Term Follow-Up Evaluation After Vasovasostomy
   • With sperm in the vasal fluid on at least one side, this technique returns sperm to the ejaculate in
     virtually all patients.
   • Pregnancy is achieved in about half of couples, more when female factors are excluded; outcome
     depends on interval since vasectomy and female partner age.

   KEY POINTS
    • The vas deferens should not be stripped of its sheath.
     • For a very large gap, gain length by freeing the convoluted vas from the epididymal tunica and by
       blunt dissection to the external ring; the convoluted vas should not be unravelled.
     • Success relies on accurate mucosa-to-mucosa approximation, a tension-free and leakproof
       anastomosis, good blood supply, healthy mucosa and muscularis, and atraumatic technique.
     • The ReVas technique creates a reinforcing loop of perivasal tissue, giving a tension-free anastomosis
       and higher success.

 Concept — An anastomosis between two tubes without clotting factors is only as good as its tissue,
 tension and mucosal sutures — every step, from preserving the sheath to recutting gritty muscle to the
 ReVas loop, serves those three.

 SURGERY OF THE EPIDIDYMIS
   • Sperm acquire motility and fertilising capacity along a single, tightly coiled tubule of about 200 µm
     diameter and 12–15 feet length.
   • After vasoepididymostomy even short epididymal lengths adapt; improvement may continue for up to
     2 years — yet preserving the greatest functional length gives the best sperm.
   • The wall is thinnest at the caput and thickens distally with more smooth muscle → distal anastomoses
     are technically easier and more successful.
   • Corpus and cauda are one tubule → injury anywhere obstructs at that level; magnification is essential
     — loupes for macrodissection, microscope for anastomosis.

 Long-Term Follow-Up Evaluation and Results
   • In experienced hands sperm appear in the ejaculate in the majority; intussusception patency exceeds
     that of classic end-to-side/end-to-end.
   • Pregnancy is higher the more distal the anastomosis, regardless of technique.
   • Older methods: a substantial minority of initially patent anastomoses shut down late; with
     intussusception late shutdown appears lower, but long-term data are lacking.
   • Bank sperm intraoperatively and as soon as they appear in the ejaculate, whatever the technique;
     banked sperm serve ICSI if counts stay poor or azoospermia persists.
   • Persistent azoospermia without banked sperm → redo vasoepididymostomy and/or sperm retrieval
     with IVF/ICSI.

 TRANSURETHRAL RESECTION OF THE EJACULATORY DUCTS
   • EDO is usually congenital — the opposite end of the spectrum of excurrent duct anomalies that begins
     with CBAVD and absence of most of the epididymis; atresia confined to the terminal vas/ejaculatory
     duct is correctable by transurethral resection (TUR).
   • Acquired: prior urethral instrumentation, prior genitourinary infection, extrinsic compression by
     prostatic or seminal vesicle cysts. Higher ejaculatory duct pressures have been measured directly in
     EDO.

   • Suspect EDO in the azoospermic or severely oligo- and/or asthenospermic man with at least one
     palpable vas, low volume, acidic pH and negative/equivocal/low fructose; entertained if FSH normal
     and biopsy shows normal spermatogenesis (workup in Chapter 69).
   • Digital rectal examination (DRE) may feel a midline cyst; TRUS is key to both diagnosis and treatment

 VARICOCELECTOMY
   • The most commonly performed operation for male infertility. Varicocele: about 15% of men, 35%
     with primary infertility, 75–81% with secondary infertility.

Chapter 70 — Surgical Management of Male Infertility                                              VARICOCELECTOMY
   • Associated with a progressive, duration-dependent decline in testicular function.
   • Repair halts further damage, improves spermatogenesis in many, improves live birth and IVF/ICSI
     outcomes and enhances Leydig cell function.
   • The urologist's preventive role demands a technique that minimises complications and recurrence.

 Scrotal Approach
   • Scrotal drainage has deep and superficial networks; the deep network drains testis, epididymis and
     vas into the pampiniform plexus, intimately entwined with the testicular artery until it becomes a
     single testicular vein above the internal ring.
   • The more distal the approach, the smaller the veins and the greater the risk to the artery; arterial
     injury defeats the purpose — atrophy, impaired spermatogenesis, chronic pain.
   • Choice of approach is usually by surgeon preference and patient age.

 Retroperitoneal Approach
   • Incision at the level of the internal inguinal ring, external and internal obliques split, internal
     spermatic artery and vein exposed retroperitoneally near the ureter (Palomo approach).
   • Advantages: proximal, near drainage into the left renal vein; only one or two large veins; artery not
     yet branched and often separate; fewest veins ligated; still common in children.
   • High recurrence when the artery is preserved, especially in children and adolescents — failure from
     periarterial venae comitantes communicating with larger veins; less often parallel inguinal or
     retroperitoneal collaterals bypassing the ligation; cremasteric and scrotal collaterals cannot be
     reached.
   • Artery (1.0–1.5 mm) hard to identify, especially in children; work is in a deep hole with vessels
     ligated in situ; lymphatics hard to preserve → hydrocele.
   • In children/adolescents, intentional testicular artery ligation markedly reduces recurrence in
     children/adolescents (takes the periarterial veins) and reverses growth failure — but benefit to later
     spermatogenesis is uncertain and unlikely; in adults bilateral artery ligation has caused azoospermia
     and atrophy.

 Laparoscopic Varicocelectomy
   • In essence a retroperitoneal approach — same level as Palomo, similar pros and cons; recurrence
     from collaterals near or entering the renal vein.
   • Magnification shows internal spermatic vessels and vas at the internal ring; artery preserved in most,
     lymphatics with experience; recurrence low in recent series but variable.
   • Artery ligation with lymphatic sparing markedly reduces hydrocele in children.
   • Complications more serious than open (bowel, vessel or visceral injury, air embolism, peritonitis) and
     general anaesthesia required; microsurgery needs local/regional and a 2–3 cm incision, no larger
     than the sum of port incisions; pain and recovery equal to subinguinal.
   • Reasonable for bilateral varicoceles in experienced hands.

 Microsurgical Inguinal and Subinguinal Operations: The Preferred Approaches
   • Subinguinal microsurgical varicocelectomy is the most popular: cord delivered so artery, lymphatics
     and small periarterial veins are seen; access to external spermatic and gubernacular veins; access to
     testis for biopsy, epididymal inspection or hydrocele repair.
   • Conventional (non-magnified) inguinal: 5 cm incision over canal, external oblique opened, cord
     delivered, internal spermatic veins ligated, vas and its vessels preserved, artery and lymphatics

     sought, external spermatic veins ligated. Lowers recurrence versus retroperitoneal but not hydrocele
     or artery injury.
   • Post-varicocelectomy hydrocele is due to lymphatic ligation (fluid analysis). Artery injury is probably
     under-reported; bilateral → azoospermia. Transplant series: dividing the cord and sparing only vas
     and vasal vessels → frequent atrophy and hydrocele.
   • Microsurgery substantially reduces hydrocele (lymphatics seen) and allows preservation of the small
     testicular artery, avoiding atrophy and azoospermia.
   • The claim that deferential and cremasteric arteries suffice is refuted: testicular artery diameter
     exceeds both combined; one-stage Fowler–Stephens orchidopexy (artery deliberately ligated) often

 Anaesthesia
   • Local 0.25% liposomal bupivacaine + oral sedation (oxycodone, midazolam); regional or light general

   • Skin and subcutaneous infiltration, then cord infiltrated proximally to avoid haematoma at the
     dissection site; blind cord block carries a small arterial risk → 30-gauge needle.

     more veins, artery more often wrapped in a venous network and already divided into 2–3 branches,

 Results
   • Infertility with abnormal semen parameters → consider surgical varicocelectomy (AUA/ASRM
     guideline). Semen improves in most; pregnancy rates vary widely by series.
   • Madgar trial (1995) — randomised controlled trial of surgery versus no surgery: pregnancy at 1 year
     44% versus 10%.
   • Large microsurgical series: high pregnancy rates by 1–2 years, higher when female factors excluded.
   • NOA: no definitive evidence for varicocelectomy before assisted reproduction (AUA/ASRM); data are
     small uncontrolled series, and some NOA men show sperm on later analysis without intervention —
     interpret with caution.
   • Large varicoceles improve more after repair but start worse → pregnancy rates similar regardless of
     size.
   • Younger age at repair → greater improvement and recovery.
   • Recurrence, artery ligation and postoperative hydrocele predict poor results.
   • In infertile men with low testosterone, microsurgical varicocelectomy alone substantially raises
     serum testosterone.

 Summary
   • Varicocele is common and causes progressive, duration-dependent injury; larger varicoceles do more
     damage and gain more from repair; earlier repair recovers more; repair improves Leydig function,
     live birth and IVF/ICSI outcomes.
   • The main complications — hydrocele, artery injury, persistence/recurrence — are reduced by
     microsurgery, an inguinal or subinguinal route and exposure of external spermatic and scrotal veins.

   KEY POINTS
    • Early repair of varicoceles prevents both future infertility and Leydig cell dysfunction.
     • In select patients varicocelectomy may treat symptomatic, age-related androgen deficiency.
     • The micro-Doppler is extremely useful in identifying arteries.
     • Microsurgery through an inguinal or subinguinal approach decreases recurrence and hydrocele.
     • As long as one set of deferential veins remains intact, venous return will be adequate.
     • At completion only testicular arteries, cremasteric arteries, lymphatics and the vas with its vessels
       remain.
     • Postoperative hydrocele is a direct result of failure to preserve lymphatic drainage.

 Concept — Each complication maps to a structure mishandled — recurrence = a vein missed, hydrocele =
 a lymphatic tied, atrophy = an artery tied; the level of approach trades fewer veins against a less visible
 artery, and microsurgery with testicular delivery is the only approach that addresses all three.
