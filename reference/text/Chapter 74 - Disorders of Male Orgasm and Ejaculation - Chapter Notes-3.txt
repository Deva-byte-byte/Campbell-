Chapter 74 — Disorders of Male Orgasm and Ejaculation
Chris G. McMahon · Ege Can Serefoglu

 • Ejaculatory dysfunction (EjD) is the most common male sexual disorder.
 • Spectrum: premature ejaculation (PE) → delayed ejaculation (DE) → complete inability to ejaculate
   (anejaculation); also retrograde ejaculation (RE), painful ejaculation, ejaculatory anhedonia and the
   recently described postorgasmic illness syndrome (POIS).
 • Sexual response cycle (Masters and Johnson): desire → arousal → orgasm → resolution. Arousal rising to
   a threshold triggers the ejaculatory response, which usually terminates the male sexual episode.
 • Orgasm is a distinct cortical event — the perception, via pelvic sensory neurons, of striated muscle
   contraction and semen expulsion, experienced both cognitively and emotionally.
 • Ejaculatory latency is a continuum (onset of penile stimulation → ejaculation), varying between and
   within men. Most ejaculate after several minutes and are satisfied; some ejaculate very rapidly, even
   before penetration; others only with great difficulty or not at all.
 • RE: pharmacological, anatomical or neurological factors that prevent bladder neck contraction.
 • Painful ejaculation (dysejaculation, odynorgasmia, postorgasmic pain, dysorgasmia, orgasmalgia) is
   prevalent and commonly misdiagnosed and mistreated.
 • Haemospermia (blood in semen) alarms patients but is typically benign and self-limiting.

THE ANATOMY AND PHYSIOLOGY OF THE EJACULATORY RESPONSE
 • Reflex arc: sensory receptors and areas → afferent pathways → cerebral sensory areas → cerebral motor
   centres → spinal motor centres → efferent pathways.
 • The brain circuitry of ejaculation is part of a wider network controlling the whole sexual response.
 • Neurochemistry: a complex interplay of central serotonergic and dopaminergic neurons, with secondary
   cholinergic, adrenergic, oxytocinergic and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) involvement.
 • Periphery: synergistic activation of autonomic (sympathetic and parasympathetic) and somatic divisions;
   nonadrenergic noncholinergic (NANC) innervation may modulate the accessory sex glands.
 • Five groups of spinal nuclei (thoracolumbar and lumbosacral) give the autonomic and somatic efferents;
   their coordination is by lumbar interneurons at L3–L5 — the spinal generator of ejaculation. This spinal
   network is strongly influenced by stimulating or inhibiting genital sensory and supraspinal inputs.
 • Brain nuclei activated during ejaculation: posteromedial bed nucleus of the stria terminalis,
   posterodorsal medial amygdala, posterodorsal preoptic nucleus and parvicellular subparafascicular
   thalamus.

 • Receptor pair (rat data): 5-HT2C agonism → delays ejaculation; postsynaptic 5-HT1A stimulation →

PREMATURE EJACULATION
Classification of Premature Ejaculation
 • Historical types B and A (1943) → renamed (1989) lifelong (primary) and acquired (secondary); later
   descriptors: global versus situational, substance-related.
 • Lifelong PE: early ejaculation at nearly every intercourse — mostly 30–60 s, a minority 1–2 min — with
   every partner, from the first encounters.
 • Acquired PE: develops after previously normal ejaculation, often situational; distinguished by time of
   onset and reduction from a previously normal latency.
 • Normative intravaginal ejaculatory latency time (IELT) data: IELT under 1 min is rare, yet many men with
   normal IELT complain of PE → the Waldinger–Schweitzer four-subtype classification, by IELT, complaint
   frequency and course in life.
 • Confirmed in Turkish and Chinese cohorts: the complaint of PE affects roughly one-fifth to one-quarter of
   sexually active men; subtype order variable > subjective > acquired > lifelong.
 • Shared dimensions of lifelong and acquired PE (lack of control, negative personal consequences) justified
   a single unifying definition, possibly to be extended later to variable and subjective PE.

Definition of Premature Ejaculation
 • Research depends on the definition used. The literature holds many univariate and multivariate
   definitions, each built from some of: ejaculatory latency, perceived control, reduced sexual satisfaction,
   personal distress, partner distress, interpersonal distress.
 • All the earlier definitions were authority-based — none supported by evidence-based clinical research.

The International Society for Sexual Medicine Definition of Premature Ejaculation
 • Enabled by the objective IELT plus validated patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures.
 • First International Society for Sexual Medicine (ISSM) ad hoc committee, October 2007 → first evidence-
   based definition of lifelong PE (2008). All three criteria required:
   (1) ejaculation always or nearly always before or within about 1 min of vaginal penetration; (2) inability
   to delay on all or nearly all penetrations; (3) negative personal consequences — distress, bother,
   frustration, avoidance of intimacy.
 • The committee could not define acquired PE — insufficient data.
 • Second committee, Bangalore, April 2013: lifelong and acquired PE are distinct populations but share the
   three constructs → unified definition; the added key dimension of acquired PE is a clinically significant
   and bothersome reduction in latency, often to ≤ about 3 min.
 • First evidence-based definition of both; the basis for office diagnosis and for trial design.
 • Limited to vaginal intercourse — few data in men who have sex with men or other sexual expression.
 • Deliberate conservatism with flexibility: the 1-min cut-off is not absolute (a minority of treatment-
   seeking lifelong PE have IELT 1–2 min); "about 1 minute" allows diagnosis up to 90 s; the acquired ≤3-min
   threshold is applied with clinical judgement.
 • Latency alone never suffices: short latency with adequate control and no negative consequences is not
   PE.
Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Definition of Premature Ejaculation
 • Built on the same data as ISSM; the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition
   (DSM-5, 2013) now has an objective latency criterion: ejaculation in partnered activity within
   approximately 1 min of vaginal penetration and before the individual wishes, for ≥6 months, on 75–100%
   of occasions, with clinically significant distress.
 • Specifiers: lifelong or acquired; generalised or situational.
 • Severity: mild 30 s–1 min; moderate 15–30 s; severe before or at the start of sexual activity, or <15 s.
American Urological Association/Sexual Medicine Society of North America Definition of Premature
Ejaculation
 • Disorders of Ejaculation panel formed 2018 by the AUA with the Sexual Medicine Society of North
   America (SMSNA); systematic review with an independent methodology team.
 • Critique of ISSM: a substantial minority of treatment-seekers ejaculate after 60 s; the cut-off based on
   two standard deviations from the mean latency is statistically valid but arbitrary, unnecessarily
   conservative and clinically dubious as regards bother and control; the original data lacked non-PE
   comparison groups and independent criterion validation.
 • Supporting data: men selected on poor control have median and mean latencies above 1 min; men at 60–
   120 s report distress like those under 60 s, but unlike those over 120 s.

 • Lifelong PE (AUA): poor control + bother + ejaculation within about 2 min of initiation of penetrative sex,
   present since sexual debut. Extending to 120 s raises true positives (fewer false negatives, type 2 error) at

 • Acquired PE (AUA): consistently poor control + bother + latency markedly reduced from prior experience

   reduction of ≥50% from before; acknowledged to lack evidence.

Concept — Every contemporary definition is the same three-construct test — latency, control, consequences;

Prevalence of Premature Ejaculation
 • Community estimates vary widely; PE is often called the most common self-reported male sexual
   disorder.
 • Disparity between self-report or poorly validated definitions and stopwatch data.
 • Community stopwatch IELT: distribution positively skewed, median 5.4 min (range 0.55–44.1 min), falls
   with age, varies between countries; IELT ≤1 min is statistically abnormal.
 • Self-report prevalence is appreciably higher than clinician diagnosis using the conservative ISSM
   definition.
 • Global Study of Sexual Attitudes and Behaviours (GSSAB), age 40–80: high self-reported prevalence;
   perception of "normal" latency varied by country and between patient and partner; limitation — no men
   under 40. The Premature Ejaculation Prevalence and Attitude (PEPA) survey (age 18–70) likewise found
   high self-reported prevalence.
 • Treatment-seekers: series differ on whether lifelong or acquired predominates, but lifelong and acquired
   PE make up most patients seeking treatment, whereas variable and subjective PE make up most of the
   community and seldom seek treatment.
 • Acquired PE is uncommon in the community but its sufferers are more likely to seek (and plan to seek)
   treatment than those with lifelong PE.

 • Historical biological and psychological theories are mostly speculative.
 • Lifelong and acquired PE share short latency, poor control and negative consequences, but remain distinct

 • PE is more prevalent with metabolic syndrome.
 • Circumcision: two systematic analyses show no difference in PE incidence.
Premature Ejaculation and Sexual Performance Anxiety, Psychological or Relationship Problems
 • Anxiety is entrenched in sexual-medicine folklore as the likeliest cause, with scant empirical support.
 • Proposed mechanism: anxiety → sympathetic activation → earlier emission → lower ejaculatory
   threshold.
 • Bidirectional with desire: hypoactive desire may drive PE (unconscious wish to shorten unwanted
   penetration); chronic frustrating PE may in turn reduce desire.
 • Partner dysfunction — anorgasmia, hypoactive desire, aversion, arousal disorder, vaginismus — may
   relate to acquired PE.
Premature Ejaculation and Comorbid Erectile Dysfunction
 • As many as half of men with ED also have PE.
 • Mechanisms: need for higher stimulation to achieve an erection → deliberate "rushing" to beat
   detumescence of a partial erection → brief latency; compounded by ED-related performance anxiety.
 • Caution: potent men with PE often confuse maintaining an erection before versus after ejaculation,
   answer the Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM) inconsistently (especially questions 3 and 4) and
   receive a false-positive ED diagnosis.
Premature Ejaculation and Prostate Disease
 • Acute and chronic lower urogenital infection, prostatodynia and chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain
   syndrome (CP/CPPS) are associated with ED, PE and painful ejaculation; PE is the main sexual symptom
   in CP/CPPS.
 • Mechanism unknown — inflammation altering sensation and reflex modulation is hypothesised, without
   convincing evidence.
 • Antibiotic treatment of microbiologically confirmed bacterial prostatitis in acquired PE → longer IELT
   and better control.
Premature Ejaculation and Hyperthyroidism
 • Most patients with thyroid disorders have sexual dysfunction; PE correlates with suppressed thyroid-
   stimulating hormone (TSH).
 • Normalising thyroid function markedly reduces PE.

Evaluation of Men Complaining of Premature Ejaculation
Medical History
 • Full medical and sexual history + focused examination + erectile function inventory + investigations
   suggested by these.
 • Partner involvement is important but not mandatory: uninvolved partners may resist change; a
   cooperative partner builds confidence, skills, self-esteem and control, improving the whole relationship.
   No controlled PE studies; ED data show interpersonal focus is critical.
 • Patients expect clinicians to ask but are often embarrassed; asking gives permission and screens
   associated risks (cardiovascular disease, ED).

Assessment of Erectile Function
 • Assess comorbid ED with a validated instrument — International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) or IIEF-5
   (SHIM).
 • Normal erectile function: IIEF erectile function domain ≥26; IIEF-5 ≥22.
 • ED is much commoner in men with PE than without (raised odds across all regions in GSSAB); more
   prevalent in acquired than lifelong PE; PE becomes more common as ED severity rises, after age
   adjustment.

Treatment of Premature Ejaculation
 • Subtype-directed: lifelong → pharmacotherapy alone or with graded patient and couple psychosexual
   therapy; acquired → aetiology-specific treatment (psychosexual counselling, ED pharmacotherapy) ± PE
   pharmacotherapy; variable and subjective → psychosexual education and graded psychotherapy.
 • Lifestyle: PE is associated with ED, metabolic syndrome, sedentary lifestyle, alcohol and BMI → counsel
   more exercise and better overall lifestyle.

Psychosexual Therapy
 • Every man seeking treatment should receive basic psychosexual education or coaching: prevalence and
   population latency data to dispel myths; enjoyable activities to broaden the couple's repertoire; strategies
   for sexual avoidance and reluctance to discuss sex.
 • Aim: confidence to try medical treatment, less performance anxiety, modified maladaptive sexual scripts.

Pharmacological Treatment
 • Options: topical local anaesthetics, SSRIs, tramadol, phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (PDE5Is), α-
   adrenergic blockers.
 • Topical anaesthetics (lidocaine, prilocaine, benzocaine, alone or combined) to reduce glans sensitivity are
   the oldest pharmacological treatment (1943).
 • SSRIs (paroxetine, sertraline, fluoxetine, citalopram) and the tricyclic antidepressant (TCA) clomipramine
   revolutionised treatment: block 5-HT transporter reuptake → more synaptic 5-HT → postsynaptic 5-HT
   receptor stimulation.

Dapoxetine
 • Approved in over 50 countries; not approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA).
 • Rapid-acting SSRI with a short half-life → suited to on-demand use.
 • No drug–drug interactions reported, including with PDE5Is.
 • 30 or 60 mg, 1–2 h before intercourse (trial dosing; Table 74.3 gives 1–3 h): better than placebo from the
   first dose — longer IELT, more control, less distress, more satisfaction (randomised controlled trials, RCTs).
 • Equally effective in lifelong and acquired PE; effective and tolerated in PE with ED on PDE5Is.
 • Adverse effects uncommon and dose-dependent: nausea, diarrhoea, headache, dizziness; fewer than
   with daily SSRIs.
 • No increased suicidality; little withdrawal on abrupt cessation.
 • Real-world discontinuation is very high within a year — cost and loss of spontaneity; satisfaction tracks
   response.
Off-Label SSRIs and TCAs
 • Daily dosing of paroxetine, clomipramine, sertraline, fluoxetine or citalopram usually delays ejaculation
   (doses in Table 74.3).
 • Paroxetine exerts the strongest delay (meta-analysis).
 • Onset 5–10 days; full effect 2–3 weeks; sustained long-term.
 • Adverse effects usually minor, begin in week 1, fade over 2–3 weeks: fatigue, yawning, mild nausea,
   diarrhoea, sweating. Reduced libido and ED anecdotally less frequent in non-depressed men with PE.
 • Agitation and hypomania in a few → avoid SSRIs with a history of bipolar depression.
 • Platelet serotonin matters for haemostasis → upper gastrointestinal bleeding risk, especially with aspirin
   or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).
 • Priapism — rare; urgent treatment.
 • Long-term: weight gain and increased type 2 diabetes risk.
 • Semen: sperm DNA fragmentation and altered semen parameters → possible fertility impact; relevant
   because most treatment-seekers are in their mid-30s.
 • Suicidality: a small increase in youth, not adults, in depression/anxiety trials; not seen in non-depressed
   men with PE → caution in under-18s and in comorbid depression, especially with suicidal ideation.
 • Avoid abrupt cessation or rapid reduction of daily SSRIs → SSRI withdrawal syndrome.

Topical Local Anaesthetics
 • Lidocaine and/or prilocaine cream, gel or spray — well established, moderately effective.
 • Mechanism: reduced glans sensitivity may inhibit the spinal ejaculatory reflex arc.
 • Drawbacks: penile hypoaesthesia; transvaginal absorption → vaginal numbness and female anorgasmia
   unless a condom is used.
 • Fortacin: aerosolised eutectic lidocaine–prilocaine metered spray; penetrates the glans mucosa but not
   keratinised shaft skin; European Medicines Agency approval 2014.
 • Two phase III placebo-controlled trials: applied at least 5 min before intercourse → marked IELT increase
   with better control, satisfaction and distress scores; minimal hypoaesthesia or partner transfer.
 • ISSM: level 1a evidence for off-label on-demand topical anaesthetics in lifelong PE.
 • The majority stop effective topical treatment (loss of spontaneity); long-term data scarce.
PDE5 Inhibitors
 • EAU recommends off-label on-demand or daily PDE5Is for PE with or without ED, alone or with PE
   pharmacotherapy.
 • Rationale speculative: the NO/cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) system mediates inhibitory
   NANC nitrergic transmission in the urogenital tract.
 • Early systematic reviews: no robust evidence except PE with comorbid ED.
 • Well-designed RCT of on-demand vardenafil in potent men with lifelong PE: large rise in geometric mean
   IELT and better Index of Premature Ejaculation domains (control, confidence, satisfaction, distress).
 • Meta-analyses of RCTs: PDE5I > placebo; PDE5I + SSRI > SSRI alone.
 • Current guidelines therefore recommend PDE5Is with or without ED, alone or with SSRIs.
 • Online text only: tramadol, α1-adrenoceptor antagonists, intracavernosal injection, investigational drugs,
   acupuncture, surgical neurotomy, cryoablation and dorsal penile nerve neuromodulation.

 KEY POINTS ON PREMATURE EJACULATION
   • PE is a common sexual dysfunction.
   • PE carries negative psychological consequences — distress, bother, frustration — affecting quality of
     life (QoL), relationships, self-esteem and self-confidence, and can obstruct single men forming new
     relationships.
   • The evidence-based ISSM definition of lifelong and acquired PE should form the basis of office
     diagnosis.
   • Limited evidence suggests lifelong PE has a genetic basis; acquired PE is most often due to
     performance anxiety, psychological or relationship problems and/or ED.
   • Oral SSRIs, topical anaesthetics and PDE5Is are effective and safe.
   • Psychosexual cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) has a limited first-line role in lifelong PE but an
     important adjunctive role, especially in acquired PE from performance anxiety.
   • Acquired PE secondary to ED, hyperthyroidism, chronic lower urogenital infection, prostatodynia or
     CPPS needs etiology-specific treatment alone or with an SSRI.

Concept — PE pharmacotherapy is serotonergic at its core; everything else is peripheral desensitisation
(topicals) or treatment of a co-driver (PDE5Is for ED), and the practical limiter is adherence, not efficacy.

Definition, Terminology and Characteristics of Men With Delayed Ejaculation
 • DE, RE and inhibited ejaculation (IE) are probably the least common, least studied, least reported and
   least understood male sexual dysfunctions — yet they deny fulfilment to both partners, worse when
   procreation is a goal.
 • Range: varying delay → complete inability; reduced volume, force and sensation may accompany.
 • Extremes: anejaculation (time) and RE (direction); DE is commoner.
 • Anorgasmia: perceived absence of orgasm or sexual anhedonia, independent of whether the
   physiological events of ejaculation occur.
Terminology and Definition
 • Synonyms used interchangeably: DE, inadequate ejaculation, IE, idiopathic anejaculation, primary
   impotentia ejaculationis, psychogenic anejaculation. IE, if distinguished, means complete absence (no
   consensus). DE is the preferred umbrella term for any delay or absence.

Treatment of Male Pelvic Cancers
 • Sexual function and QoL are now central outcomes; modern surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy

Prostate cancer
 • Commonest non-skin malignancy in Western men; external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), brachytherapy and

 • Climacturia is less frequent after EBRT than after RP; decreased orgasmic sensation occurs after both.

 • Aetiology-specific; address infertility in men of reproductive age; psychoeducation and/or psychosexual
   therapy, pharmacotherapy or integrated treatment.
 • Before pelvic surgery, counsel reproductive-age couples about anejaculatory infertility and the availability

 • Behavioural: low risk — alternative practices, scripts, sexual enhancement devices; at minimum, open
   discussion of needs.
 • Lifestyle: more time together for intimacy, minimal alcohol, sex when not tired, maximal penile
   stimulation, pelvic floor training.
 • Drugs — limited success, all off-label: central dopaminergic, anti-serotonergic or oxytocinergic, or
   peripheral adrenergic. None approved; most have limited efficacy, significant side effects or are

 • Bladder neck reconstruction — results consistently poor.

Postorgasmic Illness Syndrome
 • Recently described, poorly characterised "orphan" disease: severe myalgia, fatigue and a flu-like state
   within 30 min of orgasm, with debilitating physical and psychological effects.

CONCLUSIONS
 • Epidemiological and observational research has clarified PE and its negative psychosocial effects; the
   multivariate, evidence-based ISSM definition gives a more discriminating tool and should be the basis of
   office diagnosis of lifelong PE.
 • Aetiology: limited evidence for a genetic basis of lifelong PE; acquired PE is mostly performance anxiety,
   psychological or relationship problems and/or ED, less often CP/CPPS or hyperthyroidism.
 • Treatment: psychosexual CBT has a limited role; oral SSRIs and topical anaesthetics are effective and safe;
   dapoxetine, despite a modest effect, has a place that market forces will settle once regulatory approval is
   met. Tramadol, intracavernosal injection and alternative delivery methods cannot be recommended
   pending large well-designed RCTs.
 • DE and anejaculation become commoner with age, have manifold organic and psychogenic causes, affect
   both partners and may cause infertility; their treatment is among the most significant challenges in
   sexual medicine, with often disappointing outcomes.
