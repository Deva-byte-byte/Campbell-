Chapter 153 — Epidemiology, Aetiology and Prevention of
Prostate Cancer
EPIDEMIOLOGY AND AETIOLOGY
 • Long natural history, often decades; risk factors may act at different stages of carcinogenesis (initiation →
   promotion → progression), which makes them hard to identify in population studies.
 • The optimal end point is unclear. In most cancers, diagnosis is the relevant end point; in prostate cancer it
   may not be.
 • Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening is itself one of the strongest risk factors for diagnosis since the
   late 1980s/early 1990s; factors that predict access to care and health-seeking behaviour therefore predict
   "risk".
 • "Incident" cancer is rarely truly incident: diagnosis designates a cancer present for years. Screening
   timing and intensity alter the spectrum of cases, the point in the natural history and the age at diagnosis.
   Of two men with similar latent cancers, only the one who is screened and biopsied is diagnosed.
 • Consequence: PSA-era studies may identify determinants of diagnosis rather than aetiological factors.
 • Preferred end point: aggressive disease — advanced stage at diagnosis, or progression to metastasis or
   death. Risk factors may act only on cancers able to progress, which are the clinically important ones.
 • Lifestyle evidence is almost entirely observational (diet, physical activity, body weight, alcohol, tobacco)
   and cannot prove causality. Causality is judged against criteria set, ideally, before analysis: consistency
   across diverse groups, adjustment for confounders, freedom from bias (especially detection bias),
   magnitude, biological plausibility.
 • Few studies test change in lifestyle, so even for causal factors it is unknown at what age risk is reversible;
   observational work should establish when in life a factor operates.

Incidence, Prevalence and Mortality of Prostate Cancer
 • Second commonest non-skin cancer and fifth leading cause of cancer death in males worldwide.
 • 2020 (185 countries): ~1.4 million cases and 375,000 deaths — 7.3% of all cancer cases, 3.8% of cancer
   deaths.
 • Global age-standardised incidence rate (ASR) 31/100,000; lifetime cumulative risk 3.9%.
 • First for incidence in over half of countries (112/185); second cause of cancer death in 48 countries,
   after lung cancer.
 • By Human Development Index (HDI): ASR 37.5 in high/very-high HDI countries (e.g. Switzerland, United
   States) vs 11.3 in low HDI (e.g. Niger, Yemen).
 • Incidence varies ~13-fold (6.3 South Central Asia → 83.4 Northern Europe); mortality ~9-fold (3.1 South
   Central Asia → 27.9 Caribbean).
 • Since 2000, incidence is rising in 65 countries, stable in 25 and falling in 45.
 • Drivers of variation: diagnostic practice and PSA use, reporting quality and absent registries, heritability,
   environment.

Europe
 • Commonest cancer in Europe and third leading cause of cancer death.
 • Overall ASR 63; Northern 83.4, Western 77.6, Southern 59.1, Eastern 46.4 — variation attributed mainly to
   diagnostic practice.
 • Incidence highest in Northern/Western and lowest in Eastern European countries; mortality highest in
   Northern Europe.
 • 2020: ~108,000 deaths; mortality ASR 11.1. Mortality has fallen ~7% since 2015 with therapeutic
   advances — Poland the only country with rising mortality.

North America
 • United States, Canada and Mexico combined: ASR incidence 73, mortality 8.3.
United States.
 • Commonest solid tumour in males. Data principally from the National Cancer Institute's Surveillance,
   Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) programme, population-based since 1975.
 • Incidence rising ~3%/year (2014–2019) after two decades of decline.
 • 2023: ~288,300 cases and 34,700 deaths; 29% of all male cancer diagnoses.
 • Lifetime risk ~1 in 8 (12.6%). Probability by age: 0.2% <50 → 1.8% at 50–59 → 5.2% at 60–69 → 9.2% at
   ≥70.
 • ASR incidence 72, mortality 8.2 (2020).
 • 5-year survival 97%, yet second leading cause of cancer death (11% of cancer deaths).
 • Metastatic presentation rising since 2012, especially in males under 70; burden projected to rise 42% by
   2025.
 • Black males: prostate cancer is 37% of cancer diagnoses and 17% of cancer deaths. Disparity with White
   males is multifactorial, including unequal access to care.
Canada.
 • Commonest solid tumour in males; lifetime risk ~1 in 9; 20% of new male cancers; 5-year survival 91%;
   ASR incidence 80, mortality 8.9.

 • Incidence rose 1969–2010 with peaks in 1993 (PSA introduced) and 2011 (updated screening
   recommendations), then fell; it kept rising under 70 and fell at ≥70.
Mexico.
 • Global Burden of Disease 2019: ASR incidence 52.3, mortality 19.4.

Latin America and the Caribbean
 • Heterogeneous rates, mostly from differences in screening and reporting; trend data limited.
 • South America: ASR incidence 62.5, mortality 22.0. Incidence rising in Colombia, Ecuador and Costa Rica,
   stable in Brazil. Mortality rising in Brazil, Colombia and Ecuador; falling in Argentina, Costa Rica and Chile;
   lowest in El Salvador, Bolivia and Costa Rica.
 • Caribbean: third-highest incidence (75.8) and highest mortality in the world (27.9). Highest national
   incidence: Martinique (182.0) and Guadeloupe (176.6); also Jamaica, Barbados, Tobago. Highest
   mortality: Barbados, Jamaica, Haiti.
 • Afro-Caribbean males have more aggressive disease and worse outcomes — socioeconomic constraint,
   later-stage diagnosis and possibly genetics.

Hispanic Ethnicity
 • United States Hispanic/Latino males present with more advanced disease, higher grade and higher PSA
   than non-Hispanic Whites, yet have lower prostate cancer mortality.
 • Hispanic migrants have lower prostate cancer mortality than both non-Hispanic Whites and United States-
   born Hispanics.
 • "Hispanic paradox": comparable or better outcomes despite disparities in access and socioeconomic
   status.

Africa
 • ~93,000 cases and ~47,000 deaths — a high death-to-case ratio.
 • ASR: Southern 65.9 > Middle 40.8 > Western 33.1 > Northern 16.6.
 • Variation from screening, medical access, registry completeness and lifestyle. PSA screening uncommon
   in sub-Saharan Africa (single-digit percentages, e.g. Ghana, Senegal).
 • Westernisation of diet and lifestyle, with rising obesity, is a proposed contributor.

Asia
 • Historically lowest incidence: ASR 14; ~371,000 cases (2020). South Central 6.3 < South-Eastern 13.5 <
   Eastern 16.8 < Western 28.6.
 • Highest national incidence: Japan, Israel, Armenia. Incidence rising — fastest in the Republic of Korea (up
   to 13.8%/year) — from PSA screening and better biopsy technique.
 • Mortality lowest of all regions (ASR 4.4); highest in Armenia, Georgia, Turkey; rising in the Philippines,
   Korea, Thailand, China and Iran — obesity, Western diet, inactivity and better reporting.
 • Migrant effect: risk in native Asians is one-third lower than in Asian Americans — less screening in Asia
   plus Western diet and lifestyle.

Oceania
 • Australia/New Zealand: ASR 75.8 (level with the Caribbean, among the highest worldwide); mortality
   10.3.
 • Polynesia: lower incidence (56.5) but higher mortality (18.8).
 • Highest incidence in the region: New Caledonia.

 KEY POINTS: EPIDEMIOLOGY AND AETIOLOGY
   • Long natural history (decades) makes epidemiological study difficult.
   • Lifestyle evidence is observational and cannot prove causality.
   • Incidence and mortality vary substantially by region; first for incidence in 112/185 countries, second
     cause of cancer death in 48.


PROSTATE-SPECIFIC ANTIGEN SCREENING
 • PSA is made almost exclusively by the prostate and reaches blood by leakage, so anything that increases
   leakage raises PSA: benign enlargement, inflammation and cancer.
 • Mainstay of early detection since the mid-to-late 1980s; still controversial. Whether a man is screened has
   a large effect on his measured risk and mortality.

Impact on Incidence
 • PSA screening is one of the strongest predictors of detection.
 • Best measured in the European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer (ERSPC): >182,000
   men randomised to PSA every 2–4 years (by country) vs no screening.
 • Excess incidence in the screened arm shrinks with time as controls are diagnosed later, at a later stage;
   unrecorded screening in controls may add to the erosion.

 • Pattern: the incidence excess decays while the mortality benefit builds and then plateaus — benefit is
   time-dependent.
 • Intention-to-screen underestimates benefit: where ITT showed 20%, correcting for non-attendance gave
   27%, and also for contamination 31%.
 • Bottom line: screening vs none reduces prostate cancer death by ~20–30%. Most deaths are therefore
   not preventable by PSA alone — better screening, treatment and primary prevention are needed.

Impact on Prostate Cancer Epidemiology
 • Prevention requires first understanding the factors linked with risk.
 • "In a population with widespread PSA screening, a diagnosis of prostate cancer signifies, in most cases,
   that a man happened to have a PSA test at that time."
 • Healthy-user confounding: men with healthy habits (diet, exercise, non-smoking, lean) also seek PSA
   tests. Unadjusted, healthy behaviours appear to raise incidence yet lower mortality. This likely
   confounds much of the risk-factor literature of recent decades, forcing reliance on the few randomised
   trials.

Impact on Prevalence
 • Incidence and prevalence estimates are biased by changing screening practice; autopsy studies of latent
   cancer are less biased.
 • Latent prevalence rises steeply with age: ~5% <30 years → 15% at 40–50 → 22% at 50–59 → 29% at 60–
   69 → 59% >79.
 • Race: at 70–79, latent prevalence ~36% in White vs ~51% in Black males.
 • Stable over time globally, but rising in Asia, notably Japan.
 • Limitations: study quality, few autopsies, poor standardisation — more high-quality, diverse studies
   needed.
Concept — PSA screening inflates and advances incidence and confounds every lifestyle association;
mortality, aggressive disease and autopsy prevalence are the end points that escape its bias.

 KEY POINTS: PROSTATE-SPECIFIC ANTIGEN SCREENING
   • Benign enlargement, inflammation and cancer all raise PSA.
   • PSA screening is one of the strongest predictors of detection and reduces prostate cancer death by
     ~20–30%.
   • Unadjusted, healthy behaviours appear to raise incidence but lower mortality.


RISK FACTORS
 • Multifactorial cause. Established risk factors: advanced age, race and family history.

Age
 • ~60% diagnosed at ≥65; mean age at diagnosis 66. Risk low <40, rising steadily thereafter.
 • Historical (1974–1994) probability of diagnosis: 0.005% at ≤39, 2.2% at 40–59, 13.7% at 60–79.

 • Rising incidence in adolescents and young adults (aged 15–39): ~2%/year from 1990 to 2017, across all
   continents.
 • 55% of prostate cancer deaths occur at ≥65. Over 20 years of follow-up, the risk of dying of prostate
   cancer is higher when diagnosed at ≥70 than below 70.
 • An ageing population → growing burden and survivorship needs.

Race
 • Highest incidence in Black males (172.6/100,000 in the United States).
 • Prostate cancer-specific mortality twice that of White males — more metastatic presentation and
   younger age at diagnosis.
 • Drivers: structural racism, regional segregation, unequal access; these also raise comorbidity
   (cardiovascular disease, diabetes) linked with poor cancer outcomes.
 • Genetics: 8q24 risk alleles, identified in genome-wide association studies (GWAS), are more common in
   African ancestry. Genomic testing can identify aggressive subsets for targeted intervention.
 • Equal access → equal outcome: with similar access and treatment, Black patients with non-metastatic
   disease have prostate cancer-specific mortality similar to White patients.
 • Autopsy studies show higher latent cancer rates from very early ages.

Family History
 • ~20% of patients have a family history.
 • First-degree relative (father or brother) → 2- to 3-fold risk, rising with the number of affected relatives
   and with younger age at their diagnosis.

Shared Lifestyle
 • Familial clustering may partly reflect shared diet, activity, screening behaviour, smoking, alcohol and
   environmental toxins.
 • Family history drives PSA testing in relatives, but a Swedish study concluded familial aggregation is not
   substantially due to shared screening.
 • Shared modifiable factors are targets for family and community interventions.

Genetics
 • Genetics outweigh shared lifestyle: heritability up to 58% (twin studies).
 • BRCA1/BRCA2 (hereditary breast–ovarian cancer genes) → aggressive prostate cancer.
 • HOXB13 (homeobox B13) — prostate-specific; hereditary prostate cancer.

 • PCGEM1 — prostate-specific; overexpressed in most tumours. DD3 — marker for early detection.

Homologous Recombination DNA Repair
 • Germline DNA-repair mutations (BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, CHEK2, PALB2) → higher risk and more
   aggressive cancer.
 • Clinical uses: targeted early screening (BRCA1/2, ATM, mismatch repair genes, HOXB13, CHEK2); caution
   in active surveillance for BRCA1/2 and ATM carriers; treatment selection in advanced disease — androgen
   receptor (AR)-targeted agents, poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors (BRCA1/2, ATM), immune
   checkpoint inhibitors (mismatch repair).

Mismatch Repair Genes
 • Mismatch repair (MMR) mutations maintain genome stability; uncommon in prostate cancer (2–5%), so
   evidence is limited.
 • MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2 (Lynch syndrome) are associated with prostate cancer.
 • MMR-deficient metastatic disease shows high sensitivity to androgen deprivation therapy (ADT).

Single-Nucleotide Polymorphisms
 • GWAS have found >200 common single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), each of low-to-moderate
   effect.
 • Strongly linked with progression: HSD3B1 (hydroxy-delta-5-steroid dehydrogenase; also susceptibility),
   CYP19A1, HSD17B4. SNPs in KLK3 (the PSA gene) are linked with risk.

Genomic Regions
 • Key loci 8q24 and 17q21, important for risk differences across ancestry.
 • 8q24: more common in Black individuals; also associated in European populations. 17q21: present in 4–
   7% of those of African descent vs <1% of non-African populations.
 • Asia: ERG oncoprotein-positive cancer is uncommon in East Asian males; FOXA1 mutated more often in
   Chinese males.
 • Multi-ancestry GWAS meta-analysis: 86 new variants; risk scores differ across European, African and Asian
   ancestry.

Polygenic Risk Scores
 • Polygenic risk scores (PRS) combine risk alleles into a personalised risk to guide screening decisions.
 • They predict whether prostate cancer develops, not whether it is aggressive.
 • A multi-ethnic PRS stratifies risk in European and African ancestry. PRS for aggressive disease and survival,
   and trials of clinical utility, are needed.
Concept — Age, ancestry and family history are the only unequivocal risk factors, and genetics carries most
familial risk; the germline repair genes that matter clinically predict aggressive cancer, which is why they
change screening and treatment while polygenic scores, which predict only any cancer, do not yet.

 KEY POINTS: RISK FACTORS
   • Established risk factors: advanced age, race and family history.
   • Germline homologous recombination mutations (BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, CHEK2, PALB2) raise risk and
     aggressiveness.
   • MMR mutations (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2; Lynch syndrome) are associated with prostate cancer.


POSSIBLE RISK FACTORS
 • Body composition (height, obesity, waist circumference) and comorbidities; findings often contradictory.
   Diet and activity are covered separately.

Height
 • Taller height → higher risk, supported by recent studies and meta-analyses; ~5% higher risk per 10-cm
   increment, with estimates varying by measurement and population.
 • Association is with high-grade disease and prostate cancer death, not low-grade disease (including in the
   European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition, EPIC).
 • Height reflects childhood/adolescent hormones and diet; taller height predicts progression in Black but
   not White males.

 • Around diagnosis: weight loss is protective; weight gain raises recurrence after prostatectomy.
 • Waist circumference (abdominal obesity): positive for advanced disease in a few studies; null in the
   Health Professionals Follow-up Study (HPFS); inverse for total and localised disease and null for advanced
   disease and mortality in Swedish cohorts.
 • Mechanisms: insulin/insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), sex hormones, adipokine signalling (Fig. 153.2).
 • ~650 million obese adults worldwide and rising — the link with aggressive disease is a public-health
   concern.

Comorbidities Possibly Associated With Prostate Cancer
 • Metabolic syndrome, hyperlipidaemia, hypertension and cardiac disease are associated with higher risk;
   diabetes with lower. Older patients commonly carry several, compounding diagnosis and outcome.

Metabolic Syndrome
 • Insulin-resistance syndrome; cluster raising cardiovascular and diabetes risk; prevalence ~12–31% of
   adults.
 • Mechanisms: altered insulin and IGF-1; sex-steroid shift — ↑ oestradiol, ↓ sex hormone-binding
   globulin (SHBG), ↓ free testosterone (Fig. 153.2).
 • Meta-analyses (heterogeneous): slight association with incidence and with high-grade disease; significant
   in cross-sectional studies but not in pooled cohort and case–control studies.
 • Linked with worse features: higher Gleason score, positive surgical margins, biochemical failure.
 • Geography: positive in European studies, not in United States or Asian studies.

Hypertension
 • Affects ~one-third of adults; leading modifiable cardiovascular risk factor; linked with inflammation.
 • Increased prostate cancer risk; in a large cohort, men aged ≥45 with systolic ≥150 mmHg vs <130 mmHg
   had higher incidence and, once diagnosed, higher mortality.
 • Meta-analyses significant but heterogeneous.

Cardiovascular Disease
 • Leading cause of death, even in prostate cancer patients. Shared biology (inflammation, atherosclerosis,
   oxidative stress, hormones, cholesterol) and shared risk factors (age, race, diet, activity, alcohol). Patients
   with cardiovascular disease are more often diagnosed.
 • ADT and cardiovascular disease:

 KEY POINTS: POSSIBLE RISK FACTORS
   • Taller height is associated with higher risk.
   • Obesity: mixed for diagnosis, consistent for aggressive disease.
   • Metabolic syndrome, hyperlipidaemia, hypertension and cardiac disease raise risk; diabetes lowers it.
   • Observational data link ADT with cardiovascular disease.


DIET
1. Population evidence: international variation, secular trends within populations and rising rates in
   migrants from low- to high-incidence countries imply external (non-genetic) factors. Per capita fat intake
   correlates with national rates — hypothesis-generating only, as inactivity and PSA screening co-vary with
   diet.
2. Biological plausibility: food carcinogens formed in processing/cooking damage DNA; some compounds
   induce enzymes that activate or deactivate carcinogens; oxidative DNA damage is increased by
   polyunsaturated fat and excess iron and opposed by antioxidants; B vitamins support DNA synthesis,
   repair and methylation; non-mutagenic routes act through insulin and IGFs.
 • Methodological challenges: measuring intake over decades; many levels of analysis (patterns,
   macronutrients, foods, micronutrients, phytochemicals); foods act in combination; correlated intakes
   (meat with potatoes; fortified milk supplies vitamin D, calcium and phosphorus together); fat-soluble
   compounds (vitamin D, lycopene) need fat co-ingestion — vitamin D in fortified skim milk is poorly
   absorbed compared with whole milk.
 • Randomised trials are most rigorous but rare, test single micronutrients and have been mostly null — the
   Selenium and Vitamin E Cancer Prevention Trial (SELECT) (selenium 200 µg, vitamin E 400 IU) showed no
   benefit. A single high-dose micronutrient is unlikely to show a population effect; whole-diet change is
   more plausible but hard to sustain.
 • Prospective cohorts (tens of thousands followed for years) are feasible but confounded; causality is
   judged by consistency, dose–response, confounder control and plausibility.

 KEY POINTS: DIET
   • International variation implies non-genetic factors; dietary factors could act at many steps of
     pathogenesis.
   • Causal inference is difficult, and large randomised nutritional trials have generally been null.

 • A priori: Healthy Eating Index–2005, Alternative Healthy Eating Index–2010 and alternative
   Mediterranean score — no association with total, advanced or fatal cancer; Mediterranean meta-analysis
   null. No strong support.
 • A posteriori: no association for healthy or unhealthy patterns; too few studies of vegetarian variants.
 • Plant-based: vegan diet associated with lower risk in a large Adventist cohort; reviews inconsistent
   (definitions vary). In HPFS, a higher plant-based index → lower fatal prostate cancer, driven more by
   avoiding animal foods than by plant foods themselves.
 • Early evidence: diets lowering insulin and inflammation may lower risk.

Macronutrients
 • Fat: pre-PSA-era studies (Seventh-day Adventists; HPFS) linked animal fat with risk; PSA-era studies (EPIC;
   meta-analysis of cohorts) are null for total, saturated and unsaturated fat and for total or advanced
   disease. WCRF/AICR 2018: fat, protein and carbohydrate "limited/no conclusion".
 • Carbohydrate: sugar-sweetened beverages → increased risk; glycaemic index/load null.
 • Dairy: the non-fat milk fraction (protein, minerals) raises IGF-1, a risk factor.

 • Among fruits and vegetables, only tomato products were associated with lower risk; focus fell on

 • Effect confined to processed/cooked tomato, especially sauce: lycopene in raw tomato is tightly bound
   and lipophilic — bioavailable only when cooked in oil.

 • Pooled analysis of 15 studies: serum lycopene inverse with advanced cancer. Evidence for lycopene

Coffee Consumption
 • Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory compounds. Meta-analysis of cohorts: highest intake → modestly
   lower risk, stronger for advanced and fatal disease, in the highest-quality studies and with fuller
   adjustment (smoking, BMI, PSA screening).
 • HPFS: inverse association with advanced disease for both regular and decaffeinated coffee → non-
   caffeine components responsible.

Alcohol
 • Raises risk of at least seven cancers; acetaldehyde is an established carcinogen. WCRF/AICR 2018:
   "limited–no conclusion" for prostate cancer.
 • Case–control plus cohort meta-analysis: dose–response with prostate cancer mortality, raised at ≥45 g/
   day ethanol (>3–4 standard drinks); a cohort-only meta-analysis found no consistent association.
 • Beverage type: liquor raises risk at lower doses than wine, which does so only at high doses; red-wine
   polyphenols may offset ethanol at low intake.

PHYSICAL ACTIVITY AND SEDENTARY BEHAVIOUR
 • A probable protective factor for several cancers; unclear for prostate. Plausible via insulin signalling,
   inflammation, immunity and weight control. WCRF/AICR: no clear conclusion for incidence.
 • Limitations: measurement error; differing effects of light, moderate and vigorous activity and of leisure vs
   occupational activity; differing effects by subtype (advanced vs organ-confined).
 • 2018 meta-analysis: no clear association with total cancer; regular recreational activity → lower
   advanced/aggressive cancer (only two studies).
 • HPFS: vigorous (not moderate) activity → lower advanced (not total) cancer, after accounting for PSA
   screening; repeated 2-yearly measurements improved accuracy.
 • Swedish cohort (minimal PSA screening): lifetime occupational activity and walking/cycling → lower total
   cancer.

 • Sedentary behaviour may raise aggressive cancer risk, mediated by adiposity.
Concept — Consistent, long-term, vigorous activity is linked with fewer clinically significant cancers; as with

COMPOSITE LIFESTYLE
 • A composite approach outperforms any single factor.
 • HPFS and Physicians' Health Study (PHS): a healthy-lifestyle score (smoking, activity, body weight, diet) →
   30–50% lower lethal prostate cancer.
 • HPFS: adherence to smoking, activity, weight and alcohol guidelines could prevent 21% of fatal cancers in
   the cohort and 40% in United States White patients.
 • Highest polygenic-risk quartile: lifetime lethal risk 5.3% with unhealthy vs 1.6% with healthy lifestyle →
   genetic predisposition is not deterministic.
Concept — Genes set the baseline, but lifestyle modifies lethality even in the highest genetic stratum.

 KEY POINTS: COMPOSITE LIFESTYLE
   • A composite lifestyle approach is more effective than any single factor in reducing incidence or
     mortality.


SUPPLEMENTS, VITAMINS AND MINERALS
 • Use is high: over half of United States adults (and about a third of children) in the past 30 days; lower but
   substantial elsewhere (e.g. South Korea).
 • No strong evidence of benefit for healthy people eating a balanced diet. United States Preventive
   Services Task Force (USPSTF): little or no benefit for cancer, cardiovascular disease or death, except a
   small benefit in cancer incidence with multivitamins.
 • For prostate cancer: no consistent benefit, and some harm. Where large trials in prostate cancer patients
   exist (secondary prevention), they are noted.

Vitamin D
 • Prostate cancer cells and normal prostate express the vitamin D receptor; vitamin D induces apoptosis in
   vitro.
 • Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D meta-analyses conflict; one study found a U-shaped relationship (high and
   low levels both linked with risk).
 • No large prostate-prevention trial; pooled trials show no effect on overall cancer. Vitamin D and Omega-3
   Trial (VITAL): >25,000 randomised to 2000 IU vitamin D3 daily vs placebo for 5 years — no significant
   effect on prostate cancer (192 vs 219 cases, non-significant); significantly fewer metastatic or fatal
   cancers overall, which merits confirmation.
 • In prostate cancer patients, six randomised trials: no effect on PSA or mortality.

Selenium
 • Selenised-yeast trial: primary end point (skin cancer) null, but a large secondary reduction in prostate
   cancer → prompted SELECT.
 • SELECT: 35,533 men; selenium, vitamin E, both, or placebo; planned 7–12 years; stopped early (median
   5.46 years) for futility. Selenium alone and with vitamin E: no benefit (hazard ratios >1).
 • Counter-arguments: wrong form of selenium; earlier protective signals only in smokers.

Vitamin C
 • Very limited data: an early meta-analysis found dietary vitamin C inversely associated; later studies refute

 • At high doses vitamin C may act as a pro-oxidant; supplemental antioxidants above the median →
   increased risk, including advanced and high-grade disease.

Vitamin K
 • Reduces tumour growth preclinically; human observational studies show no association.

Calcium
 • Bone component and intracellular signal. Often co-prescribed with vitamin D for ADT-induced
   osteoporosis — so its link with risk matters clinically.
 • Meta-analyses: dietary and supplemental calcium → increased total (possibly advanced) cancer; another
   found dairy (not non-dairy or supplemental calcium) linked with incidence, but supplemental calcium
   linked with fatal disease. Phosphorus may be the dairy component linked with fatal cancer. Serum
   calcium: mixed.
 • Verdict: no evidence of benefit; use calcium supplements cautiously in those at risk.

Zinc
 • Prostate zinc levels are very high relative to the rest of the body; most "prostate health" supplements are
   high in zinc.
 • Meta-analysis: no significant relationship, with a suggestion of harm at higher intake. HPFS: supplemental
   zinc ≥75 mg/day or for ≥15 years → higher lethal and high-grade cancer.
 • Verdict: avoid prolonged high-dose zinc.

Phytochemicals
 • Naturally occurring plant molecules; plausible given plant-rich diets. None has shown benefit in a large
   randomised prevention trial; several tested in patients with mixed results.

Phytoestrogens
 • Oestrogen-like phytochemicals; of interest because prostate cancer is androgen-dependent.

Soy.
 • Whole food (protein, milk, fibre); can be pro- or anti-oestrogenic. Low Asian risk suggested soy, but many
   lifestyle factors differ.
 • Meta-analyses: soy, genistein, daidzein and fermented soy → lower risk; an update confirmed soy food
   (not isoflavones).
 • Enthusiasm dampened by a randomised trial: soy protein did not prevent recurrence in high-risk disease.

Flaxseed
 • Rich in fibre, lignans and ω-3; reduced prostate cancer in engineered mice. No risk data; a pre-
   prostatectomy trial showed reduced tumour Ki-67 (proliferation marker), without follow-up studies.

Curcumin
 • Principal component of turmeric (ginger family); potent in vitro but poorly absorbed and rapidly
   metabolised. Large literature, much of it recent; no population data. Most trials in patients: no PSA effect;
   the positive ones combined curcumin with other herbs.

Ellagitannins
 • Found in pomegranate; the chapter groups green tea with them. Ellagitannins are converted by the gut
   microbiome into urolithins, which are absorbed and purportedly anticancer. Studied mainly in patients,
   not primary prevention.
 • Pomegranate: single-arm study slowed PSA rise in biochemical recurrence → placebo-controlled trial
   failed to confirm; no tumour effect before prostatectomy (higher urolithin A → less oxidative stress); on
   active surveillance no effect on PSA, IGF-1 or PSA doubling time, but lower oxidative stress and AR
   expression.
 • Green tea: in high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (HGPIN) or atypical small acinar proliferation
   (ASAP), trial meta-analysis showed a large reduction in progression to cancer; observationally, a linear
   dose–response significant only beyond 7 cups/day — impractical; extracts need study.

Lycopene
 • Systematic review: over half of lycopene–cancer studies concern the prostate. Dietary studies mostly
   positive (11 of 20); interventional studies mostly null (4 of 5).
 • In animals, tomato powder, but not lycopene, inhibited cancer → whole-food benefit may not reduce to
   one agent.
 • Fat-soluble: an ultra-low-fat diet, even plant-based, may deliver little lycopene — food combinations
   matter.

Resveratrol
 • Stilbenoid polyphenol (peanuts, blueberries, cranberries, grape skin, red wine). Mixed preclinical data,
   including harm; poor solubility and bioavailability; 4 months had no effect on PSA in healthy men.

ASSORTED RISK FACTORS

Transgender Identity
 • Transgender females (assigned male at birth) retain the prostate after gender-affirming surgery and
   remain at risk.
 • Claims-based and chart-review studies: incidence significantly lower than in cisgender males.
 • Those on gender-affirming hormone therapy show the most aggressive markers (higher PSA density,
   more high-grade disease) — delayed diagnosis or selection of androgen-deprivation-resistant, more
   aggressive clones.
 • Reasons: unawareness that a prostate is present, lower screening, oestrogen suppression of cancer
   development, and false reassurance from "normal" PSA despite castrate testosterone.
 • Median PSA 0.02 ng/mL on hormones vs 1 ng/mL in cisgender males (fiftyfold lower) → PSA cut-points
   must be adjusted.

 KEY POINTS: TRANSGENDER IDENTITY
   • Incidence appears significantly lower in transgender females than in cisgender males.


Vasectomy
 • Detection bias: urologists who perform vasectomy also screen for prostate cancer.
 • Meta-analysis: modest overall excess; after PSA adjustment, only a very small excess of localised cancer
   and no link with high-grade, advanced or lethal disease.
 • American Urological Association (AUA): clinicians need not routinely discuss prostate cancer in pre-
   vasectomy counselling — vasectomy is not a risk factor.

Sunlight
 • Sunlight converts 7-dehydrocholesterol → previtamin D, activated in liver and kidney (possibly also the
   prostate).
 • Darker skin absorbs less ultraviolet radiation → lower vitamin D at equal exposure — a proposed
   contributor to Black–White disparities.
 • Meta-analysis: highest vs lowest sun exposure → significantly lower risk (moderate exposure non-
   significant); no aggressiveness data; weigh against skin cancer.

Ejaculation Frequency
 • Higher frequency → lower risk (proposed clearance of infectious insults, less inflammation); "lower-risk"
   thresholds range from ≥4/month to ≥21/month.
 • Gene expression in benign prostate, not tumour, varies with frequency.
 • Alternative: conditions limiting ejaculation may be causal — frequency then an indirect marker.

Sexually Transmitted Infections
 • Most sexually transmitted infections (gonorrhoea, chlamydia) classically do not affect the prostate but
   may spike PSA. Not a major clinical factor; a meta-analysis suggests STIs overall, and gonorrhoea
   specifically, may raise risk.

Human Papillomavirus
 • Human papillomavirus (HPV) is linked with cervical, head and neck, anal and penile cancer.
 • Meta-analysis: modest (~28%) increase, with heterogeneity and publication bias; contrast ~95% of
   cervical cancer attributable to HPV. Given prostate cancer's frequency, even a modest real effect would
   matter.

Chronic Inflammation
 • Inflammatory infiltrates are near-universal: in a prevention-trial biopsy series most men had chronic
   inflammation, with or without cancer.
 • Inflammation raises PSA → spurious detection-driven link.
 • Genetic, molecular and pathological evidence nonetheless supports a causal link → rationale for anti-
   inflammatory chemoprevention.

Chronic Prostatitis
 • Clinical pain syndrome affecting urinary and sexual function; episodic, usually without an identifiable
   organism. Meta-analysis shows increased risk, but PSA detection bias leaves the implication unclear.

Sleep
 • Bidirectional relationship with health. Insomnia → modest increase in risk (>200,000 people); metabolic
   disturbance is a plausible mediator — recommend good sleep regardless.
 • More sleep disturbance among Black males (shift work, discrimination, stress, noisy neighbourhoods) may
   contribute to their higher risk.

Shift Work
 • International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC)/World Health Organization (WHO) 2007: circadian-
   disrupting shift work "probably carcinogenic to humans" — based mainly on breast cancer.
 • Circadian disruption enhances carcinogenesis in animals; night work inverts the clock, but daylight still
   entrains it → incomplete sleep — a model for sleep effects.
 • Some studies link shift work with aggressive disease; meta-analysis evidence weak.

PRIMARY PREVENTION

Drugs
 • High prevalence and morbidity make prostate cancer a prime chemoprevention target, yet no agent is
   approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) despite several large, often positive,
   randomised trials.

Chapter 153 — Epidemiology, Aetiology and Prevention of Prostate Cancer                                   Primary Prevention
 • The bar has moved from preventing total to preventing aggressive cancer; no agent has met it in phase
   III. Prostate cancer death is rare in healthy men, making benefit hard to show.
 • Proposal: target high-risk cohorts and mechanism-matched patients — but predictive biomarkers are
   lacking, or so clear that withholding the drug is unethical (a statin in hypercholesterolaemia).
 • Effort has therefore shifted to secondary prevention (delaying progression), largely beyond this chapter.

5α-Reductase Inhibitors
 • 5α-Reductase inhibitors (5ARIs) — finasteride, dutasteride — block testosterone → dihydrotestosterone
   (DHT); in enlarged prostates they shrink volume ~25% and lower PSA ~50%.
 • Rationale: androgen dependence; androgen–risk links; 46,XY infants with 5α-reductase deficiency have
   only rudimentary prostates.
 • The best-studied and most controversial preventive drugs.

SECONDARY PREVENTION
 • High prevalence and long natural history shift interest from primary prevention (avoiding diagnosis) to
   secondary prevention (avoiding progression). Drugs are covered elsewhere; natural agents and lifestyle
   here.
 • Prostate Cancer Lifestyle Trial: 93 men on active surveillance (Gleason <7, PSA 4–10 ng/mL); 1-year

 • Hard end points (metastasis, prostate cancer death) come mainly from observational studies.

Physical Activity
 • Cohort meta-analysis: higher recreational activity → ~31% lower prostate cancer-specific mortality.
 • Reverse causation (progressing cancer limits activity) addressed by a 4–6-year lag; results persisted,
   strongest for vigorous activity.

   matter; wine polyphenols may be anti-inflammatory.

Anti-inflammatory
 • Given inflammation's proposed role, several anti-inflammatory approaches have been tested against
   progression.

Pomi-T
 • Polyphenol-rich whole-food capsule: pomegranate, green tea, curcumin (turmeric), broccoli. 6-month
   placebo-controlled trial in 203 men on active surveillance/watchful waiting or with biochemical
   recurrence: significantly smaller PSA rise in both groups; well tolerated (slight, non-significant bloating,
   flatulence, loose stools).

Pomegranate
 • Single-arm slowing of PSA rise not confirmed by the placebo-controlled trial → no strong evidence,
   despite wide use in biochemical recurrence.

SUMMARY
 • Very common, especially subclinically (autopsy) → anything that increases screening increases diagnosis,
   hindering identification of true risk factors.
 • Age, family history and race are unequivocal; family history acts through genetics and shared lifestyle.
 • Increasingly strong evidence for obesity and smoking, especially for lethal disease.
 • Diet probably matters, but the ideal anti-prostate cancer diet remains elusive.
 • No drug is approved or widely used for prevention.
