Chapter 164 — Treatment of Locally Advanced Prostate
Cancer
 • At least 10% of new diagnoses are locally advanced (T3, NX or N+, M0) despite PSA-driven stage
   migration; the proportion has fallen slightly but has been broadly constant for a decade.
 • These men, with those presenting with metastases, account for a disproportionate share of prostate
   cancer deaths.
 • No consensus on optimal management. Unlike low-grade localised disease, where several modalities do
   equally well, any single modality carries a significant risk of recurrence → interest in combination
   therapy, better risk assessment and imaging.
 • Competing evidence: several analyses favour radical prostatectomy (RP) for disease-specific mortality and
   metastasis in higher-risk men; other data favour external-beam radiation therapy (EBRT) + brachytherapy
   boost + androgen deprivation (AD).

DEFINITION
 • Traditional clinical definition (digital rectal examination, DRE): extracapsular spread (cT3a), seminal
   vesicles (cT3b), adjacent organs (cT4).
 • PSA era: most cancers are non-palpable (cT1c), so PSA, Gleason and imaging, not T stage, now identify
   risk of failure.
 • Working definition: "high-risk" disease, including regional or nodal spread without distant metastasis
   (T3–4, N0 or N+, M0).

Contemporary Risk Assessment
 • Combined variables beat single ones for predicting pathological stage; many "organ-confined" tumours
   are more advanced. Knowing likely stage improves counselling, choice of therapy and trial selection.
 • Key pathological predictors after RP: Gleason score, margin status, non-organ-confined disease
   (extracapsular extension [ECE], seminal vesicle invasion [SVI], nodes).
 • Biochemical disease-free survival (BDFS; PSA-free survival) is the usual endpoint but only an
   intermediate surrogate; timing of recurrence and PSA kinetics refine risk.
 • Recurrence reflects unrecognised micrometastases or persistent locoregional disease.
 • Low risk does well; intermediate and high risk are heterogeneous and need finer nomograms.

 KEY POINTS: RISK ASSESSMENT
   • Risk assessment is best performed by combining serum PSA, T stage, cancer grade and extent of
     cancer on biopsy.
   • Imaging has had a limited role in identifying high-risk men in whom local therapy may fail, but newer
     modalities will change this.


Imaging Modalities
 • Grey-scale transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS) is no better than DRE for ECE or SVI; early high accuracy
   was not reproduced. It understages because of interobserver variability, subtle signs and today's lower-
   volume tumours. Directed biopsy of the seminal vesicles or capsule can confirm cT3.
 • Colour/power Doppler and micro-ultrasound (29 MHz) are under investigation.
 • Endorectal and multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) may separate T2a/b from T3; its staging role and the need
   for the coil remain controversial. ECE accuracy varies widely (sensitivity 13–91%, specificity 49–97%) with
   interpretation, so adopt PI-RADS v2 structured reporting.
 • At 3 tesla, the PI-RADS v2 score next to the neurovascular bundle has poor specificity, so it cannot by
   itself decide nerve preservation.
 • MRI is best reserved for higher-risk men (more than three positive cores, abnormal DRE, PSA >10 ng/mL),
   where its specificity for pT3 is high.
 • Positron emission tomography (PET) tracers approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration: ¹⁸F-
   fluciclovine for biochemical recurrence; ¹⁸F-sodium fluoride for bone.
 • Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) PET detects limited nodal disease and oligometastases. For
   pelvic nodes, ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11 specificity is very high (≥95%) but sensitivity only moderate (~40–66%).
   Whether it replaces standard imaging is debated.
 • proPSMA (high-risk men; 30% had nodal or distant metastases): PSMA PET–computed tomography (CT)
   accuracy 92% vs 65% for conventional imaging (sensitivity 85%, specificity 98%), with lower radiation,
   excellent interobserver agreement, fewer equivocal findings and cost-effectiveness.

Novel Markers
 • Clinical and pathological parameters predict local extent poorly. Comparative genomic hybridisation
   aberrations discriminate organ-confined from locally advanced tumours (Table 164.1); a stepwise six-
   aberration model was highly accurate.
 • TMPRSS2–ETS rearrangements (androgen-regulated) → higher pathological stage; sarcosine (glycine
   metabolite) raised in metastatic disease.
 • CALGB 90203 profiling (high-risk localised): frequent TMPRSS2–ERG fusion, TP53, PTEN, FOXA1, SPOP
   alterations but no androgen receptor (AR) amplification or mutation; marked genomic and immune-
   infiltrate heterogeneity; Wnt signalling correlated with immune suppression.
 • Commercial genomic classifiers (Decipher, Oncotype DX, Prolaris) are applied variably to refine risk (NCCN
   2023).
 • After RP with adverse pathology, genomics identifies higher disease-specific mortality and may guide
   adjuvant therapy. Unresolved: how to use these tests and whether they improve outcomes.

TRENDS IN INCIDENCE AND TREATMENT
 • CaPSURE (Cancer of the Prostate Strategic Urologic Research Endeavor): D'Amico high-risk presentation
   fell from about 41% (1989–1990) to 15% (2001–2002), driven by PSA screening (far fewer PSA >20 ng/mL);
   clinical T3–T4 fell from about 12% to 3.5%. High Gleason grade now defines most high-risk patients, as
   also seen at Johns Hopkins.
 • National Cancer Database (2004–2016): high-risk proportion rose (about 12% → 20%) with RP use (about
   23% → 41%).
 • cT3 and pT3 at RP have fallen steeply (cT3 about 25% in 1987 → under 3% in 2001) through PSA
   screening, selective surgery and better RT; ECE fell across every clinical, PSA and Gleason stratum (Table
   164.2).
 • NCCN (National Comprehensive Cancer Network) 2023: high risk includes cT3a and cT3b–T4. Bone scan +
   CT or MRI for these and for PSA >20 ng/mL, grade group 4–5 or primary Gleason pattern 5; PSMA PET
   may substitute (more sensitive).
 • RP + extended pelvic lymphadenectomy only for high-risk tumours that can be completely excised; the
   alternative is AD + RT.
 • The pattern has swung back: historically low RP rates (CaPSURE; Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End
   Results [SEER]) have given way to a significant rise in surgery for CAPRA 6–10.

 KEY POINTS: TRENDS IN INCIDENCE AND TREATMENT
   • Fewer men present with locally advanced prostate cancer.
   • Organ-confined cancers found at RP have increased.
   • Non-surgical treatment of high-risk cancer increased, but this has changed in recent years.

Concept — Stage migration has redefined "high risk". What used to mean a palpable T3 tumour now mostly
means high Gleason grade. Contemporary high-risk cohorts are therefore less advanced than the historical
series on which treatment was built, and that is part of why surgery has returned.

NATURAL HISTORY
 • Active surveillance with deferred treatment is viable; disease-specific mortality is low and concentrated
   in higher grade and stage, though even low-risk cancers can progress over long follow-up. Genomic tests
   help selection. Higher-risk disease progresses faster, so healthy men usually warrant intervention.
 • Few relevant series of observed locally advanced disease; older ones are of little relevance, and reported
   5–10-year progression, metastasis and survival vary widely.
 • Veterans Administration Cooperative Urological Research Group (VACURG) placebo arm, stage III: 5-year
   OS 58%. Medical Research Council (MRC) delayed-AD arm (locally advanced M0): median time to clinical
   progression 10 months, to prostate cancer death 48 months.
 • Grade drives cancer death under conservative management: grade 3 carries about tenfold the disease-
   specific mortality of grade 1.
 • Swedish population cohort (Table 164.3): in localised disease, deferred and initial treatment gave similar
   15-year corrected survival; locally advanced disease did worse. Cancer death rose from rare (well
   differentiated) to over half (poorly differentiated).
 • Connecticut Tumour Registry (pre-PSA watchful waiting): 15-year cancer mortality rises with Gleason (6 <
   7 < 8–10; Gleason 8–10: 60–87%); in Gleason 6, competing mortality exceeds it. Annual mortality from
   low-grade cancer is stable beyond 15 years, unlike the late rise in the Swedish cohort.
 • SEER contemporary cohort: better outcomes, but Gleason 8–10 still >20% cancer-specific mortality at 15
   years. Untreated high-risk disease carries significant risk of progression and cancer death.
 • Undertreatment: in Sweden, fit men in their 70s with life expectancy >10 years received RT in only 40%
   and surgery in 10%, despite life expectancy comparable to younger treated men; the UK National Prostate
   Cancer Audit found the same.

matters for a given man. A fit man in his 70s with high-grade disease will live long enough to die of it, which is
why withholding treatment from him is an error.

RADICAL PROSTATECTOMY
 • RP for locally advanced disease declined: surgery alone is often insufficient, risk assessment improved, RT
   delivery improved and combined RT + AD beat monotherapy. Yet RP can cure some high-risk men, and
   adding secondary therapy may improve on it.

Surgery for Clinical Stage T3 Prostate Cancer
 • OS after RP for cT3 (all series): 64–96% at 5, 12.5–72% at 10, 20–51% at 15 years; older series carried
   inaccurate staging and occult nodes.
 • Contemporary disease-specific survival: 85–92% at 5 years and 79–82% at 10 years, regardless of
   adjuvant therapy.
 • Surgery alone in cT3a: about half recurrence-free at 8–10 years without adjuvant AD. Mayo Clinic cT3 at
   20 years: local recurrence-free, systemic progression-free and disease-specific survival 72–81%.
 • Clinical overstaging of cT3 occurs in 7–27%, so men should not be excluded from RP on clinical stage
   alone.
 • Higher survival in men who completed RP with node dissection; over a third of high-risk RP specimens
   are specimen-confined (pT2–pT3a), predictable by a nomogram (PSA, age, clinical stage, biopsy Gleason).
 • Selection is key: many cT3 tumours have regional spread, but in lower-volume disease local control is
   usual and complete excision possible.
 • Biochemical progression is confounded by adjuvant therapy: >60% at 5 years without secondary
   treatment; 42–49% (5 years) and 59–62% (10 years) with variable adjuvant use.
 • Clinical progression (biopsy-proven local or objective metastasis): 12–45%, 39–49%, 50–71% at 5, 10, 15
   years, little affected by adjuvant therapy. Survival benefit over alternatives is unproven.

Outcomes of Prostatectomy for Pathologically Advanced Disease
 • A significant minority of clinically organ-confined tumours are pT3. cT3 tumours tend to be larger, higher
   grade and more often regionally spread than upstaged pT3; most pT3 today are high risk by PSA or
   Gleason, and the groups overlap. Pathological stage is a powerful predictor (Fig. 164.7).
 • Positive margin carries the greatest relative risk — more than ECE, SVI or nodes — so completely remove
   all prostatic tissue regardless of stage.
 • SVI, node-negative: 15-year DSS about 80% although BDFS only about a third.
 • Biochemical recurrence depends on threshold: 0.2 ng/mL → up to 65–72% at 5 years; 0.4 ng/mL →
   about 26% at 5 and ≥50% at 10 years.

 • pT3bN0 at 10 years: biochemical recurrence-free about 25%, metastasis-free 70%, DSS 80%.
 • SVI also raises local recurrence (historically nearly half), now lower through selection, technique and
   earlier secondary treatment.
 • Minimally invasive RP in high risk: oncological and functional outcomes comparable to open;
   lymphadenectomy extent varies.
 • PSA-free survival after RP in high-risk men ≈ 50% at 5–7 years; higher biopsy Gleason and PSA raise the
   risk.
 • Kattan preoperative nomogram, community cohort (concordance index 0.68): underestimated
   recurrence in lower-risk and overestimated it in higher-risk men.
 • Up to half with Gleason 8–10 or PSA >20 ng/mL have prolonged disease-free survival after RP alone;
   better markers must identify them.

Outcomes of Prostatectomy for Node-Positive Disease
 • Renewed interest in surgery for N+ disease (multimodal therapy, longer life expectancy, oncological and
   symptomatic benefit of removing the primary). Selection is unresolved; ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11 PET may identify
   limited or excisable disease.
 • Feasibility: cytoreductive RP for M1 has acceptable complications and continence; surgery after
   neoadjuvant systemic therapy does not appear to raise perioperative complications (CALGB 90203 to
   address).
 • N+ after RP: about half alive at 10 years. Single positive node: nearly half without clinical progression,
   most never needing AD, DSS >80%.
 • N+ without adjuvant AD or RT can still achieve reasonable 10-year OS and DSS. Poor predictors: Gleason
   >8 and >3 positive nodes.
 • Oligometastatic (≤5 bone metastases), RP + extended node dissection + almost universal adjuvant AD
   (small cohort): about half progression-free, most free of cancer death at about 5 years.
 • Nodal disease as first site of clinical recurrence carried better DSS. Oligometastatic local-therapy trials are
   ongoing.

Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy and Chemotherapy–Hormonal Therapy
 • Chemotherapy was confined to the most advanced disease: taxanes in hormone-refractory disease give
   PSA falls >50% in over half and responses in 28–75%; mitoxantrone + low-dose steroid improves pain
   relief and is approved. Hence earlier use in high risk (Table 164.8).
 • CALGB 90203 (neoadjuvant AD + docetaxel before RP): primary endpoint (3-year biochemical recurrence)
   not met, but overall biochemical recurrence, MFS and OS improved.
 • Estramustine + etoposide without NAD: grade 3–4 toxicity in about a third, surgery never delayed,
   outcomes comparable; early failures were node-positive.
 • Docetaxel alone and nanoparticle paclitaxel: well tolerated, no pathological complete responses;
   docetaxel gave PSA falls >50% in a proportion.
 • Docetaxel + dose-escalated mitoxantrone (16 weeks): half relapse-free at 5 years; margins positive in a
   third.
 • Also tried: granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor + thalidomide; gefitinib (epidermal growth
   factor receptor inhibitor). Roles of custirsen and immunotherapy (sipuleucel-T, ipilimumab, PD-1/PD-L1
   inhibitors) undetermined.

Adjuvant Radiation Therapy
 • Why defer further therapy until after RP: no delay to surgery, less operative morbidity, and most
   importantly, pathology identifies who truly needs it, sparing the rest. The difficulty: benefit depends on
   the site of failure (local or distant), which is unknown.
 • Older studies: no effect on metastasis or DSS; newer data: better biochemical control. Adjuvant RT BDFS
   50–88% at 5 years vs 30–50% after surgery alone in high risk (Table 164.9).
 • Immediate RT vs observation (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer [EORTC]
   22911, SWOG 8794, ARO 96-02; table below) reduced clinical local recurrence.
 • EORTC 22911 detail: the positive-margin benefit (HR 0.38) held regardless of SVI or a postoperative PSA
   above or below 0.2 ng/mL; even pT3b with a positive margin benefited.
 • SWOG 8794 detail (median 10.6 years; PSA <0.4 ng/mL after RP): biochemical recurrence 35% vs 64%;
   metastases 16% → 7%; less subsequent AD. With SVI, 10-year BDFS improved (36% vs 12%) but MFS and
   OS did not.
 • ARO 96-02 detail: ECE without SVI predicted a more favourable response to RT.
 • Adverse features are not equivalent, yet SWOG 8794 pooled ECE, SVI and margins.
 • Decision analysis: benefit is confined to likely local recurrence (low–intermediate grade, no SVI); consider
   adjuvant RT for extensive or multiple positive margins.
 • Salvage RT: about half progress afterwards; high Gleason and SVI predict poor response, yet selected
   adverse-feature men (high grade, rapid PSA doubling time) respond durably — over half with Gleason 8–
   10 and positive margins reached a nadir ≤0.1 ng/mL without rise.
 • Early secondary therapy (RT or AD) may help selected men; salvage RT in pT3 was associated with better
   OS. Optimal timing and effects on local control, metastasis and survival remain undefined.
 • pT3b: high distant risk, but some recur only locally; with undetectable PSA, adjuvant RT improved 3-year
   BDFS.
 • Favourable pT3b for adjuvant RT: PSA <0.3 ng/mL after RP or positive margins. A PSA never reaching
   undetectable → occult nodal or distant disease, poor prognosis.
 • 24-gene signature: high-score men given postoperative RT had less 10-year metastasis, so it may predict
   RT response.
 • Adjuvant versus early salvage RT (RADICALS-RT, GETUG-AFU 17, RAVES and a pre-planned meta-analysis):
   early salvage is preferred for most men.
 • Trap: very high-risk men (Gleason 8–10, pT3b) made up only ~20% of these trials, so adjuvant RT may still
   benefit them.
 • Dose: traditionally lower than salvage (45 to >60 Gy); contemporary >60 Gy. BDFS better above 61.2 Gy;
   with detectable PSA, ≥64 Gy improved freedom from failure (a dose response like primary RT). RAVES,
   RADICALS-RT, GETUG-AFU 17 used 64–66 Gy.
 • RTOG 0621 (Radiation Therapy Oncology Group; high risk after RP, mostly pT3–T4, Gleason ≥8): 6 months
   AD + RT + six cycles docetaxel → 3-year freedom from progression 73% vs 50% expected, supporting
   phase III trials.
 • GETUG-AFU 16: 3 months of AD with RT suffices — 10-year PFS 64% vs 49% (HR 0.54).

   • Observation with early salvage RT appears preferable after prostatectomy.

Concept — Adjuvant RT treats a local problem. Its benefit concentrates where residual disease is local (a

why, for most men, observation with early salvage matches immediate treatment.

 • Rationale: early-AD benefit in MRC and VACURG, and continued AD after RT.

   • RP alone gives disease-free survival in at least half of men at 8–10 years, despite clinically advanced

RADIATION THERAPY
 • The trend is non-surgical treatment of high-risk disease. Many older cT3 series predate PSA and modern
   techniques — three-dimensional conformal RT (3D-CRT), intensity-modulated RT (IMRT), whole-pelvis RT.
 • Dose: ≥81 Gy improves biochemical control in higher risk; 78 vs 70 Gy helped most with PSA >10 ng/mL.
 • EBRT or permanent brachytherapy monotherapy is probably inadequate; the focus is RT + AD, often with
   whole-pelvis RT (Table 164.10).
 • Trap: the demonstrated benefits of AD + RT over RT alone often do not include overall survival.
 • RT alone, stage C: OS 60–70% at 5 years, <50% at 10; high-risk 5-year PFS typically <50%.

 • Rationale: smaller target volume and cytotoxic synergy with radiation. Early RTOG trials (75-06, 83-07,

 • Canadian three-arm trial (RT vs 3 months NAD + RT vs NAD + RT + 6 months adjuvant AD): residual cancer

FOCAL ABLATIVE THERAPY
 • Minimally invasive, usually focal ablation to spare whole-gland side effects; cryotherapy and high-
   intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) are commonest; photodynamic therapy, focal laser ablation and
   irreversible electroporation need validation. Technologies are not rigorously compared and high-risk data
   are limited.

Cryoablation
 • Primary cryoablation (many high-risk men): 5-year BDFS (PSA <1 ng/mL) about 40%, and about 35% with
   PSA ≥10 and/or Gleason ≥8.
 • With stricter thresholds (0.5 or 0.2 ng/mL), 2-year biochemical failure exceeds 80%.
 • Randomised trial, cT2c–T3, 6 months of AD + cryoablation vs RT (66 Gy): DSS and OS did not differ, but
   cryoablation gave shorter PSA-free survival (28 vs 41 months) and more biochemical failure (87% vs
   53%).

High-Intensity Focused Ultrasonography
 • Data are confounded by frequent omission of risk stratification and common use of AD to reduce prostate
   size.
 • AD + HIFU, locally advanced: good 1-year control (PSA <0.3 ng/mL, negative sextant biopsies in most; no
   clinical progression).
 • NAD improves HIFU in D'Amico high risk (fewer positive biopsies and biochemical failures); worse 3-year
   DFS independently with stage ≥cT2c (HR 2.34) and no NAD (HR 2.19). As with RT, NAD helps mainly
   intermediate and high risk.
 • UK HIFU registry: D'Amico high-risk men had about 65% failure-free survival at 7 years.

ANDROGEN DEPRIVATION AND ITS TIMING
 • VACURG I (placebo vs DES 5 mg vs orchiectomy ± DES): the absent OS benefit was not explained by DES
   cardiovascular deaths alone, because orchiectomy + placebo also did not beat placebo.
 • VACURG II (DES 0.2, 1 or 5 mg): 1 and 5 mg delayed stage III progression. Immediate oestrogen was most
   beneficial under 75 years with Gleason 7–10.
 • VACURG III (stage II): 5-year survival 48% with placebo vs 75% with DES 1 mg.
 • VACURG overall: early AD helps progression, and possibly survival, only in aggressive subsets; avoid
   oestrogen (DES cardiotoxicity at higher doses). PSA kinetics and imaging now time delayed AD better.
 • MRC (orchiectomy or LHRH agonist; about half locally advanced): two-thirds of deaths were from
   prostate cancer; the reduction in cancer deaths with immediate AD was mainly in M0 disease.
 • MRC as benchmark (cT3M0): median time to cancer death 90 months and OS 64 months, both not
   reached in EORTC 22863 → part of the AD + RT benefit may be AD alone.

   local therapy appeared to benefit, but primary bicalutamide in localised disease raised death risk (HR

 • Adjuvant flutamide 750 mg after RP (pT3–T4N0): recurrence-free HR 0.51, OS unchanged (HR 1.04);

 • Aim: less AD morbidity. Protocol: total AD → PSA <4 ng/mL at 24–32 weeks → stop at 36 weeks →

 • Optimal timing is undetermined, particularly in light of the trial of intermittent vs continuous AD in

 • Cardiovascular: adjuvant AD in RTOG 85-31 (median 4.2 years) did not raise cardiovascular mortality;
   short-term NAD in RTOG 86-10 did not raise fatal cardiac events.
Concept — Early AD buys progression-free time, not reliably survival. Survival gains appear only in
aggressive subsets and when AD is paired with local RT. AD alone neither cures nor controls the primary
tumour, and its toxicity has to be paid for with a real benefit.

MANAGEMENT OF DELAYED SEQUELAE
 • RP and RT are hard to compare (mostly historical data). Leaving the prostate in situ matters because local
   control may affect survival and local recurrence may need further treatment: 10-year local recurrence
   after RT for cT3 is 24–74%.
 • After RT alone: TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate) for obstruction in about a third,
   hydronephrosis 20%, incontinence 13%. Untreated stage C (older series): infection 80%, outlet
   obstruction 75%, gross haematuria 45%, ureteric obstruction 40%.
 • AD does not prevent local progression: about a third needed TURP 60 days after orchiectomy;
   orchiectomy, RT or both gave comparable local control; with early or deferred AD over half needed TURP,
   and deferred AD was prompted by ureteric obstruction (25%), anticipated local complications (10%) and
   rectal infiltration (4%).

 • Stage and quality of life (Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy [FACT]; UCLA Prostate Cancer Index
   urinary subscale; Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [HADS]): higher stage worsens nearly every FACT
   subscale, mainly physical, with no link to HADS psychological symptoms.
 • RT monotherapy, high risk: bowel function near normative but more bowel bother and diarrhoea;
   progression or need for AD worsened sexual, urinary, social function and sleep.
 • CaPSURE: AD with surgery or RT → sexual loss recovering within 9 months; EBRT + brachytherapy →
   continuing decline in urinary function and bother, worse bowel at 1 year and urinary irritation through 3
   years vs EBRT alone.
 • SWOG 8794: adjuvant RT worsened bowel and urinary function, not erectile function; more symptom
   distress; global quality of life initially worse, then steadily improved.
 • NCCN high/very high risk: more intense therapy → worse urinary symptoms at 1 year, converging later.
Concept — A prostate left untreated or unremoved causes its own morbidity (obstruction, haematuria,
ureteric obstruction) that AD does not prevent. This is the functional argument for definitive treatment of the
primary, and it has to be weighed against the added toxicity of multimodal therapy.

CLINICAL TRIALS
 • Suboptimal outcomes → consider trial enrolment (clinicaltrials.gov).
 • SPCG-15 (newly diagnosed cT3N0M0, PSA <100 ng/mL): RP vs RT + AD, with disease-specific survival as
   the primary endpoint.
 • Genomic Umbrella Neoadjuvant Study: AD + apalutamide while tumours are sequenced, then targeted
   agents added (PARP inhibitor for DNA repair defects, PD-L1 inhibitor for microsatellite instability).
 • Open questions: type and timing of combination treatment, role of chemotherapy, biomarker-directed
   selection.
