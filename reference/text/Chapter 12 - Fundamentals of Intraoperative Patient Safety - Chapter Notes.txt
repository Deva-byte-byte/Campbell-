Chapter 12 — Fundamentals of Intraoperative Patient Safety

Chapter 12 — Fundamentals of Intraoperative Patient
Safety
 • "First do no harm": treating patients carries the duty to minimise risk.
 • Surgery is a team effort inside hospital systems larger than any individual, yet the surgeon remains team
   leader and ultimately responsible when complications arise.

BACKGROUND
 • Institute of Medicine (IOM), To Err Is Human (1999), landmark: 44,000–98,000 deaths and >1 million
   injuries a year from medical error → catalysed patient-safety research.
 • A 2016 report judged this a severe underestimate: medical error as the third leading cause of US death,
   after heart disease and cancer.
 • ~50% of health care adverse events involve surgery, and ~50% of those are preventable.
 • Closed malpractice claims: three-quarters of errors arose wholly or partly in the intraoperative phase —
   making the operating room (OR) error-free is indispensable.

SURGICAL TIME-OUTS AND SAFETY CHECKLISTS
 • Complexity plus stress and fatigue make human error almost inevitable.
 • A checklist lists tasks, components or criteria required for a task; it is a mnemonic device that aids recall,
   standardises steps and ensures best-practice adherence — borrowed from aviation.
 • Pronovost (2006), landmark: a central-line checklist across 108 intensive care units cut catheter-related
   bloodstream infection by 66% over 18 months.
 • World Health Organization (WHO) Surgical Safety Checklist (2008), 19 items (Fig. 12.1); pilot at 8
   institutions worldwide (~4000 operations): death ↓47%, major complications ↓36%.
 • Institutions add antibiotic and venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis, normothermia and other
   items; some form of checklist is now standard everywhere.

WRONG-SITE SURGERY PREVENTION
 • Safety starts with correct patient, procedure and site. Wrong-site surgery = wrong side, wrong patient or
   wrong part — all sentinel, reportable never events.
 • Extremely rare (~1 in 112,000 operations) but devastating.
 • The Joint Commission (independent not-for-profit, founded 1951, accredits >20,000 US organisations)
   published the mandatory Universal Protocol (2004), by expert consensus, with three components:
       preprocedure verification → surgical site marking → time-out immediately before starting
   It is now the foundation of intraoperative checklists.

Preprocedure Verification Process
 • Up to 30% of wrong-site root causes arise before the patient reaches hospital: wrong or incomplete
   clinic notes, mislabelled imaging, laboratory mix-ups of similar names.
 • Confirms three things: correct identity, correct procedure, exact site.
 • Review notes, allergies, imaging and laboratory results for correct, relevant indication.
 • Identify with multiple identifiers — full name, date of birth, medical record number — on a durable
   tracking system (barcoded wristband).
 • History and examination recent and updated for interim change.
 • Consent form names the responsible surgeon and procedure, signed by patient or designate (reconfirmed
   if signed earlier); list contingent procedures that depend on intraoperative findings.
 • Use professional medical interpreters — never family — for procedural consent.
 • Resolve any missing or discrepant information before proceeding.

Site Marking
 • Required where more than one site is possible (e.g. unilateral radical nephrectomy, radical
   orchidectomy).
 • Mark must be clear, unambiguous, permanent enough to survive skin preparation; by the responsible
   surgeon or delegated resident/advanced practice provider — ideally someone present at the time-out.
 • Mark with an awake, alert patient before sedation where possible.
 • Mark only the correct site — never "No" or "X" on the wrong side.
 • Lower-tract endourology: mark sidedness somewhere visible (e.g. wristband), not the urethral meatus.
 • Barriers: delegation to someone absent from the case; marking the wrong site after erroneous record
   review; marking dressings, not skin; non-permanent markers; persistent old marks (take-backs); site
   impossible to mark → hospital-approved alternative process.

Time-Out
 • A purposeful brief pause just before incision or the invasive step, confirming identity, site and procedure.
 • Extended versions: normothermia, euglycaemia, β-blockade, antibiotics, spinal precautions, critical steps,
   duration, blood need, fire risk, VTE prevention, and "any concerns?".
 • All members participate actively; leader (surgeon or circulating nurse) is institution-specific; no
   interruptions or side conversations; proceed only with full agreement.
 • Repeat the time-out at every change of surgeon or transition to a separate procedure (multispecialty
   cases).

 • Common errors: background noise (music) continues; proceeding while a member is mid-task; key
   member on break; going through the motions ("we all know the patient").
Concept — The Universal Protocol is layered redundancy: verification catches errors made before the day,
marking fixes the side on the body, and the time-out forces a shared final check. Each layer is only as good as
the attention paid to it, which is why "going through the motions" is the characteristic failure.

SYSTEMS FAILURES
 • "Swiss cheese model" (Fig. 12.2): each barrier is a slice with unpredictable holes; hazards passing one
   hole are usually stopped by the next (near miss); harm occurs when the holes align.
 • Layers (Fig. 12.2): organisational influences → unsafe supervision → preconditions for unsafe acts (latent
   failures) → unsafe acts (active failures) → error.
 • Explains why never events persist despite the Universal Protocol: many stakeholders, departments and
   levels — harm is usually systemic.
 • Risk circumstances for wrong-site surgery: emergencies; unusual anatomy (deformity, morbid obesity);
   time pressure; multiple surgeons; multiple procedures in one sitting.
 • Rushed situations demand the checklist most; checklists do not delay start times, and in urgent
   operations they significantly reduce complications and mortality.
 • Environmental root causes: hierarchy and power dynamics that silence concern; staffing shortages and
   temporary staff untrained in local protocols; unprecedented events (SARS-CoV-2) forcing real-time
   workflow change.
Concept — Errors reaching the patient are system failures, not individual lapses: no single barrier is reliable,
so safety comes from several independent barriers and from preventing the latent conditions — hierarchy,
staffing, time pressure — that make the holes line up.

RETAINED SURGICAL ITEMS
 • Retained surgical item (RSI): material, instrument or equipment unintentionally left in the patient;
   preferred to "retained foreign object" (distinguishes from shrapnel or bullets).
 • Consequences: infection, pain, death; medicolegal liability; cost.
 • Joint Commission definition: an RSI if discovered after skin closure — even with the patient still
   anaesthetised in theatre.
 • A never event, theoretically 100% preventable, yet occurring between 1 in 10,000 and 1 in 2800
   operations.

Risk Factors
 • Emergency surgery; unexpected change of procedure; obesity; multiple teams; open body cavity;
   unusually many instruments; incorrect count; long operation; attending absent at closure.
 • Trainee presence is associated with lower RSI risk.
 • Human factors — leadership and communication — dominate the contributing causes in sentinel-event
   reviews.

Prevention Techniques
 • Multipronged, because no strategy is foolproof.
 • Foundation: the standardised count of sponges and needles before, during and at close; in the US the
   Association of periOperative Registered Nurses (AORN) sets the standard.
 • Count discrepancy → ~100-fold odds of RSI; if found before closure, delay closure until the item is found.
 • Radiographic screening — routine or only for count discrepancy. With routine post-procedure films after
   abdominal-cavity cases, most RSIs detected had correct counts — a correct count does not exclude an
   RSI.

Concept — Counting is necessary but not sufficient: a wrong count flags danger strongly, but a right count
does not guarantee safety, so counting is backed by imaging or tagging where risk is high.

SHARPS SAFETY
 • The HIV epidemic of the 1980s focused attention; from the late 1980s "Universal Precautions" (blood and
   body fluids from all patients) became mandated by bodies such as the Occupational Safety and Health
   Administration.
 • Mucous membrane or skin contact with patient blood occurs in up to half of operations.
 • Surgeons and first assistants sustain most injuries (up to ~60%); most are self-inflicted, but up to a
   quarter are inflicted by a team member.
 • About one-third of injuring devices recontact the patient → risk of surgeon-to-patient transmission.

FIRE SAFETY
 • Surgical fire: on or near the patient during surgery; ~600 a year in the US.
 • 85% occur in the head, neck or upper chest; 81% under monitored anaesthesia care (MAC); injuries
   disfigure, and claims involve the whole team.
 • Fire triangle (Fig. 12.3): oxidiser + fuel + ignition in proximity; each side has an owner.

                                         ignite even in endourology

Surgical Fire Risk Assessment
 • Silverstein Fire Risk Assessment Tool — 1 point each: (1) open oxygen source (mask, nasal cannula); (2)
   ignition source (diathermy, laser); (3) site above the xiphoid. Score 1 low, 2 intermediate, 3 high.
 • Most urology is low or intermediate (open oxygen above the xiphoid is rare) → standard protocols.
 • Near the airway of an intubated patient (e.g. buccal mucosa harvest): FiO₂ ≤30%, stop nitrous oxide, and
   give anaesthesia 3–5 minutes' warning before diathermy.
 • Include fire risk in the time-out; pre-agree roles; each member reassesses the element they control.

Fire Management
   Warning (smoke, unexpected flash or pop) → pause and look → water/saline (wet towels for small fires)
   → remove drapes and burning material to the floor (hidden smouldering) → extinguisher if not out →
   RACE if the fire persists
 • RACE: Rescue/remove the patient from the fire; Alert/activate alarm and call emergency services; Confine
   — medical gases off, doors closed; Extinguish/evacuate — use extinguisher while leaving.
Concept — Fire needs all three sides of the triangle, and each side belongs to a different team member — so
prevention is a team task, and removing any one side (low oxygen, dry prep, no diathermy) is enough.

ENVIRONMENTAL DISTRACTIONS
 • Sources: equipment alarms and noise, music, pagers and smartphones, corridor noise, case-irrelevant
   "small talk"; distracting events are frequent (many per operative hour).
 • Effects: in aviation, impaired prospective memory and safety checks; in theatre, technical errors, longer
   operations, teamwork communication errors → restrict to the strictly necessary.
 • "Sterile cockpit" (aviation): no non-essential talk or activity in pre-defined critical phases — music off,
   minimal traffic and shift changes, no small talk; define phases at the briefing/time-out (e.g. hilar clamping
   and unclamping in partial nephrectomy).

 • Smartphones blur personal and professional use and are linked to poor behaviour and outcomes; ACS
   guidance: silence ringtones, avoid personal calls, redirect to the OR desk or hard-wired phone, distinct
   ringtones for urgent calls. A ban is unrealistic.
 • Alarms and music: lower noise → fewer complications. Frequent non-actionable alarms cause sensory
   overload and alarm fatigue (real events missed) → individualise alarm settings; develop "smart alarms".
 • Music: very common and mostly viewed positively; effect on performance is controversial and genre
   effects do not generalise → consider volume, genre and lyrics for the whole team.

COMMUNICATION AND TEAM-BASED FACTORS
 • Communication failure underlies up to 70% of OR adverse events; no protocol works without it.
 • Non-technical skills are as important as technical skills, and dedicated training improves teamwork and
   safety.

Basics of Communication
 • Communication = information exchange and "constructing shared realities".
 • Components (Fig. 12.4): sender, receiver, message, channel; a message is symbolic (words, body
   language, gestures, visuals); channels (e-mail, face to face) differ in speed.
   Sender encodes idea into symbols/medium → channel → receiver decodes meaning — each within their
   own context, surrounded by noise
 • Encoding and decoding depend on personal knowledge, experience and bias; overlapping contexts
   make communication efficient, mismatches cause error.
 • Noise = anything disrupting accurate encoding/decoding: physical (music), physiological (fatigue),
   psychological (mood).

Errors in Communication
 • Communication fails in about 30% of OR exchanges, a third of which create safety risk (cognitive load,
   interruptions, tension).
 • Types: poor timing/situational awareness; missing or inaccurate information; no purpose or resolution;
   wrong audience.
 • Malpractice claims: a substantial share of harmful breakdowns are intraoperative, mostly verbal between
   two individuals, and many occur at handoffs.
 • Risk factors: high cognitive load, implicit bias/assumption, hierarchy, diffusion of responsibility, transitions
   of care.

Nontechnical Skills and Teamwork Training
 • Communication, situational awareness, decision making, leadership and teamwork predict surgical
   success more than technical skill, yet were not traditionally taught.
 • Situational awareness = the "big picture": Where have we come from? Where are we now? Where are
   we going?
 • Decision making: assess, share knowledge, risk-stratify options and contingencies, review outcomes.
   Leadership: listen, share information, guide to shared goals. Teamwork: a group achieving a common
   goal.
 • TeamSTEPPS (Team Strategies and Tools to Enhance Performance and Patient Safety): national standard
   released 2006 by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) with the Department of Defense
   — evidence-based, ready-made curricula, certified trainers.

TeamSTEPPS Overview
 • Five principles (Fig. 12.5): team structure, communication, leadership, situation monitoring, mutual
   support — interacting dynamically around the patient care team, yielding knowledge, attitudes and
   performance.

FIG. 12.5 TEAMSTEPPS PRINCIPLES AND TOOLS (AGENCY FOR HEALTHCARE RESEARCH AND QUALITY)
 Principle                  Tools
 Team structure             Correct assembly; clearly defined roles and responsibilities
 Communication              SBAR — situation, background, assessment, recommendation/request (urgent information);
                            call-outs (to many at once in emergencies); check-backs (closed loop)
 Leadership                 Briefs (before: roles, expectations, contingencies); huddles (ad hoc regroup, situational
                            awareness); debriefs (lessons learned)
 Situation monitoring       STEP — status of patient, team members, environment, progress to goal; I'M SAFE — illness,
                            medication, stress, alcohol/drugs, fatigue, eating and elimination (self-check)
 Mutual support             Task sharing, help sought and offered without judgement; timely, respectful, specific
                            feedback; two-challenge rule (voice a safety concern at least twice); CUS — "I am Concerned,
                            I am Uncomfortable, this is a Safety issue" — stops the line

 • Trap: STEP is monitoring the situation, I'M SAFE monitoring oneself; the two-challenge rule and CUS
   belong to mutual support, not communication.
 • In urology, TeamSTEPPS training across endoscopic, laparoscopic/robotic and open cases shortened case
   time, improved on-time first starts and reduced safety issues; other studies show better perceived
   teamwork.
 • Non-technical skills must be taught and practised. Didactic teaching alone does not change outcomes;
   simulation-based team training (SBTT) in high-fidelity environments allows mistakes without harm, with
   prompt feedback.
 • In situ simulation with TeamSTEPPS reduced perinatal morbidity and mortality in crash caesarean
   sections; didactic TeamSTEPPS alone equalled no training.
 • Urological simulation: non-technical skills taught in high-fidelity ureteroscopy simulation outperformed
   didactic teaching (randomised trial); a laparoscopic partial nephrectomy crisis scenario (anaphylaxis,
   mislabelled specimen) was rated useful, and few participants caught the pathology error — reinforcing
   checklists and closed-loop communication.
 • All levels benefit; mixed junior/senior cohorts favour juniors; training technical and non-technical skills
   together dilutes both; benefit depends on facilitator skill and debrief quality.
Concept — Most safety protocols fail at the point of human interaction, not design; communication is
therefore trained like a technical skill — structured tools (SBAR, check-backs, CUS) replace improvisation, and
immersive simulation, not lectures, changes behaviour.

 KEY POINTS
   • Time-outs and checklists minimise human error.
   • Universal Protocol: preprocedure verification, site marking, time-out.
   • Count discrepancy → 100-fold risk of an RSI.
   • Double gloving, blunt needles and a designated sharps zone reduce sharps injury.
   • Fire risk: oxidiser, fuel and ignition source.
   • Communication errors cause most OR adverse events.
