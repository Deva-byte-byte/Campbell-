PROSTATE ANATOMY

Zonal Anatomy of the Human Prostate
 • Fetal origin: epithelial cords from the primitive urethra canalise and branch in response to androgen
   action in local mesenchyme.
 • McNeal zones (1981): peripheral zone (PZ), central zone (CZ), preprostatic region (transition zone, TZ) and
   anterior fibromuscular stroma (AFMS).
 • Zones were defined partly by where focal disease arises: benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and cancer
   (table).
 • Orientation axes: base (bladder neck) → apex (junction of prostatic and bulbar urethra); anterior (top) →
   dorsal (bottom).
 • The prostatic urethra bends approximately 35° before the dorsal entry of the ejaculatory and prostate
   ducts.
 • 13–15 prostate ducts open onto the dorsolateral prostatic urethra along the base-to-apex axis, best
   shown by dorsal nano computed tomography (nanoCT).
 • Verumontanum: a bulge distal to the urethral crest that marks the entry of the ejaculatory ducts (seminal
   vesicle and vas deferens).

Cellular Anatomy of Human Prostate
 • Cell types, once defined by morphology, location and selective protein or RNA expression, are now
   defined by pan-transcriptomic profile (single-cell or single-nucleus RNA sequencing) plus in situ location
   and morphology; modern prostate anatomy is the distribution of these discrete cell types, divided into
   epithelia and stroma.
 • Validated types are annotated in the Cell Ontology (a structured vocabulary for animal cell types) and
   placed anatomically in Uberon (a cross-species anatomy ontology). The Human BioMolecular Atlas

Luminal Epithelia of Prostate
 • The "workhorse" of the gland, responsible for epithelial barrier integrity and protein secretion.
 • Their products, important for sperm function, include KLK3 (kallikrein-related peptidase 3, prostate-
   specific antigen, PSA), prostatic acid phosphatase (PAP) and androgen receptor (AR).
 • Polarity: rows joined by adhesion molecules; base anchored to basement membrane by integrins; basal
   nucleus beneath a Golgi-rich clear zone (2–8 µm); apex with microvilli and abundant secretory granules.
   Secretions pass acinus → ducts → urethra.

Basal Epithelia of Prostate
 • Basal cells have condensed chromatin and rest on the basement membrane, wedged between the bases
   of the luminal cells. Their progenitor role is limited to certain situations; their main function is ductal
   integrity.

Club and Hillock Epithelia of Prostatic Urethra
 • The prostatic urethra is lined by a transitional epithelium containing club and hillock cells, like those of
   the proximal lung.
 • Club and hillock cells extend into the proximal ducts before they transition to NKX3-1+ prostate
   epithelium. This gives a cell type–specific definition of the "transition zone".
 • Lung club cells detoxify, regulate immunity and regenerate several cell types after injury; a similar
   prostatic role is presumed but untestable in the mouse, which lacks club cells.

Neuroendocrine and Ionocyte Epithelia of Prostate and Prostatic Urethra
 • Like the proximal lung, the prostatic urethra contains neuroendocrine cells and ionocytes. Ionocytes are
   thought to break down seminal fluid, as they break down mucus in the lung.
 • Neuroendocrine cells are chemo- and mechanosensory; in lung and intestine they release hormones and
   antimicrobial peptides under stress and interact with nervous, endocrine and immune systems. Their
   prostatic function is poorly characterised.
 • Their presence in male and female urogenital sinus epithelium before prostate development suggests a
   lineage independent of prostate epithelium; lineage tracing suggests most are neural crest–derived.
   What triggers epithelial transdifferentiation or neural crest migration is undefined.

Prostate Fibroblasts and Smooth Muscle
 • A dense population of interstitial fibroblasts surrounds the prostatic urethra and its ducts, presumably
   providing structural support during voiding and ejaculation.
 • Periepithelial and interstitial fibroblasts were discovered only recently. Transcriptomic signatures suggest
   roles in stress response and immune response respectively.

Prostate Vasculature
 • Vascular smooth muscle (large vessels) and pericytes (microvessels) are both mural cells: they are
   embedded in basement membrane and extend long processes that interact with and regulate
   endothelium.

Prostate Immunology
 • Healthy young donor prostate (single-cell RNA sequencing) has a small resident population of
   macrophages, dendritic cells and T cells, also found in adult mouse prostate.
 • BPH tissue has prominent T cells, B cells and dendritic cells and resembles a mucosal inflammatory
   disorder.

 • The mouse lobes are named for their urethral site of origin; each has a unique branching pattern,
   histology and transcriptionally distinct luminal epithelium, and lobes have been likened to human zones.

Prostatic Secretions
 • The prostate's overarching function is to synthesise and secrete factors that support the safe passage of
   sperm from the urethra to the egg.
 • Semen contains cells, salts, sugars, hormones, carbohydrates, lipids and more than 2000 proteins; some
   prostate-selective factors serve diagnosis, imaging or treatment.

 • Combined, the acidic and basic fluids give semen considerable buffering capacity.
Ion trapping and drug distribution
 • Prostatic fluid (pH about 6.3) is more acidic than plasma (7.4). Un-ionised drugs diffuse; ionised drugs
   need transport, so a drug accumulates where it is most ionised — one reason macrolides concentrate in
   prostatic fluid.
 • Inflammation alkalinises prostatic fluid (above pH 8.3), and pH also rises with age; both alter
   intraprostatic drug distribution.
Coagulation and liquefaction
 • Sequence: prostatic and seminal vesicle fluids mix → coagulation (a gel entraps and immobilises sperm
   during ejaculation) → liquefaction (kallikreins and other proteases degrade semenogelins) → motile
   sperm.

 • Normal liquefaction: within 20–30 minutes. Delayed if not begun by 30 minutes; nonliquefaction if not
   complete by 60 minutes.
 • Hyperviscosity is a suspected cause of male infertility. Kallikrein inhibition to block liquefaction is under
   study as male contraception.
Kallikreins
 • Kallikreins are among the most abundant proteins in semen. They form the largest continuous protease
   cluster in the human genome (chromosome 19q13.4), which encodes 15 structurally similar proteins.
 • Made as inactive propeptides, activated by extracellular autocatalytic or protease cleavage; regulated by
   zinc, citrate, sulfate and glycosaminoglycans.
 • PSA (KLK3) is the kallikrein most associated with the prostate; its mean whole-ejaculate concentration is
   about 1.2 mg/mL. It is made by benign and malignant prostate epithelium and by several extraprostatic
   tissues (table below).
 • KLK2 is also highly expressed in the prostate. It is related in sequence to KLK3 but has distinct protease
   activity.

Zinc
 • The prostate has the highest zinc concentration of any human tissue. The adult median is about 109 mg/
   kg, with wide variation, and the concentration rises with age. Seminal zinc (0.5 mg/mL) far exceeds
   plasma zinc (2 µg/mL).
 • Transporters: ZIP proteins (Zrt-, Irt-like proteins; solute carrier 39, SLC39) carry zinc into epithelial cells
   from plasma or prostatic fluid; ZnT proteins (zinc transporters; SLC30) carry zinc out into prostatic fluid.
 • Seminal zinc supports fertility (low levels → infertility); it reduces oxidative stress, prevents citrate
   oxidation, inhibits liquefaction and correlates with motility.
 • Zinc inhibits the sperm voltage-gated proton channel (Hv1), blocking capacitation — the changes a
   sperm needs to penetrate and fertilise the egg — until diluted in the female tract.
Citrate and energy metabolism
 • In most cells, citrate is an intermediate of glucose oxidation that is oxidised through the Krebs cycle to
   adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Mitochondrial aconitase converts citrate to isocitrate.
 • In luminal cells high zinc inhibits aconitase, so citrate is stored and secreted, and benign cells derive
   energy from oxaloacetate imported through a transmembrane transporter — a unique, inefficient
   strategy.
 • Citrate exceeds 100 mM in the expressed prostatic secretions of most men without cancer, reaching up to
   200 mM, compared with 0.1 mM in plasma. Its primary roles are to maintain seminal pH and facilitate
   semen liquefaction.

Concept — the prostate is built for secretion at the expense of its own metabolism. Zinc blocks aconitase, so
citrate is exported rather than burned. Malignant transformation reverses this by losing zinc and regaining
efficient citrate oxidation.

 KEY POINTS
   • Prostate anatomy has moved from morphological landmarks to a cellular anatomy that defines
     structure and provides a framework for function.
   • Mouse and human prostate show key cellular and anatomic differences.
   • AR regulation appears to be one of the most important "gatekeepers" of prostate development and
     physiology.
   • Secreted prostate proteases have a significant, dose-dependent effect on semen liquefaction.

PROSTATE DEVELOPMENT

Overview and Timeline
 • Why development is studied: ductal morphogenesis requires androgens (a model of androgen action); it
   shows how the microenvironment directs epithelial fate (insight into BPH, cancer and wound healing);
   and embryonic chemical exposure has lasting effects on differentiation, proliferation, testosterone
   response, innervation and smooth muscle activity.
 • Origin: the urogenital sinus (UGS), a transient urethral widening near the bladder neck, formed by cloacal
   division (timeline table).
 • Incomplete cloacal division (imperforate anus, urorectal fistula) is associated with impaired prostate
   development.
 • Rodent models are valid because rodent prostate development resembles the human.
 • The UGS is initially ambisexual: a sheath of mesoderm-derived mesenchyme around a tube of
   endoderm-derived epithelium. AR is widely expressed in both male and female UGS, and both form
   prostate if exposed to sufficient androgen.
 • The prostate is absent after castration or with an inactive AR in rodents. It is incomplete in males
   homozygous for a loss-of-function variant of SRD5A2 (steroid 5α-reductase type 2).
 • Androgen is not rate limiting. Testosterone acts on UGS mesenchyme soon after synthesis begins (about
   week 9), yet budding waits until week 10 and cannot be accelerated in vitro by physiological or
   supraphysiological androgen.
 • Epigenetic checkpoint: the AR gene is highly methylated in the primitive UGS. Demethylation raises AR
   abundance and makes the UGS receptive to androgen-induced budding.

 • Prostate development is initiated by functional AR in UGS mesenchyme, not in UGS epithelium
   (Lasnitzki and Mizuno, 1980).

Prostatic Bud Elongation and Branching Morphogenesis
 • Branching gives a large surface area-to-volume ratio, increasing capacity for fluid synthesis and storage.
 • Branching requires androgens — the prostate remains unbranched in humans with inactivating AR
   variants and is impaired by neonatal castration — acting through stromal, not epithelial, AR, with
   bidirectional paracrine signalling using many budding pathways.

Environmental Factors Influence Prostate Morphogenesis
 • Gestation is a window of vulnerability to environmental and dietary agents. Developmental origins of
   health and disease (DOHaD) hypothesis: early-life (including gestational) chemical exposures modify the
   risk of ageing diseases such as diabetes, cancer and hypertension; their effect on prostate disease is
   poorly understood.
 • Oestrogen: androgens are the principal driver, but oestrogen receptors are expressed lifelong and are
   abundant in fetal stroma and epithelium, including progenitors. Oestrogens are not required for budding
   or branching but may be required for patterning (fewer branches in mice lacking oestrogen receptor 1
   and 2 activity).
 • Oestrogenic substances are full or partial receptor agonists; gestational or lactational exposure in rodents
   causes prostatic inflammation, dysplasia, neoplasia, even adenocarcinoma in susceptible animals.

 • Innervation: smooth muscle activity and the response to α-adrenergic receptor blockers in lower urinary
   tract dysfunction vary widely between men; the controlling autonomic neurons first innervate the
   prostate during gestation.
 • Sequence: gestational TCDD → increased artemin (a neurotrophic factor) in UGS epithelium → more
   noradrenergic fibres in male fetal mice → hyperinnervation persisting into adulthood → thickened,
   hyperactive smooth muscle and increased urinary frequency.
Concept — a fetal exposure can reset the adult prostate through three routes: the progenitor epigenome,
the germline and the nerve supply. The innervation route offers one explanation for the variable response to
α-blockers.

 KEY POINTS
   • Prostatic budding, branching and cytodifferentiation are driven by signals from the stroma/
     mesenchyme.
   • The embryonic period is susceptible to external factors, such as endocrine-disrupting and other
     chemicals, that can influence anatomic development and cell differentiation.
