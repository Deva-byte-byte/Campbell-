 • The multidisciplinary men's health centre is designed to maximise care of male patients while increasing
   convenience of obtaining it; such centres are increasingly prevalent.
 • Dual role: it raises the quality of preventive and other care, and it acts as a front door for new male
   patients into urology practice; proper design drives satisfaction, retention and quality.
 • Urologists are best placed to create and run these centres, because the complaints that bring men into
   the health system as first-time users are often urologic.
 • The multidisciplinary component (primary care, cardiology, gastroenterology) requires the urologist to
   learn specialties beyond urology.

ISSUES OF MALE UROLOGIC DISEASE ACROSS ALL AGES
Introduction and General Considerations
 • Prevalence of urologic and nonurologic disease in men by age is well described.
 • The Urologic Diseases in America project quantified the incidence of benign and malignant urologic
   disease and the burden on urology providers (annual visits, procedural volume).
 • Combined with primary care and cardiology visit data, visit volumes for middle-aged and older men are
   reasonably predictable.
 • Young adults: data less robust, but male infertility, erectile dysfunction (ED) and other sexual complaints,
   and testis cancer generate visits at steady rates.
 • Nonurologic malignancy is similarly predictable; endocrinopathy, cardiac prevention or early cardiac
   disease and other preventive measures are variable.
 • Accurate prediction of need governs provider and resource planning.

ADOLESCENCE/YOUNG ADULTHOOD (18–39 YEARS)
 • Urologic complaints, managed by the urologist or a urology-trained advanced practice provider (APP): ED,
   rule out hypogonadism, testis and pelvic pain, sexually transmitted infection exposure or urethral pain,
   Peyronie disease, premature ejaculation, fertility, testis mass and testis cancer, varicocele.
 • On-site testing: urinalysis, serum hormones and other blood tests, Doppler ultrasound. "Rule out
   hypogonadism" is a commonly occurring visit type.
 • Nonurologic disease: endocrinopathy (diabetes, thyroid), hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, physiatry
   complaints, obesity, haematologic (leukaemia, lymphoma) and solid (colon, sarcoma) malignancy, cardiac
   prevention, upper respiratory tract infection, well visits, cosmetic and dermatology care and vaccination
   — seen by a family or internal medicine physician, nurse practitioner or physician assistant, some by
   telemedicine.
 • Prevention: smoking cessation, moderation of alcohol and overeating, counselling on exercise and sleep.

MIDDLE AGE (40–64 YEARS)
 • Prostate cancer screening (Chapters 153 and 157) and colon cancer screening begin; easy
   gastroenterology referral matters, and an in-house gastroenterologist may be justified by volume.

UTILISATION OF ADVANCED PRACTICE PROVIDERS AND TELEMEDICINE
 • APP care is an appropriate adjunct to both urologic and nonurologic care at all ages; essential in centres
   serving a large geographic or small population region.
 • A specifically trained APP can deliver both — adjunct to the urologist and independent primary care.
 • Telemedicine is vital in geographically large, population-sparse regions; billing and reimbursement
   changes after the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic make it financially justifiable in almost all practices.

APPROACH TO PRIMARY CARE OF THE MALE PATIENT
 • Core tasks: prevention, early diagnosis, management of chronic conditions — methods have changed,
   goals have not; achieving them improves longevity, quality of life and wellness.
 • Wellness as the mere absence of disease is a passé concept.
 • Personalised medicine rests on the patient's genetics and phenotype and, equally, on the patient's own
   health goals.
 • Shared decision making extends beyond prostate cancer screening and management; the "one size fits
   all" approach is gone.

THE LONGEVITY GAP BETWEEN MALES AND FEMALES
 • Life expectancy is the yardstick of medical progress and partly determines national health-system
   rankings; the United States ranks poorly among developed nations, and the reasons are debated.
 • Females outlive males: the gap has narrowed but remains significant, and males have higher mortality
   across most leading causes of death (detailed below).

Lipids and statins
 • Primary cardiovascular disease (CVD) prevention = identifying and modifying risk factors before overt
   disease; the task of the primary care physician or APP.
 • LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) correlates consistently with cardiovascular events, and modifying it changes
   future risk.
 • Only LDL-C lowering benefits patients without overt CVD; raising HDL-C or correcting triglycerides
   confers no benefit in the absence of heart disease.
 • Statins (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors) are the best-established class; early trials of pravastatin,
   lovastatin and rosuvastatin reduced first cardiovascular event and even all-cause mortality in both sexes;
   meta-analysis shows strong reductions in events and mortality with and without established CVD.
 • Cardiovascular risk discussion and lipid screening from as early as age 20.
 • Elevated LDL-C in primary prevention: moderate-intensity statin plus diet and lifestyle. Rationale: no
   randomised trial compares moderate with high intensity in primary prevention; the extra LDL-C
   reduction from high intensity is small; moderate intensity is better tolerated.

GASTROENTEROLOGY IN THE AGEING MALE
 • Gastrointestinal (GI) disorders are common and impair quality of life; in the ageing man consider gastro-
   oesophageal reflux disease, constipation, diarrhoea, GI bleeding and colorectal cancer.

Gastro-oesophageal Reflux Disease
 • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is very common in US adults; prevalence rises with age, and a
   substantial minority of sufferers have weekly symptoms.
 • Reflux of gastric contents into the oesophagus or beyond (oral cavity, lungs): pain, cough, wheeze,
   hoarseness, sore throat, oesophageal or chest fullness → distress, lost productivity and sleep.
 • Mechanism: most likely increased relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LES), worsening with
   age and comorbidity; nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use increases LES relaxation.
 • Drugs damaging oesophageal mucosa: NSAIDs, potassium, iron supplements, bisphosphonates.
 • Typical presentation: heartburn or acid regurgitation — history can suffice for diagnosis. Atypical
   symptoms prompt investigation: chest pain, cough, wheeze, hoarseness, sore throat, intestinal
   symptoms, laryngitis, recurrent aspiration.
Evaluation: trial of proton pump inhibitor (PPI) or histamine H2-receptor blocker → symptoms persist →
referral for oesophageal pH studies or upper endoscopy.
 • Goals: resolve symptoms and prevent complications.
 • Lifestyle modification is the cornerstone: weight loss, tobacco cessation, selective dietary restriction
   (coffee, highly spiced and acidic food), elevate the head of the bed, no oral intake for 3 hours before
   lying down, withdraw aggravating medications.
 • Medical mainstays: PPIs and H2 blockers (both over the counter). Long-term use → primary care or
   gastroenterology review to identify Barrett oesophagus (premalignant) and mitigate oesophageal
   adenocarcinoma risk.

Chronic Diarrhoea and Constipation
 • Both are exceptionally common in ageing men and interface with male voiding dysfunction → relevant to
   every urology provider.

Occult Gastrointestinal Bleeding and Iron Deficiency Anaemia
 • Occult GI bleeding is common and significant in ageing men; it can arise anywhere in the GI tract, and the
   faecal occult blood test is often the primary indicator.
 • Blood loss → iron deficiency anaemia (IDA), which carries a more than thirtyfold increased risk of GI
   malignancy compared with normal iron status.
 • IDA rises with age — present in more than a quarter of men over 85.
 • Anaemia → frailty, reduced physical performance, falls, cognitive decline, dementia, hospitalisation,
   death.

Colorectal Cancer Screening and Surveillance
 • Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer in men and among the leading causes of male
   cancer death in the United States.
 • Incidence rises with age and is higher in males than females in every age cohort.
 • High incidence and mortality plus highly effective screening → screening from age 45 (USPSTF and other
   bodies), continued through age 75; beyond 75, shared decision making.
 • Recent decline in CRC mortality is attributed to improved screening over two decades.

Conclusions
 • GI conditions are common in men, rise with age, impair quality of life and function, and are costly; they
   often present with urologic disease, so a working knowledge is needed by men's health providers.
Concept — Bowel and bladder share risk factors, drugs and presentations: constipation drives UTI and
retention, anticholinergics cause constipation, and iron deficiency is a GI malignancy signal until proven
otherwise.

DERMATOLOGY IN THE AGEING MALE
 • About one-third of the US population sees a physician for a skin condition each year; burden rises with
   age, and those 65 or older have nearly double the skin problems of younger patients.
 • Skin cancer is commonly detected at routine examination; benign conditions also affect quality of life.

Skin Ageing
 • Men show accelerated skin ageing and are perceived as older than women of similar age.
 • Photoageing = age-related skin change worsened by ultraviolet (UV) light; men are more susceptible —
   more sunburn, half as likely to use sunscreen routinely.
 • Signs: wrinkling, dyspigmentation, telangiectasia — collectively dermatoheliosis.
 • UV reduces collagen production and worsens collagen degradation; ageing skin handles reactive oxygen
   species less well → inflammation; tobacco smoking adds further.
 • Prevention: daily sunscreen (randomised controlled trials show it slows or prevents photoageing).
   Treatment: retinoids — epidermal thickening, increased collagen production, reduced UV-induced
   collagen degradation.
 • Impaired wound healing: epidermal thinning plus elastin degradation → fragility, reduced tensile
   strength, slow healing.

Common Dermatoses
 • Xerosis (dry skin): the commonest dermatologic condition in ageing men, affecting more than half of
   those 65 and over. Severe: red, cracked, adherent plate-like scale, pruritus; severe scaling = asteatotic

 • Treatment: restore the stratum corneum and moisture — less bathing, emollients, avoid cold dry
   environments, humidify in winter; topical steroids once eczema develops.

Disorders of Skin Appendages: Nails and Hair
 • Ageing nails: thicker, altered contour, texture and colour — probably from poorer peripheral circulation
   and recurrent nail-bed trauma.
 • Onychomycosis: fungal nail infection, commoner in men and with age; risk factors peripheral vascular
   disease and diabetes. Nail-plate thickening, lifting from the bed, subungual debris.
 • Diagnosis before treatment: curette debris → direct microscopy after potassium hydroxide → fungal
   hyphae.
 • Treatment not mandatory; recommended for discomfort, infection or chronic immunosuppression. First
   line: oral terbinafine 250 mg daily — 6 weeks fingernails, 12 weeks toenails.
 • Alopecia is a marker of generalised senescence, typically after 50. Androgenetic alopecia (male pattern
   baldness) affects up to half of men by 50; strong genetic component and established androgen role.
 • First line: 5α-reductase inhibitors (reduce dihydrotestosterone) and topical minoxidil — reduce or halt
   progression but do not restore lost hair. Hair transplantation (follicle harvest and transfer) has advanced
   considerably.

Cosmetic Treatments and Procedures in the Ageing Male
 • Men are increasingly high users — roughly a tenth of all nonsurgical cosmetic procedures, a marked rise.
 • Botulinum toxin: the most performed procedure in men, for facial wrinkles; periorbital and forehead.
   Greater facial muscle mass → lower doses may fail → higher doses → higher risk of brow ptosis.
 • Soft tissue fillers (second commonest): hyaluronic acid, calcium hydroxylapatite, poly-L-lactic acid,
   polymethyl methacrylate — soft-tissue augmentation and feature enhancement.
 • Topical retinoic acid is first-line for photoageing: increases collagen, induces epidermal hyperplasia,
   decreases keratinocyte and melanocyte atypia; initial local irritation.

Conclusion
 • Dermatologic conditions are common in men and cause morbidity and mortality: benign conditions can
   be managed by men's health providers; severe conditions need immediate dermatology referral.
Concept — UV exposure and age generate most of the male skin burden, and men photoage faster yet use
less sunscreen — so prevention dominates, and triage divides benign disease managed in house from
malignancy referred at once.

PSYCHIATRIC DISORDERS IN MALES
 • Psychiatric disorders differ significantly in prevalence, type and severity between the sexes, partly
   through testosterone effects on brain structure and function.
 • Endogenous testosterone is strongly associated with mood disorders, and exogenous testosterone may
   have a treatment role → men's health requires working psychiatric knowledge.

Testosterone and the Male Brain
 • Lower male rates of depression and anxiety suggest a protective effect of testosterone.
 • Androgen receptors are dense in the hippocampus, amygdala and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex —
   regions regulating mood, arousal, stress and memory.
 • Prenatal and perinatal testosterone has organisational and activational effects; across life it influences
   neuroplasticity and neurotransmitter regulation.

 • Opioids: use has reached epidemic levels; chronic opioid use causes testosterone deficiency in about 5
   million US men. Endocrine Society: screen men on chronic opioids for testosterone deficiency and
   evaluate for testosterone replacement therapy, which relieves symptoms in some.

Major Depressive Disorders
 • Affect about 5% of the US population; lower prevalence in men but markedly higher suicide.
 • Suicide risk factors: age, race, veteran status, psychosocial stress, access to lethal means, comorbid
   conditions → psychological screening and care in the multidisciplinary setting.
 • Testosterone declines from middle age; low testosterone in about a fifth of men in primary care. It also
   falls with stress, medications, obesity, inactivity, diabetes and illness — the same factors that raise
   depression.
 • Symptom overlap between testosterone deficiency and depression: low libido, irritability, anorexia, poor
   concentration, fatigue.
 • Epidemiologic studies: low testosterone associated with depressive symptoms and illness; clinical trials
   of testosterone for depression conflicting (differing populations, doses, durations).
 • Likeliest responders: clearly testosterone-deficient, younger than 60, less severe depression.
 • Testosterone is not recommended as routine treatment for depressive illness, particularly major
   depression; responsive subgroups remain to be defined.

Conclusions
 • Testosterone and its metabolites affect neurogenesis, neurotransmitters, cerebral perfusion and
   metabolism; testosterone falls with age and comorbidity; deficiency is a risk factor for incident
   depression, but therapy is not recommended because trial results conflict.
Concept — Association without proven treatment effect: low testosterone and depression share causes and
symptoms, so the correlation is strong, but replacement is not a treatment for depression.

UROLOGIC DISORDERS OF FOCUS IN MEN'S HEALTH
 • Topics on which urologists are the expert treating clinicians, covered in depth elsewhere: testosterone
   deficiency, male infertility, ED and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) — the primary focus of care in the
   modern men's health centre.

Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia and Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms
 • BPH = nonmalignant enlargement of the prostate; a predominant cause of lower urinary tract symptoms
   (LUTS) in ageing men; high prevalence → evaluation in both urology and primary care.
 • Symptoms vary and may not be prostatic → treatment focuses on symptom relief, not on a specific
   cause.
 • Management has shifted from surgical to medical → primary care physicians now manage most
   patients.
 • Refer to urology when medical therapy is insufficient, intolerable or not desired. Transurethral resection
   or ablation remains a mainstay, with many newer minimally invasive technologies.

Erectile Dysfunction
 • Affects more than half of men aged 40–70.
 • Multifactorial → systematic medical, surgical, psychosocial and exposure history for diagnosis and a goal-
   oriented plan.
 • Every man with ED, whatever his age, is considered at risk of CVD, even without other symptoms.
 • Counsel all on physical activity and diet; some men and couples benefit from a sexual health therapist.
Treatment sequence: oral phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) inhibitors (unless taking nitrates or physically
unable to undertake sexual activity) → if inadequate or not tolerated, local therapy: intracavernosal injection
of single or combined vasodilators, or intraurethral suppository; vacuum erection device as primary or
adjunct therapy in selected men → penile prosthesis, which has the highest satisfaction and should be
considered in all men with ED, after discussion of irreversibility and implant loss from malfunction or
infection.
 • Non–FDA-approved therapies (platelet-rich plasma, low-intensity shock wave therapy) only within an
   experimental protocol.

Male Infertility
 • Definition: failure to achieve pregnancy after 1 year of reasonable effort (World Health Organization,
   1991).
 • Affects 15–20% of reproductive-age couples; male factors contribute in about half.
 • Evaluation: general and reproductive history including the partner's age; gonad-focused examination;
   semen analysis — the cornerstone test.
 • Further testing as indicated: serum reproductive hormones, genetic testing, ultrasonography, cross-
   sectional imaging, specialised semen tests, testicular biopsy.
 • Some causes are treatable medically or surgically; idiopathic cases may be neither identified nor treated
   → close partnership with reproductive endocrinologists.

Testosterone Deficiency and Hypogonadism
 • Serum testosterone falls with age from both testicular and hypothalamic-pituitary defects; many
   attributed symptoms overlap with age-related comorbidity.
 • Before testosterone replacement therapy (TRT): exclude secondary causes and perform confirmatory
   testing.

CONCLUSIONS
 • Convenience and access have become central to health-care delivery.
 • Men are under-represented among seekers of preventive care, yet carry more comorbidity across most
   disease categories and die younger.
 • Men who avoid routine care will seek care for problems "below the belt" — sexual dysfunction,
   infertility, urinary control.
 • The urologist keeps genitourinary expertise central but must hold a fundamental understanding of
   disease in multiple organ systems for initial evaluation and triage, backed by referral networks and
   partners in other specialties.
Concept — The chapter turns on one asymmetry: men avoid preventive care but not urologic care, so the
urologist becomes the entry point who must triage the whole man.
