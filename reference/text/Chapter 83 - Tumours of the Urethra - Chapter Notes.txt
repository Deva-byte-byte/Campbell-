 • Rare, documented almost entirely in small case reports; commonest are leiomyoma, haemangioma

 MALE URETHRAL CANCER

 Epidemiology, Aetiology and Presentation
   • Male primary urethral cancer (PUC) is rare: 1.3–4.3 per million males, manifesting after the sixth
     decade, more common in Black than White patients.
   • Chronic urethral inflammation underlies most anterior PUC. Stricture disease is the commonest risk
     factor, present in up to 50%, with latency to cancer of 20–30 years — hence sustained suspicion rather
     than discharge from follow-up.
   • Other risk factors: sexually transmitted infection, lichen sclerosus, urethritis, pelvic radiotherapy,
     trauma, instrumentation, human papillomavirus 16, arsenic.
   • Presentation: obstructive voiding symptoms, haematuria or bloody urethral discharge, penile or
     perineal mass. Nearly all are symptomatic at presentation.

 Pathology
   • Male urethra ~20 cm. Anterior = bulbar + pendulous + fossa navicularis; posterior = prostatic +
     membranous.
   • Epithelial sequence: posterior urothelium → anterior stratified and pseudostratified columnar →
     fossa navicularis stratified squamous.
   • Glands: paired bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands into the bulbar urethra; periurethral glands of Littré
     along the anterior urethra.
   • Histology overall: urothelial 50–80%, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) 10–30%, adenocarcinoma 5–
     10%; rare types melanoma and sarcoma.
   • Registry data overstate the urothelial share through misclassification and miscoding; in institutional
     series urothelial carcinoma is the minority histology.
   • Site–histology pair: posterior cancers mostly urothelial; up to 80% of anterior cancers are SCC.
     Adenocarcinoma relatively more common in the bulbar urethra.
   • Spread: direct extension into corpus spongiosum and periurethral tissues; lymphatics anterior →
     inguinal nodes (bulbar may also drain to external iliac), posterior → pelvic nodes.
   • Palpable inguinal nodes occur in 20–30% and almost always mean metastasis, unlike penile cancer,
     where they are frequently inflammatory — so nodes in PUC are not observed through antibiotics.

 Evaluation and Staging
   • Examination under anaesthesia with cystoscopy and bimanual palpation of external genitalia,
     urethra, rectum, perineum and inguinal nodes is required for clinical stage.
   • Tissue diagnosis by transurethral or percutaneous needle biopsy. Urine cytology has poor sensitivity
     and little utility.
   • MRI is optimal for local extent; CT or MRI of abdomen and pelvis for metastasis; CT chest in invasive
     disease.

 MALE ANTERIOR URETHRAL CANCER

 Prognosis
   • Across all males with PUC: 5-year cancer-specific survival 50–70%, overall survival 40–50%. Survival
     associated with grade, stage, location, histology.

   • Anterior tumours: 60% bulbar, 30% pendulous. Distal tumours often curable; bulbar survival as low as
     20–30%.
   • Two reproducible gradients — low stage over high stage, distal over proximal — each a twofold to
     threefold separation.
   • Proximal tumours present at higher stage, explaining much of the location effect, but stage remains
     independently prognostic after controlling for location: grade and location act largely through stage.
   • Adenocarcinoma in males may carry a more favourable prognosis.

 Treatment
   • Aggressive local control is paramount; method varies by location; interest in multimodal treatment
     grows with stage.
 Carcinoma of the Pendulous Urethra
  • Pendulous and fossa navicularis tumours are usually resectable. Options escalating with size, location
    and stage: transurethral resection → distal urethrectomy ± partial penectomy → total urethrectomy
    with perineal urethrostomy ± total penectomy.
   • Surgical monotherapy achieves local control in distal tumours (small case series); low-stage fossa
     navicularis tumours cured by distal urethrectomy alone.
   • Penectomy gives local control in nearly all pendulous tumours, but systemic relapse exceeds that of
     fossa navicularis tumours — within the anterior urethra, more proximal origin means greater systemic
     threat at equal local control.
 Penile preservation.
   • Historically urethral resection with radical or partial penectomy; excision with penile preservation is
     now an option in selected patients, endorsed by the European Association of Urology (EAU) and
     National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN).
   • Techniques, by location and stage: distal hypospadias with topical 5-fluorouracil (5-FU); distal
     urethrectomy with two-stage urethroplasty; glansectomy with skin graft; anterior urethrectomy with
     perineal urethrostomy.
   • Positive margins treated by further surgery or adjuvant radiotherapy, with no local recurrence —
     preservation costs no local control if margins are pursued.
 Carcinoma in situ.
   • SCC in situ of the perimeatal glans extends into the distal urethra; treated by partial glansectomy and
     distal urethrectomy with reconstruction or penile urethrostomy once the margin is negative.
   • Distal urethral carcinoma in situ (CIS) risks upstaging to invasive or node-positive disease — thorough
     staging and aggressive therapy are critical despite the label.
 Margins.
   • 1 cm negative margin traditional; 5 mm gives excellent local control and is the contemporary
     standard.
   • Total penectomy if invasive disease reaches the proximal penile urethra; assess proximal extent
     before contemplating partial urethrectomy.
   • Local recurrence after distal resection is low, but surveillance continues.
 Inguinal lymph node management.
   • Unlike penile cancer, prophylactic inguinal lymphadenectomy has no established survival benefit in
     cN0 anterior PUC, and no predictors of nodal metastasis exist in cN0 disease to select patients.

   • Largest squamous PUC analysis: only a small minority of cN0 groins prove pN1, most cN1 groins do;
     pN1 strongly associated with overall survival; lymphadenectomy benefited cN1 but not cN0 patients.
   • Sentinel node biopsy has been used to detect low-volume metastasis, proceeding to
     lymphadenectomy if positive. Low-volume inguinal metastasis can be rendered disease-free by
     lymphadenectomy — the rationale for aggressive nodal treatment.
   • Prophylactic lymphadenectomy for advanced pendulous PUC with cN0 groins remains controversial.
     If the primary is irradiated and nodes are cN0, prophylactic nodal irradiation should be strongly
     considered.
 Locally advanced or node-positive distal disease.
   • Surgery ± systemic therapy has variable success; cure with limited nodal disease is reported, so
     systemic therapy then inguinal lymphadenectomy should be considered for palpable nodes, with
     chemoradiotherapy an alternative.
   • Nodal control also prevents skin breakdown, wound drainage and vascular erosion — an indication in
     its own right.
 Carcinoma of the Bulbar Urethra
  • Low-stage proximal anterior lesions may be treated by transurethral resection or segmental excision
    with end-to-end anastomosis, with repeat transurethral resection considered for staging accuracy and
    completeness.
   • Few are eligible — bulbar PUC presents at advanced stage and local control is difficult.
   • Standard for advanced bulbar cancer: radical cystoprostatectomy, pelvic lymphadenectomy, total
     urethrectomy and penectomy, with resection of pubic rami and adjacent urogenital diaphragm if
     extent demands.
   • Bladder preservation in selected cases: urethrectomy with perineal urethrostomy, or bladder neck
     closure with a continent catheterisable stoma; weighed against local relapse and dissemination.
   • Positive margins or local recurrence → chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy.
 Technique — radical cystectomy with total urethrectomy and penectomy.
   1. Dorsal lithotomy, for simultaneous perineal access.
   2. Transabdominal mobilisation of the bladder, preserving endopelvic fascia and anterior pubic
      attachments.
   3. Modified lambda-shaped or inverted U-shaped perineal incision, apex mid-perineum.
   4. Inferior flap mobilised by dividing subcutaneous tissue and rectourethral muscle.
   5. Superior flap to superficial Colles fascia, then bilaterally to the adductors at the inferior pubic rami.
   6. Develop the ischiorectal fossae as in perineal prostatectomy; tunnel bluntly anterior to the rectum,
      fossa to fossa.
   7. Circumferential incision of skin and dartos at the penoscrotal junction; mobilise the corpora a short
      distance proximally to permit inferior pubectomy — not too far, or the anterior aspect of a locally
      advanced tumour is breached.
   8. Pass the penis downward through the perineal incision.
   9. Divide the scrotum in the midline for exposure if needed; testicles usually preserved.
 10. Deliver the specimen en bloc.
   • Surgery alone: five-year overall survival for proximal tumours is about one-quarter, few disease-free
     — the basis for declaring surgery alone inadequate in advanced PUC, though more aggressive bulbar
     resection may fare better.

   • A negative margin may require resection of testicles, inferior pubic rami and pelvic floor musculature,
     with myocutaneous vascularised pedicle flaps and orthopaedic and plastic surgical collaboration.
   • Pelvic nodal involvement confers high risk of recurrence and death.
 Chemoradiotherapy and Radiotherapy
  • Chemoradiotherapy ± surgical consolidation is an alternative to resection in advanced disease: 45–55
    Gy external beam to external genitalia, perineum and inguinal nodes with systemic 5-FU and
    mitomycin C (MMC).
   • Largest male series: mostly clinical T3 or N1, majority achieved complete response including some
     node-positive patients. Complete response rates are similar proximally and distally — response does
     not follow the surgical prognostic gradient.
   • Consolidative surgery frequently shows ypT0, confirming true pathological response, though its role
     after complete response is unsettled.
   • Qualifications: about a third of complete responders recurred locally and some could not be salvaged;
     every responder developed a urethral stricture, often needing complex reconstruction; non-
     responders died of disease; surgery, not chemoradiotherapy, is recommended for low-stage distal
     PUC.
   • Given the morbidity of surgery for advanced bulbar PUC, chemoradiotherapy is reasonable in
     selected patients and is preferred for advanced squamous cell PUC. Radiotherapy monotherapy is
     ineffective for bulbar PUC.
   • Primary radiotherapy: only for early-stage anterior lesions in patients refusing surgery, using parallel
     opposed fields with the penis suspended vertically by a urethral catheter. Advantage penile
     preservation; complications skin ulceration and necrosis, urethral stricture, chronic oedema; long-
     term results hard to evaluate as male series are few.
 Multimodal Therapy and Systemic Chemotherapy
  • Poor outcomes after surgery alone, particularly bulbar, make surgical monotherapy inadequate in
    advanced PUC. Multimodal therapy = surgery plus perioperative chemotherapy ± radiotherapy.
   • More likely delivered at high-volume academic centres; referral recommended. Uptake: only a small
     minority of advanced patients receive multimodal therapy, though about a third of contemporary
     cohorts receive perioperative chemotherapy.
   • Cisplatin-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy gives complete or partial
     response in a substantial minority, and prognosis correlates highly with response.
   • Chemotherapy before surgery or radiotherapy gave better survival than surgery alone or surgery plus
     adjuvant chemotherapy; all locally advanced or node-positive patients so treated in that series were
     alive at five years.
   • Postoperative radiotherapy reaches few patients but improves survival in advanced disease;
     intraoperative radiotherapy may help where a negative margin is unobtainable.
   • Medical-oncology referral cohort (majority female, SCC and adenocarcinoma predominant, almost all
     ≥T3): histology-directed platinum regimens gave complete or partial response in the majority, about
     half had surgical consolidation, and median overall survival was markedly better with surgery. Some
     node-positive patients were rendered disease-free by neoadjuvant chemotherapy then surgery.
   • Neoadjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy is reasonable for high-risk and locally advanced PUC
     (EAU, NCCN); adjuvant systemic therapy is an option after surgery for locally advanced disease.

 Guideline Position, Recurrence and Metastatic Disease
  • Non-metastatic advanced bulbar PUC: radical surgery ± perioperative chemotherapy,
    chemoradiotherapy, or radiotherapy monotherapy. Clinically node-positive disease:
    chemoradiotherapy or chemotherapy followed by surgical consolidation.
   • There is no high-quality comparative effectiveness research on treatment options for male PUC.
   • At least half of advanced patients given perioperative chemotherapy recur; risk of local or systemic
     recurrence after resection of invasive disease approaches 50%, requiring regular urethral, inguinal
     and systemic surveillance. Commonest distant recurrence: lung and extrapelvic nodes. Non-
     metastatic local recurrence can be salvaged by surgery or radiotherapy.
   • At presentation: 10–20% clinically node-positive, 5–20% metastatic, most often non-regional nodes,
     liver, lung, bone. Long-term survival is reported with cisplatin-based chemotherapy plus excision of
     the primary — metastatic disease is not uniformly palliative.
   • Genomics: microsatellite instability absent in all tumours tested, but increased programmed death-
     ligand 1 (PD-L1) staining and tumour mutational burden, particularly in urothelial and squamous
     cancers; dramatic responses to checkpoint inhibition reported in programmed death-1 (PD-1)/PD-L1-
     positive metastatic disease.

 MALE POSTERIOR URETHRAL CANCER

 Prognosis
   • Prostatic urethral carcinoma is usually found in patients with a history of bladder cancer, not as a
     primary event.
   • 20–40% with aggressive bladder cancer have synchronous prostatic involvement at radical
     cystectomy (RC); up to 39% of high-risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) recurs in the
     prostate after intravesical therapy.
   • Reported synchronous involvement in NMIBC varies from 3% to 30% — a methodological range,
     because prostatic urethral biopsies are not consistently taken at transurethral resection of bladder
     tumour (TURBT).
   • Primary prostatic urethral carcinoma without bladder cancer is rare — under 4% of all prostate
     cancers; therefore urothelial carcinoma found in the prostate mandates evaluation for coexisting
     bladder and upper tract disease.
   • May involve urothelium, ducts, acini or stroma; diagnosed by transurethral resection of the prostate
     (TURP); commonly presents as CIS. Staged by the same TNM system, with CT or MRI required.
   • Survival: superficial or ductal involvement 67–74%; stromal involvement 36%. With stromal invasion,
     prognosis follows the bladder tumour stage — about 61% with T1 bladder tumours versus under 30%
     with muscle-invasive or locally advanced disease.

 Treatment
   • Management derives from bladder cancer practice. Historically prostatic involvement was called T4
     and managed by RC, until it was recognised that not all such patients do badly and many can be
     managed less aggressively.
   • High-grade superficial disease treated with BCG achieves prostatic response of at least 70%.
     Extensive TURP before BCG removes most cancerous urothelium, improves BCG exposure, may raise
     response rates and ensures staging — though BCG without TURP remains an option.
   • With extensive endoscopic resection plus intravesical BCG, roughly half achieve complete response but
     a substantial minority progress, some requiring RC — with no progression events within the prostate
     itself.

   • Stromal invasion — radical cystectomy, urethrectomy and pelvic lymphadenectomy ± perioperative

 FEMALE URETHRAL CANCER

 Epidemiology, Aetiology and Presentation
   • Fewer than 100 cases annually in the United States; incidence 0.5–1.5 per million females; about
     one-third of all PUC, and less common in females than males.
   • Two-thirds are over 60; incidence higher in Black females and may be falling. European registries
     confirm rarity, advanced age at presentation and male predominance.
   • Risk factors: leukoplakia, glandular metaplasia, chronic irritation, stricture disease, recurrent urinary
     infection, caruncle, human papillomavirus infection.
   • Urethral diverticula predispose — 6–10% harbour a malignancy.

 Prognosis
   • Aggressive disease — five-year survival 40–65% — but females have no independently increased risk
     of death versus males; the worse crude outcome reflects later stage at presentation.
   • Stage is the dominant prognostic factor: the earliest report held that survival is determined chiefly by
     extent of neoplasm, and all later work confirms it. Independently adverse: higher stage, positive
     nodes, pan-urethral location, Black race, increased age, non-squamous histology.
   • Location gradient distal > proximal > pan-urethral, about a threefold spread between distal and pan-
     urethral; but proximal and pan-urethral tumours are more often higher stage.
   • Size gradient at <2 cm, 2–4 cm, >4 cm, survival falling steeply — though size correlates with location
     and stage and the analysis did not adjust for stage.
   • Unifying statement: stage correlates with grade, size, location and nodal status, and stage drives
     prognosis.
   • Histological subtype is contested: several series find no difference and advise identical treatment
     regardless of histology; two large population studies found SCC has a lower risk of death than non-
     squamous histologies; one cohort ranked SCC ≈ urothelial > adenocarcinoma. Urethral melanoma has
     a uniformly poor prognosis.
   • Many historical series predate cross-sectional imaging and are incompletely staged — a caveat on
     every figure above.

 Treatment
   • Evidence is small institutional cohorts and case reports; there is no standardised algorithm. Options:
     surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, alone or combined. About 40% receive radiotherapy in some
     form.
   • Governing principle: local excision may suffice for small low-stage anterior tumours; proximal and
     advanced tumours require an aggressive approach.
 Anterior Tumours — Surgery
  • Anterior tumours tend to be low stage, and at least 70% are cured by local excision alone.
   • Small non-invasive tumours — endoscopic resection or laser fulguration ± adjuvant BCG; if not
     endoscopically amenable — surgical resection or radiotherapy.
   • Small exophytic anterior tumours excised with a portion of anterior vaginal wall transvaginally, with
     frozen section of the proximal urethra to confirm a negative margin.
   • Outcomes: partial urethrectomy or endoscopic resection gives good five-year disease-specific
     survival, but about one in five recur locally and many cannot be salvaged.
   • Complications: new or worsening stress urinary incontinence in over 40% despite the anatomical
     teaching; meatal stenosis, minimised by wide spatulation.
   • Bladder-sparing options in selected advanced anterior tumours: radical urethrectomy with bladder
     neck closure and a catheterisable stoma, or ileovesicostomy, if a negative margin is achievable.

   • Radical urethrectomy extent: all periurethral tissue — laterally to the bulbocavernosus muscles,
     anteriorly to the pubic symphysis, superiorly to the bladder neck, including the anterior vaginal wall.
 Anterior Tumours — Radiotherapy
  • Effective for low-stage anterior tumours, and the alternative when urethral preservation is desired or
    surgery would compromise function.
   • External beam, brachytherapy or both, at 55–70 Gy; distal tumours commonly brachytherapy alone
     at a median 60 Gy, with good five-year survival when only part of the urethra is involved.
   • Extent of urethral involvement was the only variable predicting local control — length involved, not
     histology or size.
   • Complications 20–50%: urethral stricture, necrosis, fistula, cystitis. Higher cumulative dose raises
     risk; contemporary technique lowers it.
 Inguinal Lymph Nodes
   • Up to a third present with or develop inguinal adenopathy; up to 20% recur in inguinal nodes, usually
     within two years.
   • As in males, no clear role for prophylactic inguinal lymphadenectomy in cN0 high-risk anterior
     tumours, and unlike penile and vulval cancer, no prognostic factors predict inguinal metastasis.
     Precedent exists in vulval SCC, and further study has been advocated.
   • Prophylactic groin radiotherapy should be considered where the primary is irradiated; it appears to
     reduce inguinal recurrence, though recurrences still occur despite it.
   • Palpable inguinal nodes require aggressive treatment — usually metastatic, and some patients are
     curable. Guideline options: chemoradiotherapy, chemotherapy alone, inguinal lymphadenectomy, or
     chemoradiotherapy followed by consolidative surgery. Lymphadenectomy technique is identical to
     that for penile cancer.
   • Pelvic adenopathy carries a poor prognosis and patients usually die of disease; benefit of pelvic
     lymphadenectomy for clinically positive pelvic nodes is unclear.
 Posterior and Pan-urethral Tumours
  • More likely high stage, involving the whole urethra and invading bladder, vagina and external
    genitalia; five-year metastasis-free survival only 24–48% despite aggressive surgery or radiotherapy.
   • Usually requires anterior pelvic exenteration, which alone gives low cure rates and significant local
     recurrence — the majority treated by radical surgery alone died of disease and about a third recurred
     in the pelvis. Pelvic recurrence usually proves fatal.
   • Components: radical cystectomy + urethrectomy + extended pelvic lymphadenectomy + wide or
     complete vaginal excision where margins demand; hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-
     oophorectomy case by case; partial vulvectomy or labial excision if the lesion reaches the external
     genitalia.
 Perineal portion of anterior exenteration.
   1. Inverted U-shaped incision widely encircling the urethral meatus.
   2. Extend onto the posterior vaginal wall and labia minora, continuing anteriorly to include the clitoris
      where required.
   3. En bloc resection of pubic symphysis and inferior pubic rami if a negative pubic margin is otherwise

   • Radiotherapy for proximal tumours: similarly poor local control, median survival under three years;

 Multimodal Therapy and Systemic Chemotherapy
  • Rationale: surgery and radiotherapy have similarly low local control and poor survival when used
    alone.
   • At least 20% receive combined surgery and radiotherapy, and this is rising; multimodal therapy is
     associated with longer overall survival, though not significantly so in the institutional series examining
     it. Neoadjuvant radiotherapy then surgery may give better local control than either alone.
   • Academic centres deliver more multimodal therapy and radical surgery; referral of advanced disease
     is recommended.
   • Guidelines recommend multimodal therapy for advanced-stage female PUC — systemic
     chemotherapy with consolidative radiotherapy or surgery, or radiotherapy followed by surgery, with
     chemotherapy dictated by histology as tabulated above.
   • Systemic chemotherapy for metastatic disease; commonest distant sites lung, bone and brain.
   • Evidence is poor and confounded by treatment indication, so treatment is individualised — but
     multimodal treatment should be strongly considered in advanced disease.

   KEY POINTS: FEMALE URETHRAL CANCER
     • The three commonest histologies are urothelial carcinoma, SCC and adenocarcinoma.
     • Compared with anterior urethral cancers, posterior cancers present at a more advanced stage and
       are associated with worse survival.
     • Radiation and surgical excision are both options for low-stage anterior tumours, each with high
       cure rates.
     • Surgery and radiotherapy for proximal female urethral tumours have poor outcomes when used
       alone; multimodal therapy is recommended.

 Concept — everything is decided by one anatomical line: the distal third can be removed and the proximal
 two-thirds cannot, so distal disease has two curative options of equal standing and proximal disease has
 no adequate single modality at all.

 URETHRAL RECURRENCE AFTER RADICAL CYSTECTOMY

 The Male Urethra
   • After RC the remnant urothelium of urethra and upper tracts remains at risk, by direct growth along
     the urothelium from the primary or metachronous development from a field defect.
   • Early practice was prophylactic urethrectomy, abandoned because 64–100% of prophylactically
     removed urethras showed no disease, and where present it was most commonly CIS; even identified
     at-risk patients mostly had negative pathology.
   • Three forces drove the shift to urethral preservation: low prevalence of unrecognised synchronous
     involvement, added morbidity of urethrectomy, growing use of orthotopic diversion.
   • Risk of urethral recurrence (UR) after RC is 1–15%, larger series near 5%. Timing: most within two
     years; about 11% after five years; rarely as late as fifteen.

   • Risk factors: bladder tumour multifocality, CIS, history of NMIBC, prostatic involvement with
     urothelial carcinoma, positive urethral margin, cutaneous diversion. Risk rises with the number
     present, but applying all of them would exclude as many as 70% from orthotopic neobladder (ONB)
     eligibility — so only the highest-risk patients undergo prophylactic urethrectomy.
 Prostatic Involvement, Margin and Diversion
  • Prostate involved in 17–40% of RC specimens, most often prostatic urethra or ducts, sometimes
    stroma. Predictors: recurrent bladder tumours, trigone or bladder neck tumours, multifocality.
   • Prostatic urethral involvement raises UR risk roughly threefold — 15% versus 5%; some groups advise
     against ONB where present.
   • Preoperative transurethral prostatic biopsy has good sensitivity and high negative predictive value
     for prostatic involvement, but correlates poorly with the final urethral margin — the diagnosis it
     makes is not the question asked — so it has largely been abandoned in favour of intraoperative frozen
     section.
   • A positive urethral margin occurs in up to 7% of RC specimens and is a strong UR risk factor, in one
     cohort the strongest after controlling for prostatic involvement and stage. Many URs occur without a
     positive apical margin and some groups found no significant association, so other factors contribute.
   • Most surgeons proceed with ONB if frozen section of the distal prostatic urethral margin is normal;
     frozen section is accurate and a positive result can often be converted with further resection.
   • Dissenting view: most patients with a positive margin never develop a UR, and frozen section itself
     risks urethral shortening and false positives; one ONB cohort used none, with a low UR rate and no
     death from UR.
   • The American Urological Association Muscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer guideline recommends
     verifying a negative urethral margin before ONB, and concern for invasive carcinoma at the margin
     mandates urethrectomy rather than ONB.
   • ONB carries lower UR risk than cutaneous diversion — about 5% versus 9% at five years, a halved
     relative risk. Beyond selection bias, proposed mechanisms are altered local immune response,
     antineoplastic effects of ileum, continued exposure of the urethra to urine.
   • In the largest cohort, after adjustment for stage and margin, significant associations were ONB
     (protective), CIS, prostatic urethral involvement, bladder tumour multifocality.
 Prophylactic Urethrectomy
  • Relative, advocated by some: multifocal bladder tumours, upper tract tumours, diffuse CIS, prostatic
    urethral involvement, bladder neck involvement.
   • Absolute: positive urethral margin or gross urethral involvement.
   • Staged urethrectomy after RC has outcomes similar to planned urethrectomy at RC — timing is not
     critical.
   • Total urethrectomy after RC with cutaneous diversion is challenging, particularly excising the
     proximal urethra from the pelvic floor; that segment is at risk of positive margins, and incomplete
     resection causes retropubic relapse that is difficult to manage.
 Presentation, Surveillance and Treatment
  • URs present with bloody urethral discharge or a palpable mass, tending to be proximal but occurring
    anywhere.
   • Cytology: voided sample with an ONB, catheterised urethral barbotage with a cutaneous diversion.
     Screening cytology has not been shown to improve survival — no difference between symptomatic
     and cytologically detected recurrences, though symptomatic patients more often had invasive disease

     at urethrectomy. Guidelines still recommend considering urethral wash cytology for two years after
     RC.
   • Positive cytology or symptoms (bleeding, discharge, pain, palpable mass) require urethroscopy and
     biopsy; false positives occur, so confirm before treating. Pelvic CT or MRI and full restaging.
   • Total urethrectomy is the treatment of choice, and the entire urethra must go — incomplete removal
     of the distal urethra leaves a risk of distal recurrence.
   • Urethrectomy with a cutaneous diversion risks a positive proximal margin from the scarred pelvic
     floor. After orthotopic diversion, radical urethrectomy generally requires conversion to a cutaneous
     diversion, though subtotal urethrectomy with perineal urethrostomy is described.
   • Non-invasive URs: endoscopic resection and topical therapy. Intraurethral BCG is cumbersome but
     has high response rates for urethral CIS — not for papillary or invasive disease. Significant risk of a
     second UR after endoscopic management. Radiotherapy has also been used.
   • Prognosis is conflicting — a large meta-analysis found no impact of UR on survival, a large single-
     institution cohort found reduced cancer-specific survival. The split that matters: isolated non-invasive
     UR has a favourable prognosis and is usually salvageable; invasive UR is aggressive with median
     survival 17 months. At least one-third have concomitant pelvic or distant recurrence, and then
     prognosis is poor.

   1. High lithotomy, hips and knees gently flexed, limbs abducted in stirrups; urethral catheter aids

      inside out onto the perineum; continue to the base of the glans.
   5. Replace the penis anatomically to excise meatus and glandular urethra; incise around the meatus,
      down each side of the ventral glans.

   6. Free the distal urethra from its investments within the glans; deliver the pendulous urethra onto the

   7. Reapproximate the deep spongiosum of the glans with 4-0 polydioxanone; close the surface with
      interrupted 4-0 chromic.
   8. Sharp proximal dissection of the bulb posteriorly and laterally, close to the bulb but avoiding entry.

 10. Excise completely the ill-defined fibrotic band remaining of the membranous urethra; frozen section
     confirms a negative proximal margin.
 11. Suction drain in the urethral bed through the perineum; close bulbospongiosus, subcutaneous tissue
     and skin with interrupted absorbable sutures; light pressure dressing.

 Total Urethrectomy After Orthotopic Diversion
   1. Abdominoperineal approach, lithotomy with adjustable stirrups, catheter into the neobladder.
   2. Urethrectomy as above, to the membranous urethra.
   3. Abdominal exploration with lysis of adhesions and mobilisation of the ONB to the urethral
      anastomosis.
   4. Working from above and below, free the urethroneovesical anastomosis; excise a circular area of
      pouch with the urethra for an adequate margin; deliver through the perineum. Hazard: bleeding
      from tunnel musculature after excision of the membranous urethra — suture ligature or
      electrocautery.
   5. Diversion usually by ileal conduit, often from bowel of the ONB itself, reconfigured as needed —
      incise along visible lines of previous closure, preserve the mesentery, excise the remaining pouch.
   6. An afferent limb, if present, can become the conduit without reimplanting the ureters.
   7. Conversion to a continent cutaneous diversion may be possible, by anatomy and patient motivation.

 The Female Urethra
   • Urethrectomy at female RC is easy and was traditionally standard, but better understanding of the
     female continence mechanism and the low rate of urethral involvement have made preservation
     with ONB popular.
   • Synchronous urethral involvement at female RC is 8–13%; the main risk factor is bladder neck
     involvement, present in up to 25% of female cystectomy specimens.
   • The decisive asymmetry: nearly all urethral tumours have bladder neck involvement, but only 30–
     50% with bladder neck involvement have urethral tumours — so bladder neck involvement is not an
     absolute contraindication to preservation.
   • Anterior vaginal wall involvement may also increase risk, though it usually coexists with bladder neck
     involvement. Direct extension is the likeliest mechanism, but skip lesions occur.
   • Proposed ONB criteria: absence of bladder neck, urethral and anterior vaginal wall involvement plus
     a negative intraoperative frozen section, which most surgeons regard as essential and which can
     often be converted from positive to negative with further resection.

   • Trigonal involvement or positive nodes do not preclude ONB provided the urethral frozen section is
     negative. Females not having an ONB should undergo concurrent urethrectomy.
   • UR after ONB in females is rare — under 5%, explained by less at-risk urothelium in a shorter urethra
     and cephalad extension of squamous epithelium after the menopause.
   • Presents with obstruction, haematuria and pain, most commonly in the proximal urethra, usually
     two to four years postoperatively, though one recent report found a median of eight months.
   • In large selected ONB cohorts, UR rates were very low and only one of seven recurrences had a
     positive final margin at RC — most therefore arose from skip lesions.
   • Treatment is usually urethrectomy with conversion to cutaneous diversion, though BCG is reported.
     Isolated UR has a good prognosis with curative treatment; UR with synchronous pelvic recurrence,
     distant metastasis or locally advanced disease is dismal.
