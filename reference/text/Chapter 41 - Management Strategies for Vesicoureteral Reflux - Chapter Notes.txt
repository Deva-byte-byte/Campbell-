Chapter 41 — Management Strategies for Vesicoureteral
Reflux
 • Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) is the retrograde flow of urine from the bladder to the upper urinary tract. It
   is associated with acquired scarring and with congenital renal dysplasia.
 • Historically managed dichotomously; now viewed as a spectrum of severity, which calls for risk-
   stratified management. Despite several large randomised trials, aetiology, diagnosis and treatment all
   remain contested.

EMBRYOLOGY OF THE URETEROVESICAL JUNCTION
 • Weeks 4–6: the ureteric bud arises from the mesonephric (wolffian) duct. The early ureter and vas form
   the two arms of a Y, with the common mesonephric duct as the stem.
 • The stem is absorbed into the urogenital sinus (UGS) until the two arms enter separately: the ureter into
   the bladder, the vas and ejaculatory duct into the prostatic urethra (in the female, the vestigial Gartner
   duct in the vagina).
 • Mackie and Stephens: the arms rotate on contact with the UGS, so the ureteric orifice ends up proximal
   to the ejaculatory duct orifice.

                       Early budding                            Late budding
 Contact with UGS      Too soon → over-rotation                 Too late → insufficient rotation
 Orifice position      High and lateral                         Distal and medial
 Result                Short intramural tunnel → VUR Ectopic ureter, often obstructed at the bladder neck or beyond
 Kidney                Abnormal bud–metanephros contact → dysplasia, hypoplasia or agenesis

 • The nephric duct may not form the trigone but undergo apoptosis, a pivotal step in ureteric
   transposition, possibly driven by vitamin A–induced signals from the primitive bladder.
Concept — One event, bud position, sets both the orifice and the kidney. That is why high-grade reflux and
renal dysplasia travel together and why some “scars” are congenital.

FUNCTIONAL ANATOMY OF THE ANTIREFLUX MECHANISM
 • VUR reflects an imbalance among three factors: (1) the ureter, a dynamic conduit whose peristalsis
   actively opposes reflux; (2) the anatomy of the ureterovesical junction (UVJ); (3) bladder dynamics.
 • The intramural ureter runs within the detrusor. At the hiatus, the outer ureteric muscle merges with
   outer detrusor to form Waldeyer's sheath, contributing to the deep trigone. Recent
   immunohistochemistry suggests Waldeyer's sheath may be a feature of the whole ureter, not unique to
   the UVJ.
 • Passive flap-valve: the intramural ureter is compressed by the bladder wall during filling. This needs
   adequate intramural length and fixation.
 • Paquin: tunnel length to ureteric diameter about 5:1 in non-refluxing UVJs versus 1.4:1 in refluxing
   ones. Later work and computer modelling add that orifice configuration matters as much as length. Both
   theories neglect the intrinsic qualities of the ureter, its ability to collapse with filling.
 • Opening (active): the longitudinal tunnel muscle contracts → the tunnel shortens and widens → the
   bolus passes. Cystoscopically, the orifice moves laterally with each jet. Closure: compression plus return
   to full tunnel length as the muscle relaxes.

   • Antireflux integrity depends on the balanced anatomical and functional relationship between ureter
     and bladder.
   • VUR may be anatomical or functional in origin, at the UVJ, bladder or outlet.

Concept — The UVJ is a dynamic valve, opened actively and closed passively. Reflux follows whenever
tunnel geometry, ureteric function or bladder pressure falls out of balance.

DEMOGRAPHICS

Prevalence
 • Prevalence is hard to measure because VUR is sometimes transient. Historical screening found VUR in
   about a third of hospitalised children without urological disease, higher in infants. Screened normal
   newborns showed 1–4% (interpret with caution). Up to 38% occurs with postnatal upper-tract
   sonographic anomalies.
 • Best estimate (meta-analysis): about 30% of children with urinary tract infection (UTI), and about 17%
   of those imaged for other reasons such as hydronephrosis.

Vesicoureteral Reflux in the Fetus
 • Prenatal hydronephrosis occurs in 1–5% of pregnancies. Routine VCUG screening of all such infants has
   been abandoned.
 • Voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) in the newborn only for higher-risk renal bladder ultrasonography
   (RBUS) findings: higher-grade (especially bilateral) hydronephrosis, cortical abnormality, ureteric
   dilatation, suspected ureterocele or duplication, bladder abnormality.
 • VUR is found in about a fifth of those with persistent postnatal hydronephrosis who reach VCUG, mostly
   with higher grades. Older screening series found 10–40%, with bilateral dilating VUR far commoner in
   high-grade prenatal hydronephrosis.
 • Clinical significance is usually low: few infants with screen-detected VUR go on to UTI. Infants not
   screened who later have a febrile UTI frequently prove to have VUR. The implication is to individualise
   rather than VCUG every hydronephrotic infant.

INHERITANCE AND GENETICS

Sibling Vesicoureteral Reflux
 • Sibling prevalence about 30% (up to 50%), higher in younger siblings. Almost 90% of screened siblings
   under 3 had VUR in one series. Symptomatic siblings had more high-grade VUR and scarring. In older
   siblings, prevalence may fall to 7%, consistent with spontaneous resolution, though not all series agree.
 • That series recommended screening siblings under 3 of index patients with grade III–IV, but it cannot
   guide siblings of grade I–II index cases, who are the majority.
 • Identical twins: very high, even complete, concordance. Screen-detected twins had higher grades but no
   scarring, unlike their index twins.
 • Screened refluxing siblings showed renal abnormalities, more often over age 2, which was read as
   progressive damage. But this ignores early congenital damage magnified by growth and does not
   separate dysplasia from acquired scar.
 • The real target is renal damage, not VUR itself. Screen non-invasively for cortical abnormality, and
   reserve VCUG for UTI or bladder and bowel dysfunction (BBD). An older sibling with normal kidneys and
   no BBD gains little from detection: treat a febrile UTI as in any child.
 • American Urological Association (AUA) guideline: VCUG for siblings with cortical abnormality or renal
   size asymmetry on ultrasound, or a UTI history. This is especially so with grade 3–4 hydronephrosis;
   observe grade 1–2.

GRADING OF VESICOURETERAL REFLUX

International Classification of Vesicoureteral Reflux
 • Proposed by the International Reflux Study Committee (1981). It grades the VCUG appearance of ureter,
   pelvis and calyces into five grades.
TABLE 41.3 INTERNATIONAL CLASSIFICATION OF VESICOURETERAL REFLUX

 Grade        Ureter                                      Pelvis and calyces
 I            Into a non-dilated ureter only              Not reached
 II           Not dilated                                 Reached, no dilatation
 III          Mild–moderate dilatation                    Mild–moderate dilatation, minimal forniceal blunting
 IV           Moderate tortuosity                         Moderate dilatation
 V            Gross dilatation and tortuosity             Gross dilatation; papillary impressions lost

 • Pattern: I–II are non-dilating (the ureter alone, then the pelvis); III–V are dilating, with progressive calyceal
   change. The step from II to III is the first dilatation; IV adds tortuosity; V loses the papillary impressions.
 • Shortcomings: ureteric and calyceal dilatation are not always concordant (Fig. 41.4). Interobserver
   variability is large: in RIVUR, all three readers agreed in only about 60% of ureters, worst for grades II–IV
   and best at the extremes.
 • Radionuclide cystography (RNC) cannot assign international grades; it collapses them into three grades
   (Fig. 41.5). Contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography has no adopted grading system yet.
 • Grade is a snapshot, modulated by bladder dynamics, outlet, diuresis, filling volume, the number of
   cycles, filling versus voiding reflux, and ipsilateral UVJ obstruction. None of these is built into the grading.
Figure legends, Figs 41.4–41.5 — A ureter with marked lower-segment dilatation but an undistorted
collecting system does not behave like typical grade II. On nuclear cystography: grade 1 = international I;
grade 2 = II–III; grade 3 = IV–V.

Other Tools
Ureteral Diameter Ratio
 • Ureteral diameter ratio (UDR) = largest ureteric diameter within the false pelvis ÷ distance from L1 to L3
   vertebral bodies.
 • It reduces grading variability, especially for intermediate grades. It correlates with grade, predicts
   spontaneous resolution better than grade, and predicts breakthrough UTI (BUTI) independent of grade.
   It has been built into resolution and BUTI models and may predict endoscopic injection success.

Secondary Vesicoureteral Reflux
 • Spina bifida neurogenic bladder: VUR in up to 30%. Look for occult dysraphism in a child with UTI or
   incontinence: sacral dimple, hairy patch, gluteal cleft anomaly, reduced anal tone, leg deficit, refractory
   constipation or encopresis.
 • Known dysraphism plus VUR → proactive management: early clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC),
   anticholinergic or β3-agonist, serial imaging, and a low threshold to investigate any progression.
 • Infant (especially male) secondary VUR: high voiding pressures from inadequate sphincter relaxation,
   with overactivity or obstructive voiding patterns, mostly in boys. Filling is largely normal. This resolves as
   voiding matures, which explains the infant male predominance, high male resolution and the sex shift
   with age.
 • Older children: BBD → rising voiding pressures and incomplete emptying → UTI. The pressures distort the
   UVJ and can create secondary VUR. Normal bladders void at about 100 cm H₂O without reflux, so
   structural UVJ failure is the key. Reflux at low pressure or early filling = poor prognosis for resolution.
 • Detrusor overactivity is the commonest urodynamic abnormality with VUR in neurologically normal
   children. Targeted treatment (timed voiding, biofeedback, anticholinergics, α-blockers) makes resolution
   or improvement by two or more grades more likely. Anticholinergics can eliminate VUR in a substantial
   share.

 • Primary and secondary VUR are not mutually exclusive: “primary” reflux often has a secondary,
   treatable component (overactivity, BBD).
Concept — Every refluxing UVJ sits somewhere between an intrinsically weak valve and a normal valve
under excessive pressure. Identifying the pressure component is what makes VUR modifiable.

DIAGNOSIS AND EVALUATION OF VESICOURETERAL REFLUX

Confirmation of Urinary Tract Infection
 • Acquired reflux nephropathy is largely preventable and requires infected urine plus VUR. Major
   decisions, including surgery, often rest on the diagnosis of UTI, so proper urine collection and
   confirmation are paramount.

 KEY POINTS: DIAGNOSIS AND EVALUATION OF VESICOURETERAL REFLUX
   • Accurate urine collection and UTI diagnosis avoid false positives and management errors.
   • The gold-standard VUR study requires bladder catheterisation.
   • Nuclear cystograms are more sensitive but give much less anatomical detail than VCUG.
   • Parental perceptions must be considered.
   • Upper-tract assessment is serial.
   • The imaging challenge is to separate congenital dysplasia from post-infective scarring.


Evaluating Urinary Tract Infection
 • VUR is found in about 30% of children after UTI. Higher grades carry existing dysplasia, and repeat UTIs
   → more scarring (Fig. 41.7), so recurrent UTI prompts a VUR work-up.
 • Invasiveness and radiation push towards a risk-stratified approach. The 2011 American Academy of
   Pediatrics (AAP) guideline: ultrasound first after a first febrile UTI under 2 years, VCUG only if
   ultrasound is abnormal or UTI recurs.
 • VUR found in an older child or after afebrile UTI is of questionable significance. Proceed to VCUG for
   structural anomaly, renal asymmetry, ureteric dilatation or a bladder anomaly.
 • Antenatal hydronephrosis is mostly non-refluxing. Its degree correlates poorly with VUR grade, and
   about a quarter of refluxing infants have a normal postnatal ultrasound.
Figure legend, Fig. 41.7 (AUA guideline, 2010) — More prior UTIs are associated with more baseline renal
abnormality. Sibling- and prenatally detected cohorts have the fewest UTIs and abnormalities.

Impact of Bladder and Bowel Dysfunction
 • See VUR as a primary (innate) component plus treatable secondary elements. Removing the secondary
   elements leaves only true primary VUR to treat.
 • IRSC (European arm): 18% had voiding dysfunction, with more UTIs and more persistent VUR. Both AUA
   and European Society for Paediatric Urology (ESPU) guidelines mandate treating BBD.
 • Swedish Reflux Trial: dysfunction was present in 20% at entry and 34% at 2 years. Dysfunction at follow-
   up correlated negatively with improvement and with scintigraphic abnormality. Untreated bladder
   dysfunction worsens surgical outcome.
 • AUA (2010; reaffirmed 2017): BBD is one of the most critical modifiable variables. Meta-analysis: BBD →
   more UTI on prophylaxis and after surgery, less resolution at 24 months, lower endoscopic success, but
   no reduction in open surgical success (Fig. 41.8).

LOWER URINARY TRACT INFECTION AND REFLUX
 • Management has shifted from surgery first to medical management first, with surgery for high-risk,
   refractory or progressive cases.
 • Reflux does not cause UTI; it is a clinical accelerant, mechanically delivering infected urine to the pelvis.
 • Cystitis irritates the bladder and lowers the reflux threshold. Escherichia coli endotoxin impairs the
   antireflux mechanism and causes ureteric atony; in primates, infection made VUR persist.
 • Dilated upper tracts are a reservoir that recycles colonised urine and makes sterilisation harder.
 • Swedish Reflux Trial: high-grade VUR on surveillance alone had a 57% febrile UTI recurrence; prophylaxis
   or correction cut this to about 20%.

 KEY POINTS: URINARY TRACT INFECTION AND REFLUX
   • Reflux is not a general cause of UTI.
   • Reflux facilitates pyelonephritis.

Concept — Infection, not reflux, is the injury. Reflux is merely the conveyor, which is why sterile urine and
good emptying, not the reflux itself, are the targets of treatment.

 • Routine cystoscopy has no role. Orifice appearance and tunnel length correlate poorly with VUR. Its use

Positional instillation of contrast (PIC) cystography (2003), for recurrent febrile UTI with a negative VCUG:
general anaesthetic → bladder empty → scope beak close to and facing the orifice → contrast through the
irrigation port from 1 m above the bladder with simultaneous fluoroscopy → PIC-VUR = retrograde filling of
ureter or pelvis → empty and repeat on the other side.
 • Treating PIC-detected occult VUR (mostly by endoscopic injection) cut febrile UTI about twentyfold. PIC
   grade correlates with dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA) scarring.
 • But: it is non-physiological, with no age-adjusted pressure, and may create iatrogenic VUR. It needs
   anaesthesia, so it is not first line and only for rare diagnostic dilemmas until randomised data exist.

Uroflowmetry and Electromyography
 • European Bladder Dysfunction Study: little role for invasive urodynamics in UTI or VUR; reserve it for
   refractory cases.
 • Non-invasive uroflowmetry + postvoid residual (PVR) ± patch pelvic-floor electromyography (EMG): flow
   patterns correlate with symptoms and BBD, and favourable findings predict resolution. They are worth
   including in assessment and possibly screening.
Concept — Lower-tract imaging trades anatomical detail against dose and invasiveness. VCUG for anatomy,
RNC for sensitivity and follow-up, ultrasound contrast to avoid radiation, all made unreliable by a single cycle
or an infected bladder.

ASSESSMENT OF THE UPPER URINARY TRACT

Rationale for Serial Assessment of Upper Tracts
 • Pyelonephritis with VUR → scarring, impaired renal growth, renovascular hypertension. Imaging is
   selective, guided by reflux status, age, sex, race, family history and bladder–bowel function.
 • The core goal is to distinguish VUR-related damage from congenital dysplasia, medical renal disease or
   hydronephrosis, which is best done by serial imaging.

Renal Sonography
 • RBUS is the initial mainstay: non-ionising, ideal for serial use. It measures renal volume against growth
   curves, shows the impact of intercurrent febrile UTI, and guides the need for scintigraphy or correction.
   Renal length correlates well with scintigraphic hypoplasia.
 • Loss of corticomedullary differentiation or increased echogenicity → functional abnormality. In a
   neonate with high-grade VUR and no UTI, plus a small kidney, this indicates congenital dysplasia, not
   acquired scar.
 • Limits: ultrasound cannot diagnose VUR, and a normal scan does not exclude it. It is insensitive to small
   cortical defects (scintigraphy is superior) but adequate for surveillance, large defects and size asymmetry.

Renal Scintigraphy
 • Gold standard for functional parenchymal imaging: 99mTc-DMSA, taken up only by functioning proximal
   tubules, in proportion to glomerular filtration.
 • Uses: reconstruct versus remove when function is minimal; conjugate views for differential function with
   severe hydronephrosis.
 • Acute pyelonephritis → photopenic areas (oedema impairs tubular uptake). Many resolve with prompt
   treatment; persistence = scar.

Acquired Renal Scars
 • Nephropathy from primary VUR requires bacterial colonisation with normal voiding. Sterile VUR
   damages only with secondary high pressures (PUVs) or rare megacystis states.

 • Ransley and Risdon (pig model): reflux created by unroofing the tunnel caused nephropathy only if the
   urine was infected. Clinical series confirm more scarring with multiple UTIs.
 • High pressure modulates sterile reflux: higher voiding pressures raise scarring risk. Hodson: VUR plus
   outflow obstruction caused atrophic pyelonephritis without infection, worse with bacteria. Hence
   normalising bladder and bowel function is essential while awaiting resolution.

Pathophysiology of Acquired Scarring
 • VUR is an accelerant after bladder colonisation: more pyelonephritis with high grade. After correction,
   almost no new scars despite asymptomatic bacteriuria, confirmed by meta-analysis.

Age
 • Scarring propensity is inversely related to age. Highest risk in the first year; under 4 years is more prone
   after a single UTI, though scarring still occurs beyond 5. In older children, scarring usually reflects late
   diagnosis, inadequate treatment or social factors.
 • “Big bang” theory (Ransley and Risdon): most scarring follows the first pyelonephritis, with little further
   change without new infections. It is challenged by new scars in landmark trials, dysplasia mistaken for
   scar, defects changing with growth, and weak evidence for greater infant vulnerability.

 KEY POINTS: CORTICAL DEFECTS
   • Sterile VUR is unlikely to scar.
   • The youngest are at greatest risk of post-pyelonephritic scarring.
   • Most parenchymal abnormality is dysplasia or pyelonephritis, and these are hard to tell apart.


Host Susceptibility and Response
 • Bladder colonisation depends on periurethral flora (vaginal and preputial), hygiene and bowel habit (the
   gut is the reservoir) and host adherence receptors.
 • Under 6 months, most UTIs in either sex occur in uncircumcised boys, reflecting dense E. coli
   colonisation of the infant prepuce.
 • Urinary dwell time is the greatest tangible factor. E. coli doubles in minutes, so a bladder not emptied
   for hours incubates a clinical inoculum. Regular emptying is fundamental.

Bacterial Virulence
 • Fimbrial adhesion to uroepithelial receptors is the chief virulence trait. Yet scars with VUR often arise
   from organisms with fewer than three virulence factors: reflux lets less virulent bacteria scar. Urinary
   metabolic and microbiome factors are emerging.

Hypertension
 • After essential hypertension, reflux nephropathy may be the commonest cause of hypertension in
   children, via the renin–angiotensin system and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Severity of nephropathy correlates with
   progression on ambulatory monitoring.
 • 34–38% of children with renal scarring become hypertensive in long-term adult follow-up. VUR first
   diagnosed in adulthood is strongly associated with hypertension.
 • It is unclear whether acquired scar, congenital dysplasia or both drive it. If dysplasia already exists,
   preventing infection may not prevent hypertension.
 • Correcting VUR does not lower blood pressure, because the mechanism is parenchymal microvascular.
   Segmental resection guided by selective renal vein renin, or nephrectomy of a small dysplastic or
   shrunken kidney, can cure it in selected patients.

Renal Growth
 • VUR correction poorly predicts catch-up growth. Contralateral compensatory hypertrophy exaggerates
   the apparent ipsilateral deficit. The worst growth occurs with VUR plus UTI in a solitary kidney.
 • No superiority of medical over surgical therapy for growth or scarring. IRSC at 10 years: no difference in
   somatic growth. Surgery may help when BUTI shows prophylaxis has failed.

Renal Failure and Somatic Growth
 • Renal failure from primary VUR is now rare, thanks to Smellie's paradigm (1970s–1980s): prophylaxis and
   infection control. United States Renal Data System (USRDS): reflux nephropathy as a cause of renal
   replacement therapy (RRT) fell from 1.9 to 0.6 per million (1996–2014).
 • Yet reflux nephropathy is the second commonest primary diagnosis in non-Black paediatric transplant
   recipients, and in Australia and New Zealand the second cause of paediatric RRT after
   glomerulonephritis. Some shift may be reclassification as congenital dysplasia.
 • Medical renal disease with scarring: hyperfiltration, concentrating defect, proteinuria, microalbuminuria,
   renal tubular acidosis, raised fractional sodium and magnesium excretion. Concentrating defects occur
   even with sterile VUR, are proportional to grade and improve after cessation, suggesting a functionally
   obstructive element.
 • Somatic growth is a global marker: children with bilateral VUR and damage fall below centiles, and
   suppressing pyelonephritis allows catch-up. Prophylaxis was associated with a higher body mass index
   (BMI) over 1 year and a non-significant fall in linear growth under 1 year.
Concept — Every downstream harm of reflux, whether scar, hypertension, growth or renal failure, splits into a
preventable infective component and a fixed congenital component. Treatment can only ever act on the
first.

Controversies in Usage of Invasive Investigations
 • Catheterisation distress and the arrival of low-morbidity endoscopic correction have challenged
   convention. Every assumption is disputed: scarring incidence, the VUR–renal failure link, the age of peak
   susceptibility, and whether resolution is even necessary.
 • Distress mitigation: sedation, topical urethral anaesthetic, hypnosis. Each test must be justified by how it
   changes the decision tree.

ASSOCIATED ANOMALIES AND CONDITIONS

Ureteropelvic Junction Obstruction
 • VUR in UPJO: up to about 5–9% in contemporary series. UPJO in VUR: 0.75–3.6%, and high-grade VUR is
   five times more likely to coexist with UPJO. Half of high-grade sonographic hydronephrosis with high-
   grade VUR showed an obstructive diuretic renogram. A shared ureteric bud anomaly is proposed.
 • Coincidental VUR with UPJO is usually low grade and resolves. A minimally dilated ureter with a huge
   pelvis means the VUR grade is overestimated.
 • Four VCUG signs of UPJO with VUR: (1) dilated ureter with little pelvic filling; (2) dilute, faint pelvic
   contrast; (3) ureter–pelvis dilatation discrepancy; (4) pelvis that fails to drain postvoid.
 • Correct the obstruction first (confirmed by scintigraphy with catheter drainage). Sterile VUR can be
   observed, but obstruction harms even without infection. Repairing VUR first risks worse dilatation from
   lower-ureter oedema and infection of an obstructed pelvis.
 • Simultaneous open repair of both threatens ureteric blood supply. Endoscopic injection at pyeloplasty is
   reasonable, despite lower success.

 • VUR is the commonest abnormality in complete duplication, usually into the lower pole. The Weigert–

Renal Anomalies
 • Multicystic dysplastic kidney (MCDK): contralateral VUR in about a quarter, half low grade. It resolves
   spontaneously (mean about 4 years) regardless of grade, and surgery is rarely needed. Contralateral VUR
   blunts compensatory hypertrophy.
 • Renal agenesis: contralateral pathology 32–46%. VUR is the commonest (24–28%), then UVJ and UPJ
   obstruction. Meta-analysis suggests about 17% (close to background), with a high share dilating, half of
   which resolve. Knowing it rarely changes management.

Megacystis-Megaureter Association
 • Massive bilateral VUR: urine voided partly up the ureters returns to the bladder after voiding → a large
   thin-walled bladder and massive hydroureter (Williams; Burbige). It is detectable in utero and
   commoner in males.
 • It is non-obstructive, like cardiac dilatation from valvular regurgitation. VCUG shows a normal posterior
   urethra, distinguishing it from PUVs or prune-belly syndrome.
 • Large residuals → recurrent UTI. Temporise with vesicostomy or cutaneous loop ureterostomy →
   reimplant (UVJ failure is the primary lesion) → postoperative bladder rehabilitation, after which volume
   and contractility normalise.
 • Contraindicated: procedures on an already patent outlet, which risk infection and miss the cause.

Obstructive Refluxing Megaureter
 • It mimics high-grade dilating VUR, and contrast may not ascend, so it is under-graded. Key sign: contrast
   in the ureter much more dilute than in the bladder.

Other Anomalies
 • Over 40 syndromes include VUR (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man), notably VACTERL (vertebral,

a duplex or diverticulum is managed as for the reflux unless the anatomy precludes resolution, and a non-
obstructive megacystis is cured by fixing the UVJ, not the outlet.

FOLLOW-UP AND TRANSITIONING INTO ADULTHOOD
 • Childhood VUR carries a persistently higher adult UTI risk, even after resolution or correction. In women,
   BBD persisting into adulthood predicts febrile and afebrile UTI regardless of VUR status.
 • Dextranomer/hyaluronic acid copolymer (Dx/HA) mounds calcify in over half and mimic stones. After
   cross-trigonal or advancement reimplants, retrograde catheterisation and ureteroscopy are harder.
   Knowing the surgical history avoids misadventure.

Pregnancy and Reflux
 • Pregnancy reduces bladder tone (oedema, hyperaemia) and causes physiological upper-tract dilatation.
   Slow drainage favours bacteriuria and pyelonephritis.
 • VUR in late pregnancy → far more pyelonephritis (about a third versus under 5% without VUR).
 • Prior UTI → bacteriuria; scarring plus persistent VUR → pyelonephritis.
 • Renal scars → about threefold hypertension, about eightfold pre-eclampsia, and more obstetric
   intervention. Bilateral scars carry more pre-eclampsia than unilateral.
 • Normal blood pressure and renal function → uneventful pregnancy; impaired function → fetal loss and
   accelerated maternal renal disease.
 • Reimplanted in childhood → still at UTI risk in pregnancy but not at excess miscarriage risk. Mother and
   fetus are at significant risk only with scarring or hypertension.
 • Meta-analysis: renal scarring, not the presence of VUR, drives pregnancy morbidity. Long-term follow-
   up links scarring to UTI, pregnancy hypertension and low birth weight. About 43% of offspring who had a
   VCUG had VUR.
 • Persistent VUR without scars: risk is poorly defined. Some correct VUR persisting beyond puberty in girls,
   while others stop prophylaxis and monitor. Long-term follow-up and transition programmes are
   essential.

 KEY POINTS: PREGNANCY AND REFLUX
   • Pregnancy physiology increases VUR-associated pyelonephritis risk.
   • Correction of VUR may be considered before pregnancy, absent definitive evidence otherwise.

Concept — In adult life, the scar, not the reflux, carries the risk. That is why correction does not erase the
pregnancy and UTI hazard and why women with childhood VUR need lifelong follow-up.

Spontaneous Resolution
 • Resolution is roughly inversely proportional to initial grade. Later in life, it depends on initial grade and
   age at presentation.
 • Mechanism: remodelling of the UVJ (elongation of the intravesical ureter) plus stabilising bladder
   dynamics. Persistent BBD explains failure to resolve.

 • Age at presentation matters more than grade. VUR is commonest and most likely to resolve in neonates

CURRENT APPROACH TO MEDICAL MANAGEMENT

Risk Stratification
 • One principle supersedes all: preserve renal function and prevent UTI and scarring. After the 2011 AAP
   shift and fewer cystograms, practice is risk-based. The tension comes from open correction succeeding in
   over 95%, and robotic correction being likely less morbid.
Eight tenets: (1) most VUR resolves, helped by correcting BBD → (2) higher grades resolve less, especially in
older children after UTI → (3) sterile VUR rarely damages → (4) preventing UTI matters more than resolving
VUR → (5) prophylaxis is safe and beneficial, especially at high risk → (6) medical management suits most
VUR → (7) multivariable risk stratification predicts resolution, UTI and nephropathy → (8) long-term follow-
up, because VUR is dynamic.
 • Evidence summary:
     ◦ Little CAP benefit at grade ≤II, especially without BBD; benefit at grade ≥III, at least in girls.
     ◦ About 15% have a recurrent febrile UTI within 2 years, and about 15% of those scar.
     ◦ BBD → recurrent UTI in about 45% versus 15% without, on or off antibiotics.
     ◦ Higher grade → more pyelonephritis and new damage. The effect of age is uncertain.
     ◦ Uninterrupted follow-up, especially with scars: hypertension, medical renal disease, and women
       through childbearing years. Transition programmes support this.
 • Classic approach: daily low-dose CAP while awaiting resolution, whatever the grade. Extensive scarring,
   high grade, globally reduced function or dysplasia lower tolerance for another infection and favour
   correction.
 • Stopping CAP after toilet training or once BBD resolves: about 8–10% then develop febrile UTI, which
   mandates reassessment and possible correction. Select low-risk children prospectively. Most children with
   asymptomatic VUR need little follow-up beyond lifelong bladder and bowel habits and prompt review of
   any UTI.
 • Unresolved controversy: asymptomatic bilateral high-grade VUR with a normal ultrasound in an older
   boy. Girls were traditionally corrected after toilet training for pregnancy reasons, an approach not yet
   proven.

Current Indications for Continuous Antibiotic Prophylaxis
 • “Water-hammer” sterile renal damage has never been reliably demonstrated. What is managed is
   infection risk, not VUR.
 • Daily low-dose oral CAP, preferably at night, which gives the longest bladder concentration during the
   longest retention, when infection is likeliest.

Individualizing Patient Care
 • Minimum initial work-up: an accurate history of the number and severity of clinically significant UTIs
   plus high-quality renal and bladder ultrasound; then decide on VCUG.
 • Multiple documented febrile UTIs, admissions or upper-tract findings → VCUG imperative. Afebrile
   “infections” with doubtful urine studies and a normal ultrasound → a conservative approach with little
   risk.
 • Risk stratification (Fig. 41.19): in validation, about two-thirds of children with VUR were low risk (BUTI
   about 9%), about a quarter intermediate (about 27%) and only a few high risk (about 63%). This mirrors
   the low-risk predominance of all-comer cohorts such as RIVUR.

   • Reflux tends to resolve given time; likelihood is inversely proportional to grade but modified by other
