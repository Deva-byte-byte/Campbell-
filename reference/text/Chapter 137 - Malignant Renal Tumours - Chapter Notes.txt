HISTORICAL CONSIDERATIONS
 • First documented nephrectomy — Wolcott, 1861: performed in error, removing a tumour believed to be
   a hepatoma.
 • First planned nephrectomy — Simon, 1869: for a persistent ureteral fistula; the patient survived with cure
   of the fistula. First planned nephrectomy in the United States — Gilmore, 1870 (Mobile, Alabama), for
   atrophic chronic pyelonephritis.
 • Harris, 1882: reported 100 renal extirpations, the first series large enough to analyse the clinical and
   pathological features of surgical renal disease.
 • Tubular origin recognised early: first accurate gross description of kidney tumours by Konig (1826); Robin
   (1855) concluded that renal carcinoma arises from renal tubular epithelium, supported by Waldeyer
   (1867).
 • Grawitz (1883) — the adrenal-rest error: proposed that clear-cell renal tumours arise from adrenal rests
   within the kidney (struma lipomatodes aberrata renis), reasoning from their fat content, subcapsular
   location, proximity to the adrenal and dissimilarity to uriniferous tubules.
 • "Hypernephroma": the term hypernephroid tumours (origin above the kidney) was introduced in 1894;
   this semantic and conceptual mistake produced the long-lived term hypernephroma. Albarran and Imbert
   (1903) and Wolff's four-volume work (1883–1928) later clarified the histopathology.
 • Modern era: renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is recognised as a family of distinct subtypes arising from
   different parts of the nephron, each with its own genetic basis and biology.
 • Surgical evolution: classic radical nephrectomy (RN) introduced in the 1960s, followed by a trend
   towards nephron-sparing surgery and minimally invasive approaches.
 • RCC remains primarily a surgical disease: immune-based and targeted molecular therapies occasionally
   give durable responses, but cure is rarely seen without complete surgical excision or effective ablation.
 • Incidence is gradually rising, and despite earlier detection, mortality remains high.
Concept — The history is a correction of an anatomical error: once RCC was understood as tubular, and later
as several distinct nephron-derived diseases, management moved from the uniform radical operation
towards tailored, nephron-sparing treatment.

CLASSIFICATION
 • Renal masses are classified two ways: by pathology (malignant, benign, inflammatory — Table 137.1) or
   by radiographic appearance (simple cystic, complex cystic, solid — Table 137.2). Both schemes have been
   updated to reflect the distinct RCC subtypes.
 • Malignant renal tumours: RCC, urothelium-based malignancies, sarcomas, embryonic or paediatric
   tumours, lymphomas and metastases. Benign tumours are diverse (Chapter 136). Inflammatory and
   vascular entities must also enter the differential diagnosis.

Imaging and Clinical Risk Stratification of Renal Masses
 • Clinical evaluation estimates the risk of malignancy and metastatic potential in a given patient: patient
   characteristics, mode of presentation, imaging, laboratory tests and renal mass biopsy (RMB).
 • Composite models and nomograms predict malignancy poorly — concordance indices only 0.55–0.65.
 • Strongest predictors of malignancy: male sex and increasing tumour size. Males carry a nearly threefold
   risk of malignancy compared with females (meta-analysis of resected tumours). Benign histology is
   commoner in females, but RCC still predominates in both sexes.
 • Size and benignity: 20–30% of masses ≤4 cm and about 40% of masses ≤2 cm are benign. The odds of
   malignancy rise with each centimetre of diameter.
 • Size and aggressiveness: tumours ≤1 cm are frequently benign and rarely high-grade or high-stage,
   whereas at ≥7 cm few are benign and about half are high-grade.
 • Many cT1a (≤4 cm) cancers are indolent: <2% present with or develop metastasis in surgical series, and
   ≤2% progress to metastasis on active surveillance (AS) in the short to intermediate term.

 • Complex cystic masses and cystic RCC have an excellent prognosis and a diminutive metastatic risk,
   whatever the management strategy chosen.
 • Because size drives risk and most masses are found incidentally, risk stratification begins with imaging.

Radiographic Evaluation of Renal Masses
 • Imaging is the strongest predictor of malignancy and metastatic potential. Guidelines recommend
   multiphase cross-sectional imaging for every suspected renal mass, because of the wide differential
   diagnosis and the overlap between benign and malignant lesions.
 • Intravenous pyelography and renal arteriography are no longer recommended. Multiphasic cross-
   sectional imaging characterises the mass, detects locally advanced features and intra-abdominal
   metastases, and excludes angiomyolipoma (AML) by showing intralesional fat.

 • NSF was commonest on dialysis, in CKD stages 4–5, in acute kidney injury and after multiple doses of
   earlier-generation gadolinium agents. Newer macrocyclic gadolinium agents carry a very low NSF risk,
   allowing contrast MRI even on dialysis or in severe CKD.
 • Strict ultrasound criteria for a simple cyst: smooth wall; round or oval shape without internal echoes;
   through-transmission with strong posterior acoustic enhancement. Anything that fails these criteria
   proceeds to multiphasic cross-sectional imaging.
 • Sestamibi mechanism: uptake by mitochondria, abundant in oncocytomas; combined with selective RMB,
   it offers a cost-effective way to avoid surgery for benign tumours.
 • Every imaging report should state: diameter in three planes; morphology and relation to hilum, renal vein
   and collecting system; enhancement; lymphadenopathy; abdominal metastases.
 • Solid architecture predicts malignancy more than cystic, although many complex cystic masses are RCC.
 • Tumour complexity scores — R.E.N.A.L. nephrometry score (Radius, Endophytic/exophytic, Nearness to
   collecting system, Anterior/posterior, Location relative to polar lines), PADUA score and C-index — guide
   partial versus radical nephrectomy, open versus minimally invasive approach, and complication risk;
   higher complexity may also correlate with malignancy and aggressive histology.
 • Infiltrative growth broadens the differential: urothelial carcinoma, lymphoma, infection, high-grade or
   sarcomatoid RCC.
 • Papillary RCC (PRCC) is often hypoenhancing relative to other subtypes, but enhancement pattern alone
   cannot define biology — clear cell RCC, oncocytoma and PRCC overlap considerably.

 • Simple cyst (water density) → observe unless symptomatic.
 • Complex cyst or solid mass, no significant contrast allergy:
 • eGFR >45 → dedicated renal CT with and without contrast, or MRI with and without gadolinium.
 • eGFR 30–45 → periprocedural hydration, then the same studies.
 • eGFR <30 → non-contrast CT or MRI with gadolinium (preferred over ultrasound or CEUS, which remain
   reasonable alternatives). Macrocyclic gadolinium is safe in CKD and on dialysis.
 • Significant contrast allergy → ultrasound or CEUS, or MRI with or without gadolinium.
 • Interpretation: simple cyst → observe; complex cyst → define malignant risk; fat (−20 HU or less) → likely

Evaluation of Cystic Renal Lesions
 • Distinguishing benign cysts from cystic RCC is one of the commonest and hardest problems in renal
   imaging. Simple cysts carry a near-zero malignant risk; risk rises with complexity — wall thickness and
   contour; number, contour and thickness of septa; amount, character and location of calcification; fluid
   density; margination; and solid components.
 • The Bosniak classification (CT-based) sorts cystic lesions into categories distinct in malignant likelihood. A
   2019 update proposal added detailed definitions and incorporated MRI, aiming to reduce inter-reader
   variability; its impact is not yet proven, and the fundamental concepts are unchanged since the 2005
   revision.

 • Bosniak I — by far the commonest cystic lesion; no treatment or surveillance if asymptomatic.
 • Bosniak II — minimally complex: non-enhancing septated cysts, cysts with mural or septal calcium,
   infected cysts and hyperdense cysts (classically <3 cm, pre-contrast attenuation >20 HU from old or
   clotted blood).
 • The key discriminator is unequivocal contrast enhancement — absent in II and IIF.
 • Bosniak III — cannot be confidently distinguished from malignancy: thickened irregular walls or septa, or
   any measurable enhancement. Bosniak IV — large cystic components, shaggy margins and, above all,
   enhancing solid portions, which make the diagnosis of malignancy.
 • Surgical exploration is often indicated for healthy patients with Bosniak III or IV unless trauma or
   infection explains the findings.
 • RMB or aspiration is rarely used for cystic lesions: sampling error, poor diagnostic yield and risk of
   tumour spillage.
 • Paradigm shift: surgery for Bosniak III and IV is no longer mandatory. Complex cystic masses are indolent,
   grow slowly, rarely progress through the Bosniak categories, and do well whatever the treatment; cystic
   renal cancers fare better than size-matched solid masses, so AS and alternatives to surgery suit many
   patients.
 • Trap: cystic masses must be distinguished from necrotic solid cancers that mimic cysts, such as high-
   grade papillary subtypes.
Concept — Risk stratification of a renal mass rests on two imaging variables, size and enhancement;
everything else — the Bosniak categories, the fat rule for AML, the complexity scores — refines those two,
and the recognition that small and cystic masses are mostly indolent is what now licenses surveillance.

INCIDENCE
 • RCC accounts for 2–3% of adult malignancies and is the most lethal of the common urological cancers.
 • About 76,000 new cases and 16,000 deaths yearly in the United States; about 430,000 cases and
   130,000 deaths worldwide.
 • About 16 new cases per 100,000 per year; male-to-female ratio 2:1.
 • Disease of older adults: typical presentation 55–75 years; median age at diagnosis worldwide about 75
   years. Diagnosis is rising fastest in those under 40.
 • Ethnic and geographic variation: incidence and death rates about 50% lower in Asian-American/Pacific
   Islander and 5–10% higher in African American populations (cause unknown). Worldwide mortality is
   highest in Europe and lowest in South Central Asia, Melanesia and Middle Africa.
 • Sporadic in the great majority; only 4–6% familial.
 • Incidence has risen about 3% per year since the 1970s, largely from incidental detection on ultrasound
   and CT, with more localised tumours and better 5-year survival. Stage migration has plateaued, with
   about 70% organ-confined at diagnosis.
 • Mortality peaked in 2002 (3.8 per 100,000) and fell to about 3.1 per 100,000 by 2018, credited to
   targeted and immunomodulatory systemic therapy and to overdiagnosis of small cT1a masses.
 • Paediatric renal tumours are about 7% of childhood cancers; about 90% are Wilms tumours, and RCC is
   only 1–6%. RCC equals Wilms tumour in frequency in the second decade. Mean age of paediatric RCC is
   8–9 years, with no sex difference.
 • RCC in children and young adults is more often symptomatic, locally advanced, high grade and of
   unfavourable subtype. The commonest subtype carries TFE3 translocations (Xp11), producing TFE3

   overexpression and mixed clear and papillary features; up to 15% follow chemotherapy for another
   malignancy.
 • Stage for stage, the young respond well to surgery, with long-term survivors even after RN and
   lymphadenectomy for node-positive disease, so formal lymphadenectomy at RN is recommended when
   RCC is suspected in a child or young adult.
Concept — The epidemiology is dominated by incidental detection: incidence has risen far faster than
mortality because imaging finds small, often indolent tumours — the same fact that underlies the modern
emphasis on avoiding overtreatment.

 • Cell of origin: clear cell and papillary RCC arise from the proximal convoluted tubule; chromophobe RCC
   (ChRCC) and collecting duct carcinoma arise from the distal nephron.

 KEY POINTS: INCIDENCE AND AETIOLOGY OF RENAL CELL CARCINOMA
   • RCC accounts for 2–3% of adult malignancies, male-to-female 2:1.
   • RCC typically presents between 55 and 75 years.
   • Incidence is rising, largely from incidental detection.
   • Strongest risk factor: tobacco, relative risk 1.4- to 2.5-fold versus controls.
   • Hypertension and obesity are also associated with RCC.
   • About 4–6% of RCC is familial.


Familial Renal Cell Carcinoma and Molecular Genetics
 • Since the early 1990s, several familial RCC syndromes have been identified, and the tumour suppressor
   genes and oncogenes driving both sporadic and familial RCC characterised. This drove revision of the
   histological classification and the development of targeted agents and checkpoint inhibitors.
 • Knudson two-hit hypothesis (from retinoblastoma): both alleles of a tumour suppressor gene must be
   inactivated. Familial patients are born with one mutant allele in every cell, so tumours are early-onset
   and multifocal; sporadic tumours need two somatic hits in one cell, so they are late-onset and unifocal.
   The hypothesis holds for RCC, and familial kindreds enabled linkage analysis.

Von Hippel-Lindau Disease, VHL Gene and Genetics of Clear Cell RCC
 • VHL disease is the familial form of clear cell RCC (CCRCC): autosomal dominant, about 1 in 36,000.
 • Major manifestations: RCC, phaeochromocytoma, retinal angiomas, and haemangioblastomas of the
   brainstem, cerebellum or spinal cord — all highly vascular. CNS lesions can cause paralysis or death;
   retinal lesions, blindness. Others: renal and pancreatic cysts, inner-ear (endolymphatic sac) tumours,
   pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours, epididymal papillary cystadenomas.
 • Penetrance high (70–87%) but incomplete for individual traits; phaeochromocytoma clusters in particular
   families.
 • RCC develops in about 50%, with early onset (third to fifth decades), bilateral and multifocal. With
   better CNS management, RCC is now the commonest cause of death in VHL.

 • Gene discovery: cytogenetics showed loss of chromosome 3 (especially in clear cell RCC); linkage in VHL

 • Genotype–phenotype: missense (type 2) mutations → full-length but non-functional protein →

Hereditary Papillary Renal Carcinoma
 • Non-clear-cell RCC rarely shows chromosome 3 or VHL abnormalities, implying a separate genetic basis.
   PRCC — the second commonest subtype — shows trisomy 7 and 17, plus changes in chromosomes 1, 12,
   16, 20 and Y.
 • HPRC (described 1995): median age about 45, multifocal bilateral PRCC; reported in only about 35
   families worldwide (incidence <1 in 1,500,000). No tumours in other organs, unlike VHL.
 • Type 1 versus type 2 PRCC was formerly used (type 1: sporadic/HPRC; type 2: HLRCC) but has been
   abandoned because type 2 was heterogeneous.
 • HPRC tumours are type 1-like, less aggressive → favourable course; avoid overtreatment. CKD from
   parenchymal replacement and repeated interventions contributes to morbidity.
 • Mechanism: activating missense mutations of the c-MET proto-oncogene (7q31) — the receptor
   tyrosine kinase for hepatocyte growth factor — mostly in the tyrosine kinase domain, causing
   constitutive activation. Inheritance of the mutation puts every renal cell at risk from birth (early onset,
   multifocality); incomplete penetrance implies modifying loci.
 • c-MET mutations are also found in a subset of sporadic PRCC; MET inhibitors are in early trials.

Hereditary Leiomyomatosis and Renal Cell Carcinoma
 • Cutaneous and uterine leiomyomas with aggressive PRCC-like tumours; mean age at diagnosis early 40s.
   Renal tumours are often solitary and unilateral (unusual for familial RCC) and more aggressive than other
   familial RCC; collecting duct carcinoma also occurs.

 • Gene: fumarate hydratase (FH, 1q42–43) — a Krebs-cycle enzyme, acting as a tumour suppressor;
   germline mutations in 90% of HLRCC; biallelic somatic loss occurs in sporadic cases. Mechanism relates to
   the Warburg effect.
 • Nomenclature: "HLRCC syndrome-associated RCC" when familial; "FH-deficient RCC" when histological or
   clinical diagnosis is uncertain.
 • Penetrance for RCC only 15–20%, whereas almost all develop cutaneous leiomyomas and (if female)
   uterine fibroids, usually at 20–35 years; many women have had a hysterectomy before the syndrome is
   recognised; leiomyosarcoma occasionally.
 • Prompt surgical management of renal tumours is recommended — in contrast to the conservative
   approach in most other syndromes. FDG-PET can detect small metastatic foci (high metabolic activity).
   Some young FH carriers have low-grade oncocytic tumours resembling SDH-RCC.

Succinate Dehydrogenase-Deficient RCC
 • Bilateral, multifocal, early-onset (≤40 years) renal tumours, often with phaeochromocytoma, head and
   neck paraganglioma and gastrointestinal stromal tumour.
 • Germline mutations in Krebs-cycle SDH subunit genes (SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, SDHAF2) — another
   Warburg-effect cancer.
 • Early reports suggested about one-third metastasise; more recent series show a good prognosis when
   low-grade. High grade, coagulative necrosis or sarcomatoid features predict aggression.

Tuberous Sclerosis Complex
 • Autosomal dominant; AMLs, cortical tubers, subependymal nodules or subependymal giant cell
   astrocytomas, pulmonary lymphangioleiomyomatosis, cardiac rhabdomyomas and facial
   angiofibromas.
 • Renal AMLs are multifocal, bilateral and often large; angioembolisation or partial nephrectomy (PN) may
   be needed to treat or prevent bleeding from tumours ≥5 cm.
 • Genes: TSC1 (9q34, hamartin) or TSC2 (16p13, tuberin). The TSC1/TSC2 complex downregulates mTOR;
   mTOR inhibitors (everolimus) are active against TSC-associated AMLs.
 • RCC, typically clear cell, in 1–3%.

Birt-Hogg-Dubé Syndrome
 • Cutaneous fibrofolliculomas, lung cysts, spontaneous pneumothoraces and renal tumours of distal-
   nephron origin; named after three Canadian physicians (1977).
 • Tumours: ChRCC, oncocytomas and hybrid oncocytic tumours; occasionally CCRCC.
 • Renal penetrance 23–34%, but tumours are often bilateral and multifocal; prevalence about 2 per
   million; mean age at renal diagnosis about 50. Mostly limited aggressiveness, though metastasis and
   death are reported.
 • Gene: folliculin (FLCN, 17p11.2), a tumour suppressor interfacing with mTOR; germline mutation in 88%
   of kindreds. Loss of folliculin activates mTORC1 and mTORC2, increasing TFE3 nuclear translocation.
   Autosomal dominant; genetic testing available.

Cowden Syndrome
 • One of the PTEN hamartoma tumour syndromes (germline PTEN mutation).
 • Lifetime risks: female breast cancer 50%, RCC 34%, epithelial thyroid carcinoma 10%.
 • Patients with Cowden features but no PTEN mutation may carry KILLIN mutations (adjacent tumour
   suppressor), also raising RCC risk.

 • >31-fold increased RCC risk → screen with CT or MRI. RCC is most often papillary; chromophobe and

BAP1 RCC Susceptibility
 • Autosomal dominant: RCC, melanocytic tumours, cutaneous or uveal melanoma, malignant
   mesothelioma.
 • BAP1 is a nuclear deubiquitinating enzyme with chromatin-modifying function like PBRM1 and SETD2 —
   all on chromosome 3.
 • BAP1 and PBRM1 have distinct signatures; BAP1 mutation associates with necrosis, higher grade and
   worse survival.

MiTF-Associated Cancer Syndrome
 • A germline missense variant (p.E318K) of microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MiTF) confers
   a >5-fold risk of melanoma and RCC.
 • MiTF regulates HIF-1α; the mutation activates HIF-1α, potentially promoting RCC.
Concept — Every familial syndrome converges on a small number of pathways — HIF/VEGF (VHL), MET,
Krebs-cycle metabolism (FH, SDH) and mTOR (TSC, BHD, PTEN) — and the pathway predicts both the
histology and the drug; the one clinical outlier is HLRCC, where the tumour's aggression overrides the 3 cm
surveillance rule.

Tumour Biology and Clinical Implications
Resistance to Cytotoxic Therapy
 • Cytotoxic chemotherapy response only 5–7% — the historical reason for poor metastatic outcomes.
 • Multidrug resistance (MDR) proteins pump out hydrophobic drugs; RCC also resists cisplatin, MDR
   inhibitors and non-MDR substrates, implying redundant resistance mechanisms.
 • Understanding the VEGF and mTOR pathways and checkpoint inhibition produced effective drugs
   (Chapter 144).

Angiogenesis and Targeted Pathways
 • RCC is one of the most vascular cancers. VEGF, suppressed by wild-type VHL, is the primary angiogenic
   inducer in CCRCC; serum and urine VEGF are higher with hypervascular tumours.
 • VEGF is a ligand family regulated by HIF, mTOR and VHL, binding VEGF receptors (VEGFR) → tyrosine
   phosphorylation → endothelial survival and proliferation. Promiscuous ligand–receptor interactions
   make each TKI's effects hard to predict.
 • Bevacizumab was the first successful agent to target the VHL pathway.
 • Cabozantinib inhibits VEGF, MET, RET and AXL — active even second-line, suggesting multiple pathways
   must be targeted in refractory disease.
 • Belzutifan: 50% response in VHL (renal tumours, retinal haemangioblastomas, pancreatic lesions); FDA
   approved 2021 for VHL; now studied with TKIs and ICIs in sporadic RCC.

Cancer Genome Research
 • The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) characterised CCRCC in 2013, and in 2018 published data on 843 RCCs
   (488 clear cell, 274 papillary, 81 chromophobe), showing shared and subtype-specific features.
 • 16 significantly mutated genes; VHL is the commonest somatic mutation (30–40%). FH may be more
   often mutated in the general population but with low penetrance. Pathways: chromatin remodelling,
   VHL/HIF, HIPPO, NRF2/ARE, PI3K/AKT.

Microbiome
 • Microbes outnumber human cells by nearly 2:1.
 • Evidence in RCC: certain microbiota are protumorigenic in immune-responsive tumours; antibiotics
   during ICI therapy nearly halved median overall survival in one study; responders and non-responders
   to ICI have different microbial fingerprints; bacterial strains alter anti-CTLA-4/anti-PD-1 responses in
   mice; targeted antibiotics against deleterious species improved response; tumour and normal renal
   tissue carry different microbiota.

driven hypervascularity made anti-angiogenic drugs work, and immune evasion through checkpoints made

Pathology
 • Most RCCs are round to ovoid with a pseudocapsule (compressed parenchyma and fibrous tissue), not a
   true capsule. Unlike upper-tract urothelial carcinoma, most are not grossly infiltrative — exceptions:
   collecting duct carcinoma and sarcomatoid variants.
 • Size: usually 4–8 cm. Tumours <3 cm are typically indolent RCC or benign, though small tumours rarely
   metastasise.
 • Gross: yellow, tan or brown, with fibrosis, necrosis or haemorrhage. Cystic degeneration in 10–25%,
   associated with better prognosis; calcification in about 10% (stippled or plaque-like).
 • Nuclear grade is an independent prognostic factor, based on nuclear size, shape and nucleoli. The four-
   tier WHO / International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) system is validated for clear cell and
   papillary RCC and is the recommended system.

 • The logic: grades 1–3 turn on the magnification at which nucleoli become visible; grade 4 is
   morphological — rhabdoid or sarcomatoid differentiation is automatically grade 4.
 • Aggressive local behaviour in about 20%: invasion of perinephric fat, renal sinus or collecting system.
   Gerota's fascia usually prevents spread to adjacent organs, although high-grade tumours can breach it.
 • Venous involvement in about 10% — more than any other tumour type: thrombus into renal vein and
   inferior vena cava (IVC), as high as the right atrium. Thrombi are often arterially vascularised; direct
   invasion of the venous wall worsens prognosis.
 • Most sporadic RCCs are solitary. Bilateral RCC (synchronous or asynchronous) in 2–4% of sporadic cases,
   commoner in familial forms. Multicentricity in 10–20%, commoner in PRCC and familial RCC.
 • Satellite lesions are small and may be invisible on imaging, intraoperative ultrasound or inspection — the
   main cause of ipsilateral recurrence after PN.
 • Clonality: multifocal CCRCC in one kidney is usually clonal; a contralateral tumour is likely independent if
   synchronous, metastatic if asynchronous.
 • Intratumour heterogeneity: multiregion sequencing shows single biopsies may miss heterogeneity and
   confuse driver with passenger mutations — a challenge for biomarkers and personalised medicine.
 • All RCCs are adenocarcinomas of renal tubular epithelium. Most share proximal-tubule ultrastructure
   (microvilli, complex junctions); ChRCC, SMARCB1-deficient medullary carcinoma and collecting duct
   carcinoma arise distally.
 • Classification history: traditionally four subtypes (clear cell, papillary, chromophobe, collecting duct/
   medullary) by cytoplasm and location; revised by WHO in 2022 to add newly described entities.

Clear Cell Renal Cell Carcinoma
 • 70–80% of RCC — the "garden variety". Yellow and highly vascular, with delicate sinusoids between
   sheets or acini of cells.
 • Clear cytoplasm results from glycogen, cholesterol, cholesterol esters and phospholipids dissolved
   during processing; granular eosinophilic (mitochondria-rich) cells may predominate.
 • Sarcomatoid features in 5–8%; venous extension more likely than any other subtype.
 • The prognostic paradox: localised CCRCC does worse than PRCC or ChRCC even after stage and grade
   adjustment, yet metastatic CCRCC does better, because it responds to VEGF-targeted therapy and ICIs.
 • 3p alterations in >90% (VHL, PBRM1, SETD2, BAP1).

Papillary Renal Cell Carcinoma
 • Second commonest (10–15%). Basophilic or eosinophilic cells in papillary or tubular arrangement; gross
   beige-white, spherical, frequent haemorrhage that may mimic cystic change on imaging.
 • Multicentricity approaching 40% in some series, more so in ESRD and acquired renal cystic disease.



                                                          page 18 of 44
Chapter 137 — Malignant Renal Tumours                                                                AETIOLOGY

 • Type 2 removed from the 2022 WHO classification as heterogeneous, giving a more homogeneous PRCC
   category.
 • TCGA: MET altered in 81% (mutation, splice variant, fusion, copy number); trisomy 7 and 17, loss of Y*;
   also gains of 12, 16, 20 and loss of heterozygosity on 14. MET is being targeted with cabozantinib.*
 • Hypovascular relative to clear cell, probably from absent VHL mutation.
 • 5-year cancer-specific survival 86–92%, likely better now aggressive variants are excluded.
 • Papillary adenomas — ≤15 mm, low-grade, common at autopsy, share PRCC genetics, considered
   indolent.

Chromophobe Renal Cell Carcinoma
 • 3–5% of RCC, from the distal convoluted tubule. Low mutational burden — only TP53 and *PTEN
   frequent; FLCN* in BHD, but most are sporadic.
 • Classic "plant cell": transparent reticular cytoplasm and prominent cell membrane; eosinophilic subtype
   about 30%. A perinuclear halo suffices for diagnosis on haematoxylin and eosin.
 • Hypodiploid; whole-chromosome losses (1, 2, 6, 10, 13, 17) in 86%; mitochondrial dysfunction is a
   common feature.
 • Better prognosis than CCRCC; >90% cancer-free at 5 years; poor outcome with sarcomatoid features or
   metastasis. Aggressive tumours may carry mTOR-activating mutations; TKIs, mTOR inhibitors and ICIs
   have limited activity in metastatic disease.

Other Oncocytic Renal Neoplasms
 • Chromophobe versus oncocytoma remains hard, with poor agreement even among expert pathologists.
 • 2022 WHO descriptive term: "oncocytic renal neoplasm of low malignant potential, not otherwise
   specified" — for solitary, sporadic tumours; a clinical management category, heterogeneous but indolent
   with exceedingly low metastatic potential.

Collecting Duct Carcinoma
 • Carcinoma of the collecting ducts of Bellini — rare, poor prognosis. Mostly large infiltrative masses from
   a medullary pyramid invading cortex; dilated tubules and papillae lined by cuboidal cells (cobblestone
   appearance).
 • A diagnosis of exclusion: many historical cases were FH-deficient or medullary RCC, urothelial carcinoma
   with glandular differentiation, or metastasis (e.g., lung). Strict criteria require PAX8 expression and
   exclusion of these — now vanishingly rare.
 • High grade, advanced, unresponsive to conventional therapy; shares features with urothelial carcinoma,
   so some respond to cisplatin- or gemcitabine-based chemotherapy; multimodality therapy outperforms
   nephrectomy alone in metastatic disease.

Molecularly Defined Renal Cell Carcinoma
 • 2022 WHO update adds subtypes redefined molecularly, communicating biology more accurately,
   especially when morphology is ambiguous.
 • Former type 2 PRCC comprised at least three entities: MET-altered tumours; MiT family translocation
   RCC; FH-deficient RCC.

Screening and Clinical Associations
 • Population screening is not feasible: low incidence of lethal kidney cancer and no cheap, effective test —
   although localised RCC is highly curable and metastatic RCC is not.
 • Limiting factors: incidence only about 16 per 100,000 per year; about 80% of newly diagnosed small
   renal masses are benign or indolent versus about 20% potentially aggressive. A test would need

   near-100% specificity, and yield would still be too low to be cost-effective. Even high-risk groups (male,
   older, heavy smokers) carry only twofold to threefold relative risk.
 • Dipstick haematuria yields very little RCC — small RCCs are parenchymal, not urothelial, and often cause
   no haematuria. Ultrasound or CT screening finds RCC at 23–300 per 100,000 with more organ-confined
   tumours, but is unlikely to be cost-effective.
 • Candidate biomarkers (none validated): urinary aquaporin-1, perilipin-2, microsatellite alterations, VHL
   mutation or hypermethylation, VEGF, CA-IX, serum kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), circulating tumour
   cells and circulating tumour DNA.
 • Screening must target defined populations: ESRD, tuberous sclerosis, familial RCC.

Screening in Cystic Kidney Disease
 • 80% of ESRD patients develop acquired renal cystic disease; 1–2% of these develop RCC. Relative risk of
   RCC in ESRD fivefold to twentyfold; 15% have metastases at presentation.
 • Screening is recommended, but limited by short life expectancy, adenomas in 20–40%, distorted
   anatomy and cost — compromise: screen younger, fitter patients from the third dialysis year.
 • Renal transplant recipients remain at risk in native kidneys (RCC in 1.4–2.3% within 3 years) → continue
   periodic imaging after transplantation.
 • ADPKD: imaging very difficult and no significantly increased RCC risk → screening not recommended.

 • Trap: compare studies across eras with care — the same tumour may carry different stages.
 • TNM limitations: stage is set by the single worst feature, losing information (e.g., adrenal involvement
   plus supradiaphragmatic thrombus); continuous variables made discrete lose accuracy. Record all adverse
   features parenthetically; integrated nomograms often outperform staging.
 • Clinical staging begins with history, examination, imaging and selected laboratory tests.
 • Suggest advanced disease: weight loss >10%, cachexia, poor performance status; palpable mass or
   lymphadenopathy; non-reducing varicocele or leg oedema (venous involvement); anaemia,
   hypercalcaemia, abnormal liver function or sedimentation rate, raised alkaline phosphatase or lactate
   dehydrogenase.
 • Radiographic staging: high-quality abdominal CT plus chest radiograph in most; MRI for locally advanced
   disease, poor renal function, venous involvement or contrast allergy; chest CT as indicated.
 • Perinephric fat: stranding is non-specific; a distinct enhancing nodule in perinephric space is more
   definitive but uncommon; overall CT/MRI accuracy for perinephric invasion is low (microscopic spread).
 • Adrenal: CT plus intraoperative inspection. Enlarged or indistinct adrenal, extensive replacement with no
   plane, or palpably abnormal gland → treat as involved. Normal on imaging and inspection → no routine
   adrenalectomy.
 • Lymph nodes: nodes ≥2 cm on CT almost always malignant — confirm surgically or by biopsy if not a
   surgical candidate. Smaller nodes are often inflammatory and should not preclude surgery. MRI
   distinguishes vessels (lumbar veins) from nodes.
 • Invasion of adjacent organs: MRI is the premier study; loss of fat plane (e.g., with liver) on CT is usually
   not true invasion, but be prepared to resect bowel, liver, spleen or pancreas with large infiltrative masses.
 • Venous thrombus: CT sensitivity 78% for renal vein and 96% for IVC thrombus. Signs: venous
   enlargement, abrupt calibre change, filling defect, collaterals. False negatives mostly right-sided (short

   • TNM is the preferred staging system.
   • T stage reflects size and extension into renal or extrarenal structures.
   • Pathological stage is the most important prognostic factor.
   • T4, N1 and M1 confer a poor outcome.
   • Other key pathological features: nuclear grade, tumour size, histological subtype.
   • Clinical signs, tumour factors and laboratory findings also predict cancer-specific survival.
   • Integrated systems such as nomograms often outperform stage alone.

 • About 20% of solid, enhancing cT1 masses are benign — usually oncocytoma or atypical AML. Young to

 • Size tracks aggressiveness: in tumours <4 cm, fat invasion, venous or nodal involvement and high grade

 • Male sex and size are the strongest predictors of malignancy; age, BMI and incidental presentation are

 KEY POINTS: RENAL MASS BIOPSY
   • Safe: haematoma about 5%, clinically significant pain about 1%, gross haematuria about 1%,
     pneumothorax <1%, transfusion <0.5%.
   • Tumour seeding almost unreported in contemporary series.
   • A positive biopsy is reliable: specificity 94%, positive predictive value 98.8%.
   • Non-diagnostic rate about 14%, substantially reduced by repeat biopsy.
   • Subtype accuracy ≥90%; grade accuracy variable (60–80%).
   • A non-malignant result may not mean the lesion is benign.

 • Downsides: non-diagnostic in about 14%, occasional false negatives, spillage risk with cystic tumours.
   Office-based ultrasound-guided RMB by urologists is increasing.
 • Molecular profiling of RMB is a research priority, analogous to early prostate cancer. Molecular imaging
   — anti-CA-IX antibody PET for aggressive subtypes (CCRCC, former type 2 PRCC); sestamibi SPECT/CT for

Renal Function After Partial or Radical Nephrectomy: Survival Implications
 • Surgery remains the mainstay of cure. Robson (1969) established RN as the gold standard, supplanting
   simple nephrectomy. RN is still preferred for very large or invasive tumours, but has fallen out of favour
   for small masses because of CKD.
 • RN predisposes to CKD, linked to cardiovascular events and mortality. In a landmark cohort of apparently
   ideal elective-PN candidates, about a quarter already had CKD (GFR <60) — RCC patients are not
   comparable to screened living kidney donors — and CKD was far commoner after RN than PN (65%
   versus 20%).
 • CKD severity and death: population data show relative death rates rising steeply as eGFR falls (about 1.2
   at 45–60, 1.8 at 30–45, 3.2 at 15–30 and 5.9 at <15 mL/min/1.73 m²), after adjustment for confounders.
 • Retrospective meta-analysis favoured PN: less severe CKD, better overall survival and better cancer-
   specific mortality — but PN cannot be oncologically superior to RN, so the cancer-specific advantage
   exposes selection bias, which likely inflates the overall-survival benefit too. An Agency for Healthcare
   Research and Quality systematic review: cancer-specific survival depends on size and stage, not
   strategy; overall survival on age and comorbidity.

Radical Nephrectomy
 • Prototypical RN (Robson): early ligation of renal artery and vein → removal outside Gerota's fascia →
   ipsilateral adrenalectomy → extended lymph node dissection (LND).
 • Perifascial dissection is essential — about 25% of cT1b/T2 RCCs involve perinephric fat.
 • Early arterial ligation remains accepted, though collateral supply in large tumours may prevent hilar
   control before mobilisation.
 • Adrenalectomy is not routine without radiographic enlargement or invasion, unless the tumour
   extensively involves the kidney or is locally advanced.
 • Routine extended LND is not justified: RCC often spreads haematogenously, lymphatic drainage is
   variable, and no cohort with therapeutic benefit has been defined. LND when lymphadenopathy is
   suspected on imaging or at surgery; selectively in locally advanced disease — primarily for staging.
 • Open approach (Chapter 141): extended subcostal preferred by the authors; midline alternative;
   thoracoabdominal for very large upper-pole tumours; extraperitoneal flank for elderly or high-risk
   patients (limited exposure).
 • Laparoscopic RN is established and less morbid for tumours without gross local invasion, with limited or
   no venous involvement and manageable lymphadenopathy, reproducing the oncological tenets; selected
   obese, previously operated or large-tumour patients may qualify. Its appeal has driven overuse of RN for
   small masses.
 • Recurrence after RN is stage-dependent → risk-adapted follow-up. A post hoc analysis of the ASSURE
   adjuvant trial showed published recurrence algorithms perform little better than stage and grade, so the
   AUA risk groups rest on those; histology (especially sarcomatoid or rhabdoid) also matters.

Partial Nephrectomy
 • First described by Czerny (1890); little used until the 1980s. Drivers: better imaging, renovascular surgical
   experience, ischaemia protection, incidental tumours, appreciation of CKD harms, good long-term
   survival.
 • Definition: complete local resection of the tumour with maximal preservation of functioning
   parenchyma. Sequence (Figure 137.19): temporary occlusion of the pedicle → excision with a rim of
   normal parenchyma → collecting system closure and ligation of transected vessels → capsular
   reconstruction.

Thermal Ablative Modalities
 • Cryoablation and RFA are established nephron-sparing alternatives for small localised tumours, usually
   percutaneous, with a favourable morbidity profile relative to PN and limited effect on renal function —
   reasonable for a small tumour in a solitary kidney, though PN remains preferred there.
 • Oncological efficacy is strong, but excision remains the reference standard: local recurrence is higher
   after primary TA (Figure 137.23: meta-analysis risk ratio about 0.55 favouring PN, with no difference once
   secondary ablations are counted). Other concerns: RMB may miss heterogeneity; no pathological
   staging. Most local recurrences are salvaged by repeat ablation; cancer-specific survival for cT1a is high
   whatever the strategy; overall survival depends on age and health.
 • Candidates: elderly or comorbid patients wanting proactive treatment (AS now often used instead); local
   recurrence after PN; hereditary multifocal RCC; informed patient preference.
 • Size: success highest and complications lowest below 2.5–3.0 cm; local recurrence-free rates >90% in
   some centres. Lower success with avidly enhancing masses and clear cell histology → preliminary RMB.
   Location near hilar vessels, bowel, pancreas or spleen raises risk — hydrodissection (saline) or
   laparoscopic mobilisation may help.
 • AUA 2021: TA is an alternative for cT1a masses <3.0 cm, even in healthy patients; RMB before TA
   preferred.

Active Surveillance
 • Early AS series: small (≤3.5 cm) solid enhancing masses grew about 0.36 cm per year; those excised after
   exceeding 3 cm were organ-confined, mostly low grade; no metastases.
 • Pooled series: median growth 0.09–0.34 cm per year; metastasis <2% over 2–5 years.
 • Limitations: selection bias (small, homogeneous masses); 20% or more may have been benign (no RMB);
   a subgroup grows rapidly (in one series a quarter doubled in volume within a year), and delay may forfeit
   the window for PN or TA.
 • Elderly or high-risk patients can be safely observed with imaging every 6–12 months; competing
   mortality and intervention risk often exceed RCC risk, and treating small masses in the elderly may
   confer no survival benefit.
 • Prospective data support AS even in healthy patients with low-potential masses (≤2 cm, incidental).

Nephron-Sparing Surgery in VHL and Other Familial RCC
 • VHL RCC: younger, multiple bilateral tumours, generally low stage but can metastasise and frequently
   cause death. Histology: solid clear cell tumours plus cysts lined by carcinoma or hyperplastic clear cells
   (incipient carcinoma).
 • Options: bilateral nephrectomy with renal replacement, or nephron-sparing PN or TA to avoid ESRD —
   preferred, even for central tumours; enucleation favoured to preserve parenchyma.
 • Ipsilateral recurrence is frequent (occult microscopic disease). In a multicentre VHL series, cancer-specific
   survival was high at 5 years and fell by 10 years, and freedom from ipsilateral recurrence after PN fell
   steeply between 5 and 10 years — long-term surveillance is mandatory.
 • The 3 cm rule (National Cancer Institute): no metastases in solid VHL tumours <3 cm observed for about
   5 years, versus about a quarter metastasising above 3 cm, rising with size. Withhold PN until the tumour
   reaches 3.0 cm. Applies also to HPRC and BHD; HLRCC is the exception — treat aggressively even when
   <3 cm.
 • Most VHL patients develop recurrence needing re-intervention; repeat PN is difficult (fibrosis), so TA may
   reclaim control.
 • Belzutifan (oral HIF-2α inhibitor) slows growth and reduces interventions: renal response 30–50%, most
   tumours shrink or stabilise; commonest side effects anaemia and fatigue.
 • Transplantation after removal of all renal tissue is generally safe despite the tumour diathesis.
 • Before renal surgery in VHL, identify phaeochromocytoma and CNS haemangioblastoma. Genetic
   counselling and screening for other manifestations are part of comprehensive care.
Concept — Management of the localised mass is a three-way balance of oncological risk, renal function and
competing mortality: small, indolent tumours are oversold by RN, so the guidelines push towards PN,
ablation or surveillance and reserve RN for tumours whose aggression, complexity and a healthy
contralateral kidney all justify giving up nephrons.

Locally Invasive Renal Cell Carcinoma
 • T4 tumours are uncommon (<2% of surgical series) and usually present with pain from posterior
   abdominal wall, nerve root or paraspinal muscle invasion.
 • Large tumours metastasise more readily than they invade: only about 40% of suspected direct invasion
   is confirmed pathologically — classic scenario: upper-pole tumour "invading" the liver with intact
   planes at exploration.
 • Differential of a large invasive retroperitoneal mass: adrenocortical carcinoma, urothelial carcinoma,
   sarcoma, lymphoma, as well as invasive RCC.
 • Surgery is the only curative option → en bloc resection of bowel, spleen, distal pancreas or abdominal
   wall. Prepare: bowel preparation, vaccination for splenectomy, endocrine replacement if both adrenals
   involved.
 • Prognosis remains poor even with complete resection — most die of disease within about a year, and
   median survival is far shorter with metastases → neoadjuvant systemic therapy is a valid "litmus test" for
   potentially unresectable RCC.
 • Debulking (incomplete excision) is rarely indicated — 1-year survival only 10–20%. Radiotherapy
   traditionally has little role (bowel toxicity), though stereotactic techniques may change this.

Lymph Node Dissection for Renal Cell Carcinoma
 • Nodal metastasis defines a high-risk group; LND stages accurately, but therapeutic benefit is
   controversial.
 • Only 30–43% of radiographically enlarged nodes are malignant. In high-risk patients, the chance of
   malignancy rises with node size (about 20% at 7 mm, 29% at 10 mm, 90% at 30 mm). Node positivity is
   also stage-specific.

   • Isolated local recurrence after RN: 2–4%; full metastatic work-up; if negative, excision (often en bloc).

     nephrectomy, TA or AS.
   • Local recurrence after TA usually reflects incomplete eradication; options: repeat TA, AS or salvage

OTHER MALIGNANT RENAL TUMOURS

Sarcomas of the Kidney
 • 1–2% of adult renal malignancies, peak in the fifth decade; less common but more lethal than sarcoma
   at any other genitourinary site.
 • Sarcoma versus sarcomatoid RCC is often hard to distinguish; any feature of an RCC subtype excludes
   primary renal sarcoma.
 • Presentation mirrors large RCC (mass, pain, haematuria). Features favouring sarcoma: origin from the
   capsule or sinus; large size without lymphadenopathy; fat or bone (liposarcoma, osteosarcoma);
   hypovascularity — except haemangiopericytoma (highly vascular). Suspect with any very large or
   rapidly growing mass.
 • Biology: mesenchymal, lacking natural barriers; pseudocapsule infiltrated by tumour, extending unseen
   into surrounding tissue → frequent local recurrence even after wide excision. High-grade: lung
   metastases, death within months; low-grade: indolent with repeated local recurrence.
 • Key prognostic factors: margin status and grade. The first resection is the best chance of cure → often
   RN with en bloc excision of adjacent organs; MRI for planning; intraoperative margin assessment.
 • Chemotherapy: traditionally doxorubicin and ifosfamide; gemcitabine and docetaxel; TKIs investigational
   — responses disappointing.
 • Radiotherapy: chemoradiation effective adjuvantly in extremity sarcoma but less so retroperitoneally;
   preoperative radiotherapy may reduce positive margins; intraoperative radiotherapy shows some
   benefit. Multimodal approach if fit.
 • Subtypes: leiomyosarcoma commonest (50–60%), then liposarcoma — the reverse of retroperitoneal
   sarcoma, where liposarcoma is commonest. Outcomes in all series are poor, with most dying within
   months to a few years.
 • Leiomyosarcoma: from smooth muscle of the capsule or perinephric structures; female predominance,
   fourth to sixth decades; displaces rather than invades parenchyma; histology shows spindle cells with
   blunt-ended nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm; rapid growth, frequent metastasis and recurrence; most
   die within 2 years of RN.
 • Liposarcoma: fat distinguishes it from RCC, but confused with AML or lipoma; grows very large; adjuvant
   radiotherapy and cisplatin-based chemotherapy for high grade or positive margins.
 • Osteogenic sarcoma: calcified, rock hard; extensive calcification in a large hypovascular mass; plain film
   may mimic a staghorn calculus (mass effect suggests xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis or, rarely,
   osteosarcoma).
 • Others: rhabdomyosarcoma, fibrosarcoma, malignant fibrous histiocytoma; malignant
   haemangiopericytoma — highly vascular; preoperative embolisation may help.

Renal Lymphoma and Leukaemia
 • Renal involvement found at autopsy in about one-third of those dying of lymphoma or leukaemia, but
   clinically uncommon — usually silent and late. Urologist's role: differentiate from other tumours, obtain
   tissue, preserve renal function.
 • Non-Hodgkin > Hodgkin; diffuse > nodular. Primary renal lymphoma is rare (little native lymphoid
   tissue). Haematogenous spread in about 90%, the remainder by direct extension from retroperitoneal
   nodes. Commonest pattern: multiple small interstitial nodules that coalesce, and may replace the kidney
   → renal failure.
 • CT is the modality of choice for diagnosis and response; MRI for renal insufficiency, contrast allergy or the
   young.

 • Presentation: usually silent; haematuria, flank pain or renal failure; B symptoms (fever, weight loss,

Metastatic Tumours
 • Metastases are the commonest malignant renal neoplasms: 12% of patients dying of cancer have renal
   metastases at autopsy. Almost all haematogenous (rich vascularity); direct invasion (pancreas, colon,
   adrenal) less common.
 • Sources: lung, breast, gastrointestinal, melanoma, haematological — lung commonest among solid
   tumours. Usually multifocal with widespread disease, but lung, breast and colon metastases may be
   large and solitary, mimicking RCC.
 • CT: isodense, only moderately enhancing (5–30 HU). Suspect with multiple lesions plus metastases or
   known non-renal cancer; percutaneous biopsy confirms.
 • Management: systemic therapy or palliation; nephrectomy almost never (except haemorrhage
   refractory to embolisation).
 • Trap: a solitary, strongly enhancing mass with a history of organ-confined non-renal cancer is more
   likely RCC, especially after a long interval — in a consecutive series, no patient without other evidence of
   progression had a renal metastasis.
