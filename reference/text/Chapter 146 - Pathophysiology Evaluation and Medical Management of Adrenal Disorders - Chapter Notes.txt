Chapter 146 — Pathophysiology, Evaluation and Medical
Management of Adrenal Disorders
• Small, deceptively simple glands (formerly the suprarenal glands) at an anatomical crossroads beside
  major vessels, nerves and viscera; now recognised as central to homeostasis and responsible for several
  major human diseases.
• Medical management is multidisciplinary (endocrinology, nephrology, cardiology); surgical ownership has
  historically been split between urologists, general, oncological and endocrine surgeons, largely by local
  institutional tradition.
• Evaluation and surgery sit naturally with urologists because of minimally invasive skill, familiarity with
  retroperitoneal anatomy and approaches, and the close linkage of renal and adrenal pathophysiology.

HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
• Overlooked by Galen, da Vinci and Vesalius; first described by Bartholomaeus Eustachius (mid-16th
  century).
• Thomas Addison (1855) linked adrenal insufficiency to the glands at autopsy — the eponymous Addison
  disease.
• Brown-Séquard (1856): bilateral adrenalectomy in animals was uniformly fatal — the adrenals are
  indispensable to life.
• Osler (1896): first hormonal replacement — crude porcine adrenal extract produced weight gain in one
  patient with Addison disease.
• Adrenaline discovered and localised to the medulla; shown to produce a sustained rise in blood pressure.
  Its failure to sustain life after bilateral adrenalectomy established Addison disease as a disorder of the
  cortex alone.
• Cortisol isolated in the 1930s; its use in rheumatoid arthritis earned the 1950 Nobel Prize (Kendall, Hench,
  Reichstein). Aldosterone isolated from bovine adrenal in 1952.
• Late 20th-century pioneers: Conn, Wilkins, Liddle, Sutherland.

ADRENAL EMBRYOLOGY AND ANATOMY

Overview
• Paired retroperitoneal organs of cortex and medulla: spiculated mustard-yellow cortex, distinct from
  perinephric fat; brown central medulla on cross-section.
• 4–5 g each; 4–6 cm long, 2–3 cm wide. Right triangular, left crescent-shaped.
• Position either “capping” the upper pole or “cradled” superomedially just above the renal vessels —
  visible on computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and surgically relevant,
  especially on the left and in patients with abundant perinephric fat, when identifying the adrenal vein.

 • The medullary migration through the cortex explains the brown stippling of the cortex.
 • Fetal adrenal at birth is twice adult weight but immature; the fetal cortex is resorbed by 12 months.
   Glomerulosa and fasciculata continue to develop, but the zona reticularis completes differentiation only
   at 3 years — reflecting the late importance of adrenal sex steroids. Adrenocortical stem cells may
   contribute to development and homeostasis.
 • Unilateral adrenal agenesis is rare; its reported association with renal agenesis is probably spurious (poor
   imaging of the ipsilateral adrenal) because adrenal and renal development are separate. With renal
   agenesis, malrotation or malascent the adrenal usually develops normally, discoid and in its normal
   position.
 • Adrenal rests (accessory tissue, cortical or medullary) lie along the path of gonadal descent: present in up
   to half of neonates, persisting in about 1% of adults.
 • In congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), testicular adrenal rests may become hyperplastic and present
   as a testicular mass — consider before orchiectomy in CAH.
 • Adrenal heterotopia: incomplete separation of adrenal mesoderm from liver or kidney, with partial or
   complete incorporation into that organ.

 KEY POINTS: ANATOMY AND EMBRYOLOGY
   • Two embryologically and functionally distinct components: cortex (endocrine) and medulla
     (neurocrine).
   • Arterial supply variable; venous drainage predictable — short right vein to IVC, longer left vein to left
     renal vein in most patients.

Concept — The adrenal is two organs sharing a capsule: a mesodermal steroid factory and a neural-crest
sympathetic ganglion. Its surgical anatomy is governed by one asymmetry — the short right vein on the IVC
versus the long left vein on the renal vein.

ADRENAL PHYSIOLOGY
 • Cortex and medulla are best conceived as two distinct organs — the former endocrine, the latter
   neurocrine.

Adrenal Cortex Physiology
 • All cortical steroids derive from cholesterol, chiefly from low-density lipoprotein (LDL); the ratio and
   type of enzymes in each zone determine its product (Fig. 146.4 — steroid synthesis from cholesterol to
   mineralocorticoid, glucocorticoid and androgen).
 • No membrane receptors: lipophilic steroids diffuse into the cell, bind intracellular receptors, and the
   hormone–receptor complex binds DNA to modulate transcription.
Zona Glomerulosa
 • The only zone expressing aldosterone synthase (CYP11B2) — hence the sole source of aldosterone.
 • Aldosterone acts on the distal nephron: reabsorbs Na+ and Cl−, secretes K+ and H+. Sodium reabsorption
   carries free water, so aldosterone changes total body fluid volume, not sodium concentration. Salivary
   and colonic effects are physiologically minor.
 • Regulated chiefly by angiotensin II (renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, RAAS) and directly by serum
   potassium; adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) is a weak stimulus — so the glomerulosa alone does not
   atrophy in pituitary failure.
 • Atrial natriuretic peptide is the main inhibitor (with somatostatin, dopamine) — a cardiac–adrenal–renal
   link.

Zona Fasciculata
 • Glucocorticoid synthesis via 17α-, 21- and 11β-hydroxylase. Cortisol is the principal human
   glucocorticoid, under tight ACTH control in a classic negative-feedback loop.
 • Strict circadian rhythm: most cortisol secreted in the early morning. Glucocorticoids are essential to life
   — metabolism, immunity, intravascular volume, blood pressure, mood, sleep and possibly memory.
Normal hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis physiology
 • Fasciculata secretes up to 20 mg cortisol per day.
 • Hypothalamic corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) (with oxytocin and vasopressin) → anterior
   pituitary corticotrophs cleave pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) to ACTH → adrenal cortisol and androgens
   → cortisol feeds back on hypothalamus (CRH) and pituitary (ACTH). Fig. 146.5 depicts the loop.
 • Stress, physiological or psychological, is the dominant modulator; splanchnic innervation also influences
   output.
 • ACTH is trophic as well as secretagogue: without it (e.g., suppression by exogenous steroid) all but the
   mineralocorticoid-producing cells stop synthesis and undergo apoptosis.
 • CRH follows the suprachiasmatic nucleus: cortisol peaks in the morning, nadir about 11 pm; even small
   perturbations of this rhythm are pathological.

Zona Reticularis
 • 17α-hydroxylase and 17,20-lyase → dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), sulphated DHEA (DHEA-S) and
   androstenedione; some androgen also from the fasciculata. ACTH-controlled and circadian.
 • The largest steroid output (>20 mg/day) but the least important for adult homeostasis.
 • Pharmacological suppression of adrenal androgens is an increasingly used strategy in advanced prostate
   cancer; developmental aberrations cause CAH.

Adrenal Medulla Physiology
 • <10% of adrenal mass; unrelated to the cortex in function and embryology; an integral part of the
   autonomic nervous system.
 • Chromaffin cells receive preganglionic sympathetic fibres from T11–L2 — analogous to sympathetic
   ganglion cells.
 • Secretes epinephrine 80%, norepinephrine 19%, dopamine 1% — catecholamines synthesised from
   tyrosine, modulating the stress response via organ-specific adrenoceptors (Table 146.3). Stored in vesicles,
   released by exocytosis.
 • Phenylethanolamine-N-methyltransferase (PNMT) converts norepinephrine to epinephrine and is
   relatively unique to the medulla (also brain and organ of Zuckerkandl). Glucocorticoids potentiate PNMT
   — one of the few cortex–medulla links — and explain why the medulla is the main source of circulating
   epinephrine.
Catecholamine metabolism (Fig. 146.6)
 • Tyrosine → L-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA) → dopamine → norepinephrine → (PNMT) → epinephrine.
 • Catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) methylates norepinephrine → normetanephrine and epinephrine
   → metanephrine. Most adrenal metabolism occurs within medullary cells themselves, not in liver or
   kidney.
 • >90% of circulating metanephrine and ≥20% of normetanephrine derive from the adrenal medulla — so
   their rise is the basis of phaeochromocytoma diagnosis. Urinary metabolites are largely sulphated.
 • Monoamine oxidase (MAO) and other enzymes produce vanillylmandelic acid (VMA), the end product,
   formed largely in the liver; sympathetic-nerve catecholamines also end as VMA.
 • Terminology: “free” = unconjugated; “fractionated” = individual compounds reported separately.

 KEY POINTS: PHYSIOLOGY
   • Glomerulosa → mineralocorticoid, fasciculata → glucocorticoid, reticularis → androgen; zonal
     enzyme ratios determine the product.
   • Aldosterone is driven by angiotensin II and potassium; ACTH is minor, so the glomerulosa survives
     pituitary failure.
   • Cortisol is under tight ACTH control; adrenal androgen suppression is a target in advanced prostate
     cancer.
   • Catecholamines derive from tyrosine and modulate the systemic stress response.

Concept — Each zone is defined by which enzymes it expresses and which signal it obeys: the glomerulosa
answers to angiotensin II and potassium, the inner zones to ACTH. That single split explains why pituitary
failure spares aldosterone, and why intra-adrenal methylation makes metanephrines the diagnostic currency
of the medulla.

EVALUATION OF ADRENAL LESIONS IN UROLOGICAL PRACTICE
 • Urologists meet adrenal pathology as a referred new mass, an incidental mass in an existing patient, or
   occasionally a clinically suspected syndrome before imaging.
 • Two questions drive management: (1) could the lesion be malignant? (2) is it hormonally active —
   secreting outside normal regulatory control? Either → treatment, usually resection (Box 146.1).
 • Malignant potential is judged by imaging; hormonal activity by laboratory testing; biopsy helps only in
   rare scenarios.
 • The pregnant patient with an adrenal mass needs a tailored multidisciplinary plan (outside the chapter’s
   scope).

Imaging of Adrenal Masses
 • Imaging is the primary tool for malignant potential; several guidelines address it.
 • No imaging feature reliably separates functional from non-functional adenomas — endocrine testing can
   never be omitted on imaging grounds.
Intracellular Fat Content
 • Benign adenomas are lipid-rich and can often be diagnosed on this feature alone.
Unenhanced computed tomography
 • The first and perhaps single best, most easily interpreted test for intracellular lipid; diagnoses adenoma
   in >70% of cases.
 • <10 Hounsfield units (HU) = high intracytoplasmic lipid = adenoma in patients without another known
   malignancy.
 • ≤0 HU is 100% specific but insensitive (a minority of adenomas reach it) — hence the working 10-HU
   threshold: highly specific, sensitivity reported from about 70% to 100% in recent series; confirmed in
   surgical series.
 • Limitations: up to 7% of adrenal metastases measure <10 HU (European Society of Endocrinology–
   European Network for the Study of Adrenal Tumors, ESE–ENSAT), and rare low-density
   phaeochromocytomas exist — metabolic work-up is mandatory for all lesions.
 • Measure density yourself on digital images if the report omits it — avoids unnecessary further imaging.
Magnetic resonance imaging
 • Opposed-phase chemical-shift imaging: fat–water signal interference; signal loss on out-of-phase versus
   in-phase sequences = intracellular lipid = adenoma. Usually assessed qualitatively (quantitative methods
   use the spleen as reference).
 • Test characteristics broadly equivalent to unenhanced CT; possible advantage for lipid-poor adenomas is
   disputed. CT washout is more sensitive than chemical-shift MRI.
Contrast Enhancement
 • Post-contrast attenuation overlaps heavily between adenomas and non-adenomas — enhanced phases
   are rarely useful alone, except for lesions that do not enhance at all (haemorrhage, cyst fluid).

 • Histogram analysis of a region of interest on enhanced CT: if >10% of pixels are <0 HU, the lesion is

Washout Studies
 • Lipid-poor adenomas wash out contrast as rapidly as lipid-rich ones — the basis of the washout study,
   now routine for lipid-poor lesions; highly sensitive and specific.

 KEY POINTS: SIZE AND GROWTH
   • Larger tumours are more likely malignant, yet imaging features remain informative in large masses; in
     healthy, especially young, patients, masses ≥4 cm should be resected or watched very closely.
   • Follow growth kinetics in lipid-poor masses or those >4 cm that are not resected.

Other Imaging Characteristics
 • Large heterogeneous masses invading surrounding structures suggest carcinoma; irregular margins and
   an enhancing rim are quite specific for malignancy. Cysts and acute/subacute haemorrhage have
   characteristic appearances.
Macroscopic fat
 • Macroscopic fat almost always means myelolipoma and is treated by most guidelines as definitively
   benign (reported 100% sensitive for myelolipoma).
 • No agreed definition: areas <−30 HU proposed as fat density; series have used −130 to −23 HU. Also easily
   seen on MRI.
 • Fat proportion discriminates: isolated myelolipomas are usually >50% fat; lesions with <10% fat were
   mixed myelolipoma with other pathology (hyperplasia, functioning or non-functioning adenoma). Rare fat-
   containing ACCs were symptomatic with <5% fat; “myelolipomatous degeneration” of adenomas had
   <20% fat. Teratoma, metastasis, lipoma and angiomyolipoma may contain fat (the latter two need not be
   distinguished clinically).
 • Distinguish from retroperitoneal liposarcoma: aggressive, irregular borders, infiltrative. Some experts
   biopsy when in doubt.
Calcifications
 • Some guidelines treat calcification as definitively benign, but it occurs in up to 30% of ACCs — the authors
   reject that confidence.
 • Fine punctate calcification suggests ganglioneuroma but is not pathognomonic.

Other Imaging Modalities
Ultrasonography
 • Suboptimal for detection and characterisation, yet the commonest source of incidentalomas where it is
   first-line imaging.
 • Right-sided lesions detected more often: the IVC and liver give a window on the right; the left adrenal is
   hidden or mistaken for spleen, pancreas, para-aortic nodes or stomach.
Functional imaging
 • Rarely needed; useful when the origin is unclear or malignancy is suspected but unconfirmed.
 • 18F-FDG PET: high sensitivity and specificity for malignant lesions (ACC, metastases) but reserved for
   inconclusive CT/clinical data; CT washout may be more helpful in patients with known cancer. Caution
   again with RCC and hepatocellular carcinoma metastases.
 • Cortex-specific tracers: NP59 (radiolabelled cholesterol analogue) is taken up by adrenocortical cells —
   separates adenoma from metastasis; planar imaging is limited for small lesions, improved by single-
   photon emission CT (SPECT-CT). 11C-metomidate and 123I-iodometomidate (inhibitors of 11β-
   hydroxylase and aldosterone synthase) identify cortical origin but cannot distinguish adenoma from ACC,
   and 11C has a short half-life.

 KEY POINTS: IMAGING
   • <10 HU on unenhanced CT = high intracellular lipid — extremely specific for adenoma; MRI is similar.
   • CT washout is the gold standard for adrenal imaging: it separates even lipid-poor adenomas (about
     30% of adenomas) from other lesions; gadolinium MRI washout is not used.
   • RCC metastases may mimic lipid-poor adenomas.

Concept — Adrenal imaging is a lipid assay followed by a perfusion assay: unenhanced density detects the fat
that defines adenoma, and washout rescues the lipid-poor adenoma. Everything that defeats the method —
RCC and hepatocellular metastases, low-density phaeochromocytoma — is why imaging never replaces
biochemistry.

Assessing for Hormonal Hypersecretion
Overview
 • All adrenal lesions >1 cm need testing for hormone excess: cortisol and catecholamines in every case;
   aldosterone if hypertension or unexplained hypokalaemia.
 • Rationale: >10% of incidentalomas are metabolically active (Table 146.4). Yet an estimated 75–80% of
   adrenal masses are inadequately evaluated.
 • Myelolipoma: pure myelolipomas are never active, but the authors still advise hormonal work-up.
 • Catecholamine testing in <10 HU lesions: observational data and a meta-analysis found ≥10 HU detected
   100% of phaeochromocytomas, so testing may be unnecessary for <10 HU asymptomatic lesions — now
   incorporated in Canadian Urological Association (CUA) guidance.
 • Never biopsy, sample (AVS) or excise any adrenal lesion before excluding catecholamine excess —
   manipulation can provoke a fatal catecholamine surge.

 • All lesions >1 cm; 5–8% of incidentalomas secrete excess glucocorticoid.

 • Exceedingly rare in incidentalomas. Commonest sex-steroid-secreting mass: ACC co-secreting cortisol.

Biopsy of Adrenal Masses
Overview
 • Limited role: (1) imaging with clinical data diagnoses well; (2) histology cannot reliably separate
   adenoma from carcinoma; (3) biopsy carries risk.
 • ESE–ENSAT: no biopsy without a history of malignancy. Main role: confirm suspected metastasis (rarely
   necessary); tissue in metastatic ACC before systemic therapy.
Test Characteristics of Biopsy
 • Without known malignancy: solitary adrenal metastasis as first presentation is extremely rare; sensitivity
   for malignancy at best 81–84%, for ACC at best 70%; specificity ≈99%. 18–19-gauge needles improve
   yield without more complications; adequacy about 88% (67–100%).

 • With known extra-adrenal malignancy: >50% of new adrenal lesions are metastases; biopsy accurately
   separates metastasis from benign lesion, with excellent negative predictive value once non-diagnostic

Complications of Biopsy
 • 2.8–12%; bleeding commonest; also pneumothorax, haemothorax, abscess, bacteraemia, pancreatitis.

 • Needle-tract seeding in ACC — some advise avoiding biopsy if ACC suspected (only two cases reported).
 • Exclude phaeochromocytoma biochemically first — biopsy can precipitate a life-threatening hypertensive

 • Community biopsies before tertiary referral never changed management in one review. Radiology reports

 • Biopsy only when imaging limits are reached and the result will change management — chiefly a known

ADRENAL NEOPLASIA

Adrenal Incidentaloma
Definition
 • Adrenal mass >1 cm found on cross-sectional imaging performed for an unrelated reason. Strictly
   excludes masses found during cancer staging or in patients later found to have adrenal-related
   symptoms.
 • Work-up aims to reclassify the incidentaloma into a specific neoplasm, which then dictates management.
Epidemiology
 • About 5% on imaging, matching autopsy data; rises with age from <0.5% in the 20s to up to 7% at ≥70
   years.
 • The retroperitoneum allows silent growth beyond 20 cm, yet median size at presentation ≈3 cm.
 • “Incidental” does not mean “insignificant”: nearly 20% are potentially surgical (Table 146.4).

Adrenal Adenoma
Overview and Epidemiology
 • Commonest adrenal neoplasm, cortical in origin; about 6% at autopsy; >85% of incidentally imaged
   adrenal neoplasms are adenomas; prevalence rises with age.
 • Task: separate the non-functional benign adenoma from functional or malignant lesions.
Pathophysiology and Clinical Characteristics
 • Yellow from abundant lipid; histologically may resemble carcinoma — distinguished by the Weiss criteria
   and immunohistochemistry (Box 146.6).
 • Benign by definition; most metabolically silent: in pooled series ≈90% of incidentalomas were benign
   adenomas, of which ≈93% were inactive, ≈6% cortisol-secreting and ≈1% aldosterone-secreting.
 • Testosterone-secreting adenomas are exceedingly rare and may contain Reinke crystals (normally in
   Leydig cells).
 • Associations of non-functional adenoma with insulin resistance and left ventricular dysfunction are
   unconfirmed.
 • Most remain stable; about 9% grow >1 cm over 3 years.
Hormonally Inactive Adenoma
 • No immediate intervention. Repeat testing and imaging are controversial, with society disagreement.

 • About 10% of Cushing syndrome; usually a dominant unilateral hyperplastic nodule, sometimes

 • Confirm hypercortisolism and its ACTH-independent cause before resection.

 • Hypogonadism is common in men — test if symptomatic. Urolithiasis in up to 50% of Cushing syndrome

Mild autonomous cortisol secretion
 • Adrenalectomy is controversial — individualise (ESE–ENSAT). Surgery may improve glucose control,

 • Opposing views: operate only when signs present (hypertension, obesity, glucose intolerance, osteopenia)

Postoperative care
 • Postoperative adrenal insufficiency: ≈65% after unilateral adrenalectomy for MACS; ≈99.7% for overt
   Cushing syndrome — autonomous cortisol suppresses and atrophies the contralateral gland.
 • Endocrine Society replacement: hydrocortisone 10–12 mg/m²/day in 2–3 doses, first dose on waking.
 • Common perioperative protocol: hydrocortisone 100 mg IV on the day of surgery → slow oral taper
   (cortisone acetate 25–37.5 mg/day or hydrocortisone 25–50 mg/day) once oral intake resumes.
 • Deaths from hypocortisolism have been reported after surgery for Cushing syndrome, none after MACS.
 • Alternative: dexamethasone 0.5 mg 6-hourly from surgery (not detected by the cortisol assay) → early-
   morning cortisol <50 nmol/L confirms successful resection → switch to hydrocortisone.
 • Taper: unstudied; one scheme checks morning cortisol 3-monthly and starts ACTH stimulation tests once
   it exceeds 7.4 µg/dL.
Prognosis
 • Median recovery of adrenal function: 6.5 months (MACS) vs 11 months (Cushing syndrome); a few (≈2%
   and ≈7%) never recover.
 • Normalising cortisol reduces but does not eradicate cardiovascular risk; cushingoid features resolve in 7–
   9 months in most, occasionally persisting for years.
Concept — A cortisol-secreting adenoma switches off the ACTH that sustains the opposite gland, so its
removal leaves an atrophic contralateral adrenal. The degree of preoperative autonomy predicts how certain
and how long the postoperative adrenal insufficiency will be.

Phaeochromocytoma
Overview and Epidemiology
 • Tumour of medullary catecholamine-producing cells; 1–2 per 100 000 per year; causes only ≈0.5% of
   hypertension.
 • ≈5% of incidentalomas; incidental discovery accounts for 10–25% of all diagnoses.
 • Paragangliomas = extra-adrenal phaeochromocytomas (10–25%; Fig. 146.20: adrenal 75–90%) arising
   from paraganglia along sympathetic and parasympathetic chains — head, neck, thorax, abdomen, pelvis
   including bladder. The organ of Zuckerkandl (aortic bifurcation to inferior mesenteric artery root) is a
   common site. Some reserve “paraganglioma” for head and neck tumours, which are usually non-
   functional.
Pathophysiology
Overview
 • Adrenal phaeochromocytomas possess PNMT and can make epinephrine; tumours vary in enzyme
   content and self-metabolism, so secretory profiles and clinical behaviour vary.

Treatment
Overview
 • Phaeochromocytoma is a surgical disease: complete resection whenever possible; laparoscopic
   adrenalectomy is standard, open for large or difficult tumours. Lifelong follow-up for all; familial and

NONFUNCTIONAL BENIGN ADRENAL LESIONS

Myelolipoma
Epidemiology
 • Rare, benign, metabolically silent; all ages; <0.1% at autopsy. About 15% extra-adrenal, up to half of
   these presacral.
 • Equal sex ratio; no side preference; bilateral cases reported; may exceed 10 cm; usually asymptomatic,
   occasionally abdominal or flank pain.
Pathophysiology
 • Clonal stem-cell proliferation: mature fat plus trilineage haematopoietic tissue (megakaryocytes may
   abound). Cellularity does not fall with age; no contribution to circulating blood — capillary venous
   sinuses are absent.
 • Induced in rats by necrotic tissue and ACTH → the adrenal-stress-response hypothesis; cause unknown.
Diagnosis
 • Large macroscopic fat component on cross-sectional imaging; interdigitating enhancing myeloid tissue;
   calcification in over a quarter; haemorrhage common. Fig. 146.24: macroscopic fat in a 1.5-cm lesion is
   pathognomonic.
Additional Workup
 • Hormonal testing recommended for all, although some guidelines advise against it: over two dozen
   myelolipomas coexist with hormone excess, usually hypercortisolaemia, generally from a coexisting
   functional adenoma rather than the myelolipoma itself.
Management
 • Surgical excision is definitive when needed; embolisation only for acute haemorrhage. Most do not need
   resection (Fig. 146.23: a rare 15-cm symptomatic lesion).
 • Indications: symptoms, or concern about spontaneous haemorrhage (fewer than 50 cases; smallest
   bleeding lesion 6.3 cm; bleeders average 10–14 cm). No deaths reported; emergency surgery or
   transfusion rare → value of prophylactic excision unclear — individualise.

Ganglioneuroma
Overview and Epidemiology
 • Extremely rare benign neuroectodermal tumour of ganglion and Schwann cells; 0.3–2% of
   incidentalomas.
 • Sites: mediastinum ≈39%, retroperitoneum ≈31%, adrenal ≈21%, occasionally pelvis and neck. Young:
   half aged 10–29; only 20% over 40.
Pathophysiology/Clinical Characteristics
 • Rare malignant transformation; peripheral nerve sheath tumours and composite ganglioneuroma–
   phaeochromocytoma reported; nodal ganglioneuroma cells may represent matured neuroblastoma.
 • Can grow very large and encase vessels without narrowing the lumen. May secrete vasoactive intestinal
   polypeptide (diarrhoea); most are asymptomatic.
Diagnostic Tests
 • No definitive preoperative diagnosis; imaging clues: well circumscribed; attenuation ≤ muscle on
   enhanced and unenhanced CT; calcification in 20–69% (classically fine and punctate, often coarse; limited
   value because ACC also calcifies).
 • MRI: T1 homogeneously low; T2 heterogeneous, intermediate to high. PET standardised uptake value
   usually <3.0.
Treatment and Prognosis
 • Diagnosis almost always at resection, so natural history is undefined; beware large size and encasement
   of retroperitoneal vessels.
Summary
 • Rare, largely pathological diagnosis, nearly always benign course.

 KEY POINT: GANGLIONEUROMA
   • Rare benign tumours of the young that can grow to encase critical structures.


Adrenal Oncocytoma
Overview and Epidemiology
 • Familiar from the kidney; also salivary and endocrine glands. Adrenal oncocytomas are exceedingly rare
   (about 50 reported).
Pathophysiology and Clinical Characteristics
 • Large, mitochondria-rich, granular eosinophilic cells; pathogenesis unclear.
 • Female:male ≈2.5:1; left:right ≈3.5:1; up to 20 cm; cases in pregnancy.
 • Mostly inactive; ≈10% secrete sex steroids, cytokines and/or cortisol.

   • Unlike renal oncocytomas, a large proportion of adrenal oncocytic lesions are malignant.

MALIGNANT ADRENAL NEOPLASMS

Adrenocortical Carcinoma
Overview and Epidemiology
 • Rare: 0.7–2 per million. Bimodal — children in the first decade, adults in the 4th–5th decades (most
   patients are adults); slight female predominance (1.5–2.1:1).
 • Mostly sporadic and unilateral; 2–6% bilateral, possibly hereditary.
 • Associated syndromes: Li–Fraumeni, Beckwith–Wiedemann, Lynch, Carney complex, MEN-1, NF-1,
   familial adenomatous polyposis, McCune–Albright.
 • Surgery is the cornerstone and the best chance of cure; advanced disease needs multimodal therapy.
 • Despite more incidental imaging, there has been no stage or size migration and no survival improvement
   over two decades; administrative data show only a weak relationship between size and metastasis or
   survival.

Management
 • Most present advanced; localised disease still has high local and distant recurrence (60–80%) →
   multidisciplinary, multimodal care. Only one randomised trial exists in metastatic disease; the rest is
   retrospective.
Surgery
 • Complete excision is essential — best chance of cure, control of hormonal and local symptoms.
 • Open surgery preferred for tumours >6 cm or with local invasion (higher peritoneal seeding with
   minimally invasive approaches); smaller organ-confined tumours may be approached laparoscopically with
   caution (spillage risk; outcomes may favour open).
 • En bloc resection of involved organs for negative margins; regional lymphadenectomy for control and
   staging; IVC thrombus may need bypass, replacement or interruption.
 • Metastatic disease: cytoreduction if >90% of tumour burden can be removed — may palliate and
   facilitate other therapy, survival benefit uncertain. If complete debulking is impossible, do not operate —
   it delays systemic therapy.
 • Resect recurrences (improved survival in retrospective series), best in low-grade tumours with long
   disease-free intervals; radiofrequency ablation or angioembolisation if unresectable.
 • Surveillance: CT chest, abdomen, pelvis every 3 months for 2–3 years → every 6 months to 5 years;
   monitor initially raised hormones; PET for indeterminate findings; beyond 5 years individualised (<3%
   recur after 5 years).
Radiation
 • Treatment of choice for bone and brain metastases; limited role for the primary.
 • Adjuvant radiotherapy lowers 5-year local recurrence (≈86% without vs ≈11% with) but not disease-free
   or overall survival; best for patients without metastasis with positive or indeterminate margins.
Medical therapy
 • Mitotane — oral dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT) derivative, the most used agent: adrenolytic
   (direct cortical cytotoxicity, free-radical damage, steroidogenic enzyme inhibition) → steroid replacement
   needed. Toxicity: gastrointestinal upset, lethargy, depression, feminisation in men, rash, raised liver
   enzymes and binding globulins, hypothyroidism, dyslipidaemia, thrombocytopenia → titrate to tolerance
   and serum levels.
 • Single-agent response 14–36%; streptozotocin combination ≈36%; etoposide–doxorubicin–cisplatin
   combination ≈49%.
 • Symptom control while mitotane takes effect: ketoconazole, metyrapone, aminoglutethimide, etomidate
   (adrenostatic) and mifepristone (glucocorticoid receptor antagonist).

Neuroblastoma
 • Neural-crest malignancy (adrenal medulla, sympathetic ganglia); commonest extracranial solid tumour of
   childhood — covered in Chapter 63.

Adrenal Sarcoma
 • Extremely rare primary sarcomas: epithelioid angiosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, liposarcoma, Kaposi
   sarcoma, alveolar and synovial sarcoma.
 • Cannot be distinguished preoperatively from other adrenal malignancies (benign adenoma is excluded by
   the usual work-up); definitive diagnosis can be hard even after resection.
 • Wide local excision with negative margins; mixed carcinoma–sarcoma features → treat as sarcomatoid
   carcinoma.

Metastases
Overview and Epidemiology
 • Common site: at autopsy, >25% of patients with melanoma or lung carcinoma have adrenal metastases;
   many other primaries (RCC, breast, medullary thyroid, contralateral ACC, gastrointestinal, prostate, cervix,
   pancreas, urothelial, seminoma, others) also spread there.
 • With a history of malignancy, >50% of new adrenal lesions are metastases.
 • RCC reaches the adrenal directly or haematogenously, yet ipsilateral adrenalectomy at radical or partial
   nephrectomy only if the gland is radiologically abnormal or thrombus reaches the adrenal vein.
 • Resection of an isolated metastasis may improve survival in melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer
   (NSCLC); a randomised trial now supports metastasectomy in lung cancer.
Pathophysiology and Clinical Characteristics
 • Adrenal insufficiency from metastases is exceedingly rare (though Addison described it); bulky (>4 cm)
   bilateral disease seems necessary. The oft-quoted “20% of metastatic RCC have adrenal insufficiency”
   rests on a single small abstract. Advanced cancer often mimics addisonian symptoms without biochemical
   insufficiency.
Diagnostic Tests
 • Metastases look like adenomas (well circumscribed, homogeneous, rarely necrotic) but lack lipid: >10 HU
   and poor washout (Fig. 146.28: 38-HU left adrenal RCC metastasis years after right nephrectomy).
 • Exceptions: up to 7% of metastases are <10 HU; RCC and hepatocellular carcinoma metastases can wash
   out like adenomas → growth kinetics become decisive; MRI signal intensity may help separate RCC
   metastasis from adenoma. Biopsy helps with a known primary.
 • Unlike renal metastases (usually with widespread disease), adrenal metastases may be early and solitary
   — making resection feasible.
 • Metabolic work-up still required — functional lesion rates are similar with or without prior malignancy,
   and pigmented phaeochromocytoma has been mislabelled as metastatic melanoma.
Treatment and Prognosis
 • RCC: resection of ipsilateral or contralateral adrenal metastasis as part of cytoreduction in selected
   patients; benefit unproven prospectively; role may change with targeted and immunotherapy.
 • Non-urological primaries: multidisciplinary decisions with the medical oncologist; best data for NSCLC and
   melanoma.

 KEY POINTS: ADRENAL METASTASES
   • Common, but bilateral bulky (>4 cm) disease is needed for biochemical adrenal insufficiency.

 • Chronic: non-specific fatigue and anorexia for months.

 • What it tests: the features that exist only in primary disease — salt craving, hyperkalaemia,

Diagnostic Tests
 • Clinical suspicion → morning cortisol and ACTH; primary disease shows abnormal aldosterone and renin;
   confirm with the corticotropin (ACTH stimulation) test; most autoimmune cases have anti-21-
   hydroxylase antibodies.
 • Secondary insufficiency needs specialist HPA-axis testing.
Treatment
 • Hydrocortisone or cortisone acetate, with half to two-thirds of the daily dose in the morning and the
   rest in one or two later doses (circadian mimicry). Fludrocortisone only in primary disease. Androgen
   supplementation for persistent constitutional symptoms (some experts).
 • Perioperative stress dosing is debated — usual dose is often enough, but coverage is given because the
   downside is negligible and crisis catastrophic. Mineralocorticoid is unnecessary for patients on chronic
   steroids (aldosterone intact).

Prognosis
 • Effect of primary insufficiency on life expectancy unknown, but quality of life is impaired (low energy,
   libido, psychological effects); secondary (hypopituitary) insufficiency causes premature death.
Summary
 • In urology: surgical or pharmacological loss of adrenal tissue; high index of suspicion; previous ipsilateral
   nephrectomy before contralateral surgery is the classic at-risk setting; consider in patients on chronic
   steroids and the critically ill; low threshold for endocrinology input.

 KEY POINTS: ADRENAL INSUFFICIENCY
   • Mineralocorticoid deficiency only in primary disease; pituitary or hypothalamic failure (including
     chronic steroid suppression) spares aldosterone.
   • Prior partial or radical nephrectomy + contralateral renal or adrenal surgery = risk of postoperative
     adrenal crisis — review old reports and imaging for the remaining adrenal.

Concept — Whether insufficiency is primary or secondary decides whether aldosterone is lost, and that one
fact separates the two clinically (hyperkalaemia, salt craving, pigmentation) and therapeutically
(fludrocortisone). For the urologist, the danger is iatrogenic: the last functioning adrenal tissue is what an
operation removes.

Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia
 • Autosomal recessive steroidogenic enzyme defect with low cortisol; 21-hydroxylase deficiency in >95%.
   Loss of negative feedback → high ACTH → cortical hyperplasia and androgen excess. Usually diagnosed in
   childhood (Chapter 59).
 • Patients and carriers are prone to benign cortical adenomas, not carcinoma; undiagnosed non-classic 21-
   hydroxylase deficiency can present with adrenal neoplasms later in life.
 • No CAH screening for incidentaloma patients — genetic screening has not shown excess undiagnosed
   CAH.

CONCLUSIONS
 • The adrenals are essential to life-sustaining homeostasis yet, despite frequent incidental lesions, are
   infrequent primary initiators of disease.
