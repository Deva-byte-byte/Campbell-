NEUROBLASTOMA
 • Commonest extracranial solid tumour of childhood; over half present with metastases.
 • Arises from neural crest cells of the adrenal medulla and sympathetic ganglia — anywhere along the
   sympathetic chain (neck, thorax, retroperitoneum, pelvis, adrenal).
 • 75% retroperitoneal — 50% adrenal, 25% paravertebral.
 • Behaviour ranges from spontaneous regression through differentiation to a benign tumour to extreme
   malignancy; biological factors explain much of this variance.

Epidemiology and Genetics
Incidence
 • 8–10% of childhood cancers; 10 per million live births (United States); commonest malignancy of
   infancy.
 • Median age 19 months; most under 5 years; almost all diagnosed by 10 years.
Genetics
 • Familial cases (<1%): autosomal dominant; median age 9 months; ≥20% bilateral adrenal or multifocal
   (rare in sporadic disease); sibling or offspring risk <6%.
 • Linkage to 16p12–13; predisposition genes PHOX2B and ALK.
Constitutional Chromosome Abnormalities
 • Deletions, translocations and amplification all carry prognostic weight (frequencies and direction in the
   marker table under Biologic Variables).
 • Aneuploidy here means an abnormal chromosome number (hyperdiploidy); it is favourable against
   diploid or tetraploid content.
 • MYCN amplification = more than 10 copies at 2p24; drives rapid progression and treatment failure.
   Neuroblastoma was the first tumour whose chemotherapy intensity was set by biological markers, not
   stage and histology alone.
 • 1p deletion: consensus region 1p36.1–2, candidate suppressor CHD5; independent; constitutional 1p
   abnormalities also reported.
 • Unbalanced 11q loss of heterozygosity (LOH) = LOH of 11q with retention of 11p (versus whole-
   chromosome 11 LOH). 1p loss travels with MYCN amplification; 11q loss rarely does, and flags poor
   outcome in low-stage, non-amplified tumours.
 • 17q gain (often by translocation with 1 or 11; 17q22–qter): on multivariate analysis the strongest
   prognostic factor, then stage IV, then 1p deletion.

Embryology and Spontaneous Regression
 • In situ neuroblastoma (term of Beckwith and Perrin): incidental adrenal neuroblast nodules, histologically
   identical to neuroblastoma, found at autopsy in infants under 3 months at 40–45 times the clinical
   incidence → most regress. Such nodules exist in all fetuses.

 • Prenatally detected tumours follow a favourable course; neonatally resected adrenal masses, cystic or
   solid, mostly carry favourable markers.
 • Expectant observation of screen-detected infants → high spontaneous resolution, documented
   radiographically. This led to the Children's Oncology Group (COG) study of observation for infants with
   small perinatal adrenal masses and favourable catecholamine ratios.
Concept — Regression is the normal developmental fate of fetal neuroblasts, so a small favourable-biology
tumour in early infancy behaves as a persisting embryonic remnant, not as an early stage of the lethal
disease of older children.

Pathology
 • Neuroblastoma → ganglioneuroblastoma → ganglioneuroma: increasing maturation.
 • Shimada classification, revised as the International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification (INPC): age-
   linked; adds prognostic information independent of age. First division: stroma-poor versus stroma-rich.
        ◦ Stroma-poor is split into favourable and unfavourable by age, degree of maturation and mitotic rate;
          unfavourable stroma-poor: survival below 10%.
        ◦ Stroma-rich: nodular, intermixed, well differentiated; the last two resemble ganglioneuroblastoma or
          immature ganglioneuroma and survive better.
 • Ganglioneuroma is benign and fully differentiated. Metastases maturing into ganglioneuroma favour
   origin by maturation rather than de novo. It grows very large before causing compression or intraspinal
   extension. Survival is independent of extent of resection → operate only for significant symptoms.

Concept — Histology is graded against age: the same immaturity is tolerable in an infant and unfavourable

Clinical Presentation and Pattern of Spread
 • Usually abdominal pain or a fixed, hard mass; otherwise metastatic features — bone or joint pain,
   periorbital ecchymosis. Thoracic tumours: cough, dyspnoea; intraspinal extension: cord compression.
 • 65% abdominal (adrenal slightly commoner in children than infants); pelvic, from the organ of
   Zuckerkandl, 4% → urinary retention and constipation.
 • Metastases in 70% at diagnosis.

Diagnosis
Laboratory Evaluation
 • Urinary vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) and homovanillic acid (HVA) raised in 90–95%; they fall with
   therapy → monitor response and relapse.
 • Anaemia with marrow disease. Marrow staging: two aspirates + two biopsies (biopsy adds substantially);
   immunocytology may eventually replace biopsy.
Imaging
 • Plain film: calcified abdominal or posterior mediastinal mass.
 • Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) define local extent and vessels; renal
   parenchymal invasion is uncommon but CT-detectable. MRI is better for intraspinal extension and great-
   vessel relationships.
 • Intratumoral calcification and vascular encasement favour neuroblastoma over Wilms tumour.
 • 123I-meta-iodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scan is required by COG for staging, replacing skeletal survey

   and bone scan. Uptake is by adrenergic secretory vesicles; it is more sensitive than 131I-MIBG or bone
   scan for relapse. Non-avid tumours → positron emission tomography (PET).
 • Bone metastases: long bones and skull.
Screening
 • Urinary catecholamine screening in Japan (>20 years) aimed to detect earlier stage. Diagnoses before 1
   year rose steeply, mostly low stage, with almost uniform survival — but advanced disease after 1 year
   did not fall. The aggressive tumours of older children therefore do not arise from the infant tumours.
 • Screen-detected tumours: MYCN amplification essentially absent, favourable (hyperdiploid) ploidy,
   excellent survival → many would regress untreated.

Staging
 • Stage determines adjuvant therapy. The International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INSS) is clinical,
   radiographic and surgical; systems diverge most in intermediate disease.
 • The International Neuroblastoma Risk Group (INRG) Staging System is imaging-based and pre-
   treatment: L1 has no image-defined risk factor (IDRF); L2 has one or more of 20 IDRFs (chiefly vessel or
   nerve encasement); M; MS. L1 has better 5-year event-free survival (EFS) than L2, about 90% versus 78%.
 • The IDRF–resectability link is disputed, but IDRFs predict worse survival and more operative
   complications in localised disease. In unresectable disease, IDRFs are unchanged by chemotherapy in
   half and new in one-fifth. Midline perivascular IDRFs carry more complications and lower EFS and overall
   survival (OS).
 • The INRG Pretreatment Classification combines stage, age, histology, grade, MYCN, 11q and ploidy (COG
   revised version, 2021). It is the most accurate guide to treatment intensity.
 • Resection-based staging is institution-dependent.

Prognostic Factors
Clinical Variables
 • Age: historically ≤1 year does better, reflecting favourable biology. Age behaves as a continuous variable:
   a cut-off of 460 days maximised the difference, and INRG places the risk step at 18 months. Teenagers
   and adults have indolent but relentless disease.
 • Site: non-adrenal primaries do better. Thoracic tumours present younger and localised and do better
   even after correction for age and stage, being less often amplified and more often DNA index >1.0.
 • Stage is powerful and independent:
      ◦ Stage I completely resected — all survive.
      ◦ Stage II is favourable even when excision is incomplete.
      ◦ Stages III and IV fare worse.
      ◦ Historical OS for stages I, II and 4S was 75–90%, against a stage IV 2-year disease-free survival of 19–
        30% despite marrow transplantation.
      ◦ Stage distribution is age-dependent, and infants outperform older children at the same stage.
 • Stage 4S (INRG MS):
      ◦ Small primary with spread to liver, skin and marrow (<10%); no radiographic bone metastases.
      ◦ 7–10% of cases; OS 80–88%; frequent spontaneous regression.
      ◦ Adverse features:
          ▪ neuron-specific enolase (NSE) >100 ng/mL
          ▪ ferritin >280 ng/mL
          ▪ urinary dopamine >2500 nmol/mmol creatinine
          ▪ MYCN amplification
          ▪ 1p deletion
      ◦ Deaths occur in infants under 2 months with massive abdominal disease, respiratory compromise or
        disseminated intravascular coagulation.

Biologic Variables
 • Homogeneously staining regions and double minutes (about one-third) are the cytogenetic signature of
   amplification; MYCN maps there. Amplification is almost always present at diagnosis, and its adverse
   effect is independent of age and stage. Not every poor outcome is amplified: many non-amplified
   advanced tumours still recur.
 • Flow cytometry: hyperdiploid favourable; diploid and tetraploid adverse.
 • COG risk assignment: age, stage, MYCN, histological grade, DNA ploidy.
 • Telomere length was the sole parameter identifying a favourable subgroup within high-risk disease.

Treatment
 • Surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy, weighted by stage, age and biology (Table 63.2).
Surgery
 • Goals: diagnose → stage → excise if localised → tissue for biology. Resectability weighs site, mobility,
   IDRFs and overall prognosis.
 • Neoadjuvant chemotherapy improves resectability; never sacrifice vital structures at diagnosis,
   especially in young children with excellent prognosis.
Low-Risk Disease (Stages I, II and 4S)
 • Stage I: surgery alone, disease-free survival >90%. Chemotherapy only at recurrence, unless MYCN
   amplification with unfavourable histology; no radiotherapy. Risk grading now identifies the few who
   used to recur.
 • Resection alone for stages I–II gave excellent OS; supplemental therapy was rarely needed. OS was lower
   with MYCN amplification, unfavourable histology, age >2 years and positive nodes.

 • COG P9641 treated asymptomatic disease with surgery alone. Chemotherapy was reserved for symptoms,
   those at risk of symptoms, or <50% resection with progression. Results: 5-year EFS about 89%, OS about
   97%. In stage 2B, outcome was worse with unfavourable histology or diploidy and at ≥18 months. About
   one in nine observed patients recurred or progressed.
 • Removing normal organs, particularly the kidney, is not justified. Radiotherapy to residual stage II
   disease does not change progression-free survival → keep it for chemotherapy-refractory relapse.
 • Stage III, or stage II around the renal vessels: preoperative chemotherapy reduces nephrectomy (Figure
   63.5).
 • 4S: most tumours have entirely favourable markers; a small adverse-marker fraction progresses fatally.
      ◦ Resection of the primary is not mandatory — survival is equal after complete resection, partial
        resection or biopsy.
      ◦ Survival falls sharply with diploidy, MYCN amplification or unfavourable histology.
      ◦ Extensive metastases with amplification = high risk → multimodal therapy. Favourable and
        asymptomatic → supportive care ± limited chemotherapy.
High-Risk and Advanced Disease
 • More than half of new patients are high risk; the value of extensive resection in stage IV is contested
   (table).
 • As systemic control improves, local control matters more. Gross total resection plus external-beam
   radiotherapy gives 84–90% local control. Double autologous bone marrow transplantation (ABMT) with
   aggressive surgery and radiotherapy gives local recurrence of only 2.6%.
 • Defer resection until after initial chemotherapy: tumours become smaller and firmer, with less rupture,
   haemorrhage and nephrectomy, and more complete resections. Timing: 13–18 weeks, after 3–4 courses;
   some remain inoperable.
 • Complications:
      ◦ Diarrhoea from division of autonomic nerves anterior to the aorta at the coeliac and superior
        mesenteric origins.
      ◦ Chylous ascites from nodal resection — usually self-limiting.
 • Infants with extensive local or stage IV disease do better than older children but worse than 4S; biology
   separates high from intermediate risk. In European trials, non-amplified infants with midline-crossing
   primaries or a positive skeletal survey did well without chemotherapy; those with overt skeletal, lung or
   central nervous system metastases did well with ≥4 courses.

Chemotherapy
 • High-risk sequence: intensive induction → myeloablative consolidation with stem-cell support →
   biological therapy for minimal residual disease. Five-year survival rose from <15% to 30–40%; relapse
   remains the problem; dose intensity correlates with response and survival.
 • The Memorial Sloan Kettering (MSKCC) induction — cyclophosphamide, vincristine and doxorubicin
   alternating with cisplatin and etoposide — gives high response rates and is widely adopted.
 • Myelosuppression limits dose → ABMT after myeloablative chemotherapy or total-body irradiation.
   Complete remission is seen in up to half of stage IV, but with late relapse.
 • Bulky disease fails → debulk before ABMT. Transplant toxicity can be lethal and late effects are unknown;
   this is accepted because cure is rare without it.

New Innovative Biologic Therapies
 • Double ABMT has reached the ceiling of myeloablation. 13-cis-retinoic acid differentiates tumour cells,
   including recurrent ones; it is given for 6 months after cytotoxic therapy.
 • GD2 disialoganglioside is uniformly expressed on neuroblastoma. Antibody is now combined with an
   irinotecan–temozolomide backbone. Humanised antibodies aim to reduce the dose-limiting pain, and
   front-line GD2 trials are planned. Temozolomide and irinotecan (phase I–II in relapse) will advance to
   front-line trials.
 • 131I-MIBG therapy delivers radiation via tracer uptake. It achieves responses even after heavy
   pretreatment (about 28–39%) and is now in COG trials.

Radiotherapy
 • Effective for local control; relapse risk tracks biology. No benefit in low stage; improves local control in
   stage IV and bulky stage III. No randomised comparison with surgery. Dose 15–30 Gy by age, site and
   residual disease.
Spinal Cord Compression
 • Symptomatic in up to 5%; radiographic intraspinal extension in up to 13%.
 • Laminectomy, radiotherapy and chemotherapy give similar neurological outcomes; severe motor deficits
   recover little.
 • Sequence: chemotherapy first → laminectomy only for progressive deterioration (laminectomy causes
   late scoliosis); radiotherapy avoided (impairs spinal growth).
Concept — With equal neurological results from every modality, the choice rests on late morbidity —
scoliosis after laminectomy, growth arrest after radiotherapy — which leaves chemotherapy first.

 KEY POINTS: NEUROBLASTOMA
   • Most common extracranial solid neoplasm in children.
   • Children younger than 18 months survive better, attributed to favourable biology.
   • Complete resection is curative for low-stage disease.
   • MYCN amplification (about 20% of primaries) is associated with progression and a poor prognosis
     independent of age and stage; biological markers now drive treatment protocols.


WILMS TUMOUR

Epidemiology
 • Wilms tumour (WT), nephroblastoma: 6–7% of childhood cancers, 95% of kidney cancers under 15
   years; an embryonal tumour from immature renal remnants.
 • Incidence 7.5 per million person-years (0–14 years). Over 80% before 5 years; median 3.5 years;
   occasionally adults.
 • Younger in bilateral and syndromic disease, and in males; slight female excess overall.

Chapter 63 — Paediatric Urologic Oncology: Renal and Adrenal                                    WILMS TUMOUR

 • Lower in East Asian, higher in Black populations. The variation follows race, not geography, so
   environmental factors play a minor role. Few exposures are established.
 • Black children present more advanced; whether biology or social determinants of health explain this is
   unclear. In Africa, survival is poor but improving; delayed presentation and treatment abandonment
   persist.

Biology/Genetics
 • Mostly somatic; several events are required, so the Knudson two-hit model does not explain most cases.
 • 10–15% are due to a germline variant or early embryonic epigenetic change (11p15), with or without a
   syndrome.
 • Familial 1–2%, two-thirds unexplained.
 • Commonest constitutional changes: WT1 and 11p15.5. Systematic testing finds a cause in up to one-third.
 • Whether to refer all patients for genetics is debated. Accepted indications are:
      ◦ bilateral or multifocal tumours
      ◦ age under 2 years
      ◦ nephrogenic rests
      ◦ overgrowth or predisposition features, or family history
 • In unselected cohorts, 11p15 / Beckwith-Wiedemann spectrum is commonest, then WT1 syndromes,
   then DIS3L2.

WT1
 • First WT gene, found through 11p13 deletions in WAGR (Wilms tumour, aniridia, genitourinary

 • Serial renal ultrasonography (US) every 3–4 months in high-risk children. The interval does not lengthen

Favorable-Histology Wilms Tumor
 • A pseudocapsule of compressed parenchyma distinguishes WT from rests. Tumours are soft, friable,
   necrotic or haemorrhagic, which raises the risk of intraoperative spill.

 • 12% multicentric unilateral. Extrarenal WT is rare, arising from displaced metanephric or mesonephric
   remnants.
 • Derived from metanephric blastema, WT may contain heterotopic muscle, cartilage or squamous
   epithelium. Classic triphasic pattern = blastema + epithelium (tubules, rosettes, glomeruloid bodies) +
   stroma (Figure 63.7). Biphasic or monomorphous tumours are diagnostically hard.
 • Epithelial-predominant tumours have low aggressiveness and are mostly stage I, but are resistant when
   advanced.
Anaplastic Wilms Tumor
 • Recognised through National Wilms Tumor Study Group (NWTSG) central review. Three criteria:
        ◦ nuclear enlargement ≥3× adjacent nuclei
        ◦ hyperchromasia
        ◦ abnormal mitoses
 • Seen in ~2% under 2 years and ~13% over 5 years. Causes chemoresistance, with a poor prognosis even
   at stage I. Focal versus diffuse: diffuse (throughout the kidney or extrarenal) is worse.
 • Later age and absence from rests mean anaplasia is acquired by WT cells; TP53 is mutated in the
   majority.
Pathology After Preoperative Chemotherapy
 • The International Society of Paediatric Oncology (SIOP) grades response by volume and histology.
 • Stromal- and epithelial-predominant tumours are enriched after chemotherapy: they shrink little but are
   excellent if excised.
 • Blastema responds, but residual blastemal predominance → relapse and worse survival.
 • Progression during chemotherapy occurs in ~5% (SIOP 93-01), with lower OS.
 • Chemotherapy and radiation may induce cytodifferentiation (e.g. rhabdomyomatous), which confounds
   biopsy; differentiated tumours are chemoresistant → excise.
 • Necrosis correlates positively with pre-treatment blastema and negatively with epithelium.
 • Stage I low-risk after nephrectomy → no further chemotherapy.

Preoperative Evaluation and Staging
 • Over 85% symptomatic — mass, pain, haematuria. Asymptomatic masses are found by parents or
   doctors, and are more often stage I–II. The mass may be huge and cross the midline.
 • Haematuria ~20%; hypertension ~25% (renin); fever, anorexia and weight loss 10%; rarely an acute
   abdomen from rupture. Gross haematuria → cystoscopy + retrograde pyelogram at nephrectomy, to
   detect collecting-system extension.
 • Supine-persistent varicocele or hepatomegaly → inferior vena cava (IVC) thrombus; atrial thrombus →
   hypertension or heart failure (<10% symptomatic). Examine for aniridia, hemihypertrophy and
   genitourinary anomalies.
 • Operate as an emergency only for active bleeding or rupture.
 • Bloods: blood count, liver enzymes, electrolytes including urea, creatinine and calcium.
 • Acquired von Willebrand disease occurs in up to 8% — check coagulation; prothrombin and partial
   thromboplastin times may be normal. Correct with desmopressin (DDAVP), or intravenous
   immunoglobulin or plasmapheresis if needed; it reverses after resection or chemotherapy.
Imaging
 • Paediatric renal tumours overlap radiologically: WT cannot be reliably separated from renal cell
   carcinoma (RCC) or clear cell sarcoma of the kidney (CCSK). A minority of presumed WTs treated without
   biopsy prove to be other diagnoses (SIOP-9; United Kingdom Children's Cancer Study Group, UKCCSG).
 • Clinical clues:
      ◦ A syndrome → WT; bilateral or multicentric → WT (lymphoma can mimic).
      ◦ Neonate → congenital mesoblastic nephroma (CMN), though favourable-histology WT and rhabdoid
        tumour of the kidney (RTK) also occur early.
      ◦ WT can mimic neuroblastoma.
 • In National Wilms Tumor Study (NWTS)-3, misdiagnoses were mostly unimaged children, who had more
   complications. What matters is establishing a solid renal tumour, so that a major cancer operation is
   planned.

 • Sequence: US first → contrast CT or MRI of abdomen and pelvis in all → chest CT. US triages; avoid non-

 • Cross-sectional imaging shows extrarenal spread and synchronous lesions (Figure 63.9). No contralateral

 • Rupture: CT has poor sensitivity; ascites beyond the cul-de-sac is the best predictor.

Staging
 • Histology and stage are the key determinants; COG staging is surgical-pathological.
 • Renal sinus vessel invasion is the first sign of spread (stage II), then capsular penetration, which requires
   tumour in perirenal fat. Any spill → stage III.
 • Stage III is commonest in favourable histology; anaplastic tumours present more often at III–IV.

Treatment
Surgical Considerations
 • Radical nephrectomy, transperitoneal.
 • Explore for local extension, liver and nodal metastases, peritoneal seeding.
 • Palpate renal vein and IVC before ligation, using intraoperative US when equivocal. IVC extension occurs
   in ~6%, and more than half are asymptomatic.
 • Spare the adrenal if it is clear of tumour.
 • Lymph node (LN) sampling is essential; formal dissection is not. En bloc resection yields fewer positive
   nodes.
 • Omitted sampling is the commonest protocol violation (10–20%) and causes under- and over-staging.
   More nodes sampled → more positives found.
 • Basins: hilar; paracaval (right); para-aortic (left); interaortocaval (both). Skip metastases occur
   interaortocaval.
 • Node yield: 5–10 is ideal, with at least 5–7 sampled. COG requires ≥5; ≥6 is achievable after SIOP
   chemotherapy. Extended sampling adds no morbidity.
 • LN density predicts survival, but thresholds vary widely (0.22–0.65).
 • Avoid spill: it occurs in ~10%, more with right-sided and larger tumours, and causes local abdominal
   relapse.
 • Local recurrence risk factors: spill, unfavourable histology, incomplete removal, no LN sampling (highest in
   stage II). With all spill now staged III, the stage III risks are positive nodes, combined LOH and residual
   disease; spill itself no longer predicts relapse, because extra therapy is given.
 • Minimally invasive surgery (MIS):
       ◦ Usually done after chemotherapy; cases are few.
       ◦ Oncology must not be compromised, and upfront MIS raises spill concerns.
       ◦ SIOP UMBRELLA accepts it for central tumours unsuitable for nephron-sparing surgery (NSS).
       ◦ Node sampling is harder.
 • Complications ~11% (NWTS-4), chiefly haemorrhage and small-bowel obstruction. Risk factors: higher
   stage, >10 cm, wrong diagnosis, thoracoabdominal incision, caval extension, other-organ resection.

Partial Nephrectomy for Unilateral Tumors
 • Renal failure after unilateral nephrectomy is only 0.6% at 20 years. The risk is higher in genitourinary
   anomalies, DDS and WAGR, because WT1 is needed for renal development. Syndromic children have
   small screen-detected tumours suited to NSS.
 • Most tumours are too large. After chemotherapy, NSS is possible in 10–15% with an aggressive approach,
   ~3% under the SIOP 2001 criteria (polar, peripheral, non-infiltrating), and <10% even in very-low-risk stage
   I.
 • Concerns are staging after chemotherapy and local recurrence; intra-abdominal relapse markedly
   reduces survival.
 • SIOP 2016 UMBRELLA NSS criteria: <300 mL, substantial remnant, negative nodes. MIS for NSS has scant
   evidence and risks upstaging by spill.
 • The adult 4-cm small-renal-mass cut-off helps; manage a small mass by NSS after multidisciplinary
   review, with a margin of normal kidney and frozen section.
Concept — In bilateral disease the aim shifts from clearance to nephron preservation within a fixed 12-week
clock; prolonging chemotherapy for shrinkage buys differentiation and anaplasia, not parenchyma.

 • Thrombus into the renal vein, IVC or atrium occurs in ~10%.
 • Consensus: thrombus at or above the hepatic veins → preoperative chemotherapy, to avoid bypass.
 • Viable tumour remains in 36–60% of thrombi, without affecting survival.
 • Chemotherapy beyond 6–12 weeks adds no regression and is discouraged.
 • COG: upfront nephrectomy below the hepatic veins, with the thrombus removed en bloc. Piecemeal
   removal → stage III → radiotherapy.

Late Effects of Treatment
 • Childhood cancer 5-year survival exceeds 80%, at the cost of normal-tissue injury. Data come from the
   NWTSG Late Effects Study and the Childhood Cancer Survivor Study (CCSS) cohorts (1970–1986 and
   1987–1999).
 • WT survivors: any chronic condition 65% at 25 years, still rising; severe (grade 3–4) 24%.
Mortality
 • Risk of death stays elevated for years. It fell for patients treated in the 1990s, reflecting less radiotherapy
   and fewer anthracyclines.
Fertility and Pregnancy
 • Males: gonadal radiation causes dose-dependent hypogonadism and temporary azoospermia. Leydig
   cells are more radioresistant than germ cells; high doses give low testosterone and delayed puberty.
   Chemotherapy also harms the testis.
 • Females: pelvic radiation and alkylators cause ovarian failure and premature menopause; few
   pregnancies follow whole-abdominal radiotherapy.
 • Pregnancy after radiotherapy:
      ◦ Offspring are at risk of low birth weight and prematurity.
      ◦ Pelvic fields or doses >20 Gy raise miscarriage.
      ◦ Hypertension in pregnancy is commoner after abdominal radiotherapy.
 • Most WT patients are low gonadotoxic risk. Cyclophosphamide, carboplatin and whole-abdomen
   irradiation (anaplastic, high-risk and relapsed disease) raise it → counsel families. Prepubertal tissue
   cryopreservation is experimental but increasing.
Second Malignancies
 • 1% at 10 years, rising thereafter. Radiation is a leading risk, with tumours mostly in-field. Leukaemia
   peaks in the first 5 years; solid tumours appear later.
Cardiac Effects
 • Congestive heart failure in 4.4% after DOX, higher with whole-lung or left-flank irradiation. Almost none
   occurred below the contemporary cumulative dose of 150 mg/m²; subclinical toxicity is unassessed.
   Cardiac disease is the second commonest cause of death >30 years after WT (British Childhood Cancer
   Survivor Study).
Concept — Late effects track two exposures — radiotherapy and anthracycline — so falling late mortality
mirrors their reduction, and every trial that safely omits irradiation is also a late-effects trial.

Clear Cell Sarcoma of the Kidney
 • CCSK is 3% of renal tumours, named for its clear cytoplasm.
 • Survival is better with lower stage, younger age, DOX and absence of necrosis; adding DOX improved
   survival.
 • Stage I survival is 98%, yet stage I still needs radiotherapy.
 • Late relapse (~30% after 3 years; up to 10 years) → long follow-up.
 • No bilateral cases, no WT-associated anomalies.
 • Four-drug NWTS-5 therapy was no better than NWTS-4; stage is strongly predictive.
 • BCOR internal tandem duplication is detectable in cell-free DNA.
Rhabdoid Tumor of the Kidney
 • Most lethal renal tumour of childhood, 2%. It occurs renal and extrarenal (as does CCSK), suggesting a
   non-organ-specific mesenchymal origin.
 • Biallelic SMARCB1/INI1 loss (22q11.2), germline in one-third; INI1 immunostaining is negative.
 • Features: median ~16 months, advanced stage, chemoresistance, brain metastases; younger is worse.
 • There is no standard therapy, and high-dose chemotherapy with stem-cell transplant failed.
 • EZH2 is upregulated → tazemetostat; a COG trial is planned.

 • Commonest infant renal tumour (mean 3½ months) and commonest antenatal renal tumour.

 • Radical nephrectomy alone usually cures, but it may extend into the hilum → complete resection.

 • Nodes are less ominous than in adults (translocation RCC with positive nodes ~93% survival). Chemo-
   and radioresistant.
 • COG prospective study: median age 13 years, the 13–14-year crossover above which RCC outnumbers
   WT. Translocation RCC ~60%; node-positive just over 30%. Resected stage I–II was excellent, and node-
   positive disease did better than in adults. SIOP results are similar.
Angiomyolipoma
 • Hamartoma, rare in children. Adults are mostly sporadic; children have tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC),
   often bilateral.
 • Angiomyolipoma (AML) occurs in 50–80% of TSC. It enlarges through childhood, with a growth spurt in
   adolescence.
 • Growth is fastest over 11 years and in lesions <2 cm → annual US, with MRI when both apply.
 • Other TSC lesions: cysts (up to 30%), polycystic disease, RCC.
 • TSC1 (chromosome 9) / TSC2 (chromosome 16) mutations are found in 85%; LOH drives growth.
 • Risks: haemorrhage and renal impairment.
 • First-line for growing lesions: mTOR inhibitor (Figure 63.13); embolisation or surgery is second-line.
 • Embolisation infarcts adjacent parenchyma, does not prevent new lesions, and needs repeating in 35% →
   reserve it for active bleeding.
 • TSC loss hyperactivates mTOR. Sirolimus shrinks lesions only while taken, and they regrow after stopping
   → continuous therapy.
 • EXIST-2 (everolimus, adults): ≥50% volume reduction in 42% versus 0% on placebo; extension showed no
   bleeding or nephrectomy.
 • Fat-poor AML mimics RCC → biopsy. Use nephron-sparing approaches in TSC.

 KEY POINTS: RENAL TUMOURS
   • WT1 deletions (11p13) occur with aniridia and WT; WT1 mutations occur in DDS.
   • Local recurrence rises with spillage and nodal involvement; survival falls markedly after relapse.
   • Bilateral, multifocal and syndrome-associated unilateral WT → preoperative chemotherapy, allowing
