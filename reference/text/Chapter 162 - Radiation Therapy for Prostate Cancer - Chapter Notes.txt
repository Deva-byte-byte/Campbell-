RADIATION THERAPY BACKGROUND AND TECHNOLOGIC ADVANCES

Early Radiation Treatment With External Beam and Brachytherapy Approaches
 • Roentgen described the x-ray in 1896 ("Concerning a New Kind of Ray") and received the first Nobel Prize
   in Physics in 1901. The mechanism of tissue damage — ionisation causing deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)
   damage — was identified only decades later, after DNA itself had been described.
 • First prostate radiotherapy: Pasteau and Degrais, 1913 (published 1914), using an intraurethral radium
   source. Hugh Hampton Young, pioneer of the radical perineal prostatectomy, adopted and popularised it.
 • Kilovoltage external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), 1930s–1950s: these low-energy beams deposit their
   dose in skin and superficial soft tissue, so an effective prostate dose could not be reached without
   unacceptable toxicity.
 • Three requirements for treating a deep pelvic target. These drove every later advance:
    1. Penetrating beams that deposit little dose along their path, sparing skin and tissue.
    2. Sparing of adjacent organs: rectum, bladder and femoral heads.
    3. Minimal targeting error from physiological organ motion and tissue deformation between visits.

Linear Accelerators
 • 1960s: megavoltage EBRT made curative treatment possible, with acceptable normal-tissue damage and
   less skin toxicity.
 • Mechanism: electrons are accelerated along a linear path of oscillating electric potentials → strike a
   target (tungsten) → photons (x-rays) are produced → the beam is shaped by lead blocks, tungsten
   collimators and leaves.
 • Higher photon energy → deeper dose deposition and greater skin sparing. Orthovoltage (100–500 kV)
   and cobalt-60 (1.17–1.33 MV) units deposit their maximum dose at or just below the skin surface.
   Modern linear accelerators (6–20 MV) place the maximum dose several centimetres deep.
 • 1980s–1990s: computer-based planning → CT-based three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-
   CRT), then intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT).

CT-Based Treatment Planning and Three-Dimensional Conformal Radiotherapy
 • Conventional radiation (until the mid-1980s): fields were designed from surface and skeletal landmarks.
   The prostate was localised on plain films using a contrast-filled Foley catheter or urethrogram and a rectal
   tube, with portals centred on the pubic symphysis and femoral heads.
 • 1990s: computed tomography (CT) entered radiation oncology. The prostate could now be seen at
   planning, and computing power allowed dose calculation corrected for the differing electron densities of
   bone, air and soft tissue.
 • Planning workflow: simulation (a CT scan in the immobilisation device used for daily treatment) → the
   radiation oncologist contours the target (prostate) and the normal tissues (regions of interest, ROIs) →
   prescribes the target dose, its acceptable variation and the normal-tissue constraints → dosimetrists and
   physicists calculate and optimise → dose–volume histograms (DVHs) record the dose each structure
   receives.
 • 3D-CRT: the beams conform to the target's shape. Its purpose is to improve the therapeutic index,
   escalating prostate dose while respecting adjacent organs. Planning without CT data produced frequent
   errors in field placement and portal size.

Basics of Fractionation in Radiation Therapy
 • Coutard (1932): many small fractions give disease control with acceptable normal-tissue tolerance.
 • Conventional fractionation = 1.8–2.0 gray (Gy) daily. One gray is the absorption of 1 joule per kilogram.
   This fraction size rests on early clinical experience, animal data and mathematical models.
 • Linear–quadratic model: the most widely used model for predicting a tissue's response to fraction size,
   based on the tissue's α/β ratio.
 • Why small fractions were preferred: late-responding normal tissues were thought more sensitive to
   fraction size than tumour. Late toxicity (≥90 days after treatment) is reduced by fractionation, so more
   fractions spare late-responding tissue more than tumour.

Radiobiologic Basis for Hypofractionation
 • Most models assume tumours have more cycling cells than late-responding normal tissue. Prostate
   cancer has relatively few cycling cells, so it may respond to fraction size and dose rate like a late-
   responding normal tissue.
 • Classic α/β estimates are ~3 Gy for normal tissue and ~10 Gy for tumour. Prostate cancer's α/β is
   estimated as low as ~1.5 Gy, lower than that of the surrounding late-responding organs.
 • Consequence: using fractions larger than 2 Gy allows a lower total dose, which means less normal-organ
   injury with the same or better cancer control.
 • Evidence: the first modelling study pooled brachytherapy and EBRT outcomes and found α/β ≈1.5 Gy best
   fitted the data. It was criticised for mixing modalities that differ in both dose per fraction and spatial dose
   distribution. Larger EBRT-only cohorts then confirmed α/β ≈1.0–1.7 Gy (varying with risk group) and ≈1.4
   Gy.
Concept — Prostate cancer's low α/β reverses the usual fractionation logic. Because the tumour, not the
rectum, is the tissue most sensitive to fraction size, larger fractions favour tumour kill over late toxicity, and
every hypofractionation and stereotactic regimen later in the chapter follows from this.

Transrectal Ultrasound-Guided Brachytherapy
 • Brachytherapy (BT), from the Greek brakhus ("short"): radioactive sources placed in or near a tumour.
 • Historical sequence: Pasteau placed temporary radium needles through the urethra (1914) → Young used
   intracavitary radium in the bladder, rectum and urethra, "cross-firing" into the prostate (1922) →
   Barringer (Memorial Hospital, New York) implanted radon needles transperineally → Whitmore
   performed retropubic 125I implantation with lymph node dissection (1970s).
 • Retropubic "freehand" technique: open exposure; needles passed anterior to posterior, guided by a
   gloved finger in the rectum. Complications: bleeding from the periprostatic venous plexus, needles
   passing through the rectum or bladder, and infection.
 • Holm: transrectal ultrasound (TRUS)-guided transperineal technique, giving live image guidance. Set-up:
   high lithotomy → TRUS probe held on a stepper fixed to the table or a floor pedestal → a predrilled
   template with parallel holes sits on the perineum, its grid overlaid on the ultrasound image → needles
   inserted through the template → sealed seeds deployed as the needle is withdrawn.
 • Trans-sacral CT-guided and intraoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-guided implantation have
   been studied but are not in routine use.

Concept — Every technical step in this history (megavoltage, CT planning, conformal shaping, TRUS guidance)
answers one of three problems: penetration, organ sparing and targeting accuracy. Fractionation adds a
separate, biological way of controlling toxicity.

CONTEMPORARY TECHNIQUES AND ADVANCES IN IMAGING

Multiparametric Magnetic Resonance Imaging in Radiation Treatment Planning
 • Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) gives anatomical and functional information at diagnosis (see Chapter
   152) and upgrades or upstages disease. It predicts seminal vesicle invasion more accurately than
   extracapsular extension.
 • Limitation: against whole-mount pathology in the Prostate Imaging–Reporting and Data System version 2
   (PI-RADS v2) era, MRI still misses some clinically significant cancers, particularly those <10 mm.
 • So the workup before definitive radiotherapy uses systematic ± targeted biopsies. Several cores from the
   same target count as one core when calculating the proportion of positive cores (National
   Comprehensive Cancer Network, NCCN).
 • MRI simulation: MRI carries no electron-density information, so it cannot calculate dose on its own. It is
   fused with the CT simulation to delineate the prostate and tumour.
 • MRI is most useful when CT is compromised, for example by a hip prosthesis or a small prostate. Some
   centres also use it in post-implant quality assurance for LDR seed distribution.
 • Erectile vessel-sparing radiotherapy, planned on T2 sequences and MR angiography: a prospective single-
   arm study found most men still sexually active at 5 years. The radiosensitivity of neurovascular structures
   is poorly quantified, so the technique remains investigational.

Metabolic Imaging in Prostate Cancer
 • Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography (PET)/CT is appropriate
   before treatment for unfavourable intermediate-risk and high-risk intact disease (see Chapter 152).
   Prospective trials using 68Ga-PSMA or 18F-DCFPyL show that up to about half of these patients have their
   treatment recommendation changed.
 • After prostatectomy, 68Ga-PSMA, 18F-DCFPyL, 18F-fluciclovine and 18F-choline all detect microscopic
   disease better than conventional imaging.

Intensity-Modulated Radiation Therapy
 • IMRT intensifies dose to the prostate while reducing dose to the surrounding tissues. It evolved from 3D-
   CRT.
 • Evolution: static beam angles with moving shields in the linear accelerator head → inverse planning, in
   which the user enters the prescription, normal-tissue tolerances and structure priorities → the optimiser
   generates a candidate plan → clinical review.
 • IMRT produces steep dose gradients at the edge of the prostate. Fig. 162.2 — IMRT plan, 79.2 Gy (7920
   cGy) in 44 fractions to the prostate, sparing most of the rectum and bladder.

 • Volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT): a subtype of IMRT that treats continuously during a 360°

Heavy Particle Beam/Proton Therapy
 • Particles in clinical use: electrons (shallow penetration → skin and superficial tumours); protons;
   neutrons; helium ions; heavy ions (neon, argon, carbon); negative ions. Heavy particles need large
   infrastructure and are hard to control.
 • Physical advantages: a higher linear energy transfer (LET), the energy deposited per unit path length →
   denser DNA damage and less repair → a slightly higher relative biological effectiveness (RBE) than
   photons.
 • Bragg peak: a sharp rise in dose, then a cut-off at the end of the particle's range. Tumours are thicker than
   a single peak, so the beam is modulated into a spread-out Bragg peak (SOBP).
 • Fig. 162.6 — dose against depth: photons deliver a surface dose and an exit dose; protons have a lower
   entrance dose, a Bragg peak and no exit dose, and the SOBP covers the tumour depth. (From Leeman et
   al., Lancet Oncol, 2017.)
 • Uncertainties: range uncertainty is far more sensitive to set-up, organ motion and tissue heterogeneity
   than with photons, and RBE varies at the edges of the SOBP. Either can underdose the target or overdose
   normal tissue.
 • Protons are the most available heavy particle (produced by a linear accelerator, cyclotron or synchrotron).
   Neutrons were historically more common, and helium and carbon ions are used in a few countries outside
   the United States.

 KEY POINTS: CONTEMPORARY TECHNIQUES — EXTERNAL BEAM RADIATION THERAPY
   • Margins now subcentimetre; IMRT the commonest curative method; hypofractionation routine;
     ultrahypofractionation a standard option for all risk groups; protons have higher RBE and need more
     infrastructure, with no randomised comparison with IMRT.

Concept — Modern EBRT is a margin-reduction chain: imaging (MRI, PSMA) defines the target, IMRT shapes
the dose, and IGRT or MRgRT shrinks the margin. Only smaller margins make large fractions safe. Protons
improve the physics of the low-dose region, but toxicity comes from the high-dose region, which is why no
clinical benefit has been shown.

CONTEMPORARY TECHNIQUES: BRACHYTHERAPY

Low-Dose-Rate Brachytherapy Technique and Trends
 • LDR BT: sources are implanted permanently and deliver dose gradually until they have fully decayed.
   "Low dose rate" describes the rate, not a low dose.
 • Low photon energy (~21–30 kiloelectron-volts, keV) → dose falls off within a few millimetres. This
   spares surrounding tissue but demands precise placement, or tumour-bearing regions are underdosed.
 • Utilisation: BT for low-risk disease rose through the 1990s (Cancer of the Prostate Strategic Urologic
   Research Endeavor [CaPSURE] database), then fell steeply (by about 60%). Proposed causes: competition
   from ultrahypofractionated EBRT, robotic prostatectomy, and urology practices owning linear
   accelerators. Evidence that adding BT to EBRT improves control and survival in intermediate- to high-risk
   disease may reverse the decline.
 • Planning: computer algorithms arrange the sources to maximise prostate ablation while limiting dose to
   the intraprostatic urethra and rectum (Fig. 162.7 — (A) preoperative MRI-based plan; (B) intraoperative
   ultrasound-based plan).
 • Peripheral (modified peripheral) loading: more seeds at the edge of the gland and fewer centrally →
   urethral sparing → less urinary morbidity.

Assessment of Permanent Implant Quality
 • Post-implant imaging at 14–30 days (isotope-dependent), by CT and/or MRI: seed count and
   arrangement are checked to confirm a tumoricidal prostate dose with minimal dose to adjacent structures
   (Fig. 162.8 — post-implant dosimetry: isodose lines at 50–200% of prescription and the DVH for prostate,
   urethra, rectum and bladder).
 • D90 = the minimum dose received by the "hottest" 90% of the prostate (the highest isodose line covering
   90% of the gland). V100 = the percentage of the prostate receiving the prescribed dose. The American
   Brachytherapy Society (ABS) recommends D90, V100 and V150.

High-Dose-Rate Brachytherapy
 • High-dose-rate (HDR) BT: temporary catheters (hollow transperineal needles) → imaged by TRUS, CT or
   MRI → a single high-activity iridium-192 (192Ir) source (gamma, mean 380 keV) in a remote afterloader
   steps through set dwell positions → treatment over minutes → the source is withdrawn.
 • Conformality comes from varying dwell time and position. Robotic source control protects staff. Because
   dose is calibrated in real time and the source is removed, no post-implant dosimetry is needed.
 • Evolution: first used as a boost with EBRT in intermediate- to high-risk disease; now given as

High-Dose-Rate Monotherapy
 • Established schedules: 27–28 Gy in 2; 31.5 Gy in 3; 34–38 Gy in 4 fractions. They give excellent
   biochemical control with limited toxicity.
 • Single-fraction 19 Gy fails. A prospective series gave disappointing disease-free survival and substantial
   biopsy-proven local failure at about 4 years. In the randomised comparison of 19 Gy × 1 vs 27 Gy in 2
   (median follow-up 60 months), 5-year biochemical disease-free survival was 73.5% vs 95% and 5-year
   local failure 29% vs 3%. Multifraction HDR monotherapy remains the standard for low- and
   intermediate-risk disease.

 • The only randomised LDR vs HDR monotherapy trial used HDR 19 Gy × 1. LDR gave a deeper PSA nadir

Patient Selection for Brachytherapy
 • The main acute toxicity is obstructive and irritative uropathy, which leads to a temporary catheter in a
   small proportion of patients. This is why urinary function drives eligibility.
 • Guidelines: ABS and Groupe Européen de Curiethérapie–European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology
   (GEC-ESTRO) consensus guidelines for LDR and HDR.
 • Prerequisites: the patient must be fit for general anaesthesia (the standard; local anaesthesia appears
   only in limited reports) and able to undergo TRUS, because the technique depends on strict spatial
   accuracy and TRUS visualisation.

RELATIVE CONTRAINDICATIONS TO BRACHYTHERAPY
 • Large TURP defect, or TURP within the previous 3–6 months → incontinence risk and technical difficulty.
 • Poor urinary function or obstruction: maximum flow rate (Qmax) <10 mL/s; IPSS >20; large median
   lobe.
 • Prior pelvic radiotherapy.
 • Connective tissue disease or inflammatory bowel disease.
 • Pubic arch interference: needles cannot reach the anterior or lateral prostate.
 • Lithotomy position not possible.
 • Rectal fistula.
 • Cytoreduction of a large gland or median lobe: a short course of ADT or androgen receptor blockade
   shrinks the gland by up to ~30%, but carries toxicity including increased cardiovascular mortality. 5α-
   reductase inhibitors shrink it by only ~20% but are a less morbid alternative.
 • ADT with BT: usual in high-risk disease treated with BT + EBRT; its role is unclear in unfavourable
   intermediate risk, or high risk with few risk factors.

Brachytherapy Combined With External Beam Radiotherapy
 • The combined regimen is 45 Gy of conventionally fractionated EBRT + BT at ~60–70% of the
   monotherapy dose. EBRT covers periprostatic tissue and raises the gland dose.
 • A pooled analysis of two randomised trials showed that biochemical failure rose with risk group
   (favourable intermediate → unfavourable intermediate → high) with no measurable benefit from adding
   EBRT to BT.
 • With ADT, biochemical disease-free survival after combined therapy in high-risk disease is 80–90%. HDR
   series with ≥5 years of follow-up show control of 83–100% in intermediate risk and 74–91% in high risk.

ROLE OF ANDROGEN SUPPRESSION COMBINED WITH RADIATION

Background and Potential Mechanisms
 • Huggins and Hodges (1941) showed that prostate cancer is androgen-dependent. This provided the
   rationale for adding hormonal therapy to RT.
 • From 1967, hormonal therapy was added to RT for stage C (T3) tumours. These were often large, and
   cytoreductive ADT gave better geometry for irradiation and less tumour burden. Its use remained
   controversial through the 1970s–1980s, when early series were often negative.
 • RTOG 75-06, an early favourable report: patients with histologically unfavourable tumours given androgen
   suppression + EBRT had disease-free and overall survival similar to patients with favourable tumours
   who did not receive it.
 • Mechanisms of synergy:
     ◦ Apoptosis: androgen-responsive cells undergo programmed cell death when deprived of androgen.
       Like radiation-induced death, this is p53-independent.
     ◦ Radiosensitisation: castration → less Ku70 repair protein → impaired nonhomologous end-joining →
       more unrepaired double-strand breaks after RT.




                                                             page 14 of 27
Chapter 162 — Radiation Therapy for Prostate Cancer                              Role of Androgen Suppression Combined With Radiation
     ◦ RT upregulates androgen receptor (AR) signalling durably. Patients with a post-RT rise in AR-regulated
       hK2 were about threefold more likely to fail biochemically. Adjuvant ADT after RT works by blocking
       this AR upregulation.

Significance of Nadir Value and Doubling Time
 • Conventional-dose RT (~66–72 Gy): median nadir 0.4–0.5 ng/mL with NED, >1.0 with local failure, >2
   with distant failure.
 • Nadir thresholds after EBRT: nadir >2 ng/mL at 1 year predicts biochemical failure, distant metastasis and
   death. >1.5 ng/mL at 2 years is an independent risk factor for distant metastasis. >0.5 ng/mL predicts all-
   cause mortality (unfavourable risk, 70.2 Gy ± 6 months ADT).
 • After BT: nadir <0.5 ng/mL → better long-term freedom from biochemical failure. PSA <0.2 ng/mL at 5
   years → excellent 10-year control. PSA ≤0.2 ng/mL at 4 years → disease-free survival beyond 10 years in a
   very large multi-institutional analysis. With 125I D90 of 160 Gy, only ~10% have PSA >0.2 at 5 years (two-
   thirds still falling), and the proportion below 0.2 keeps rising for 7–8 years.
 • BT gives a deeper and later nadir than EBRT: mean nadir about ten-fold lower after LDR or HDR + EBRT
   (~0.05–0.1 ng/mL) than EBRT alone (~0.5 ng/mL). Time to nadir is about 2.5 years after BT vs 1.5 years
   after EBRT.
 • PSA doubling time (PSADT): <3 months → associated with prostate cancer mortality. <12 months →
   markedly worse disease-specific survival (RTOG 92-02 analysis; in TROG 96.01 the HR for prostate cancer
   death was ~23). Practical use: consider earlier intervention if PSADT <12 months, and observation is
   more comfortable if it is longer.
 • Caveat: PSADT is an imperfect surrogate. Its reliability depends on having many values over a wide range.

Brachytherapy and the Benign Bounce Phenomenon
 • Bounce (spike) = a rise >0.2 ng/mL followed by a durable decline. It occurs after LDR and HDR BT and
   also after EBRT.
 • Seen in about a third of men after LDR, and up to about two-thirds of younger men. The mean rise is ~1
   ng/mL (up to 10 ng/mL); it can last 12–18 months, and double peaks occur.
 • A bounce is associated with better outcome: improved biochemical control and survival compared with
   no bounce.
 • A biopsy during a bounce may show residual cancer with treatment effect (indeterminate). MR
   spectroscopy shows a diffuse rise in choline:citrate across the gland, not the focal change expected with
   tumour. There is no reliable way to tell a bounce from failure in the first 3 years.
 • Management of a rising PSA after BT: within 30 months → monitor. If it approaches 10 ng/mL →
   systemic staging. If it persists beyond 30 months → prostate biopsy.

Biochemical Failure and Impact of Neoadjuvant Hormones
 • With neoadjuvant ADT, PSA is often already undetectable when RT starts, so a true nadir and time to
   nadir cannot be determined.
 • When testosterone recovers after EBRT + ADT, PSA often bounces (median ~2 years after treatment, ~0.8–
   0.9 ng/mL), then falls again as the RT effect continues. Many more of these bounces meet the ASTRO
   definition of failure than the Phoenix definition.
 • Only about a fifth of bounces occur during testosterone recovery; the rest come after testosterone has
   normalised. This suggests a delayed testosterone response, inflammation or instrumentation. More
   frequent testing detects more bounces.
 • Phoenix (nadir + 2) makes fewer false calls of failure than ASTRO (three rises).

Post–Radiation Therapy Biopsy, Timing, and Interpretation
 • PSA cannot distinguish local from systemic failure, so biopsy assesses the local effect. The problems are
   timing, indeterminate biopsies with marked radiation effect and sampling error.
 • Timing: radiation causes postmitotic cell death, meaning fatally damaged cells survive a few divisions
   before dying. Biopsies taken as early as 6 months were once called failures. Histological resolution runs
   parallel to the PSA nadir. The optimal biopsy time is 30–36 months, although 24-month biopsies strongly
   predicted 7-year disease-free survival.
 • Grade only when radiation effect is absent or minimal. Gleason grading was built on untreated surgical
   specimens and depends on gland architecture, which RT disrupts. Grading disintegrating glands gives a
   false-positive, falsely high-grade result and unnecessary salvage. ADT-treated cancer can also mimic
   Gleason 4 or 5.
 • Radiation atypia in benign glands can mimic cancer. High-molecular-weight cytokeratin labels the basal
   cell layer, which is present in benign glands and absent in cancer.
 • Radiation-effect scoring systems (Box 162.1) matter because they separate biopsies with no or minimal
   effect from those with marked effect. Ignoring the distinction dilutes the prognostic value of the biopsy
   and leads to inappropriate salvage. Proliferation markers (proliferating cell nuclear antigen, Ki-67) help
   judge residual tumour.
 • Prognostic value: 2-year biopsy status predicts 10-year biochemical NED, freedom from distant metastasis
   and disease-specific survival. Biopsies showing marked treatment effect behave as negative. Biopsy
   status and Gleason score are the two strongest determinants of biochemical disease-free survival.

 • ≥5 Gy per fraction = ultrahypofractionation (SBRT, SAbR). The evidence is mostly phase I/II series (Table

OUTCOMES OF MODERN TECHNIQUE

Treatment Morbidity and Quality-of-Life Outcomes
 • 70 Gy was long considered the maximum safe dose. Conformal RT made higher doses possible, but
   toxicity tracks dose to the organ. In the MD Anderson trial, 5-year grade ≥2 complications were about
   twice as common at 78 Gy as at 70 Gy. Rectal complication risk rose substantially when more than 25% of
   the rectum received 70 Gy.

Rectal Toxicity and Management
 • Acute rectal symptoms are self-limiting. Late radiation proctitis with bleeding usually appears 6–18
   months after treatment.
 • Management ladder: steroid suppositories or sucralfate enemas (for minor, self-limited cases) → argon
   plasma coagulation (APC) or hyperbaric oxygen → diverting colostomy in the worst cases. APC controls
   bleeding in most patients but can cause ulceration and, rarely, colovesical fistula.
 • IMRT to 81 Gy: 10-year grade ≥2 GI toxicity ~5%, grade 3 <1%. Low with or without fiducial-based image
   guidance.
 • Patient-reported bowel symptoms: urgency and frequency peak at 2 months and partly recover.
 • ProtecT: RT (3D-CRT) caused more bowel bother than active monitoring or RP, worst at 6 months and
   improving thereafter, with a residual increase in bloody stools at 2–6 years. No difference in loose stools
   or faecal incontinence.
 • BT: significant rectal complications in 1–2% and bleeding in ~1%, lower than after EBRT. Recto-urethral
   fistula is rare but feared; a colostomy rate of ~0.3% was found in Medicare claims.
 • Rectal dose limits for BT: complications are rare when the rectal wall receives ≤85% of the prescription.
   For BT alone, rectal V100 <1.0 cm³ on day-1 dosimetry or <1.3 cm³ on day-30 dosimetry (ABS). For BT +
   EBRT, bleeding risk rises with rectal V100 >0.5 cm³ and EBRT rectal V30 >35%.

Rectal-Sparing Strategies
 • Options: precise MRI delineation of the anorectum; endorectal balloon; a saline-filled balloon between
   prostate and rectum; and, most widely reported, a transperineal spacer (biodegradable hydrogel or
   hyaluronic acid) placed in Denonvilliers' fascia to separate the prostate from the anterior rectal wall (Fig.
   162.9 — axial T2 MRI showing hydrogel between the prostate/seminal vesicles and the rectum).
 • Safe with LDR, HDR, EBRT, SBRT and combined therapy. It can be placed before treatment or after
   suboptimal dosimetry from a permanent implant.
 • Hydrogel randomised trial (IG-IMRT 79.2 Gy): late rectal toxicity 2.0% vs 7.0% (P = 0.04).
 • Hyaluronic acid phase III trial (60 Gy/20; 2:1; spacer + fiducials vs fiducials alone): grade ≥2 rectal toxicity
