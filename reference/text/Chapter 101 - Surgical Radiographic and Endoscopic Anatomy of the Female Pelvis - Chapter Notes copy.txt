Chapter 101 — Surgical, Radiographic, and Endoscopic
Anatomy of the Female Pelvis
 • The female pelvis houses three systems — urological, reproductive and gastrointestinal — and the
   urological surgeon must know all three, because the relationships between them determine where injury
   occurs.
 • Anatomy is organised by surgical approach — transvaginal, transabdominal and endoscopic — followed
   by imaging (ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging, MRI).
 • Rationale: anatomical knowledge prevents complications and underpins good outcomes in both benign
   and malignant pelvic surgery.

TRANSVAGINAL APPROACH TO THE FEMALE PELVIS
 • Order of description: external soft tissue → bony pelvis (foundation) → musculature, nerves and vessels.
 • Structures must be understood in lithotomy (the operating position) and in their functional standing or
   sitting position.

External Genitalia
 • Vulva = mons pubis, labia majora, labia minora, clitoris and vestibule.
 • Labia majora: most superficial skin folds; meet anteriorly at the mons pubis (fat pad over the symphysis
   pubis, covering most of the clitoris).
 • Labia minora: lie within the labia majora, more prominent anteriorly; fuse around the glans as prepuce
   (anterior, hood-like fold) and frenulum (posterior fold); join posteriorly at the fourchette, usually torn at
   vaginal delivery.
 • Vestibule: the cleft between the labia minora, into which the vagina and urethral meatus open.
 • Introitus: anteroposterior slit fringed by the hymen. External urethral meatus lies 1 cm anterior to the
   vaginal opening.
 • Skene's (paraurethral) glands: right and left ventral aspect of the meatus; ducts drain to the vulva;
   lubricate the meatus, secretion possibly antibacterial. Embryological homologue of the prostate; mature
   secretory and basal cells make it a functional genitourinary organ.
 • Perineal membrane is pierced by the vagina and urethra.
 • Erectile tissue (crura and vestibular bulbs) attaches to the inferior surface of the perineal membrane,
   covered as a pair: crura → ischiocavernosus; vestibular bulbs → bulbospongiosus.
 • Crura attach to the medial ischiopubic rami → form the clitoral shaft → turn downward and backward →
   capped by the glans.
 • Vestibular bulbs surround urethral and vaginal openings, fuse in the midline at the glans; the posterior
   end of each bulb expands over the greater vestibular (Bartholin's) gland — site of Bartholin's cyst or
   abscess, requiring surgery.

Perineum
 • Two triangles, divided by a line between the ischial tuberosities: anterior urogenital triangle (pubic
   symphysis, pubic and ischial rami, deep transverse perineal muscles) vs posterior anal triangle (contains
   the anus).

 • Colles fascia: laterally fixed to the fascia lata at the ischiopubic ramus (defines the perineal thigh crease);
   apex at the arcuate ligament of the pubis; posteriorly wraps the superficial transverse perineal muscle and
   fuses with the perineal body; anteriorly covers the clitoris, analogous to dartos in the male.
 • Deep perineal fascia overlies the superficial perineal muscles and fuses with the suspensory ligaments of
   the clitoris and the rectus sheath and external oblique fascia.
 • Perineal body: central point of the perineum (muscle, collagen, elastic fibres); convergence of
   bulbospongiosus, external anal sphincter, and superficial and deep transverse perineal muscles;
   attached to the posterior border of the perineal membrane, between vestibule and anal canal.
 • Obstetric relevance: perineal body damage at delivery injures the external anal sphincter → episiotomy is
   angled laterally to spare those fibres.
 • Bulbospongiosus: splits around the introitus, attaches to the clitoris anteriorly and perineal body
   posteriorly, covers the bulbs; contraction constricts the vaginal orifice and expresses vestibular gland
   secretions.
 • Ischiocavernosus covers a clitoral crus and promotes clitoral erection. Superficial transverse perineal
   muscles overlie the posterior vagina.

 KEY POINTS: EXTERNAL GENITALIA AND PERINEUM
   • The vulva describes the mons pubis, labia majora and minora, clitoris and vestibule.
   • The crura of the clitoris and the vestibular bulbs are covered by the ischiocavernosus and
     bulbospongiosus respectively.
   • The perineal body is the convergence of bulbospongiosus, external anal sphincter, and superficial and
     deep transverse perineal muscles.


Bony Pelvis
 • The bony pelvis supports the abdominal and pelvic viscera.
 • An oblique line from the sacral promontory to the superior margin of the pubic symphysis (the pelvic
   brim, defining the pelvic inlet) separates true pelvis (below the brim) from false pelvis (above the brim).
 • True pelvis = four bones: two hip (innominate) bones, sacrum and coccyx. Each innominate = ilium +
   ischium + pubis.
 • Ilium: fan-shaped, wing and body. Ischium: fused to the ilium.

 • Ischial spines project posteromedially at about the level of S5.
        ◦ Located in lithotomy by palpating the sacrum → following the sacrospinous ligament laterally → ischial
          spine.
        ◦ The interspinous line divides the true pelvis into anterior and posterior compartments.
        ◦ Key landmark in prolapse surgery for a safe site of apical suspension sutures.
 • Pubis: body + superior and inferior rami. Inferior ramus joins the ischium; superior ramus joins the ilium.
   Ischiopubic ramus = lower margin of the obturator foramen; superior pubic ramus and ischium form its
   upper margin. Pubic bones meet at the pubic symphysis.
 • Sacrum: five fused vertebrae. The S1 foramina fan out laterally relative to S4 — useful when identifying
   S3 for sacral neuromodulation lead placement.
 • Sacral promontory is overlain by a neurovascular bed — avoid during sacrocolpopexy mesh fixation;
   bleeding here is hard to control.
 • Coccyx: attached inferiorly to the sacrum; posterior border of the pelvic outlet.
 • Standing position: anterior superior iliac spine and pubic symphysis lie in the same vertical plane; the
   pelvic inlet faces anteriorly, so intra-abdominal and pelvic pressure is directed onto bone rather than
   onto muscle and fascia. Surgery is described in lithotomy, but upright form and function determine
   success.

 • Sacrospinous ligament (SSL): ischial spine (laterally) → side of the lower sacrum and coccyx (medially).
   Commonly used to anchor apical suspension sutures in prolapse repair.

 KEY POINTS: BONY PELVIS
   • The pubic rami, ischial spines and sacrum are the main anchoring points for the ligaments supporting
     the pelvis.
   • Standing, forces are dispersed to the bony pelvis, minimising stress on pelvic organs, muscles and
     fascia.
   • The SSL (ischial spine → lower sacrum and coccyx) is often used as an attachment site in vaginal
     prolapse surgery.

Concept — The bony pelvis is read as a set of fixation points and danger zones: ischial spine, SSL, Cooper's
ligament and sacral promontory are where sutures go, and each has an adjacent structure (pudendal nerve,
sacral plexus, obturator canal, presacral vessels) that defines how close the surgeon may go.

Pelvic Floor Musculature and Fasciae
 • Pelvic walls: obturator internus, iliacus, psoas major and minor, levator ani complex and coccygeus.
     ◦ Obturator internus: most of the lateral side wall → through the lesser sciatic foramen → greater
       trochanter.
     ◦ Piriformis: pads the posterolateral wall → through the greater sciatic foramen → greater trochanter.
       Sacral plexus lies medial to it.
 • Pelvic floor = pelvic diaphragm, pubis to coccyx; levator ani is its largest component, crucial for support
   and function of the urogenital viscera.
 • Levator ani = puborectalis + pubococcygeus + iliococcygeus, each named by its attachments; direct
   innervation from the S3–S5 roots (principally S4).

 • Transvaginal access to the retropubic space: incise the anterior vaginal wall either side of the urethra
   (retropubic sling or transvaginal prolapse repair); the arcus tendineus is felt as a thick band of pelvic fascia
   from ischial spine to pubis — a fascial attachment point.
 • Resting tone of the pelvic floor supports the viscera, resists rises in intra-abdominal pressure and provides
   passive urinary and faecal continence. Loss of tone (muscle or nerve injury) → lax urogenital hiatus →

 • The International Continence Society (ICS) has published updated terminology for pelvic floor muscle

 KEY POINT: PELVIC FLOOR MUSCULATURE AND FASCIAE
   • Sustained resting tone supports the viscera, resists raised intra-abdominal pressure and is crucial to
     passive urinary and faecal continence.

Concept — The pelvic floor works as a horizontal levator plate under tonic contraction; prolapse follows
from anything that widens the hiatus or tilts the plate, which is why muscle and nerve injury converge on the
same clinical result.

Vasculature and Lymphatic Drainage of the External Genitalia
 • Dual arterial supply to the labia majora: superiorly — superficial external pudendal artery (from the
   femoral artery) vs inferiorly — posterior labial branches of the internal pudendal artery (from the
   internal iliac).
 • This dual supply makes the labial fibrofatty tissue — the Martius flap — an ideal interposition flap in
   vaginal reconstruction.
 • Venous drainage: external pudendal vein → great saphenous → femoral vein; internal pudendal vein →
   internal iliac vein.
 • Lymphatics: vulva → superficial inguinal → deep inguinal → pelvic nodes. Clitoris and labia minora →
   deep inguinal, possibly to internal iliac nodes.
Concept — The external genitalia sit at the junction of limb and pelvic territories — femoral and internal iliac
arteries, saphenous and internal iliac veins, inguinal then pelvic nodes — and the Martius flap exploits exactly
that double supply.

Innervation to the External Genitalia and Pelvic Floor Musculature
 • Somatic (sensory and motor: external genitalia and pelvic floor) vs autonomic (sympathetic and
   parasympathetic: pelvic organs).
 • Parasympathetic stimulation → increased vaginal secretion, clitoral erection, engorgement of erectile
   tissue.

Somatic Innervation
 • Sacral plexus: ventral rami L4–L5 and S1–S4; lies on piriformis, deep to endopelvic fascia, posterior to the
   internal iliac vessels; supplies posterior thigh and leg.
 • Exits via the greater sciatic foramen immediately posterior to the SSL → at risk during SSL fixation.
 • Pelvic floor muscles: direct innervation from S3–S5; the pelvic somatic nerves (L4–S4) travel on the pelvic
   surface of levator ani, supplying it and the striated urethral sphincter.
 • Pudendal nerve (S2–S4): arises above the sacrotuberous ligament and coccygeus → leaves via the greater
   sciatic foramen → crosses piriformis, coccygeus and the SSL close to the ischial spine (vulnerable in SSL

Relevant Thigh Anatomy
 • Fascia lata grafts: autologous pubovaginal slings, and in place of mesh in sacrocolpopexy.
 • Function: stabilises the knee, confines thigh muscle expansion (more efficient contraction), aids venous
   return. Inferiorly attaches to distal femur and tibia; laterally forms the iliotibial band.
 • Safe harvest: identify the lateral epicondyle (just lateral to the patella) → avoid harvest near the condyle,
   where the band is narrow → preserve the band's longitudinal integrity.

Urologic Organs
Urethra.
 • Length 4.7–5.1 cm; runs behind the pubic symphysis to the bladder neck. Suspended by the posterior
   pubourethral ligaments and anteriorly by the suspensory ligaments of the clitoris.
 • Muscle wall: inner longitudinal and outer circular smooth muscle, beneath a mucosa continuous with the
   bladder; longitudinal fibres shorten and widen the urethra during voiding. Elastic tissue and collagen
   surround the layers.

Ureter.
 • Retroperitoneal. Abdominal ureter: kidney to iliac vessels. Pelvic ureter: about half the total length, from
   the iliac crossing to the bladder.
 • Course (sequence): crosses the pelvic brim over the common iliac vessels near their bifurcation → along

   over the anterior vaginal fornix → bladder.
 • Injury sites: under the uterine artery at hysterectomy; at the anterior vaginal fornix in transvaginal

Female Reproductive Organs
 • Uterus: normally anteverted and anteflexed; body + cervix.
 • Cervix: about 2.5 cm, adjacent to the bladder, separated by parametrium; opens as the os. Fornices:
   shallow anterior (ventral), deeper posterior (dorsal), and lateral.
 • Uterine wall (outside → in): perimetrium (peritoneum and thin connective tissue) → myometrium (outer
   longitudinal, continuous with ovarian and round ligaments; middle circular, carrying the vessels and
   nerves; inner longitudinal) → endometrium.
 • Fallopian tubes: 10–12 cm, draped in the broad ligament; interstitial → isthmus → ampulla →
   infundibulum → fimbriae; enter the uterus at the cornua.
 • Ovary: in the ovarian fossa (bounded by obliterated umbilical artery, ureter and internal iliac artery), held
   by the mesovarium to the posterior broad ligament; suspended by the infundibulopelvic ligament
   (carries the ovarian vessels); tethered to the uterus by the ovarian ligament.

 • Vagina:

Gastrointestinal Organs
 • Descending colon (retroperitoneal) → sigmoid at the iliac crest; the sigmoid gains a longer mesentery
   and becomes intraperitoneal; fixed iliac and mobile pelvic portions.
 • Left ureter runs through the base of the sigmoid mesocolon; landmark: intersigmoid fossa.
 • Rectosigmoid junction is disputed: at the sacral promontory vs more distally at the narrowest point
   where the taeniae fuse.
 • Rectum: retroperitoneal, 12–15 cm, no taeniae or haustra; becomes the anal canal after passing through
   levator ani.

 KEY POINTS: PELVIC VISCERA
   • The ureter's relationship to the reproductive organs makes it a common site of injury in
     gynaecological surgery.
   • The rectum is retroperitoneal and differs from colon in having no taeniae or haustra.

Concept — Every pelvic organ is described by what it rests on: bladder base, urethra and bladder neck all lie
on the anterior vaginal wall, and the ureter threads between uterine vessels and vaginal fornix — so
continence, fistula and ureteric injury are all problems of the anterior vaginal wall and its neighbours.

Sacrocolpopexy dissection.
 • Sequence: expose the ALL at the sacral promontory over the L5/S1 disc → dissect peritoneum distally →
   secure sutures to the ALL over the anterior surface of S1.
 • Why S1: lower risk of discitis or osteomyelitis, and the highest tensile strength of the ALL.
 • Hazards: left common iliac vein, middle sacral artery and vein, presacral venous plexus, right ureter. In
   cadavers, the right ureter and left common iliac vein each lie about 2.7 cm from the mid-promontory.

 KEY POINTS: SUPPORT STRUCTURES FOR THE PELVIC VISCERA
   • The infundibulopelvic ligament runs from the ovary to the pelvic side wall behind the broad ligament
     and contains the ovarian vessels.
   • The uterosacral ligaments run from the sacrum to the fascia around the cervix, uterine isthmus and
     vaginal wall and are used in vaginal apex suspension.
   • The ALL overlies the anterior vertebral column and is the suture site in sacrocolpopexy.

Concept — Pelvic support is connective tissue condensed around vessels and nerves; that is why the same
ligaments that make good suspension anchors (uterosacral, cardinal) are the ones whose division or suturing
injures the ureter, pelvic plexus and sacral nerves.

Vasculature and Lymphatic Drainage of Pelvis
Arterial Supply
 • Aorta enters the retroperitoneum through the diaphragm at T12 → bifurcates at the left anterolateral L4
   into common iliac arteries.
 • Common iliac arteries: right about 5 cm, left about 4 cm → bifurcate into internal (hypogastric) and
   external iliac arteries where the ureter crosses anteriorly.
 • Middle sacral artery: arises at the aortic bifurcation, runs posterior to the left common iliac vein, joined
   by the middle sacral vein.
 • External iliac: along iliopsoas; main supply to the lower limb (via femoral) and abdominal wall (via inferior
   epigastric). Its femoral branch gives the external pudendal artery (labia majora).
 • Primary supply of the external genitalia (labia minora, clitoris, vestibule) is the internal pudendal artery,
   from the internal iliac.
 • Internal iliac: supplies pelvis and viscera; divides at the upper border of the greater sciatic foramen.

Venous Drainage
 • Pelvic viscera → internal and external iliac veins → common iliac veins → inferior vena cava (IVC).
 • Exception — ovarian veins (in the infundibulopelvic ligaments): right → IVC; left → left renal vein.
 • External and internal iliac veins join anterior to the sacroiliac joint; the common iliac veins unite at about
   L5.
 • Median sacral vein → left common iliac vein or the confluence; met during promontory dissection.
 • External iliac vein = continuation of the femoral vein (behind the inguinal ligament); receives deep
   circumflex iliac, inferior epigastric and pubic veins (the pubic vein links external iliac and obturator veins).
 • Internal iliac vein: posteromedial to the artery; tributaries even less predictable than the arteries, with
   duplicated internal iliac veins common.
     ◦ Parietal: gluteal, internal pudendal, obturator, lateral sacral veins.
     ◦ Visceral: middle rectal, vesical, uterine, vaginal.
 • Sacral venous plexus: valveless network on the anterior sacrum (median and lateral sacral veins,
   posterior abdominal wall and basivertebral veins). Injured in presacral dissection — sacrocolpopexy,
   radical hysterectomy, abdominoperineal and low anterior resection.
 • High hydrostatic pressure, especially in lithotomy: a 1–2 mm vein can bleed more than 1 L/min → dissect
   with caution or avoid the area when not needed.

Lymphatic Drainage
 • Normal nodes are impalpable, hidden in the intermediate stratum.
 • Groups: common iliac, external iliac, internal iliac, inguinal, perivisceral — named for adjacent vessels or
   organs.
 • Most visceral drainage passes through the internal iliac nodes and their branches: presacral, obturator,
   internal pudendal.
 • Inguinal nodes communicate directly with internal and external iliac chains. Common iliac nodes receive
   external iliac, internal iliac and pelvic ureteric lymph → lateral aortic nodes.

catastrophic), and the valveless presacral veins make the sacrum the one place where a small vessel produces
exsanguinating bleeding.

ENDOSCOPIC ANATOMY
 • Cystourethroscopy sequence: distal urethra (periurethral glands, mucosal folds) → coaptation distally at
   the mid-urethra (external sphincter) → coaptation proximally at the bladder neck → trigone and orifices
   → survey of the whole mucosa.
 • Urethral findings: foreign bodies (mesh, stones) and diverticular ostia.
 • Ureteric orifices along the trigonal ridge; if anatomy is unaltered (no previous surgery, disease, prolapse
   or radiation), seen with the scope at the bladder neck using a 30-degree lens.
     ◦ Patulous orifice → vesicoureteric reflux; ectopic position → duplicated system.
     ◦ Significant anterior or apical descent: sponge stick in the vagina reduces the prolapse and restores the
       vesicourethral angle.
 • Landmarks on survey: symphysis pubis indentation (anterior wall); vessels and/or bowel (lateral and
   anterolateral walls); uterine impression (proximal bladder floor and posterior bladder wall).
 • Normal findings: rugae in the empty bladder that flatten with filling; delicate vasculature, denser on the
   trigone.
 • Benign trigonal (and sometimes lateral wall) lesions: squamous metaplasia, cystitis cystica — not
   reliably diagnosed on appearance, so suspicion of malignancy → biopsy.
 • Post-radiation mucosa: friable and hypervascular.
Concept — Female cystoscopy is interpreted against the organs pressing on the bladder — pubis anteriorly,
bowel laterally, uterus posteriorly — and any deviation from the normal orifice shape or position, or any
lesion not certainly benign, is a finding to act on rather than describe.

RADIOGRAPHY OF THE FEMALE PELVIS

Ultrasound
 • Translabial, transrectal and transvaginal ultrasound: diagnosis of pelvic organ prolapse and urethral
   disorders (injury, stricture).
 • Translabial ultrasound readily shows the pubic symphysis, urethra, bladder neck, vagina, rectum and anal
   canal.
     ◦ Levator plate and pubovisceral muscles: hyperechoic area behind the anorectal junction. Cul-de-sac:
       fluid, fat or small bowel.
     ◦ Bladder neck mobility: real-time imaging during Valsalva, supine. Also measures bladder wall
       thickness and prolapse.
     ◦ Visualises transvaginal mesh, mid-urethral slings, urethral bulking agents and implants.
     ◦ Three-dimensional probes add the axial view to the midsagittal plane.
 • Endoanal (anorectal) ultrasound: anal sphincter defects in faecal incontinence.

Magnetic resonance defecography.
 • Non-invasive dynamic study of pelvic floor anatomy and function during straining and defaecation, with
   rectal contrast.
 • Reference: the pubococcygeal line (PCL) — inferior border of the pubic symphysis → last coccygeal joint.
 • Prolapse graded mild, moderate or severe per compartment (anterior, middle, posterior) by maximum
   descent below the PCL.
 • Also shows concomitant disorders, e.g. urethral hypermobility.
 • Most valuable in defaecatory dysfunction or when symptoms are out of proportion to examination.

 KEY POINTS: RADIOGRAPHY OF THE FEMALE PELVIS
   • Translabial ultrasound shows transvaginal mesh, mid-urethral slings, bulking agents and urethral
     implants.
   • T2-weighted MRI is the best technique to visualise and localise urethral diverticula.
   • MR defecography is valuable in prolapse with defaecatory dysfunction or symptoms out of proportion
     to examination.

Concept — Imaging is chosen by question: ultrasound for implants and real-time mobility, static T2 MRI for
soft-tissue characterisation (diverticulum, cysts, uterine zones), and dynamic MRI for function measured
against a fixed bony line.
