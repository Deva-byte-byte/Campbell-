Chapter 59 — Differences of Sexual Development:
Etiology, Evaluation, and Medical Management
 • Differences (disorders) of sex development (DSD): conditions in which chromosomal, gonadal or
   anatomical sex characteristics are not typically binary male or female. Familiar examples are congenital
   adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), ovotesticular DSD (OVT DSD) and androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS).
 • Care is multispecialty and constantly evolving; controversies common to many DSDs (sex designation,
   surgical timing, gonadal management) are treated together at the end of the chapter.

TERMINOLOGY
 • Nomenclature has evolved "hermaphrodite" → "intersex" → "DSD". The landmark 2006 consensus
   statement (Lee et al.), from patients, clinicians and advocates, replaced "intersex" with "DSD" and retired
   "sex reversal" and "hermaphrodite" as pejorative (Table 59.1).
 • Many affected people find "disorders" pathologising and prefer "intersex" (especially as identity),
   "differences of sex development", or their diagnosis. There is no consensus; surveys show patients and
   parents most often name themselves by diagnosis.
 • "Atypical" or "nonbinary" genitalia may be more accurate and less stigmatising than "ambiguous
   genitalia"; the chapter uses "DSD" (differences) and "atypical genitalia". Genital photography in
   publications is controversial, so photographs are limited to those of greatest educational value.

What Belongs Under the DSD Umbrella
 • A broad umbrella groups clinically similar diagnoses for research, connects people with similar
   manifestations for support, and reflects shared need for multidisciplinary expertise. Membership of
   proximal hypospadias, CAH and sex chromosome conditions is disputed.
 • Proximal hypospadias (penoscrotal, scrotal, perineal): some call all cases "46,XY DSD of unclear etiology";
   others reserve "DSD" for a named diagnosis, avoiding social and legal controversy. Because a named
   diagnosis has many benefits (Table 59.2), offer endocrine and genetic testing for proximal hypospadias,
   even with descended testes.
 • CAH is called the commonest 46,XX DSD, yet many affected people do not identify as DSD or intersex:
   they regard it as an endocrine condition, and most 46,XY individuals with CAH have typical genitalia.

TYPICAL SEX DEVELOPMENT
 • Three sequential steps: chromosomal sex → gonadal sex → phenotypic sex. Chromosomal sex is fixed at
   fertilisation by the karyotype. Gonadal sex is the histology and function of the gonad, which starts
   bipotential; gene activation and suppression produce testis or ovary.
 • Primordial germ cells (PGCs) migrate from the dorsal yolk-sac wall through the mesentery to the
   medioventral urogenital ridge, starting in week 5 (Fig. 59.1), then become spermatogonia or oogonia as
   the testicular and ovarian cords differentiate.
 • SRY (sex-determining region Y), among the earliest testis-determining genes, is a transcription factor that
   supports cells of the male urogenital ridge and activates a cascade including SOX9 upregulation.
 • These genes also act elsewhere — MYRF (heart, eye), SOX9 (bone, cartilage), WT1 (kidney), SF1 and DAX1
   (adrenal) — which explains syndromic DSDs (Table 59.3).
 • Leydig cells (interstitium) make testosterone; Sertoli cells (within tubules) support spermatogenesis.
   Testicular PGCs are held out of meiosis until puberty. Testis development precedes ovarian by about 2
   weeks.
 • Ovary (less understood) needs Rspo1/Wnt4/β-catenin and Foxl2/Runx1: cords about 8 weeks → germ
   cell nests about 10 weeks → primordial follicles with granulosa cells at 16–17 weeks → meiosis → primary
   follicles about 20 weeks. Ovarian PGCs do enter meiosis; about 1 million at birth.

 • Phenotypic sex (internal and external genitalia, secondary sex characteristics) is also bipotential: every

Male-Typical Development
 • Sertoli cells secrete antimüllerian hormone (AMH) at 7–8 weeks → müllerian regression complete by
   about 10 weeks.
 • Leydig cells make testosterone from about 9 weeks: first autonomously, then under placental human
   chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), later under fetal pituitary luteinising hormone (LH). Weeks 9–13: wolffian
   ducts → epididymis and vas deferens, opening into the urogenital sinus at the müllerian tubercle.
 • 5α-reductase converts testosterone to dihydrotestosterone (DHT), which binds the androgen receptor
   with greater affinity and stability; in tissues with 5α-reductase (prostate, urogenital sinus, external
   genitalia) DHT is the active androgen. External genitalia need systemic testosterone plus local DHT.
 • Sequence (Fig. 59.3): tubercle elongates into the penis → urethral folds fuse posterior to anterior →
   prostate forms around the proximal urethra → urogenital swellings migrate posteriorly and fuse as
   scrotum → complete by 12–13 weeks. Penile growth and testicular descent follow in the third trimester
   under androgen and insulin-like peptide 3 (INSL3).

Female-Typical Development
 • Wolffian ducts regress, induced by COUP-TF2 through inhibition of fibroblast growth factors. The ovary
   makes no significant AMH, so müllerian ducts form tubes, uterus and upper vagina.
 • Vaginal origin is debated, but broadly proximal two-thirds müllerian, distal one-third urogenital sinus.
 • Without testosterone the external genitalia stay at the 6-week appearance: tubercle → clitoris; lateral
   swellings → labia majora; urethral folds → labia minora, enclosing introitus and meatus (Fig. 59.5).

From developmental steps to classification.
 • Disruption of any step causes a DSD, and the 2006 classification mirrors the steps: abnormal karyotype →
   sex chromosome DSD; normal karyotype with abnormal gonadal development, or with abnormal
   hormone production or action → 46,XX or 46,XY DSD.

 KEY POINTS: TYPICAL SEX DEVELOPMENT
   • Sex development occurs in three steps: chromosomal, gonadal and phenotypic sex.
   • Chromosomal sex (46,XX, 46,XY or other) is established at fertilisation.
   • Gonads are bipotential until about 6 weeks; internal and external genitalia until about 8 weeks.
   • Where 5α-reductase is present at differentiation (prostate, urogenital sinus, external genitalia), DHT is
     the active androgen.

Concept — The testis imposes the male pathway through two separate hormones: AMH removes the
müllerian ducts, testosterone (via DHT) builds wolffian and external structures. Without both, the female
phenotype results — so any DSD phenotype can be reasoned from which hormone was missing, where and
when.

GENDER DEVELOPMENT
 • Gender identity: identification of self — male, female or outside the binary. Gender role (expression):
   behaviour typical of male or female social roles. Sexual orientation: choice of sexual partner.
 • Identity is shaped by biological, cognitive, social and cultural factors. Infants discriminate genders and
   form stereotypes in the first year; gender-typical behaviour appears by 2 years; by 3 years most children
   label themselves consistently.
 • In DSD these inputs may differ: identity different from designated sex is more common, and some
   develop gender dysphoria (distress from that incongruence). Avoiding dysphoria is a quality outcome,
   which drives careful designation and has shifted designation trends; anyone may nonetheless affirm a
   different identity.
Concept — Designation at birth predicts an identity only the individual can later confirm; gender dysphoria
is the outcome by which the prediction is judged.

Sex Chromosome DSD
Klinefelter syndrome.
 • 47,XXY — the commonest sex chromosome aneuploidy, 1 in 600 live-born males; historically diagnosed
   in adolescence (incomplete puberty) or adulthood (infertility). Phenotype varies.
 • Adolescent/adult features (Fig. 59.6) are summarised in the comparison table; neurodevelopmental
   differences may occur.
 • Diagnosis is moving earlier: noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) and genetic testing for developmental
   delay. Most infants have no signs, but micropenis, cryptorchidism, bifid scrotum and hypospadias occur.
 • Testicular insufficiency is the hallmark. Mini-puberty testosterone is low-normal to normal; measuring
   gonadotropins and testosterone then is recommended. Infant testosterone has not improved
   development (it may increase linear and penile growth), so it is not currently recommended.
 • Puberty starts on time; insufficiency follows in adolescence or young adulthood as seminiferous tubules
   hyalinise, giving firm testes under 3.5 cm, high gonadotropins and low or low-normal testosterone. Most
   benefit from testosterone replacement. Gynaecomastia reflects a raised oestradiol:testosterone ratio.
 • Fertility: most are azoospermic, but testicular sperm extraction with intracytoplasmic sperm injection
   (ICSI) achieves live births (meta-analysis). Warn of the high infertility rate; whether retrieval is better in
   adolescence is unclear, so discuss once spermarche is reached and the individual is ready.
 • Also: neurodevelopmental (cognitive, language, learning, attention, executive) difficulties needing
   therapy; low bone mineral density and raised fat mass; extragonadal germ cell neoplasia and male breast
   cancer (cumulative breast cancer risk by 75 years only about 1%); more autoimmune disease.

Turner Syndrome
 • Commonest female sex chromosome aneuploidy, 25–50 per 100,000 females: one X with complete or
   partial loss of the second (some or all lines 45,X). Classic: female genitalia, short stature, absent
   secondary sexual characteristics, cardiac and renal anomalies (Fig. 59.7).
 • Diagnosis needs 45,X plus at least one typical manifestation (short stature, hypergonadotropic
   hypogonadism, infertility, middle ear infection, malformations, other stigmata). International guidelines
   2017. Increasingly prenatal (NIPT) rather than at short-stature or delayed-puberty evaluation.
 • Karyotypes: 45,X 40–50%; 45,X/46,XX 15–25%; 45,X/46,iso(X) 10%; 45,X/46,XY or other Y material 10–
   12%. Mosaicism → milder phenotype.
 • Systemic features (highly variable): endocrine (glucose intolerance, type 1 and 2 diabetes,
   hypothyroidism); gastrointestinal (raised liver enzymes, coeliac, inflammatory bowel disease); coarctation
   and bicuspid aortic valve; eye; ear (infection, hearing loss); webbed neck, neonatal peripheral oedema,
   short fourth metacarpal, hypoplastic nails, pigmented naevi; neurocognitive.
 • Renal anomalies in one-third to over half (commonest in 45,X): horseshoe kidney, duplication or
   agenesis, malrotation; multiple renal arteries in most on cardiovascular imaging.
 • Screen cardiac and renal at diagnosis, then lifelong (cardiovascular, autoimmune, eye, ear). Growth
   hormone improves adult height. 45,X/46,XY with atypical or male genitalia needs the same screening
   but is not formally TS.

 • Ovarian insufficiency in about 95% → oestrogen and progesterone. A minority have spontaneous
   thelarche, fewer menarche — most likely with mosaicism, least with 45,X; TS+Y also more likely than
   45,X, with rare spontaneous live births. AMH tracks karyotype, spontaneous puberty, gonadotropins and
   follicles.
 • TS+Y: Y material in an abnormal gonad → germ cell neoplasia; gonadoblastoma is benign but may
   progress to germ cell cancer (GCC; dysgerminoma). Neoplasia risk about 12–20%; GCC about 3%, in or

   weighing GCC against gonadal function and autonomy. The decision needs nuanced discussion but is
   rarely urgent. Gonadotropins and AMH gauge function; if gonads stay, imaging is insufficient to detect

   adequate adolescent ovarian function; ovarian tissue cryopreservation is investigational, notably at
   gonadectomy for TS+Y.

Mixed Gonadal Dysgenesis
 • Mixed gonadal dysgenesis (MGD): dysgenetic testis (variable position) + contralateral streak + persistent
   müllerian structures, with variable androgen effect. Classic asymmetry: hypospadias, one palpable
   descended testis, one impalpable testis with a flat labioscrotal fold (Fig. 59.8).
 • Second commonest cause of neonatal atypical genitalia after CAH. Usually 45,X/46,XY, a mosaic seen in
   1 in 5000 births, not all of whom have MGD.
 • 45,X/46,XY is a spectrum: female genitalia with variable ovaries (TS+Y), atypical genitalia (MGD), or male-
   typical genitalia with normal testes (about 85%). All carry Turner syndromic risk (cardiac, growth).
 • Presentation: variable clitorophallus, urogenital sinus or proximal hypospadias with labioscrotal fusion,
   undescended testis. Hemiuterus, vagina and tube lie on the streak side; wolffian structures on the testis
   side — the local action of AMH and testosterone.
 • Designation is hard: marked androgen exposure predicts male identity more often; dysphoria is
   commoner when raised female (a systematic review found it in a substantial minority raised female, none
   raised male). Multidisciplinary assessment and transparent discussion precede designation; irreversible
   infant surgery is discouraged unless medically necessary.
 • Germ cell neoplasia 15–35%, in streaks and dysgenetic testes alike → remove streak gonads (biopsy first
   if tissue type unknown).

 • Remaining gonad: raised male — descended testis observed, intra-abdominal testis brought down if
   possible; raised female — remove, or consider relocation to inguinal canal or labioscrotal fold, preserving
   function should identity differ while allowing surveillance.
 • At puberty the testis may make testosterone in any position (wanted or not); many fail, needing
   exogenous testosterone. Infertility is typical; sperm extraction has succeeded.

Ovotesticular DSD
 • Ovarian and testicular tissue in one individual — separate ovary and testis, or an ovotestis. Mostly 46,XX;
   also mosaic or 46,XY; about 1 per 100,000.
 • Atypical, often asymmetric genitalia; virtually all have a urogenital sinus, most a uterus.
 • An ovary sits in the normal position; a testis or ovotestis may lie anywhere on the path of descent. In a
   large South African cohort the ovotestis was commonest (about two-thirds of gonads), ovaries more
   often left, testes right.
 • Duct rule: a fallopian tube accompanies an ovary; a vas deferens always accompanies a testis. An
   ovotestis has a tube in two-thirds and a vas (± tube) in one-third.
 • Ovarian portion usually normal; testicular portion typically dysgenetic. Biopsy is often needed — polar
   biopsies if components look distinct. Neoplasia risk slightly raised (about 3%), in either component.
 • Designation follows full assessment; historically based on predominant anatomy. Identity varies but often
   matches designation.
 • Management is individualised; irreversible procedures deferred until the patient can participate.
   Removing discordant tissue is difficult even in polar ovotestes (Fig. 59.10); nonbinary rearing eases
   deferral to adolescence.
 • Puberty follows the functioning tissue; the dysgenetic testicular part means oestrogen effect often
   predominates. Infertility is common, but births via ovum or sperm are reported.

46,XX DSD
 • Arises from abnormal gonadal development or androgen excess.

Abnormal Gonadal Development
 • 46,XX testicular DSD (formerly sex reversal): testes and male genitalia in 46,XX, usually from SRY
   translocated from Y to X; about 1 per 20,000. Normal fetal testosterone and AMH → no müllerian
   structures.
 • Azoospermia from loss of the azoospermia factor (AZF) region of Y. Formerly found at infertility work-up;
   now by prenatal genotype–phenotype discordance (cell-free fetal DNA [cffDNA] 46,XX, ultrasound male).
   Progressive testicular regression and hypogonadism are common; identity typically male.

Abnormal Gonadal Development: Ovotesticular DSD
 • OVT DSD is most often 46,XX, so it is also a 46,XX gonadal DSD (see Sex Chromosome DSD).

Excess Androgen Exposure: CAH
 • CAH is the commonest cause of 46,XX DSD: reduced activity of one of five cortisol-pathway enzymes —
   side-chain cleavage, 3β-HSD, 17α-hydroxylase, 21-hydroxylase, 11β-hydroxylase (Fig. 59.11).
 • 21-hydroxylase deficiency (21-OHD): 95% of CAH, autosomal recessive, about 1 in 15,000. Cortisol
   deficiency → excess adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) → excess adrenal androgen; aldosterone
   deficiency → salt wasting.
 • Salt-wasting severity follows residual enzyme activity and is predictable by genotype. Infants have
   variable virilisation with bilateral nonpalpable gonads, and risk adrenal crisis (hyponatraemia,
   hyperkalaemia, hypotension, shock) in the first weeks. Raised 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP) is
   diagnostic.
 • 46,XX phenotype: clitoromegaly to fused labioscrotal folds, graded by Prader (Fig. 59.12) — sometimes an
   apparently male infant (or distal hypospadias) with impalpable gonads. Ovaries are normal, so müllerian
   structures are present, often with a common urogenital sinus (vagina and urethra joining proximal to the
   perineum). Assignment is typically female; some with male designation and marked virilisation identify as
   male.

 • 46,XY CAH needs treatment but has no genital difference, so newborn screening is most crucial for 46,XY,
   who give no genital warning before crisis.
 • Biochemistry: raised 17-OHP, androstenedione, testosterone and renin; low cortisol. Measure 17-OHP
   after 36 hours (normal neonatal surge). A cosyntropin stimulation test confirms cortisol adequacy more
   quickly.
 • Salt wasting brings hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia and metabolic acidosis, but not usually before day 5–7
   — early normal electrolytes do not exclude CAH. Prompt glucocorticoid, mineralocorticoid and salt
   replacement is lifesaving.
 • Long-term: balance replacement and androgen suppression against supraphysiological steroid harm
   (growth attenuation, precocious puberty, obesity, hypertension, osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease).
   Hard because cortisol's circadian and stress responses are difficult to mimic and androgen also arises via
   the "backdoor" pathway; new drugs are in trials. Classic CAH needs lifelong replacement.
 • Fertility is preserved but reduced. 46,XX: fewer pregnancies, mainly because fewer seek motherhood,
   though ovulation may be affected. 46,XY: testicular adrenal rest tumours (TARTs) — benign, commoner
   with poor suppression, regress with more glucocorticoid; they obstruct tubules (azoospermia, peritubular
   fibrosis, Sertoli and germ cell loss), so sperm banking is advocated; excision rarely indicated. Poor
   suppression can also cause hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.
 • Surgery: feminising surgery (clitoroplasty, urogenital sinus repair/vaginoplasty) was historically done in
   infancy; now re-evaluated because it is rarely urgent unless there is urinary retention (technique Chapter
   60).
 • 21-OHD is a continuum from salt-wasting classic to nonclassic CAH (NC CAH) (table below).
 • 11β-hydroxylase deficiency (about 5%) and 3β-HSD deficiency (under 1%, least common) are both
   autosomal recessive and treated like 21-OHD; their distinguishing features are in the table. 3β-HSD also
   undervirilises 46,XY, because DHEA is weak.
 • Prenatal dexamethasone largely prevents virilisation but not the need for postnatal replacement, and
   risks cognitive, cardiovascular and metabolic harm. Genital development is first-trimester, so it must start
   (by 6–7 weeks) before fetal status is known: number needed to treat at least 4 even after NIPT excludes
   males — three unaffected fetuses exposed for each affected girl helped. The Endocrine Society deems it
   experimental, only under an Institutional Review Board (IRB)-approved protocol.

 • Rare transplacental androgen. Fetoplacental aromatase (CYP19) deficiency blocks conversion of fetal

46,XY DSD
 • Arises from abnormal gonadal development (complete or partial gonadal dysgenesis) or abnormal
   androgen production or action.
Complete and partial gonadal dysgenesis.
 • Complete gonadal dysgenesis (CGD, Swyer syndrome): about 1 per 80,000; presents as prenatal/infant
   discordance or absent puberty. Streaks have no hormonal or fertility function, so prophylactic
   gonadectomy is usual and hormone replacement lifelong; the uterus grows with oestrogen and
   pregnancy is reported with ovum donation.
 • Partial gonadal dysgenesis (PGD): testis-promoting gene disruption (e.g. WT1, SF1, SRY, SOX9, MAP3K1,
   DHX37), often with extragenital features (WT1 → Denys–Drash and Frasier). Very rare; no incidence data.
   Identity highly variable, so designation needs discussion and surgery is individualised (as in MGD, OVT
   DSD). Pubertal testosterone is partial: males may still need testosterone, females need oestrogen.

Absent (vanishing) testes syndrome.
 • Bilateral anorchia from testicular regression (unilateral is frequent, bilateral very rare). Timing sets the
   phenotype: loss after the first trimester → male genitalia, impalpable testes, no müllerian structures; loss
   during it → embryonic testicular regression with atypical genitalia.
 • Causes: often unknown — teratogen, bilateral torsion, genetic (DHX37; heterozygous NR5A1 in a series
   with micropenis).
 • Mini-puberty (about 1–6 months): undetectable testosterone and AMH, raised follicle-stimulating
   hormone (FSH) and LH. Testosterone replacement from adolescence; identity male; prostheses in later
   adolescence or adulthood.

Abnormal Hormone Production
 • Synthesis defects may give atypical genitalia but müllerian structures regress (AMH normal). Most are
   steroidogenic enzyme defects (Fig. 59.11) — 5α-reductase deficiency or rare salt-wasting CAH. Identity is
   variable; designation may need to be chosen at birth.
5α-reductase deficiency.
 • Type 2 isoenzyme (SRD5A2) defect → reduced testosterone-to-DHT conversion. At birth: female or
   atypical genitalia, urogenital sinus with labioscrotal fusion, short blind vaginal pouch into which the vasa
   terminate; testes and epididymides in labioscrotal folds, canals or abdomen.
 • Virilisation at puberty (voice, muscle, phallic growth, erections); sperm production and fertility reported.
   Mechanism: high testosterone binding the receptor despite low affinity, or the pubertal rise in the type 1
   isoform.
 • Testosterone:DHT above 20:1 — in mini-puberty, after hCG, or at puberty; confirm by SRD5A2
   sequencing.
 • Landmark: the Dominican Republic "guevedoces" ("penis at 12") cohort (Imperato-McGinley, 1979).
   Many designated female later affirm male identity; delay irreversible surgery.

Abnormal hormone action.
 • Androgen receptor variants (X-linked) cause complete or partial androgen insensitivity (CAIS, PAIS).
 • CAIS: female genitalia, inguinal or abdominal testes, short vagina, no müllerian structures; X-linked
   recessive, 1 in 20,000–90,000 46,XY births. Historically found at primary amenorrhoea work-up or when
   testes appeared at herniorrhaphy. Testosterone is made but not sensed; aromatisation produces breasts
   at puberty.
 • About 50% of CAIS have an inguinal hernia; 1–2% of girls with inguinal hernia are 46,XY CAIS → at
   female herniorrhaphy, vaginoscopy for a cervix or endoscopy through the sac for an intra-abdominal
   testis. Prenatal diagnosis by NIPT–ultrasound discordance is increasing.

   cryptorchid testes.
 • Early prophylactic gonadectomy was once standard, but prepubertal tumour risk is about 2%, so gonads
   stay until after breast development and the patient can decide; some report better quality of life on
   endogenous hormones. No gametes; a gonadal management protocol has been proposed. Dilation
   usually suffices; vaginoplasty rarely needed.

   testosterone may improve genital development. Female identity: gonadotropin-releasing hormone

Persistent müllerian duct syndrome.
 • PMDS (hernia uteri inguinale): 46,XY, male genitalia, testes with vasa and epididymides, plus müllerian
   structures — undescended testes, bilateral tubes, uterus and upper vagina draining into the prostatic
   utricle; usually found at herniorrhaphy or orchidopexy.
 • Autosomal recessive: AMH (19p13) or AMH type II receptor (12q13), one of which is mutated in the large
   majority. Three presentations (Clarnette; Fig. 59.13) in the table; PMDS underlies 30–50% of transverse
   testicular ectopia.
 • Sequelae: hernia, infertility (abnormal testes, abnormal vasa/epididymides, surgical injury), testicular
   tumours (seminoma, reflecting intra-abdominal cryptorchidism) and müllerian malignancy. Clues:
   consanguinity, familial infertility or undescended testes.
 • Orchidopexy is recommended. Because the vasa hug the uterus and proximal vagina, remnants were
   historically preserved (divided longitudinally if needed to reach the scrotum). Remnant malignancy 3–8%

   spares the vasa.

Concept — In 46,XY DSD the uterus separates the mechanisms: dysgenetic testes fail at AMH and

MRKH syndrome.

MULTIDISCIPLINARY (INTERDISCIPLINARY) CARE MODEL
 • The model changed because long-term outcomes under historical care were suboptimal. That care
   followed John Money (1975) — early designation and strongly advised early surgery (table). Intersex
   activists and clinicians challenged it, and it is now judged ill-advised and against psychosocial interests.

 • Lifelong psychosocial care is needed: anxiety, depression, suicidal ideation and related conditions are
   more common. Care is shifting towards bodily autonomy and shared decision-making.
 • The 2006 consensus made multidisciplinary care the standard; the 2016 update stressed teamwork and
   peer support groups. Teams should work interdisciplinarily — as one, not a sum of parts.
 • No "one size fits all" algorithm: care is individualised to patient, family, culture and social setting.
 • Team: endocrinologist; urologist, paediatric surgeon, gynaecologist; geneticist and genetic counsellor;
   psychologist, psychiatrist, social worker; ethicist; nursing and support staff; ideally individuals with DSD/
   intersex traits as educators and peer links.
 • Evidence is often lacking, so the team is transparent about what is known and unknown; without
   consensus it educates and supports the family's decision rather than dictating it.
 • A urologist without a team should recognise this and transfer the patient to an appropriate centre as
   soon as possible, even a newborn.
 • Despite cost pressures (lobby institutions and legislators), clinicians should see the patient together in
   one clinic — separate days are not recommended — so information is exchanged in real time and a
   shared plan agreed. The same team sees inpatients; urgent (within 24 hours) review usually arises in the
   neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for atypical genitalia.
 • Transition is harder than in most urology (complexity, rarity, many specialists); adults may be seen in
   paediatric units or by paediatric specialists in adult settings. Patients must not be "abandoned": needs
   persist or grow, especially fertility and sexual health, which are hard to raise. Needs include coordination,
   autonomy, open discussion and psychosocial care; long-term outcomes remain to be defined, so
   continuity must span life.

INFANT PRESENTATION AND EVALUATION
 • Classically atypical genitalia at birth; increasingly prenatal through cffDNA — atypical genitalia on
   ultrasound, sex chromosome aneuploidy or mosaicism, or genotype–phenotype discordance (e.g. Y
   material with female genitalia at the 20-week scan).
 • Prompt multidisciplinary medical and psychosocial evaluation, ideally in the first 24–48 hours; atypical
   genitalia may mean an emergency (salt-wasting CAH).




                                                         page 17 of 23
Chapter 59 — Differences of Sexual Development                                                 Infant Presentation and Evaluation
 • Communication: congratulate; use "your baby", they/them or the baby's name — never "it"; train
   perinatal staff, since inconsistency breeds distrust; the NICU itself can frighten families. Apart from CAH
   most DSDs are not life-threatening, so reassure that designation and treatment are not emergent.
 • Evaluation covers prenatal history, comorbidities, anatomy, chromosomes and hormones, guiding testing,
   parental education and (often) designation. Family history: infertility, recurrent pregnancy loss,
   amenorrhoea, hirsutism, relatives with DSD (e.g. CAIS, hypospadias) or atypical genitalia; infant death
   suggests CAH. Ask about maternal steroids or contraceptives.
 • Examination in gender-neutral terms (Fig. 59.15), measuring stretched clitorophallic length, glans width,
   urethral plate length and anogenital distance, and locating the meatus versus a single perineal opening.
 • Gonads are the most informative sign: bilateral impalpable gonads with atypical genitalia → CAH; any
   palpable gonad means testicular tissue, excluding all but the rarest CAH, because ovaries do not
   descend. Rarely an ovotestis reaches the canal, suspected from asymmetric pole texture and ultrasound.
 • Bilateral impalpable testes, or unilateral impalpable testis with hypospadias = DSD until proven
   otherwise. Landmark Kaefer (1999), cryptorchidism + hypospadias without atypical genitalia: DSD 30%
   overall; about 15% if the testis was palpable, 50% if impalpable; proximal meatus 65% versus 5–8%
   midshaft/distal. Later work supports testing proximal hypospadias and links penoscrotal transposition
   and bifid scrotum to a DSD diagnosis.
 • Anogenital distance reflects fetal androgen (correlating with testis size, sperm count, penile length and
   testosterone in rats and humans) but lacks normative values. The External Masculinisation Score (phallus
   size, labioscrotal fusion, gonad and meatal position) has unproven predictive value.
 • Ultrasound (pelvic/scrotal): the neonatal period is ideal for the uterus (maternal hormones enlarge it), so
   routine rectal examination is unnecessary. It finds abdominal gonads and suggests type, but normal
   versus dysgenetic testis is hard to tell and streaks are often invisible. Asymmetry suggests MGD if 45,X/
   46,XY, OVT DSD if 46,XX.
 • Karyotype at once: at least 45 cells, laboratory warned of DSD; preliminary in 2–3 days. Fluorescence in
   situ hybridisation (FISH) identifies X and Y within hours, usually to confirm a second X.
 • Endocrine timing matters. If CAH is possible, 17-OHP at about 36 hours, before discharge. At about 1
   week: LH, FSH, testosterone, oestrogen, androstenedione, DHT and AMH (Table 59.5).

OLDER CHILD/ADOLESCENT PRESENTATION AND EVALUATION
 • Classic presentation at expected puberty: primary amenorrhoea, absent puberty, or virilisation; also via
   genetic testing for short stature or developmental concerns. Late presenters have typical external
   genitalia: CGD, KS, CAIS, MRKH, TS+Y — and 5α-reductase deficiency, female at birth, virilising at puberty.
 • Basal hormones without hCG are more revealing than in infancy: high gonadotropins with low sex
   steroid → primary gonadal failure; high LH and testosterone with female genitalia → CAIS.
 • Examine with patience — adults recall childhood genital examinations negatively, some with post-
   traumatic stress symptoms. Limit clinicians present; examine after the first visit; examine only when
   management may change; use examination under anaesthesia selectively.
Concept — The adolescent axis is awake, so basal levels answer what infancy needed hCG or mini-puberty to
show; late presenters are exactly those whose genitalia look typical.

PEER SUPPORT
 • Peer support offers emotional and informational support through shared experience. Evidence is limited
   and referral and engagement are low; success depends on meeting real needs and training facilitators.
   Some prefer broad groups, others diagnosis-specific ones.
 • Members value "not feeling like the only one", navigating systems, finding specialists and learning; it is
   empowering but emotionally taxing. Advocacy (public perception, medical care, legislation) suits some
   and deters others.

CURRENT "HOT TOPICS" AND CONTROVERSIES

Sex Designation and Future Gender Identity
 • No one's future gender identity can be predicted with certainty. Identity differing from assigned sex is
   more common than once recognised, even after diligent diagnosis; a small minority transition, though
   rates differ by diagnosis.
 • Biology: lower prenatal androgen exposure → female identity, higher → male; no fetal androgen (CAIS,
   CGD) → female identity. Postnatal androgens may add, via the brain and via appearance, self-image and
   societal response. Females with CAH show more male-typical roles; 5α-reductase deficiency more often
   male.
 • Yet sex of rearing predicts satisfaction with gender identity better than androgen exposure. Designation
   weighs probable adult identity, sexual function, hormone and fertility potential, fetal androgen exposure
   and psychosocial factors.

Role and Timing of External Genital Surgery
 • The most argued controversy. Elective surgery before the age of consent may irreversibly create anatomy
   incongruent with later identity and bypasses autonomy; against this stand possible functional advantages
   and the psychosocial burden of unoperated atypical genitalia in a binary society. "Feminising" and
   "masculinising" are contested terms, used for want of alternatives.
 • Feminising genitoplasty = clitoroplasty, vaginoplasty and labioplasty, staged or combined (Chapter 60).
   Techniques and outcomes have improved; scrutiny is of timing and who decides.
 • Claimed benefits of early surgery (better tolerance, no memory, easier technique, fewer complications)
   are unproven by large studies, and Money's rationale is incorrect. For those whose identity later differs,
   early clitoral reduction can be catastrophic; others regret complications or exclusion from the decision.
 • Surveys nonetheless show parents and patients mostly prefer early surgery, including most 46,XX adults
   after feminising genitoplasty (systematic review) — limited by no control group and inclusion of
   unoperated patients.
 • Recommendation: transparent multidisciplinary discussion of early versus later or no surgery; shared
   decision-making (rarely formalised); ideally no irreversible genital surgery until the individual can
   participate. Parental consent must address the limits of predicting identity, embodiment goals and
   fertility wishes.
 • CAH: most data come from CAH, and some groups (CARES Foundation) argue CAH is not DSD and early-
   surgery concerns do not apply. But dysphoria is increased in CAH, some thrive raised male, and not all
   women want or would risk genital surgery — so infant feminising surgery is questioned even after full
   counselling. Whether CAH should be treated separately is unresolved.
 • Masculinising surgery is mostly proximal hypospadias repair, often with penoscrotal transposition. It
   retains a high complication rate even in high-volume single- and multistage series, with concern for
   erectile dysfunction after multiple operations. Whether a defined DSD raises complications is disputed:
   one series found higher rates; a prospective multi-institutional 46,XY DSD study found them similar.

Gonadal Management: A Paradigm Shift
 • From automatic gonadectomy (dysgenetic or discordant gonads removed early as nonfunctional and
   tumour-prone) to individualised assessment of tumour risk, function and autonomous choice. Risks by
   diagnosis and age are now lower than once stated and stratified (Table 59.7); tumour biology makes
   biopsy increasingly decisive.
 • Fertility is being reconsidered: future gametes may allow biological fertility even if discordant with
   identity; germ cells were found in DSD gonads removed as "nonfunctional"; in vitro follicle growth and
   sperm maturation from immature tissue are investigational. Some centres offer experimental gonadal
   tissue cryopreservation at prophylactic gonadectomy.
 • Genetic diagnosis will personalise fertility and tumour estimates. Alternatives to removal include initial
   biopsy or moving an at-risk intra-abdominal gonad to the inguinal canal or labioscrotal fold.

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
 • Traditional DSD care proved suboptimal and recommendations are evolving. The urologist's role belongs
   only within a multidisciplinary model. Care is individualised; the role and timing of surgery are being
   redefined; counselling must be transparent about the known and unknown. Long-term outcomes with
   and without surgery, and genetic insight, will improve care.
