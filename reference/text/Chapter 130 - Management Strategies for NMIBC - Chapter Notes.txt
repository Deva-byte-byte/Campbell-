Chapter 130 — Non–Muscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer                                                 INTRODUCTION


 Chapter 130 — Management Strategies for Non–Muscle-
 Invasive Bladder Cancer (Ta, T1 and CIS)
 INTRODUCTION
   • Non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) = malignant urothelial tumours that have not invaded
     the detrusor. It spans the relatively benign course of low-grade papillary tumours and the aggressive
     course of high-grade disease: urothelial carcinoma in situ (CIS) and high-grade Ta and T1.
   • Epidemiology (United States): over 80,000 new bladder cancers annually; 70–80% are non–muscle-
     invasive at presentation — of these 60–70% Ta, 20–30% T1, about 10% CIS. Incidence has declined
     over two decades, with trends varying by race and ethnicity.

 Presentation
   • Commonest presentation: painless haematuria. Bladder cancer is far more frequent with gross than
     microscopic haematuria; microscopic haematuria is defined as ≥3 red blood cells per high-power field.
   • A large multicentre prospective study confirmed visible haematuria carries a several-fold higher
     cancer yield than non-visible; prevalence with non-visible haematuria is low (0.3–4.7%).
   • Females experience delays in haematuria evaluation, which worsens bladder cancer outcomes.
   • Irritative voiding symptoms are associated with CIS: about 80% of CIS patients have irritative
     symptoms, and their presence doubles the risk of CIS in haematuria patients.
   • Hence cystoscopy and upper tract imaging are indicated for haematuria and/or unexplained irritative
     symptoms.

 Natural History
   • Survival is often favourable, but the disease is defined by frequent recurrence requiring regular
     cystoscopy; managed by transurethral surgery, often followed by intravesical immunotherapy or
     chemotherapy.
   • Recurrence risk about 40–60%, influenced by patient and disease factors and by quality metrics —
     completeness of resection and appropriate intravesical therapy.
   • Progression is the key concern: from about 6% in low-grade Ta to 17% in high-grade T1. Hence risk
     stratification is paramount.
 Concept — NMIBC is two diseases under one label: a recurrence problem (low grade) and a progression
 problem (high grade); every management decision in the chapter follows from which of the two a patient
 has.

 PATHOLOGY: GRADING, STAGING AND IMPLICATIONS FOR CLINICAL RISK
 STRATIFICATION

 Pathological Staging
   • Staging follows the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) criteria.
   • Clinical stage = findings at transurethral resection of bladder tumour (TURBT) + imaging + bimanual
     examination at TURBT; it establishes whether lamina propria and/or muscularis propria are present
     and involved, and should assess lymphovascular invasion (LVI).
   • Pathological stage = partial or radical cystectomy specimen plus pelvic lymph nodes.

   • Three histological layers: urothelium → lamina propria (suburothelial connective tissue) → detrusor/
     muscularis propria, which is absent beneath the urothelium of diverticula.
   • Tumour, node, metastasis (TNM) staging of NMIBC:
       ◦ Ta = non-invasive papillary tumour confined to urothelium, regardless of grade.
       ◦ T1 = invades lamina propria (subepithelial tissue).
       ◦ CIS (Tis) = flat, high-grade, confined to urothelium.
   • T1 substaging: extent of lamina propria invasion correlates with progression. Depth relative to the
     muscularis mucosae (discontinuous wispy smooth-muscle bands within the lamina propria) — invasion
     deep to it carries worse prognosis — but it cannot be assessed in all TURBT specimens (discontinuity,
     poor orientation).
   • T1a/T1b substaging has been proposed; the AJCC Staging Manual stresses assessing extent of lamina
     propria invasion, but no method is universally accepted.

 Pathological Grading
   • Current system: World Health Organization/International Society of Urological Pathology (WHO/
     ISUP) 2015 (building on the 2004 revision). "Urothelial carcinoma" is preferred over "transitional cell
     carcinoma".
   • Since 2004 grading is two-tiered — low grade vs high grade — replacing the three-tiered WHO 1973
     (Ash–Broder) system.
   • Current categories: hyperplasia (flat and papillary), reactive atypia, atypia of unknown significance,
     urothelial dysplasia, urothelial CIS, urothelial papilloma, papillary urothelial neoplasm of low
     malignant potential (PUNLMP), non–muscle-invasive low-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma, non–
     muscle-invasive high-grade papillary urothelial carcinoma.
   • PUNLMP: papillary tumour with orderly arrangement, minimal architectural abnormality and minimal
     nuclear atypia; distinct from both grades, unlikely to progress, essentially benign. The WHO
     recommends a report note that such patients are at risk of recurrence, occasionally as urothelial
     carcinoma (UC), so follow-up is warranted.
   • Papilloma: truly benign; no progression risk.

 Tumour Biology
   • Grade is among the most important prognostic factors.
                                    Low-grade Ta                        High-grade T1
  Recurrence                        50–70%                              >80%
  Progression                       <5%                                 50% within 3 years

   • Behaviour is grade-dependent rather than stage-dependent: high-grade tumours progress at similar
     frequency whether Ta or T1.
   • Controversy: small (≤3 cm) high-grade Ta is stratified intermediate risk, yet recurs and progresses like
     high-risk Ta (>3 cm, multifocal, high grade).
   • Prognosis also correlates with tumour size, multiplicity, papillary vs sessile configuration, LVI and
     status of the remaining urothelium.
   • Low-grade pTa may be almost benign and a separate disease entity from high-grade tumours
     (different genetic imprints) — yet low- and high-grade lesions coexist.
   • UC is a field-change disease (tumours at different times and sites). Rarely, low-grade patients develop
     high-grade tumours years later → long-term surveillance is appropriate.
   • Molecular subtypes: RNA-expression classification of NMIBC; the UROMOL project defined four
     molecular classes with distinct expression profiles and phenotypes. Clinical utility currently limited;
     potential basis for precision risk assessment.

 Pathological Characteristics and Implications for Clinical Management and Risk
 Stratification
 Ta
   • Usually low grade; recurrence common (especially if multiple), progression rare. About 7% of Ta
     tumours are high grade (range 3–18%).
   • The most important risk factor for progression is grade, not stage.

 Carcinoma In Situ
   • Not "premalignant": CIS is a flat, non-invasive UC, high grade by definition, and a precursor of
     invasive high-grade cancer.
   • Histology: severely dysplastic urothelium, disorderly, high-grade nuclear atypia; denudation from loss
     of cohesion can hamper interpretation.
   • Dysplasia/atypia reports cause confusion: mild forms are benign, but severe/high-grade dysplasia =
     CIS.
   • Untreated CIS progresses to muscle invasion in 40–83%, especially with papillary tumour.
   • "CIS alone" harbours invasion at cystectomy in up to 20%; CIS with presumed T1 markedly increases
     upstaging at cystectomy.
   • CIS is the second most important prognostic factor after grade. Multicentricity is ominous.
   • Irritative symptoms have been linked to diffuse disease, invasion and poor prognosis — no consensus.
   • Node-positive disease with CIS only at radical cystectomy: up to 3%.

 T1
   • Usually papillary on a narrow stalk; nodular/sessile appearance suggests deeper invasion.
   • Increased recurrence/progression risk with: deep lamina propria invasion (especially muscularis
     mucosae), LVI, pyuria, bladder neck involvement.
   • Hydronephrosis often indicates muscle invasion.

 Understaging
   • Understaging is common in high-grade, apparently non–muscle-invasive disease, especially T1;
     cystectomy frequently reveals more extensive disease than the transurethral resection (TUR) specimen
     suggested.
   • Restaging TURBT of high-grade T1 finds muscle invasion in up to half. Hence a thorough second
     resection — particularly when muscle was absent from the first specimen.

   KEY POINTS: PATHOLOGY AND RISK STRATIFICATION
    • Malignant tumours are low or high grade, regardless of invasion.
    • High- and low-grade cancers are essentially separate diseases (genetics, behaviour, management).
    • The most important risk factor for progression is grade.
    • CIS is a precursor and risk factor for progression, invasion and metastasis.
    • PUNLMP = orderly arrangement, minimal architectural abnormality, minimal nuclear atypia.
    • Risk stratification is essential for treatment, counselling and surveillance.

 Concept — Grade drives progression; number, size and prior recurrence drive recurrence. Stage and CIS
 matter chiefly because they signal high-grade biology and understaging — which is why every risk model is
 built on that split.

 ENDOSCOPIC SURGICAL MANAGEMENT

 Procedures
 Diagnosis and Upper Tract Evaluation
   • Tumours usually found at office cystoscopy for haematuria → map location, number and morphology
     and review before anaesthetic TUR. If found incidentally on cross-sectional imaging, may proceed
     directly to TURBT.
   • Field change → upper tract evaluation at diagnosis and, by risk, in surveillance: computed
     tomography (CT) urography commonly; retrograde pyelography during TURBT if not done;
     ureteroscopy reserved for suspicious findings.
   • Upper tract tumours occur in <5% of bladder cancer patients. Risk factors: trigonal tumours,
     concomitant CIS, high-risk disease.

 FIGURE 130.6 — CT UROGRAM
   • Filling defect along the right lateral bladder wall on CT urography, proven high-grade Ta at resection.

 Transurethral Resection of Bladder Tumour
   • TURBT is both diagnostic and therapeutic; regional or general anaesthesia, with or without paralysis;
     careful positioning to avoid lower-limb nerve injury; specimens systematically obtained and labelled.
   • Bimanual examination before and after TURBT: fixation or a persistent palpable mass after resection
     suggests locally advanced disease — though its added value with modern imaging is limited and
     possibly misleading.
   • Technique sequence: complete inspection with flexible cystoscope and/or 30° and 70° lenses →
     continuous-flow irrigation, bladder filled just enough to efface folds (overdistension thins detrusor →
     perforation; outflow suction refines volume control) → piecemeal resection debulking intraluminal
     tumour → transect base or stalk, lifting the tumour edge away from detrusor → separate deep-
     margin pass or cold-cup biopsy of the base sent separately for muscle invasion → cautery for
     haemostasis → Ellik evacuator for chips → final haemostasis check at minimal irrigation.
   • Resection quality determines outcome, especially for high-grade disease: complete visual resection is
     recommended, yet recurrence varies widely by surgeon.
   • Muscle in the specimen is required for staging; registry data show nearly half of diagnostic resections
     lacked muscle (or its mention), which translated into increased mortality.

 Special Circumstances
 Diverticular tumours.
   • Harder to see and resect; no detrusor backing → high perforation risk and unreliable staging.
   • Invasion beyond diverticular lamina propria = perivesical fat = T3a by definition. Clinical understaging
     in over half of Ta/T1 cases at cystectomy.
   • Low grade: conservative — cold-cup resection and fulguration. High grade: sample base including
     perivesical fat; strongly consider partial or radical cystectomy. No consensus on management.
 Tumour location.
   • Anterior wall and dome (capacious bladder): minimal filling + suprapubic compression; extra-long
     resectoscope; rarely a temporary perineal urethrostomy (obese patients); digital rectal or vaginal
     manipulation; angled or flat loops for posterior wall/dome.
 Ureteric orifice.
   • Pure cutting current and limited cautery → minimal scarring; resection of the orifice is permissible.
   • Resecting the intramural ureter may eradicate tumour but risks reflux of malignant cells (clinical
     significance unclear).
   • Post-resection intramural stenosis about 12%; predictors: tumour >1.5 cm and T1. T1 in the bladder
     and CIS in the intraluminal distal ureter raise upper tract UC risk.
   • Routine stenting unnecessary after careful unilateral orifice resection.
   • Aids: efflux check with intravenous indigo carmine or methylene blue, or retrograde study; with
     fluorescence cystoscopy the urine jet appears green; open-ended ureteric catheter before resection
     of adjacent tumours. Stricture rare (about 4%); most hydronephrosis resolves or reflects invasion.
     Postoperative renal ultrasonography if orifice resected or coagulation used nearby.
 Small tumours and thin bladders.
   • Small or narrow-stalk tumours: cold-cup forceps alone — useful in thin-walled bladders (elderly
     women); perforation is smaller than with the loop. Bugbee electrode for haemostasis.

 Laser Resection
   • Holmium and thulium lasers most used; equivalent safety and efficacy; en bloc resection or
     vaporisation.
   • En bloc resection: loop electrode, HybridKnife, holmium, thulium or potassium titanyl phosphate
     (KTP) laser. Excises tumour with underlying muscle intact → preserved architecture, less cautery
     artefact, more accurate staging; best for papillary tumours on a discrete stalk.
   • Meta-analyses: fewer perforations, less residual disease, more detrusor in the specimen, fewer
     obturator kicks — but no difference in recurrence. Endorsed by the European Association of Urology
     (EAU) guidelines as a viable strategy.

   • Disadvantages: large tumours need fragmentation for extraction; disruption at the resection line;
     equipment cost; excessive penetration depth.

 Repeat Transurethral Resection of Bladder Tumour
   • Second-look TURBT is a quality indicator. Rationale: incomplete resection (volume, access,
     complications) drives early recurrence; muscle in the specimen (itself a quality indicator) is critical
     where lamina propria invasion is found or invasion suspected.
   • Residual tumour is common: tumour at first follow-up cystoscopy in up to 45%; residual tumour at the
     resection site in ≥40% when re-resected within weeks; second TURBT corrects staging errors in about
     half of those with residual disease.
   • High-grade Ta: residual tumour in up to 50%, upstaging up to 15% → AUA: consider repeat TURBT
     within 6 weeks for multifocal and/or large high-grade Ta.
   • High-grade T1: worse findings or upstaging to muscle invasion in 25–30%; up to about half if no
     muscle in the first specimen → repeat TURBT within 6 weeks recommended.
   • Understaging in T1 is about twice as likely when muscle is absent.
   • Benefits: second resection changes treatment in one-third; observational data show better survival
     and fewer recurrences; randomised data show lower recurrence and progression in T1. Omission of
     muscle (or of its mention) → increased mortality.
   • Residual T1 at re-resection → progression up to 80% → consider timely cystectomy.
   • Second opinion: re-resect unless clear muscle invasion, especially if outside slides unavailable;
     subspecialty genitourinary pathology review changes management in about one-third (interobserver
     variability).
   • AUA summary: repeat TURBT of the primary site, including muscularis propria, within 6 weeks for
     incomplete initial resection and all T1; consider for high-risk high-grade Ta. Greatest benefit when no
     muscle was present initially.

 Bladder and Urethral Biopsies
   • Biopsy all suspicious areas; cold-cup biopsies avoid cautery artefact.
   • Positive cytology + erythematous velvety mucosa → biopsies may diagnose CIS.
   • Random biopsies of normal-appearing mucosa: low yield. Not indicated in low risk (low-grade
     papillary, negative cytology); consider with high-risk history or positive cytology (or multiple tumours).
   • Prostatic urethra: risk low in low-risk disease, increased with bladder neck/trigone tumours or CIS.

 Office-Based Endoscopic Management
   • Small (≤1 cm) low-grade recurrences → office thermal or laser ablation, avoiding anaesthesia.
   • Analgesia: 1–2% lidocaine with bicarbonate instilled, 15–30 minutes' dwell; fulguration of 1–5 mm
     tumours is often tolerable without it.
   • Randomised non-inferiority trial: office laser photocoagulation under photodynamic guidance was
     non-inferior to TURBT for recurrence-free survival, with better quality of life.

   • Prerequisite: no tissue is obtained, so prior tissue diagnosis and negative cytology are mandatory to
     confirm low grade.
   • Active surveillance of small low-grade tumours until significant growth is safe in selected patients.
     Bladder Cancer Italian Active Surveillance (BIAS) study: about half remain treatment-free at 2 years;
     failure predicted by multiple prior TURBTs and multiple tumours. Avoids anaesthesia, improves
     quality of life, limits financial toxicity.

 Enhanced Cystoscopic Techniques: Fluorescence Cystoscopy, Narrow-Band Imaging, Optical
 Coherence Tomography and Confocal Laser Endomicroscopy
   • White-light cystoscopy (WLC) is the gold standard, but subjective: a substantial proportion of biopsies
     of suspicious areas are negative, and imperfect sensitivity partly explains early recurrence after
     "complete" resection.
   • Enhancements: fluorescence cystoscopy, narrow-band imaging (NBI), optical coherence tomography
     (OCT), confocal laser endomicroscopy.

 Fluorescence (Blue-Light) Cystoscopy
   • Mechanism: photoactive porphyrins accumulate in neoplastic tissue and fluoresce red under blue
     light.
   • 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) caused systemic photosensitisation → replaced by hexaminolevulinate
     (HAL), which avoids it. Also termed photodynamic diagnosis (PDD).
   • Detects small papillary tumours and almost one-third more CIS than WLC; greatest gain is for CIS.
   • Recurrence: earlier prospective data showed reduced recurrence for several years; a meta-analysis
     supports reduction up to 1 year and beyond, inconsistently. However, a multicentre pragmatic
     randomised trial found no reduction in 3-year recurrence and higher cost, mainly in intermediate risk.
     Progression benefit unproven (underpowered).
   • Also assesses completeness of TUR. Office flexible blue-light cystoscopy detects about 20% more
     recurrent papillary tumours and about one-third more CIS.

 Narrow-Band Imaging
   • Mechanism: two haemoglobin-absorbed wavelengths — 415 nm penetrates superficial mucosa; 540
     nm penetrates deeper → enhances tumour vasculature.
   • Detects more papillary tumours and CIS (initially about 13% more tumours), but false-positive rate
     about 60%.
   • NBI-assisted TUR is more thorough; a randomised trial showed no overall recurrence difference, but
     reduced short-term (≤3 months) recurrence in low-risk tumours — more thorough surface resection
     without altering high-risk recurrence patterns.

 Complications of Transurethral Resection of Bladder Tumour and Bladder Biopsy
   • Common: haematuria, infection, irritative symptoms. Less common: clot retention, TUR syndrome,
     perforation requiring cystorrhaphy, trigonal or urethral scarring.
   • Major complications (uncontrolled haematuria, perforation) in 1–6.7%; subclinical extravasation on
     cystography in up to half.
   • Perforation risk factors: elderly, large posterior wall tumours, multiple prior intravesical courses.
   • Prevention: avoid overdistension; paralysis for lateral wall tumours (obturator reflex); stage resection
     of large or apparently invasive tumours.
   • Extraperitoneal (majority) vs intraperitoneal (dome):
  Extraperitoneal                       Intraperitoneal
  Majority of perforations              Dome resections
  Prolonged urethral catheter           Less likely to close → open or laparoscopic repair; open repair allows washout
  drainage                              to limit seeding

   • Tumour seeding after perforation: low risk, but extravesical recurrences reported; higher apparent risk
     after surgical repair likely reflects selection of severe cases.
   • Perforation → more recurrence and worse disease-free survival, but not worse cancer-specific or
     overall survival (more patients underwent cystectomy).
   • TUR syndrome uncommon (especially bipolar); managed as in transurethral resection of the prostate
     (TURP).

   KEY POINTS: ENDOSCOPIC SURGICAL MANAGEMENT
    • TURBT removes all visible tumour and provides specimens for stage and grade.
    • Repeat resection within 6 weeks for high-grade disease — recommended for T1 and incomplete
      resection, considered for high-risk high-grade Ta — especially if no muscle in the initial TURBT.
    • Sample all suspicious lesions; random biopsies are not required in low-risk patients.
    • Office fulguration and observation for selected low-risk patients.
    • Fluorescence cystoscopy and NBI improve visualisation of inconspicuous tumours and may reduce
      recurrence after TUR.

 Concept — TURBT is a staging operation before it is a therapeutic one: completeness and muscle in the
 specimen are the two quality levers, and repeat resection, en bloc technique and enhanced imaging all
 exist to serve them.

 INTRAVESICAL THERAPY

 Perioperative Intravesical Therapy
   • Rationale: kill floating tumour cells before implantation; recommended by multiple guideline panels.
   • Meta-analysis: single perioperative instillation reduces recurrence in low-risk and in multiple tumours;
     MMC, epirubicin and pirarubicin effective; thiotepa not (limited data, diluted doses).
   • MMC timing: within 6 hours reduces recurrence; at 24 hours it does not — consistent with an anti-
     implantation mechanism. Benefit persists even with ongoing adjuvant instillations (randomised).
     Level 1 evidence, greatest for initial solitary low-grade papillary tumour.
   • Neoadjuvant MMC (1 day and 4 hours before TURBT): small phase II trial, marked relative recurrence
     reduction, well tolerated.
   • Gemcitabine is now the standard perioperative agent in the United States (efficacy, cost, safety; level
     1 evidence).
   • SWOG S0337 (Southwest Oncology Group; landmark): gemcitabine 2 g in 100 mL saline for 1 hour vs
     saline immediately post-TURBT for suspected low-grade NMIBC; 4-year recurrence 35% vs 47%; low-
     grade only 34% vs 54%; 34% relative reduction; no difference in adverse effects.
   • An earlier negative gemcitabine trial used >20 hours of saline irrigation in both arms → continuous
     irrigation itself may reduce recurrence; sterile water and continuous saline irrigation both show
     potential.
   • Complications: irritative symptoms commonest; serious sequelae and rare deaths, especially with
     perforation; chemical cystitis, cutaneous desquamation, contracture, calcified eschar, harder later
     cystectomy.
   • Withhold chemotherapy with extensive resection or suspected perforation.
   • BCG must never be given immediately after TUR — risk of intravasation of live bacteria, sepsis and
     death.

   KEY POINTS: PERIOPERATIVE INTRAVESICAL THERAPY TO PREVENT TUMOUR IMPLANTATION
    • Single-dose chemotherapy within 6 hours reduces recurrence of low-risk tumours, most at initial
      solitary low-grade papillary presentation.
    • Incremental benefit in recurrent or multiple tumours is limited.
    • Avoid with suspected perforation or extensive resection.

 Concept — The single instillation is an anti-implantation measure, not a treatment of residual disease:
 hence the 6-hour window, the absolute bar on BCG at this point, and the largest benefit where
 implantation — not biology — drives recurrence.

 Mechanism of Action
   • Sequence: BCG binds fibronectin in the bladder wall → local immune response: cytokines in urine and
     wall and influx of granulocytes, mononuclear and dendritic cells → cell-mediated cytotoxicity and an
     antiangiogenic state.
   • Cytokines: tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-
     CSF), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), interleukins (IL) 1, 2, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 18. Tumour necrosis factor–related
     apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) release is key and associated with response.
   • Preferential IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12 = T-helper type 1 (Th1) response (effective); concomitant IL-10 →
     suppressive Th2 response.
   • Strains differ in immune pattern, but no strain is superior.
   • Response limited by immunosuppressive disease and advanced age.

 Bacille Calmette-Guérin Treatment of Carcinoma In Situ and High-Risk Disease
   • BCG is the most used intravesical agent in the United States (chemotherapy more used elsewhere);
     TICE BCG is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA).
   • Before BCG, CIS progressed about 7% per year. Initial tumour-free response up to 84%.
   • Durability: about half have a durable response (median 4 years); only about 30% free of recurrence or
     progression at 10 years, most events within 5 years → close follow-up mandatory. Non-responders
     progress far more often than responders. Contemporary cohorts with adequate BCG show better
     outcomes (high-grade recurrence-free survival at 12 months about 81–89%).
   • AUA: BCG is preferred initial treatment for high-risk tumours (especially CIS); for intermediate risk, an
     option up front or preferably after intravesical chemotherapy.

 Bacille Calmette-Guérin Treatment of Residual Tumour
   • BCG eradicates residual papillary tumour in about 60% but must not replace resection; its primary
     use is adjuvant.
   • CIS persisting after induction → second induction or first maintenance (3 weeks) yields complete
     response in a further 30–50%.
   • Prostatic urethral mucosal/superficial duct carcinoma: BCG about 50% tumour-free; a limited TURP
     or fulguration reduces burden and improves BCG contact.

 Bacille Calmette-Guérin Therapy to Prevent Recurrence
   • 6-week course after TURBT reduces recurrence (on average about 40%).
   • Maintenance reduces recurrence more than induction alone (meta-analysis). 3 years of maintenance
     is supported; intermediate risk may be managed with 1 year.
   • High-risk T1: BCG substantially lowers recurrence and progression vs TUR alone; multiplicity and
     associated CIS raise progression risk.
   • T1a vs T1b substaging did not improve prediction of recurrence or progression after BCG.

 Impact of Bacille Calmette-Guérin on Progression
   • Progression is BCG's greatest value. An early randomised trial showed delayed progression vs TUR and
     fewer cystectomies for CIS, with later cystectomy; yet only about a quarter were alive with an intact
     bladder at 10–15 years.
   • Meta-analyses: BCG reduces progression (vs chemotherapy and vs MMC specifically); benefit
     concentrated in trials using maintenance.
   • No chemotherapy trial has significantly reduced progression.
   • Long-term survival benefit of BCG is not fully defined.

 Determining Optimal Bacille Calmette-Guérin Treatment Schedule
   • Optimal dose and schedule not established; 6-week induction alone is insufficient — maintenance is
     requisite (AUA).
   • Second induction: additional response about 25% (prophylaxis) and 30% (CIS). Beyond a second
     course, progression risk 20–50%; roughly 7% added progression risk per extra course.
   • Response at 6 months predicts prognosis.

   • Interferons (IFN) = glycoproteins with antitumour actions: inhibit nucleotide synthesis, upregulate

     efficacy.

 Bacille Calmette-Guérin Shortages
   • Three shortages in a decade. Connaught strain paused 2012, discontinued 2017; TICE is now the only
     FDA-approved strain, made in a single plant supplying 70 countries; demand exceeds supply.
   • SWOG S1602 (about 1000 patients, enrolment complete): TICE vs Tokyo-172 vs Tokyo-172 with
     intradermal priming — may bring a second approved strain and test priming.
   • Shortage strategies: prioritise high-risk NMIBC → dose reduction, especially for maintenance → split
     vials (viability ≥72 hours) → intravesical chemotherapy for intermediate (single agent) and high risk
     (doublet) → early radical cystectomy for highest risk. Updates: AUA BCG shortage page.

   KEY POINTS: IMMUNOTHERAPY
    • BCG is more effective but more toxic than intravesical chemotherapy.
    • Use BCG sparingly in low-risk disease and during shortages.
    • Infectious complications are managed per Box 130.2.
    • BCG reduces recurrence and is the only agent shown to delay or prevent high-grade progression.
    • Maintenance improves outcomes (especially high risk) and should follow response to induction, as
      tolerated.
    • BCG is contraindicated with disrupted urothelium (sepsis).

 Concept — BCG works only as a sustained Th1 immune stimulus: that is why maintenance is what
 separates it from chemotherapy, why it is the only agent that alters progression, and why every
 contraindication is either a breached urothelium or a host who cannot mount the response.

 Intravesical Chemotherapy
   • Single perioperative dose: clear recurrence benefit. Adjuvant induction ± maintenance also reduces
     recurrence; MMC and gemcitabine most used.

   • Gemcitabine is the most active single agent (2-year recurrence-free survival about one-fifth in mixed
     BCG-exposed/unresponsive patients).
   • Gem/MMC: gemcitabine 1000 mg, 60–90 minutes → drain → MMC 40 mg, 60–90 minutes; durable
     recurrence-free rate about 38%.
   • Gem/Doce (more common): multi-institutional data — 1- and 2-year high-grade recurrence-free
     survival about 65% and 52%, progression to muscle invasion under 4%, only about 3% could not
     complete, 84% avoided cystectomy; CIS complete response 60% at 1 year, 43% at 2 years. Needs
     confirmation; no level 1 or prospective evidence.

 Nadofaragene Firadenovec
   • Replication-incompetent adenovirus type 5 carrying the IFN-α2b gene, with Syn3 polyamide
     surfactant to enhance urothelial transduction; FDA-approved for BCG-unresponsive disease.
   • Pivotal single-arm trial: 75 mL (3 × 10¹⁰ viral particles/mL) instilled for 1 hour every 3 months; CIS
     complete response 53% at 3 months and 24% at 12 months; papillary high-grade RFS 44% at 12
     months; progression to muscle invasion 3%; 71% cystectomy-free; serious adverse events 2%.

 Role of "Early" Cystectomy
   • Rationale: BCG failure in CIS → about 50% progression; 3-month T1 failure after BCG → 82%
     progression; up to 20% of CIS patients die of UC within 10 years; each T1 recurrence carries 5–10%
     metastatic risk; residual tumour at re-resection → high rate of muscle invasion.
   • Aggressive intravesical therapy long term: only about a quarter alive with intact bladder at 15 years;
     about half progress; one-third die of disease.
   • Immediate cystectomy for clinical T1: accurate staging and excellent 10-year disease-free survival
     (92%), falling to 64% if understaged muscle-invasive disease is found; up to 15% already have
     micrometastases; delay of even 12 weeks worsens survival.
   • "Timely" cystectomy (Chang and Cookson) balances progression risk against morbidity; 10-year
     survival after cystectomy for NMIBC 67–92%.
   • Trap: patients who progress to muscle invasion under surveillance fare worse than those presenting
     with muscle-invasive disease.
   • Sexual- and nerve-sparing cystectomy in this lower-risk group: oncological safety unknown.
     Neobladder availability reduces treatment delay.
   • Also consider cystectomy when tumour cannot be controlled endoscopically: bulky, inaccessible
     (large bladder, urethral stricture).
   • Strongly consider radical cystectomy for: high grade with deep lamina propria invasion, LVI, diffuse
     CIS, diverticular tumours, substantial distal ureteric or prostatic urethral involvement, BCG-
     unresponsive, too large or inaccessible, or patient preference for definitive therapy.
   • AUA: radical cystectomy for fit patients with persistent HG T1 on repeat resection, or T1 with CIS, LVI
     or variant histology; and for high-risk BCG-unresponsive disease.
   • Partial cystectomy: limited data; option in selected patients.

   KEY POINTS: EARLY CYSTECTOMY
    • Radical cystectomy is definitive with favourable outcomes; consider for:
    • BCG-unresponsive high-risk NMIBC;
    • High progression risk: variant histology, distal ureter and/or prostatic urethral involvement, LVI,
      deep lamina propria invasion, persistent HG T1 at re-resection;
    • Disease not amenable to complete resection (size, volume, access).

 Concept — The case for early cystectomy rests on an asymmetry: clinical T1 is systematically understaged,
 and disease that progresses under surveillance does worse than de novo muscle invasion — so the cost of
 waiting falls on exactly the patients who cannot afford it.

 SURVEILLANCE AND PREVENTION
   • Among the most expensive cancers per patient, largely from long-term surveillance.

   • Surveillance: cystoscopy + urine cytology, the accepted gold standard despite subjectivity. Adherence
     to risk-stratified schedules is a quality metric, yet fewer than half follow a standard protocol.
   • Tools: AUA/SUO stratification (Table 130.4), EAU stratification, EORTC tables. Tailor to the individual.

 Cystoscopic Surveillance
   • Office flexible cystoscopy: rapid, relatively painless; papillary tumours readily seen; CIS classically a
     velvety red patch (unreliable). High positive predictive value, but appearance cannot reliably predict
     stage or grade; sessile morphology and/or necrosis suggests high-grade, likely invasive disease.
   • Office blue-light flexible cystoscopy detects more recurrences and CIS; requires pre-procedure
     catheterisation and instillation of hexaminolevulinate (Cysview).
   • Comfort: intraurethral anaesthetic gel is near-universal but evidence conflicting; no benefit in women
     (straighter urethra); men watching the monitor report about half the pain.
   • Technique: empty bladder beforehand; 60 mL syringe aspiration via irrigant port to clear clouding;
     systematic survey mandatory. Air cystoscopy aids views in active haematuria or where sterile fluid is
     unavailable.
   • Most predictive for recurrence: tumour at the 3-month cystoscopy and number of tumours at initial
     resection.
   • Low-grade Ta clear at 3 months → annual cystoscopy is safe from 12 months.
   • Duration: AUA — beyond 10 years case-by-case; earlier work suggested stopping at ≥5 years for low
     risk; late high-grade recurrences temper enthusiasm for stopping.

 Extravesical Surveillance
 Upper Tract
   • Upper tract UC after NMIBC is uncommon (registry about 0.8%), rising with risk — low under 1%,
     high risk about 10%, and higher still in very high-risk or BCG-treated populations (13–18%).
     Surveillance is worthwhile mainly with haematuria or high-grade tumour, especially near the ureteric
     orifice. Yield of routine upper tract imaging is very low.
   • AUA: no routine upper tract imaging in asymptomatic low-risk patients; consider every 1–2 years for
     intermediate and high risk (little supporting data).
   • Imaging: multiphasic CT urography preferred; magnetic resonance urography and renal
     ultrasonography in select patients; retrograde pyelography for contrast allergy or renal insufficiency,
     often at TURBT.
   • Upper tract disease, though rare, carries 40–70% mortality. In BCG-treated high-risk disease, risk is
     greatest in the first 5 years (median detection about 5 years) but persists ≥15 years.

 Prostatic Urethra
   • Secondary prostatic urethral/ductal involvement in high-risk NMIBC: 10–15% at 5 years; 20–40% at
     10 years.
   • Refractory disease → prostatic fossa recurrence in about one-third, nearly half of these fatal.
   • Any high-grade prostatic urethral involvement = high risk. Risk factors: CIS, multifocality, bladder
     neck/trigone tumours.
   • Ductal high-grade involvement → radical cystoprostatectomy; consider urethrectomy, especially with
     tumour at or near the margin.
   • Individualise surveillance to bladder and extravesical risk.

 Prevention
   • Smoking cessation counselling is key, though its impact on recurrence is uncertain; increased fluids
     and a low-fat diet are recommended. (Stated in the key points only; no body text in the chapter.)

 PATIENT-FOCUSED OUTCOMES AND ADVOCACY
   • Burden: lifelong endoscopy and repeated instillations → reduced quality of life and financial toxicity,
     plus anxiety about recurrence and progression.
   • CISTO trial (Comparison of Intravesical Therapy and Surgery as Treatment Options): large pragmatic
     observational study of recurrent high-grade NMIBC after BCG failure — quality of life, survival,
     progression, preferences and caregiver burden, using validated patient-reported outcomes.
   • UGN-102 (sustained-release MMC reverse thermal gel): framed as more patient-centred, avoiding the
     urinary, sexual, physical and mental-health decline of repeated TURBT.
   • Bladder Cancer Advocacy Network (BCAN): patient and caregiver support, education, research
     sponsorship, advocacy.
