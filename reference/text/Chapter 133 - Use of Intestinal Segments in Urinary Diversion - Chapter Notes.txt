Chapter 133 — Use of Intestinal Segments in Urinary
Diversion
 • After extirpative surgery or for complex reconstruction, bowel segments restore genitourinary function;
   planning requires knowledge of anatomy and of the physiological consequences of interposing
   gastrointestinal (GI) tissue in the urinary tract.

SURGICAL ANATOMY
 • Ileum and colon are used most often; stomach, jejunum and rectum less commonly. Success depends on
   knowing each segment's anatomy, vascular supply and metabolic function.

Stomach
 • Blood supply is mainly from the coeliac trunk, through three branches:
      ◦ Left gastric (coronary) artery: directly from the coeliac axis → lesser curvature.
      ◦ Hepatic artery → right gastric artery (lesser curve) and gastroduodenal artery (antrum, duodenum) →
        right gastroepiploic artery.
      ◦ Splenic artery → vasa brevia (short gastrics) to fundus and cardia, and the left gastroepiploic artery.
 • Right and left gastroepiploic arteries meet and give collateral flow to the greater curve; on the
   gastroepiploic vessels a gastric pedicle can reach the pelvis. The pedicle is either the entire antrum or a
   wedge of fundus, based on the left or right gastroepiploic artery according to the portion used.
 • The left gastroepiploic artery is occasionally atretic; the right must then be used.
 • A fundal wedge must not include much antrum and must never extend to the pylorus or to the lesser
   curve.
 • On a left gastroepiploic pedicle, the short gastric vessels are ligated along the greater curve proximal to
   the pedicle; the omentum is left on the gastroepiploic vessels to support them, and may be detached
   from the transverse colon along its avascular plane for mobility.
 • After antrectomy, a Billroth I anastomosis restores GI continuity.
 • The thick seromuscular layer separates easily from mucosa, which suits a submucosal ureteric
   reimplantation.

Small Bowel
 • Length about 22 feet (range 15–30 feet); proximal two-fifths jejunum, distal three-fifths ileum, with no
   definite demarcation.
 • Diameter is largest in the duodenum and smallest in the ileum about 12 inches from the ileocaecal
   valve.

 • Arcades (from the superior mesenteric artery) give straight vessels that form an intramural anastomotic
   network.
 • Experimentally up to 15 cm of small bowel survives lateral to a straight vessel, but it is unwise to assume
   more than 8 cm will survive away from one.
 • Transect the mesentery so the segment is fed by an arcade supplied by a palpable artery of substance in
   the base of the pedicle.
 • Two small-bowel regions lie in the pelvis and are exposed to pelvic irradiation — avoid both in the
   irradiated patient:
      ◦ the last 2 inches of terminal ileum, fixed in the pelvis by ligamentous attachments;
      ◦ the 5 feet beginning about 6 feet from the ligament of Treitz, which has the longest mesentery of the
        small bowel and so descends into the pelvis.

Colon
 • Divisions: caecum, ascending, transverse, left (descending), sigmoid colon and rectum; some parts fixed
   or retroperitoneal, others free.
 • Caecum: usually fixed in the right lower quadrant (rarely free and very mobile) by two accessory
   peritoneal bands — one from distal ileum via caecum to the retroperitoneum, one from caecum to the
   lateral posterior abdominal wall.
 • Ascending colon fixed to the right posterior wall; at the hepatic flexure the hepatocolic ligament secures
   it to the liver.
 • Transverse colon free; fixed at the splenic flexure by the phrenocolic ligament; attached to the stomach
   by the gastrocolic omentum.
 • Descending colon fixed laterally; sigmoid may or may not be free; rectosigmoid intraperitoneal cephalad,
   then retroperitoneal, then subperitoneal.
 • Arterial supply: superior mesenteric, inferior mesenteric and internal iliac arteries. Named vessels —
   ileocolic, right colic, middle colic, left colic, sigmoid, superior, middle and inferior haemorrhoidal — join to
   form the marginal artery (arc) of Drummond, which gives leeway in mobilisation.
 • Middle colic: from the first part of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA), ascends the transverse
   mesocolon right of midline.
 • Right colic: usually from the SMA just below the middle colic; variably from the ileocolic or middle colic.
      ◦ If from the ileocolic → a distal ascending colon segment reaches the deep pelvis more easily.
      ◦ If from the middle colic → the right colic may need division at its origin to bring distal ascending colon
        to the pelvis.
 • Ileocolic artery = terminal SMA; supplies the last 6 inches of ileum and the ascending colon.
 • Inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) → left colic, then four to six sigmoid branches; the last becomes the
   superior haemorrhoidal artery, which anastomoses with the middle haemorrhoidal (internal iliac), which
   anastomoses with the inferior haemorrhoidal (terminal internal pudendal). The middle sacral artery
   (from the aorta) may supply the posterior rectum.

 • Anastomoses at these points usually heal with good technique, but site the anastomosis to one side of
   them, where feeding arteries are larger.
 • Mobilisation sequence. Ascending colon: divide caecal and distal ileal attachments → incise the avascular
   line of Toldt (bloodless if the mesentery is not breached). Transverse colon: divide gastrocolic omentum
   (avascular plane) → hepatocolic ligament (may carry small vessels) → phrenocolic ligament. Descending
   colon: incise the line of Toldt lateral to the colon. Further reach comes from isolating a pedicle on a major
   named artery.

 KEY POINTS
   • Clear no more than 8 cm of mesentery from the end of a small-bowel segment, to preserve perfusion
     and avoid necrosis.
   • Avoid anastomosis at the two colonic watershed areas where possible: Griffiths point (splenic flexure)
     and Sudeck's point (rectosigmoid junction).

Concept — Every anatomical rule here is about reach without ischaemia: choose the pedicle that carries a
named vessel, keep the segment's end within perfusion distance of a straight vessel, avoid watershed and
irradiated zones, and exploit variant anatomy (right colic origin, gastroepiploic arcade) to gain length.

SELECTING THE SEGMENT OF INTESTINE
 • Selection depends on the patient's condition, renal function, previous abdominal surgery, mesenteric
   mobility and the type of diversion or substitution required.
 • Neobladder: success hinges on bowel mobility for a tension-free, watertight urethral anastomosis; body
   habitus and mesenteric length decide how far bowel reaches into the pelvis.
 • Manoeuvres to gain reach: caecal mobilisation, mesenteric Z-plasty, use of a discarded segment at the
   bowel anastomosis, and bed flexion to reduce tension.

Stomach
 • Used for bladder replacement, augmentation cystoplasty, conduits and continent diversions.
 • Advantages: less permeable to urinary solutes; net excretion of chloride and protons (not absorption);
   less mucus. Urodynamically it behaves like other segments.
 • Electrolyte imbalance is rare with normal renal function, though hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis
   occurs. Earlier reports of less bacteriuria than ileum or colon are not borne out — more recent data show
   no difference between segments.
 • Urine pH usually 6–7, so no excess peristomal skin problems; urinary pH differs little between gastric and
   ileal augmentation.

 • Serum gastrin usually normal or minimally raised, depending on the portion and amount used;
   experimentally, antral exclusion raises gastrin and causes ulceration.
 • Severe ulcerative complications occur rarely → consider long-term histamine H2-receptor antagonists or
   proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs).
 • Do not use the antrum if the fundus is available; if antrum is used, reconstruct with Billroth I, whose
   complications are well documented.
 • Early complications: gastric retention (atony or anastomotic oedema); haemorrhage (usually
   anastomotic); hiccups from distension; pancreatitis from intraoperative injury; duodenal leak.
 • Delayed complications: dumping, steatorrhoea, small-stomach syndrome, altered transit, bilious
   vomiting, afferent loop syndrome, hypoproteinaemia, megaloblastic or iron-deficiency anaemia.
 • Postoperative bowel obstruction is appreciable in adults but rare in children; gastroduodenal and
   gastroureteric leaks occur, occasionally fatally.
 • Indications: severe renal dysfunction (cannot tolerate metabolic acidosis); risk of short-gut syndrome;
   dense abdominal adhesions (the stomach is usually adhesion-free and easily mobilised).
 • Haematuria–dysuria syndrome is specific to gastric segments and common: bladder spasm, suprapubic,
   penile or periurethral pain, gross haematuria or dysuria without infection, skin excoriation. Intermittent,
   self-limiting, controlled with H2 blockers. Attributed to gastric acid, though it occurs even with normal
   urine acidity.
 • Severe metabolic alkalosis with respiratory distress occurs with gastric augments or conduits (see
   Metabolic Complications).

Jejunum
 • Usually avoided: causes severe electrolyte imbalance, most concerningly hyperkalaemia.
 • Diseases that preclude ileum usually preclude jejunum. If it is the only segment available, use as distal a
   segment as possible.

Ileum and Colon
 • Most often used, in every type of reconstruction.
 • Ileum: mobile, small diameter, constant blood supply; good for ureteric replacement and conduits.
      ◦ Losing significant ileum → vitamin B12 malabsorption, diarrhoea from bile-salt loss, fat
        malabsorption.
      ◦ Excess mesenteric fat or a short mesentery can hinder mobility or pelvic reach.
      ◦ Postoperative bowel obstruction up to 10%; about half occur early.
 • Colon: must be mobilised from fixed positions; larger diameter; reaches anywhere in abdomen or pelvis.
      ◦ After pelvic irradiation, right, transverse and descending colon are outside the field and can be used
        confidently.
      ◦ Fewer nutritional problems than ileum — provided the ileocaecal valve is preserved; using the valve
        → diarrhoea, bacterial overgrowth of ileum with malabsorption, fluid and bicarbonate loss.
      ◦ Bowel obstruction requiring reoperation 5% — about half the ileal rate.
      ◦ Submucosal-tunnel antireflux ureteric anastomosis is easier in colon.
 • Both produce the same electrolyte disturbance at similar frequency; overall they are comparable, and
   neither is strongly preferred except in special circumstances.

 KEY POINTS
   • Stomach: ulcerative complications and haematuria–dysuria syndrome, but net excretion of H+ and Cl−.
   • Jejunum: severe electrolyte imbalance, most concerningly hyperkalaemia — avoid if possible.
   • Excluding significant ileum → vitamin B12 deficiency and reduced bile-salt reabsorption.
   • Colon has a lower rate of obstruction requiring reoperation than ileum.

Concept — Segment choice is a trade between what the bowel does to the urine (stomach secretes acid,
jejunum loses salt, ileum and colon absorb ammonium chloride) and what its removal does to the gut; the
stomach and jejunum are reserved for patients in whom the standard ileum or colon would fail on one of
those two counts.

PREPARING THE BOWEL FOR SURGERY AND POSTOPERATIVE RECOVERY
 • Complications from bacterial contamination are a major cause of morbidity and mortality.
   Experimentally, an anastomosis with vascular compromise that would otherwise perforate heals if the
   bowel has been antibiotic-prepared; solid faeces strain an early anastomosis and can cause ischaemia
   and perforation.
 • Faecal-contamination complications after radical cystectomy: wound infection, peritonitis, intra-
   abdominal abscess, wound and anastomotic dehiscence, systemic sepsis. Historically common, they have
   fallen substantially with current practice, though infection of some kind remains frequent after
   cystectomy — urinary infection most often, then superficial wound infection and bacteraemia.
 • Bacterial load: stomach low; jejunum, ileum and colon high. Early studies linked unprepared bowel with
   more wound infection, abscess and dehiscence, and mechanically collapsed bowel with fewer leaks.
 • Meta-analyses of randomised trials in elective open colorectal surgery do not support mechanical
   preparation for reducing leak or other complications. Small randomised studies of cystectomy with ileal
   conduit show no difference in infection with or without preparation — but they excluded prior
   radiotherapy, immunosuppression, recent antibiotics, and liver or renal dysfunction.
 • Normal flora: aerobes — Escherichia coli and Enterococcus faecalis; anaerobes — Bacteroides and
   Clostridium.

Mechanical Bowel Preparation
 • Reduces total bacteria, not concentration; some analyses even suggest more contamination after
   preparation, perhaps from liquefied stool.
 • Conventional 3-day preparations exhausted patients and worsened nutrition through low calorie intake.
 • Elemental diets failed: they neither empty the colon nor reduce flora.
 • Whole-gut irrigation: nasogastric tube (NGT) infusion of 9–12 L lactated Ringer's or saline over several
   hours; later 10% mannitol, which cleaned equally well but fed bacterial growth.
      ◦ Exhausting; may cause fluid gain (saline, mannitol).
      ◦ Contraindicated in unstable cardiovascular status, cirrhosis, severe renal disease, congestive heart
        failure, bowel obstruction.
      ◦ No better than conventional preparation for wound or septic complications, despite fewer aerobes.
 • Largely replaced by polyethylene glycol (PEG)–electrolyte solution.
 • Evidence against: limited preparations (half-volume PEG with metoclopramide; sodium phosphate) match
   full PEG; two meta-analyses found more anastomotic dehiscence with mechanical preparation (PEG
   possibly responsible); a meta-analysis of randomised trials of preparation versus none in elective
   colorectal surgery found no difference in leak, abscess or wound sepsis.
 • No adequate study for laparoscopic or robotic surgery.
 • Caveats: in these studies intravenous antibiotics were crucial; and colorectal resections involve limited
   bowel exposure, unlike urological procedures, where long segments are opened into a normally faeces-
   free urinary tract.

Antibiotic Bowel Preparation
 • Oral antibiotic plus mechanical preparation dates from the 1970s; early series showed large reductions in
   septic complications, wound infection and mortality. Antibiotics protect vulnerable bowel, letting a
   tenuous anastomosis survive.
 • Contrary data: without oral antibiotics, septic rates can be comparable and Clostridioides (Clostridium)
   difficile colitis is less frequent.
 • Of little value in bowel obstruction — they do not sterilise obstructed bowel.
 • Disadvantages: postoperative diarrhoea; pseudomembranous enterocolitis; theoretical tumour
   implantation at the suture line (not relevant to urology); candidal overgrowth (stomatitis, thrush,
   diarrhoea); with prolonged use, protein, carbohydrate and fat malabsorption.
 • Agents: kanamycin (best single agent); neomycin + erythromycin base; neomycin + metronidazole.

 • Recent evidence: a randomised trial found the lowest faecal counts with mechanical preparation + oral
   neomycin + synbiotic, but no difference in clinical infection; a large colectomy series and a cohort of left-
   colon and rectal resections showed fewer surgical site infections (SSIs), less C. difficile, fewer organ-
   space infections and leaks when oral antibiotics were added to mechanical preparation (with intravenous
   antibiotics and chlorhexidine in both arms).
 • Perioperative intravenous antibiotics are the most important measure:
      ◦ must be given before the operative event — ideally 1–2 hours before incision;
      ◦ most effective against anaerobes, so the agent must cover anaerobes;
      ◦ third-generation cephalosporins advocated; combined oral and intravenous prophylaxis supported;
        some stop oral agents for fear of C. difficile.
 • Authors' protocol. Normal diet → day before surgery: clear liquids + magnesium citrate + oral neomycin
   → sports-drink hydration until 2 hours before surgery → intravenous antibiotics 1 hour before incision,
   per American Urological Association (AUA) guidance.

Wound Protectors
 • Physical barrier between wound edges and viscera or instruments in clean-contaminated bowel surgery;
   limit bacterial inoculation and desiccation of wound edges.
 • Randomised trials and meta-analyses show fewer SSIs in urological and colorectal procedures.

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery
 • Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS): evidence-based perioperative pathways that preserve organ
   function and blunt the surgical stress response — counselling, nutritional optimisation, standardised
   analgesia and anaesthesia, early mobilisation. Avoidance of routine bowel preparation is a key
   component.
 • Originated in colorectal surgery in the 1990s (Henrik Kehlet); level 1 evidence there for fewer
   complications and shorter stay; since adopted across specialties, most recently urology.
 • In urology, best studied in radical cystectomy with diversion. Bowel complications drive prolonged stay;
   postoperative ileus (POI) is the commonest complication.
 • Cystectomy patients are at higher POI risk than colectomy patients: prolonged pelvic deperitonealisation
   during lymphadenectomy, bowel exposure to urine, longer operations, older and more comorbid patients,
   more smokers.
 • POI definition varies; usually prolonged intolerance of oral intake without mechanical obstruction.
 • Overall evidence supports ERAS in well-selected cystectomy patients; comparison is hampered by varied
   protocols and endpoints, and by cultural, expectation and reimbursement effects on stay.
 • Alvimopan: peripherally acting µ-opioid receptor antagonist; blocks opioid binding in the gut, speeding
   upper and lower GI recovery; limited blood–brain barrier penetration, so central analgesia is preserved.
   Indicated after surgery including bowel resection; inpatient use only (US Food and Drug Administration,
   FDA).
      ◦ Dosing: 12 mg 30 minutes to 5 hours before surgery, then twice daily from the day after surgery until
        bowel function returns, maximum 7 days (15 doses).
      ◦ Value should persist whenever opioids are still being given, even within ERAS.
 • ERAS has not universally shortened bowel recovery or stay, but in some series improved opioid use, time
   to diet, intensive care unit (ICU) time and early quality of life.
 • Component findings: chewing gum and metoclopramide aid return of bowel function; early enteral
   nutrition does not prevent postoperative protein depletion (total protein, albumin, lymphocyte count).

   • Antibiotic preparation decreases the bacterial concentration per millilitre of faeces.
   • Whether to give mechanical and/or antibiotic preparation should weigh risks and benefits for each
     patient.
   • Alvimopan: peripheral µ-opioid antagonist in the gut; limited blood–brain barrier crossing preserves
     central analgesia.

Concept — The evidence against bowel preparation comes from colorectal resections in healthy,
unirradiated patients under intravenous antibiotic cover; urinary diversion opens long segments into a
sterile tract, so the colorectal conclusion transfers only to the selected cancer-cystectomy patient, and
intravenous anaerobic cover before incision is the one non-negotiable.

INTESTINAL ANASTOMOSES
 • In urological bowel surgery, the commonest cause of early postoperative morbidity and mortality is
   bowel complications — at the enteroenterostomy or the interposed segment.
 • Bowel work often follows a long extirpation by a tired team, so the principles must be ingrained, not
   recalled.

Surgical Principles
 • 1. Adequate exposure. Mobilise so the anastomosis is done without struggle — ideally on the anterior
   abdominal wall; wall off with Mikulicz pads so spills are contained. Clear mesentery about 0.5 cm from
   clamps or staple line for serosal apposition without interposed mesentery.
 • 2. Good blood supply to the cut ends. Compromised by tension, excess dissection, excess electrocautery,
   over-tight sutures. A pink, freely bleeding margin indicates adequate supply (secure haemostasis before
   anastomosing).
      ◦ Sequence: transilluminate the mesentery → choose a site where good arcades supply both ends,
        allowing for the depth of mesenteric division needed → clear mesentery from serosa → divide
        between mosquito clamps → tie with 4-0 silk (or LDS stapler, bipolar device, ultrasonic shears).
 • 3. Prevent local spillage. Best achieved with prepared (faeces-free, collapsed) bowel.
      ◦ Sequence: strip contents away from the transection site → non-crushing occlusive (linen-shod) clamp
        that spares mesenteric flow → wall off → Allen clamps → transect between them (or transect with a
        stapler).
      ◦ Irrigate the isolated segment with copious saline, walled off, into a kidney dish until the efflux is clear.
 • 4. Accurate serosa-to-serosa apposition; watertight, tension-free, gentle handling with non-crushing
   forceps. Invert, do not evert: everted anastomoses can heal, but under marginal conditions an inverted
   anastomosis is more likely to hold.
 • 5. Do not strangulate tissue with sutures. Non-absorbable sutures give a slightly stronger early
   anastomosis than absorbable ones, but the difference is minimal.
 • 6. Realign the mesentery parallel, with no twist. Closing the mesenteric defect is classically
   recommended but debated; bariatric data conflict on internal hernia, and there is little direct evidence in
   urinary diversion.
 • Factors causing anastomotic breakdown: poor blood supply, local sepsis from faecal spillage (both via
   ischaemia), drains placed on an intra-abdominal anastomosis, irradiated bowel.

Complications of Intestinal Anastomoses
 • Leak, sepsis, wound infection, abscess, haemorrhage, stenosis, colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie
   syndrome) and obstruction — major contributors to morbidity and mortality.
 • In prepared bowel with contemporary technique, leak, haemorrhage and stenosis are uncommon and
   rarely fatal.
 • Leaks are diagnosed late — typically in the second postoperative week, occasionally after 30 days.
 • Many complications of radical cystectomy are attributable to the bowel segment.

Fistulas
 • Faecal or urinary; usually within the first several weeks; frequently cause sepsis, which carries mortality.
 • Neobladder–vaginal fistula after orthotopic diversion in women presents as continuous incontinence
   several months after surgery. Vaginal-sparing cystectomy reduces it by shortening opposing suture lines.

Sepsis and Other Infectious Complications
 • Wound complications are common; dehiscence and pelvic abscess are rare. Septicaemia after cystectomy
   is uncommon but carries substantial mortality; cumulatively, a large proportion of patients need
   treatment for infection after diversion.
 • Prevention: Mikulicz pads, irrigation of the segment until clear, avoid faecal spillage, wash out abdomen
   and wound.

Bowel Obstruction
 • Obstruction requiring treatment ~10% with stomach or ileum; ~5% requiring operation with colon (Table
   133.8). Half occur early; obstruction beyond 6 months is much less frequent.
 • Morbid: a significant number of ileal-conduit patients needing operation for obstruction die.
 • Causes: adhesions (commonest), then recurrent cancer — the great majority. Volvulus and internal
   hernia are rare. Anastomotic stenosis (oedema, poor technique, ischaemic bowel) or obstruction
   (improper technique) occasionally.
 • Prevention: nonirradiated, well-vascularised bowel; close all apertures; reperitonealise the segment;
   adequate GI decompression; omentum over the anastomosis; reconstitute the pelvic floor after
   exenteration. Orthotopic neobladders cause fewer obstructions than conduits or cutaneous reservoirs.
 • Reperitonealisation. Tack the antimesenteric border to the lateral sidewall peritoneum and the proximal
   mesenteric border to the posterior parietal peritoneum — an unobliterated space has trapped bowel.
 • Pelvic floor. After anterior exenteration, lay the sigmoid in the pelvis, with omentum filling any residual
   space. After total exenteration (insufficient sigmoid, thin omentum) — especially if postoperative pelvic
   radiotherapy is planned — use polyglactin mesh: sutured posteriorly to the sacral promontory and
   presacral fascia, laterally to the iliac adventitia, anterolaterally to peritoneum two-thirds of the way from
   pubis to umbilicus, then covered with omentum. Excludes bowel from the pelvis for 4–6 weeks of
   irradiation.

Complications of Intestinal Stomas
 • The single most common postoperative problem after diversion; at some point virtually every patient
   has one.
 • Early: bowel necrosis, bleeding, dermatitis, parastomal hernia, prolapse, obstruction, retraction, stenosis.
 • Reduced by: correct construction; enterostomal therapist review; proper skin products and non-irritant
   adhesives; keeping urine in the bag acidic; well-fitting appliances.
 • Parastomal skin lesions: irritative (hypo-/hyperpigmentation, atrophy); erythematous erosive (macules,
   scaling, epidermal loss); pseudoverrucous (wart-like).
 • Stomal stenosis: about 20–24% of ileal conduits and 10–20% of colon conduits; falling with better care
   and appliances; less with loop than end stomas. Catheterisable stomas in children stenose very
   frequently (over half).
 • Umbilical stomas: some report more complications, others excellent results with the concealed umbilical
   stoma.
 • Bleeding, stenosis and dermatitis respond to skin care and a well-fitted appliance on a protruding stoma;
   the rest are prevented by technique.
 • Parastomal hernia after ileal conduit: 2–6.6%; open repair effective; laparoscopic repair has mixed
   results, including high early failure with expanded polytetrafluoroethylene mesh in one series.
 • Massive conduit bleeding: usually stomal/conduit varices from hepatic dysfunction and portal
   hypertension → transhepatic portosystemic shunt, or transhepatic angiography with embolisation.

 KEY POINTS
   • Anastomotic principles: (1) exposure, (2) blood supply, (3) minimal spillage, (4) serosa to serosa, (5)
     no strangulation, (6) mesenteric alignment.
   • Sutured and stapled anastomoses have similar complication rates when properly performed.
   • The stapled side-to-side ileoileal anastomosis is the most commonly performed, for simplicity and
     reliability.
   • Stomal complications are mostly technical and are the most common problem after diversion.
   • All stomas through the belly of the rectus to minimise parastomal hernia.

Concept — Stoma design is an exchange between stenosis and hernia: protrusion and the loop configuration
protect against stenosis and skin injury, but the wider loop aperture invites herniation — which is why the
loop is kept for the obese patient in whom an end stoma cannot be built.

URETEROINTESTINAL ANASTOMOSES
 • The ureter joins colon or small bowel in a refluxing or nonrefluxing fashion; which is preferable is
   controversial.
 • Over the long term, renal failure affects a meaningful minority regardless of diversion type, and upper-
   tract deterioration after conduits is reported in 10–60%.
 • Causes of upper-tract deterioration: lack of ureteric motility, infection, stones; less commonly
   obstruction at the anastomosis.
 • Because bacteriuria is almost universal in conduits and intestine may promote colonisation, many argued
   that nonrefluxing anastomoses would protect the kidney.

 • Conduit pressure is not transmitted to the renal pelvis (ileum or colon): ureteric peristalsis damps

 • Antireflux does not prevent renal pelvic colonisation (positive pelvic aspirates despite documented
   absence of reflux).
 • Advantage of reflux in urothelial cancer: the upper tracts can be imaged by instilling contrast into the
   conduit.
 • Conclusion: reflux + impaired peristalsis + bacteriuria or obstruction → renal deterioration; reflux with a
   normal, unobstructed ureter has not been shown to harm the adult kidney.
 • General principles (all types):
      ◦ Mobilise only as much ureter as needed — no redundancy, no tension.
      ◦ Preserve periadventitial tissue (it carries the blood supply); clear adventitia only for the distal 2–3
        mm.
      ◦ Fine absorbable sutures; watertight mucosa-to-mucosa apposition.
      ◦ Bring the bowel to the ureter, not the ureter to the bowel.
      ◦ Fix the bowel near the anastomosis; retroperitonealise it or cover with a peritoneal pedicle flap.

Ureterocolonic Anastomoses
Combined Technique of Leadbetter and Clarke
 • Nonrefluxing, submucosal tunnel: combines Nesbit's refluxing elliptical anastomosis with Coffey's
   tunnel.
 • Sequence: incise anterior taenia obliquely 2.5–3 cm, near the mesenteric border → dissect mucosa off
   muscularis for the incision's length → excise a mucosal button at the distal end → spatulate ureter 5–7
   mm → mucosa to mucosa with 5-0 polydioxanone (PDS), interrupted (knots outside) or running from the
   apex → loosely close seromuscular layer so the ureter lies "as a hammock" uncompressed → fix bowel to
   peritoneum, no ureteric tension.
 • Complications: leak 2.5%; upper-tract deterioration 4.3–25%; stricture 8–14%.

Transcolonic Technique of Goodwin
 • Nonrefluxing submucosal tunnel, performed from inside the bowel (open colotomy).
 • Sequence: loose non-crushing clamp cephalad (bowel in continuity) → anterior vertical colotomy → 0.5-
   cm posterior mucosal incision → hemostat tunnels submucosally, medial to lateral, 3–4 cm → out
   through serosa → draw ureter in by traction suture → bring both ureters in before suturing either → pass
   a No. 5 feeding tube to exclude kinking → excise redundant ureter, spatulate, interrupted 5-0 PDS to
   mucosa including some muscularis → Silastic stents → ureteric adventitia to colonic serosa at entry (4-0
   PDS in the text; interrupted 5-0 PDS in the figure legend) → close colotomy in two layers.
 • Results satisfactory; no reliable complication data.

Small Bowel Anastomoses
 • Two basic types: end to side (refluxing or nonrefluxing) and end to end.

Bricker Anastomosis
 • Refluxing end-to-side; simple, low complication rate; usable in any suitable segment.
 • Original: silk adventitia-to-serosa posterior layer → mucosal plug removed → full-thickness ureter to
   mucosa (chromic) → anterior silk layer.
 • Modern: excise a small full-thickness button → spatulate 0.5 cm → full-thickness ureter to full-thickness
   bowel with 5-0 PDS → soft Silastic stent.
 • Stricture 4–22% (average 6%); leak ~3% without stents; open and robotic stricture rates comparable.

Wallace Technique
 • Refluxing end-to-end: the ureters are joined and sutured to the end of the bowel (small bowel or colon).
 • Three variants:
      ◦ Side-by-side: apices joined, posteromedial walls sutured together, anterolateral walls sutured to
        bowel.

      ◦ Y-type: posterior then anterior walls sutured together; the composite end sewn to bowel.
      ◦ Head-to-tail: end of one ureter to the apex of the other; posteromedial walls joined; anterolateral
        walls to bowel.
 • Spatulate 1.5–2 cm; 5-0 PDS; horizontal mattress where suture lines meet the bowel for watertightness.
 • Lowest complication rate of any ureterointestinal technique: stricture ~3%, deterioration ~4%, leak ~2%.
 • Historically avoided in extensive carcinoma in situ (CIS) or likely ureteric recurrence (one tumour could
   block both ureters) — but this is exceedingly rare.
 • Easier endoscopic surveillance (both orifices side by side at the proximal loop) and lower obstruction
   rate than Bricker.

Complications of Ureterointestinal Anastomoses
 • Leak, stricture, reflux (in antireflux anastomoses) and pyelonephritis.
 • Best antireflux options: colon — Pagano technique (lowest stricture, acceptable reflux); small bowel — Le
   Duc and ureterointestinal serosal apposition (lowest stricture, best reflux prevention). For stricture and
   leak overall, Wallace is best.

Urinary Fistula
 • Usually days 7–10; 3–9%; markedly reduced by soft Silastic stents. A leak causes periureteric fibrosis →
   later stricture.

Stricture
 • Antirefluxing techniques stricture more. Risk is lifelong (reported at 13 years) → scheduled lifelong
   surveillance.
 • Strictures away from the anastomosis: typically the left ureter where it crosses the aorta beneath the
   IMA — from over-stripping of adventitia and angulation at the IMA.
 • Treatment: open re-exploration, excision and reanastomosis is most successful and durable, though
   morbid; balloon dilatation is not durable; cold-knife or laser endoincision succeeds in about half to
   three-fifths at 2 years (worse with longer follow-up).
 • Poor endourological outcome: stricture within 1 year of surgery, ≥1.5 cm long, or left-sided.
 • Metal stents: for limited life expectancy; use with caution — a metallic ureteric stent series showed poor
   early patency; self-expanding metal alloy stents did better in small benign series (one stent encrustation).
   Long-term data needed.

Pyelonephritis
 • Early and late; about 10–20% after ileal conduits and lower after antirefluxing colon conduits (Table 133.8
   gives similar rates for both).

 • Significant mortality, including delayed septic deaths years after diversion in children and adults; sepsis
   with falling renal function and uraemia markedly raises morbidity and mortality. Multidrug-resistant
   organisms are a growing threat.
 • Rates across anastomotic types cannot be compared inclusively for reflux or leak; complications are
   minimised by the principles above.

 KEY POINTS
   • Complications: leak, stricture, reflux (in antireflux anastomoses), pyelonephritis.
   • Stents markedly reduce urinary intestinal leak.
   • Antirefluxing techniques stricture more than refluxing ones.
   • Wallace has the lowest complication rate.
   • Long term, most patients show progressive renal deterioration regardless of diversion type — from
     poor ureteric motility, infection, stones, or less commonly anastomotic stricture.

Concept — The kidney is protected by normal ureteric peristalsis and freedom from obstruction, not by an
antireflux valve; because every tunnel adds stricture risk, a well-made refluxing, stented, tension-free
anastomosis is the default and the antireflux tunnel the exception.

RENAL DETERIORATION
 • Reported after conduit diversion in 10–74% — wide because series mix previously normal and abnormal
   renal units; in abnormal units, diversion and underlying disease cannot be separated.
 • In previously normal units: ileal conduit 18% versus nonrefluxing colon conduit ~13–15% (Table 133.8);
   nonrefluxing continent ileocaecal reservoirs also deteriorate in a minority long term. Consequences
   include azotaemia in children with ileal conduits and renal failure after colon conduits for benign
   disease.
 • Significant, progressive decline in most patients, independent of diversion type.
 • Ureterosigmoidostomy carries more sepsis and renal failure than conduits, early or many years later.
   After more than 15 years, acquired renal disease (sepsis or renal failure) is the commonest cause of
   death (10–22% die of it), some as late as 27 years. About 6% of ileal conduit patients ultimately die of
   renal failure.

Renal Function Necessary for Urinary Intestinal Diversion
 • Required function depends on how much bowel and how long urine dwells → continent (retentive)
   diversions need more renal function than short conduits.
 • GFR (glomerular filtration rate) above 40 mL/min on average → continent diversion tolerated reasonably
   well.
 • GFR is not the sole determinant — distal tubular function is equally important: patients with moderately
   reduced GFR but intact acidification on ammonium chloride loading tolerated continent diversion with
   little acidosis.
 • Five components of renal function: renal blood flow, glomerular filtration, tubular transport,
   concentration and dilution, glomerular permeability.

URINARY DIVERSION
 • Two fundamental conduit types: small bowel (jejunum or ileum) and large bowel. Gastric conduits are
   rarely indicated and cause difficult stomal problems; not described.
 • Indications for a conduit: after cystectomy; a diseased bladder; before transplantation when the bladder
   cannot receive the transplant ureter; dysfunctional bladders causing persistent bleeding, ureteric
   obstruction, poor compliance with upper-tract deterioration, or inadequate storage with total
   incontinence.
 • Complication rates depend on follow-up length and the concomitant procedure. Orthotopic neobladders
   are covered in a separate chapter.

Ileal Conduit
 • Simplest conduit, with the fewest intraoperative and early postoperative complications.
 • Avoid ileum in short bowel syndrome, inflammatory small-bowel disease, and extensively irradiated
   ileum (usually after pelvic radiotherapy).

Procedure
 • Sequence: select a 10–15 cm segment 10–15 cm from the ileocaecal valve → mobilise caecum and ileal
   appendage (distal ileum fixed to retroperitoneum) → transilluminate, identify a major arcade → vessel
   loops beneath the bowel and at the mesenteric base to one side of the feeding vessel → incise
   peritoneum on both sides of the mesentery between loops → clamp, divide, tie with 4-0 silk → clear 2 cm
   of mesentery from bowel → repeat at the other end → wide mesenteric base; windows about 5 cm, not
   excessive (ischaemia) → Allen clamps angled so the antimesenteric side is shorter (triangle of bowel
   discarded), or staple.
 • Segment placed caudal to the ileoileostomy (Figure 133.34) → close the mesenteric window with 3-0 silk
   → flush segment until clear.
 • Ureters. Brought out of the retroperitoneum in the right lower quadrant; the left ureter passes over the
   great vessels, behind the sigmoid mesentery. Caecum mobilised cephalad to find the right ureter; line of

   the renal pelvis. After cystectomy, trace the ureters up from below the iliac vessels.
 • Stent the anastomoses — conveniently via a Yankauer sucker with its tip removed, passed from the
   distal end and cut down upon at the anastomotic site (Figure 133.36).
 • Fix the conduit base to the retroperitoneum in the right lower quadrant, retroperitonealising the

 • Authors suture the loop to the lateral peritoneal wall (no lateral internal hernia); many bring it straight to

 • Robotic and laparoscopic ileal conduits: similar 90-day complication rates in experienced hands.

Complications
 • Hard to attribute solely to the conduit (most series include cystectomy) — published rates represent the
   high end.
 • Bleeding from stoma or conduit: stomal bleeding in about 10%, a minority from the loop beneath the
   fascia. Intractable bleeding → suspect cirrhosis with varices; may be life-threatening → portal
   decompression (percutaneous transhepatic portal shunt, or transhepatic angiography with embolisation).
 • Hypertension, renal failure, declining function and death depend on the concomitant procedure, follow-
   up and pre-diversion renal status. Over 20 years, a minority need dialysis but most show upper-tract
   deterioration.
 • Salvage cystectomy: acceptable morbidity but higher complication rates than other cystectomy series.
 • Higher complications when an intestinal segment is used in patients who later need renal
   transplantation.
 • Duplex ureters: missing a second ureter → intraperitoneal urine leak and excessive morbidity. Implant
   separately if calibre allows, or spatulate, sew together and implant as one (Figure 133.37).

Jejunal Conduit
 • Jejunum has the largest small-bowel diameter and longest mesentery. Little used because of electrolyte
   abnormalities, though long-term data suggest these are mostly minor, with only a small minority
   developing severe hyponatraemic metabolic acidosis; renal calculi, parastomal hernia and pyelonephritis
   make up most other complications.
 • Advantage: avoids irradiated bowel and ureter. Justified only when ileum and colon are both unsuitable:
   extensive ileal irradiation; dense ileal adhesions with absent large bowel; absent colon with inflammatory
   disease of distal small bowel.
 • Contraindications: severe bowel nutritional disorders; another acceptable segment available.

Procedure
 • As for ileal conduit: 10–15 cm segment isolated 15–25 cm from the ligament of Treitz; plan the stoma in
   the left upper quadrant.

 • Ileocaecal. Place caudad → ileum–ascending colon anastomosis → stoma right lower quadrant →
   optionally reinforce the ileocaecal valve against reflux.

Complications
 • Death, renal failure and deterioration again depend on the concomitant procedure and follow-up. Early
   reports suggested less renal deterioration with colon conduits; recent series suggest about the same as
   ileal conduits, though long-term pyelonephritis is low and most upper tracts are preserved.
 • Ileocaecal versus ileal conduit: no difference in early or late complications or in upper-tract deterioration.
   Early: leak, obstruction, faecal leak, renal failure, fulminant hepatitis, pneumonia, GI bleeding, ileal
   perforation, heart failure, dehiscence. Late: stomal prolapse and stenosis, pyelonephritis, obstruction,
   stones, parastomal and incisional hernia, faecal leak.
 • Ileocaecal conduits reflux at high pressure, minimally at low pressure.

 • Any colon conduit may cause chronic diarrhoea.

Ileal Vesicostomy
 • Spatulated ileum on a generous transverse cystotomy, with a distal rosebud stoma → low-pressure
   reservoir draining to an abdominal appliance.
 • For spinal cord injury or significant neurological disease; best candidates have detrusor–external
   sphincter dyssynergia. Detrusor hyperreflexia (neurogenic detrusor overactivity), particularly in women,
   raises the risk of urethral incontinence.
 • Complications: urethral incontinence requiring urethral closure in a significant minority; stomal stenosis;
   bladder and renal calculi.
 • Reversible — anatomy can later be restored.

Management Common to All Conduits
 • All anastomoses stented with Silastic stents, removed individually on postoperative days 4–6; if drainage
   does not rise, remove the Jackson-Pratt closed-suction drain.
 • All conduits are retroperitonealised: posterior peritoneum sutured to conduit serosa above the
   anastomosis.
 • Drain (Jackson-Pratt or Blake) in the retroperitoneum 3–4 cm from the anastomosis; never drain the
   peritoneal cavity.

 KEY POINTS
   • Salvage cystectomy: acceptable morbidity, higher complication rates.
   • Ileal conduit: simplest, fewest intraoperative and early complications.
   • Transverse colon: least likely colonic segment to have been irradiated.
   • Sigmoid conduit: good choice at pelvic exenteration with colostomy — no bowel anastomosis.

Concept — Conduit choice follows the radiation field and the bowel already being sacrificed: ileum by
default, transverse colon when the pelvis is irradiated, sigmoid when a colostomy is being made anyway,
jejunum only when nothing else remains.

METABOLIC AND NEUROMECHANICAL PROBLEMS OF URINARY INTESTINAL
DIVERSION
 • Three categories: metabolic (altered solute reabsorption from the contained urine), neuromechanical
   (gut configuration affecting storage volume and contraction), and technical–surgical (covered with each
   procedure).

Metabolic Complications
 • Spectrum: electrolyte abnormalities, altered sensorium, abnormal drug metabolism, osteomalacia,
   growth retardation, persistent and recurrent infection, renal and reservoir calculi, consequences of
   removing gut, and urothelial or intestinal cancer.
 • Determinants of solute absorption: segment used, surface area, urine dwell time, urinary solute
   concentration, renal function, pH.

Stomach.
 • Hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis — usually trivial unless renal failure (impaired
   bicarbonate excretion) or marked dehydration.
 • Syndrome of severe metabolic alkalosis (occurs even with normal renal function): lethargy, respiratory
   insufficiency, seizures, ventricular arrhythmias; usually preceded by vomiting and dehydration.
 • Treatment ladder: H2 blocker + rehydration → life-threatening: arginine hydrochloride infusion → H2
   failure: omeprazole → persistent: remove the gastric segment.
 • Gastrin is pivotal: serum gastrin correlates with bicarbonate in gastrocystoplasty patients. Vicious circle:
   gastric proton loss adds bicarbonate → vomiting causes volume depletion, hypochloraemia, hypokalaemia
   → aldosterone rises → the kidney cannot excrete bicarbonate → alkalosis worsens.
 • Sigmoid gastrin–bicarbonate curve (Figure 133.40): over normal gastrin 10–120 ng/L, bicarbonate barely
   changes; above 120 ng/L, small gastrin rises cause large bicarbonate rises; below 100 ng/L, gastrin can
   rise considerably with little effect.
 • Distension stimulates gastrin, so failure to empty raises risk. Patients with resting gastrin >120 ng/L or
   impaired renal function must be warned about dehydration and overdistension.
Jejunum.
 • Especially with proximal jejunum: hyponatraemia, hypochloraemia, hyperkalaemia, azotaemia, acidosis.
 • Mechanism (cycle): jejunum secretes NaCl (with water) and reabsorbs K+ and H+ → dehydration,
   hypovolaemia → renin–aldosterone rises (also driven by hyperkalaemia) → kidney retains Na+, excretes

   → perpetuation.
 • Symptoms: lethargy, nausea, vomiting, dehydration, weakness, fever; untreated → moribund → death.

Ileum and colon.
 • Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis occurs to some degree in most patients, usually minor and of
   unclear significance; a minority need treatment and few need hospitalisation. Severe disturbance is far
   commoner with ureterosigmoidostomy (most patients) than with conduits or cystoplasties.
 • Continent ileocaecal or caecal reservoirs: most have raised chloride and low bicarbonate; most Mainz
   pouch patients need alkali. Early reports of ileal continent diversions showed far fewer problems.
 • Severe symptoms: fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, polydipsia, lethargy; ureterosigmoidostomy also
   worsens diarrhoea. Severe disturbance can be fatal.
 • Mechanism: ionised ammonium transport — NH4+ substitutes for Na+ on the Na+/H+ antiport, coupled
   with Cl−/HCO3− exchange → ammonium chloride absorbed in exchange for carbonic acid (CO2 + water).
   Ammonium also enters via potassium channels.

 • Renal function tests invalidated by the bowel: water-deprivation osmolality (bowel re-equilibrates

Altered Sensorium
 • Causes: magnesium deficiency (nutritional or renal wasting, like calcium), drug intoxication, abnormal
   ammonia metabolism (commonest). Hyperglycaemia without glucosuria occurs in diabetics (urinary
   glucose reabsorbed) but is not caused by the diversion.
 • Ammoniagenic coma: in cirrhosis, altered liver function, and even apparently normal liver function; most
   commonly with reduced liver function and ureterosigmoidostomy, also ileal conduits.
 • Treatment: drain the diversion (rectal tube for ureterosigmoidostomy, Foley for continent reservoirs) →
   oral neomycin → restrict protein → severe: intravenous arginine glutamate 50 g in 1000 mL 5% dextrose
   (glutamine substrate) → lactulose oral or rectal (binds gut ammonia).

Abnormal Drug Absorption
 • At risk: drugs absorbed by the gut and excreted unchanged by the kidney — re-absorbed from the
   segment → toxicity. Reported: phenytoin, some antibiotics, methotrexate (ileal conduit).
 • Cisplatin, methotrexate and vinblastine are generally tolerated with normal renal function, though toxicity
   trended higher in continent diversions.
 • Monitor antimetabolite levels; drain continent pouches during chemotherapy.

Osteomalacia
 • Reduced mineralised bone, excess osteoid (renal rickets). Reported after colocystoplasty, ileal ureter,
   colon and ileal conduits — most commonly ureterosigmoidostomy.

Growth and Development
 • Long-term follow-up of myelodysplasia patients: diversion reduced linear growth (significant for
   biacromial span and elbow–hand length).
 • Versus ileal-conduit patients, those on intermittent catheterisation with bladders retained had fewer
   fractures, malunions and non-unions; more diverted patients fell below the 10th percentile, yet height
   and weight did not differ.
 • Experimentally, ureterosigmoidostomy shortened femoral length.
 • Diversion in childhood maintained >10 years → significant linear-growth changes on careful study,
   though not obvious in most.

Infection
 • More bacteriuria, bacteraemia and sepsis; colocystoplasty more than ileocystoplasty. Acute
   pyelonephritis occurs in a substantial minority of conduits, and some die of sepsis.
 • About three-quarters of ileal conduit urines contain bacteria; many are colonised only distally (proximal
   double-catheter cultures are far less often positive) and do well.
 • Treat pure cultures of Proteus or Pseudomonas (predict upper-tract deterioration); observe mixed
   cultures if asymptomatic.
 • Continent diversions: frequent bacteriuria (about two-thirds of Kock pouches) and sepsis.
 • Why: intestine cannot inhibit bacterial proliferation (unlike urothelium), may make urine less
   bacteriostatic, and distension promotes translocation; mucosal immune defences are largely preserved.

Stones
 • Mostly calcium, magnesium and ammonium phosphate (infection stones).
 • Highest risk: hyperchloraemic acidosis, pre-existing pyelonephritis, urease-producing infection.
 • Incidence: colon conduit lowest (3–4%), ileal conduit higher (10–12%), continent caecal reservoirs
   highest (reservoir calculi ~20%).
 • Causes: infection with alkaline urine; hypercalciuria; altered urinary solutes; foreign bodies (staples, non-
   absorbable sutures) — a major cause; mucus as nidus; mucosal change impairing emptying.

Cancer
 • Ureterosigmoidostomy: cancer in 6–29% (mean 11%), after a 10–20-year latency. Histology:
   adenocarcinoma (most), adenomatous polyps, sarcoma, transitional cell carcinoma.
 • Case reports after ileal and colon conduits, augmentations, rectal bladder, neobladders and ileal ureters.
 • Tissue of origin unresolved — probably both: most are adenocarcinomas (intestinal); but urothelium
   exposed to faeces forms adenocarcinoma experimentally, ureters in ureterosigmoidostomy show marked
   dysplasia, and removing the urothelium prevents tumours.
 • Defunctioned ureterosigmoid anastomoses still produce cancer (reported 22 years after conversion to a
   conduit) → excise the old anastomosis, do not merely ligate and leave it.
 • Highest risk: transitional epithelium juxtaposed to colonic epithelium, both bathed in faeces.
 • Candidate mechanisms: nitrosamines (no convincing primary role), abnormal colonic mucin, ornithine
   decarboxylase induction, growth factors (epidermal growth factor) — cause unknown.

   • Limit bowel length when the patient cannot acidify urine below 5.8, cannot concentrate above 600

   • Metabolic complications: electrolyte abnormalities, altered sensorium, abnormal drug metabolism,
     osteomalacia, growth retardation, infection, calculi, short gut, urothelial or intestinal cancer.

   • Ileum and colon → hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis.

Concept — Nearly every metabolic complication descends from one fact — bowel absorbs what the kidney
excretes (ammonium chloride, drugs, urea, sulphate, ammonia) — so severity scales with contact time and
surface area, and the kidney's reserve decides whether the load is tolerated.

SUMMARY
 • Complications arise from the diversion itself, its specific type, and the preceding extirpative procedure.
 • Long-term complications significantly contribute to morbidity and mortality; many cancer patients die of
   cancer first, so those diverted for benign disease or cured of cancer bear the most long-term morbidity.
 • Knowledge of complication frequency and correct preparation, technique and aftercare gives the best
   chance of minimal morbidity and mortality.
