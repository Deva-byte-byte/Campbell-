INTRODUCTION
 • Why urologists need renal physiology: formal teaching is largely preclinical and the rest is learnt on the
   job; nephrology cover is available, but four reasons justify retaining it.
 • Early fluid and electrolyte management — the urologist primarily in charge who starts correct fluid,
   electrolyte and perioperative treatment early may secure a better outcome than one who waits for a
   consultant.
 • Favourable perioperative outcomes — knowing which perioperative factors impair renal function allows
   effective collaboration with anaesthetists to minimise kidney-related complications.
 • Optimal management of kidney disease — pathophysiology informs the choice of surgical approach,
   perioperative care and postoperative management.
 • Collaborative care — a shared physiological language with nephrologists, oncologists and anaesthetists
   improves multidisciplinary care.
Concept — The kidney is the organ of homeostasis, so a urologist who operates on it, obstructs it, relieves it
or doses drugs through it is altering volume, electrolytes and acid–base balance whether or not a
nephrologist is present.

OVERVIEW OF NORMAL STRUCTURE AND FUNCTION OF THE KIDNEY
The Nephron
 • About 1 million nephrons per kidney, tightly packed alongside blood vessels; each is a glomerulus (filters
   fluid and small molecules from blood) plus its paired tubule.
 • Tubule sequence: receives glomerular filtrate → reabsorbs the majority of filtered molecules → secretes
   excess metabolic waste into the filtrate → concentrates it into final urine; together the nephrons of one
   kidney excrete about 1–2 L of urine per day.
 • Nephron number is fixed at birth and declines with age from about 25 years.
 • Reduced nephron mass → hyperfiltration: after nephrectomy or with unilateral renal agenesis, remaining
   nephrons increase single-nephron filtration to maintain glomerular filtration and homeostasis.
 • Glomerulus: a tangle of capillaries lined internally by a single layer of endothelial cells and covered
   externally by podocytes (visceral epithelial cells) — highly differentiated cells whose foot processes
   interdigitate to form filtration slits bridged by slit diaphragms.
 • Glomerular basement membrane (GBM): a collagen-based layer produced by endothelial cells, lying
   between endothelium and podocyte.
 • Filtration barrier = endothelium + GBM + podocyte; it regulates passage from blood to urine by size and
   charge.
 • Mesangium — a stalk of specialised cells and matrix that holds the capillary tuft together.
 • Bowman's space: the capillary tuft invaginates into the initial tubule; this space is lined by parietal
   epithelium, whereas the podocytes (visceral epithelium) form the barrier. Filtrate then passes proximal
   tubule → loop of Henle → distal tubule → collecting duct.
 • Glomerular landmarks (Fig. 90.2): afferent arteriole bearing juxtaglomerular granular cells and
   sympathetic nerve endings; efferent arteriole; macula densa of the distal convoluted tubule;
   extraglomerular mesangial cells; urinary space draining at the urinary pole into the proximal tubule.

Renal Structure and Blood Supply
 • Four zones: cortex, outer medulla (outer stripe and inner stripe) and inner medulla. Fig. 90.1 maps
   arcuate and interlobular arteries, vasa recta, loop limbs and cortical, outer and inner medullary collecting
   ducts.
 • All glomeruli lie in the cortex, but the pair differs in tubule length:
     ◦ Cortical glomeruli — short tubules that do not travel deep into the medulla.
     ◦ Juxtamedullary glomeruli — long tubules descending deep into the inner medulla; this matters for
       medullary perfusion and ischaemic injury.

Basic Renal Homeostatic Functions
 • Excretion of waste and exogenous substances — nitrogenous waste from hepatic metabolism; drugs,
   contrast and other ingested products or their breakdown products.
 • Blood pressure and volume — sodium is the currency of volume: sodium retention raises blood volume,
   excretion lowers it.
     ◦ Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) sequence: low renal perfusion pressure or
       sympathetic activation → renin from specialised renal cells → angiotensin II (potent vasoconstrictor,
       also directly increases renal sodium reabsorption) → adrenal aldosterone → distal sodium
       reabsorption → volume retention.
 • Acid–base, water and electrolyte balance between intracellular and extracellular compartments, held
   within a tight range for cellular function.
 • Erythropoietin — produced by renal interstitial fibroblasts in response to hypoxia; drives red cell
   maturation in the marrow.
 • Calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, activated vitamin D) — hydroxylation of 25-hydroxyvitamin D in
   proximal tubule cells, stimulated by raised parathyroid hormone or low calcium or phosphate; essential
   for calcium–phosphate homeostasis and bone.
Concept — Structure dictates vulnerability: the glomerulus is a pressure-driven filter whose output is set by
arteriolar tone, and the tubule is an energy-hungry reclamation line whose deepest segments work at the
edge of hypoxia; almost every disorder in this chapter is a failure of one or the other.

MEASUREMENT OF KIDNEY FUNCTION
 • Two settings, two approaches: steady state (function stable over time, as at a routine clinic visit) versus
   dynamic state (acute fluctuation, as postoperatively).

Measurement of Kidney Function in Steady State
 • Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is the standard index of kidney function, but it cannot be measured
   directly.
 • Gold standard: serial urinary or plasma clearance of an exogenous marker (inulin, iohexol, iothalamate)
   — reliable but cumbersome and costly, so surrogates are used in practice; the most widely used is serum
   creatinine (SCr).
Serum Creatinine–Based Measures of Kidney Function
 • Creatinine is a muscle breakdown product, freely filtered at the glomerulus — hence a good filtration
   marker.
 • Small active tubular secretion from peritubular capillaries into urine is negligible in health but becomes
   an increasing share of creatinine excretion as function deteriorates.

 • 24-hour creatinine clearance (SCr, 24-hour urine volume and urine creatinine → mL/min) is little used:
   the collection is cumbersome and prone to under- or over-collection, and because of secretion it
   overestimates GFR increasingly as renal function declines.
 • SCr alone is insufficient because creatinine generation varies independently of kidney function — muscle
   mass is the most important factor, varying with age, sex and race (Table 90.1).
 • Estimating equations were derived in large, diverse populations with measured GFR, simultaneous
   creatinine and demographic data, incorporating age, sex and, historically, race.
 • Problem with a race coefficient: it raised estimated GFR (eGFR) in Black patients, affecting chronic kidney
   disease (CKD) staging and drug dosing and potentially delaying nephrology or transplant referral.
 • Current recommendation (American Society of Nephrology and National Kidney Foundation Task Force):
   laboratories report the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine
   equation, using age and sex without race — derived in a diverse cohort and meeting performance
   standards without disadvantaging any population. Online calculators make memorisation unnecessary.

Serum Cystatin C–Based Measures of Kidney Function
 • Cystatin C: a 13-kDa protein made by all nucleated cells, freely filtered, then completely reabsorbed and
   degraded by the proximal tubule — so there should be little or none in urine in health.
 • Not wholly GFR-specific: some non-GFR influences exist (Table 90.1).
 • Cystatin C equations require age and sex adjustment but have never shown race dependence.
 • Combined creatinine–cystatin C 2021 equation (without race) is more accurate than the creatinine-only
   equation, with smaller differences between race groups.
 • Indications: an adjunct to stage function when creatinine-based eGFR is likely biased (Table 90.1) or
   when staging sits at a consequential threshold and confirmation is needed.
 • Barriers: standardisation to a reference assay, higher cost than creatinine, clinician unfamiliarity — all
   expected to ease with a central reference standard, better reimbursement and experience.

Urinary Protein Excretion
 • Two defences against protein loss: the filtration barrier limits passage of albumin and non-albumin
   protein, and the proximal tubule reclaims protein that enters filtrate → normal excretion <150 mg/day.
 • Proteinuria >150 mg/day means either overproduction of non-albumin protein (paraproteins in multiple
   myeloma) or damage to the barrier or tubules.
 • Albuminuria is a sensitive marker of glomerular injury, because albumin excretion is controlled chiefly at
   the filtration barrier.
 • 24-hour albumin excretion needs concurrent creatinine to confirm completeness: expect urine creatinine
   20–25 mg/kg/day in men, 15–20 mg/kg/day in women.
 • Test hierarchy — Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2023: spot urine albumin-to-
   creatinine ratio (ACR), preferably early-morning — first line → spot protein-to-creatinine ratio (PCR) if
   ACR unavailable or large non-albumin proteinuria suspected (Bence Jones proteinuria) → reagent strip
   (automated or manual reading) third line, confirmed by formal testing.
 • Why ACR over PCR: albuminuria is more sensitive to glomerular injury and to risk of adverse outcomes,
   and the assay is easier to standardise.
 • Trap: the reagent strip detects albumin only and misses non-albumin protein such as light chains.
 • Prognosis: large prospective cohorts establish rising albuminuria as an independent risk factor for CKD
   progression, cardiovascular disease, stroke and death — hence KDIGO albuminuria categories (see
   Chronic Kidney Disease).

Measurement of Kidney Function in a Dynamic State
 • eGFR equations are invalid when function is changing — an anuric critically ill patient in the first 12 hours
   has a GFR of zero, whatever the creatinine-estimated GFR reads.
 • Guidelines therefore use change in SCr and urine output together to identify acute kidney injury (AKI);
   KDIGO currently incorporates only these two.
 • Both are insensitive to early injury. Novel biomarkers — urine neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin
   (NGAL), urine cystatin C, kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) — may detect early, subclinical injury and
   predict recovery, pending further study.
 • Kidney injury continuum (Fig. 90.3): normal → increased risk → damage → falling GFR → kidney failure →
   death, with complications arising at each step. Biomarkers identify susceptibility and injury mechanisms

     during the window for early targeted intervention; creatinine and oliguria diagnose only once GFR has
     fallen (staging); biomarkers may later track progression. GFR decline is a late, insensitive marker.
Concept — Every kidney-function number rests on an assumption: creatinine assumes stable muscle mass
and secretion, eGFR assumes a steady state, and the dipstick assumes the protein is albumin; identifying
which assumption is broken tells you when the number is misleading.

ACUTE KIDNEY INJURY
 • Circulation of the nephron: afferent arteriole → glomerular capillary bed → filtration across endothelium,
   GBM and podocytes into Bowman's space → efferent arteriole → peritubular capillaries alongside the
   tubules → venous system.
 • GFR is controlled by the Starling forces across the barrier — the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic
   pressures favouring or opposing filtration. In health the drive is overwhelmingly toward filtration,
   dominated by intracapillary hydrostatic pressure.
 • Net filtration pressure (Fig. 90.4A): 10 mm Hg = glomerular hydrostatic 60 − Bowman's capsule 18 −
   glomerular oncotic 32 mm Hg.
 • Maintaining glomerular hydrostatic pressure is the foundation of GFR, achieved by adjusting relative
   afferent and efferent tone; in AKI something interferes with this process.

Epidemiology and Impact of Acute Kidney Injury
 • AKI is common, costly and deadly. A large meta-analysis using KDIGO criteria found AKI in a minority of
   community patients but in one-fifth to one-third of hospitalised patients; pooled mortality about one in
   four, rising to about one in two when renal replacement therapy (RRT) is required.
 • United States burden: over a million cases a year; several extra hospital days per episode; costs above 9
   billion dollars annually; nearly 300,000 deaths — AKI raises mortality more than diabetes, prostate
   cancer, breast cancer and heart failure combined.
 • Even early-stage AKI confers excess risk of death, so understanding its mechanisms is needed to prevent
   progression to RRT and death.

Overview of Acute Kidney Injury Aetiology, Diagnosis, and Management
 • Three categories: prerenal, intrinsic, postrenal (Fig. 90.6), grouped by compartment; acute tubular
   necrosis (ATN) is the main intrinsic type.

Prerenal Acute Kidney Injury
Illustrative case. Gastroenteritis in a hypertensive woman on lisinopril: hypotension, tachycardia, dry mucosa;
SCr doubled, raised blood urea nitrogen (BUN); specific gravity 1.020, bland sediment, fractional excretion of
sodium (FeNa) 0.5%. With resuscitation SCr fell to near baseline within 2 days (Fig. 90.7).

 KEY POINTS: PRERENAL ACUTE KIDNEY INJURY
   • Kidney structure is normal, so the urinalysis is bland — no active sediment.
   • Correcting the prerenal insult produces immediate improvement.
   • Drugs or conditions that impair autoregulation magnify the effect of a prerenal insult.
   • FeNa is low because the kidney is reclaiming sodium to defend volume.

Aetiology and Mechanisms
 • Mechanism: volume depletion → reduced renal perfusion → fall in glomerular capillary hydrostatic
   pressure → fall in GFR.
 • Autoregulation holds renal blood flow and GFR almost constant across a wide range of mean arterial
   pressure (Fig. 90.8), by dilating the afferent and constricting the efferent arteriole — maximising inflow,
   restricting outflow.
 • Juxtaglomerular apparatus = macula densa + glomerular tuft with its afferent and efferent arterioles (Fig.
   90.2).

Diagnosis
 • Weigh history, examination and laboratory data together to build a case for or against each type; real
   patients rarely show every typical finding.
 • History: large-volume losses; consider prerenal AKI even in a normotensive patient with decompensated
   heart failure, sepsis or cirrhosis (impaired autoregulation, reduced perfusion). After surgery, review
   intraoperative and postoperative haemodynamics for transient hypotension.
 • Examination: tachycardia and hypotension — may be absent in low-flow states such as oedematous heart
   failure, where history corroborates.
 • Laboratory logic: normal tubules conserve sodium (>99% of filtered sodium can be reclaimed) and water,
   and urea is passively reabsorbed.
 • Urine sodium is unreliable when urine output is low → use FeNa; FeNa is misleading with new diuretic
   therapy or metabolic alkalosis → use fractional excretion of urea (FeUrea). Both need simultaneous
   serum and urine sodium or urea and creatinine; calculators are widely available.
 • BUN:creatinine ratio >20:1 reflects passive urea reabsorption but is not a reliable discriminator, as non-
   AKI factors alter it.
 • High vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone, ADH) concentrates the urine; the response to correction is the
   single most important test.

Management
 • Timely reversal of the prerenal state, tailored to cause: hypovolaemia → isotonic fluid; sepsis → treat
   and give volume as needed; heart failure → diuretics and fluid restriction to improve forward flow and
   decongest the kidneys; surgical patient with intraoperative hypotension → hold antihypertensives.
 • Uncorrected prerenal AKI evolves into intrinsic injury; avoiding hypotension and further insults is
   paramount.
 • Hypotension is relative to the patient's baseline: a mean arterial pressure (MAP) >65 mm Hg maintains
   GFR in healthy adults, but the autoregulatory curve shifts right in older patients with vascular disease, so
   a "normal" MAP may be too low.

Acute Tubular Necrosis
Illustrative case. A vasculopath with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is hypotensive during
partial nephrectomy, then needs ventilation and vasopressors with minimal urine output: FeNa 3%, specific
gravity 1.003, many granular casts. Haemodynamics recover in 2 days, yet SCr rises for about a week,
plateaus, then falls (Fig. 90.10).
 • ATN is a misnomer: injury extends beyond the tubules, and cell death is more often apoptosis than
   necrosis.
 • Two mechanisms: ischaemic and toxic.

 KEY POINTS: ISCHAEMIC ACUTE TUBULAR NECROSIS
   • Hospital ATN is often a combination of ischaemic and toxic insults.
   • Whether ischaemic or toxic, the mechanisms that lower GFR are similar.
   • The tubules are the most vulnerable site for hypoxic injury.
   • Diagnosis is supported by a lag in improvement after insults are removed, muddy-brown granular casts,
     and high urine sodium or FeNa.
   • Recovery is possible but may take days to weeks.

Ischaemic acute tubular necrosis.
 • Cause: a prolonged or extreme prerenal state.
 • Why tubules: they work at the brink of hypoxia even normally. The kidney receives about 25% of cardiac
   output, but medullary flow is progressively lower than cortical because supply changes to
   countercurrent flow in the vasa recta.
 • Supply–demand mismatch (Fig. 90.11): tissue oxygen tension falls from 50–100 mm Hg in cortex to 10–
   20 mm Hg in medulla (osmolality 300 → 1200 mOsm), while the heavy-workload segments — S3
   segment of the proximal tubule and medullary thick ascending limb — have high oxygen consumption;
   the outer medulla extracts a larger fraction of its oxygen supply than the heart, whereas the kidney as a
   whole extracts little.
 • GFR fall is multifactorial — haemodynamic change, endothelial damage and inflammation.
 • Polarity sequence: healthy cells are tightly packed and polarised (apical brush border; basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-
   ATPase) → prolonged ischaemia displaces Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase to the cytoplasm or apical membrane →
   proximal sodium reabsorption fails → high sodium delivery to the macula densa activates TGF → afferent
   vasoconstriction and RAAS shutdown → GFR falls.
 • This apparently maladaptive GFR fall is protective: it cuts tubular workload and solute loss, giving time
   for regeneration.
 • If ischaemia persists: cell death (necrosis or, more commonly, apoptosis) → sloughing into the lumen →
   intratubular obstruction and the pathognomonic muddy-brown granular casts — dead tubular cells
   complexed with Tamm–Horsfall protein.
 • Autoregulation is itself impaired in ATN (partly via endothelial injury), further lowering GFR and
   heightening vulnerability to new insults; peritubular capillary endothelial injury worsens outer
   medullary hypoxia; hypoxia-induced inflammation amplifies every step.
 • Recovery (regeneration → restored polarity → full basement membrane coverage) takes days, weeks or
   months; surviving cells are thought to dedifferentiate and proliferate to repopulate the tubule.

Postcontrast acute kidney injury.
 • Definition: SCr rise typically within 48 hours of intravenous iodinated contrast — usually computed

 • Mechanisms: prerenal vasoconstriction (→ ischaemic ATN if prolonged or combined with other insults)
   and direct tubulotoxicity.
 • Risk factors: older age, diabetes, CKD, anaemia, contrast volume and route (arterial versus venous).
 • Prevention: intravenous fluid before contrast in hospitalised patients is the most effective measure;
   optimal fluid and rate are unclear.

Aetiology and mechanisms.
 • Acute infiltration of the interstitium by inflammatory cells, with oedema; the interstitium is normally a
   tiny compartment because tubules are packed closely.
 • Hypersensitivity reaction to an antigen — usually a drug or pathogen. GFR falls through mainly cell-
   mediated (also antibody-mediated) injury → tubular damage, vascular injury and possibly obstruction
   from interstitial oedema.
 • Drugs cause the large majority; infection a smaller share (Table 90.6).
 • Infective AIN: consider in complicated urinary tract infection with Escherichia coli, Campylobacter or
   streptococci plus acute SCr rise.
 • Systemic causes (rare): sarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).
 • Timing: classically a few days to weeks after starting the drug; rarely later in therapy.
 • Biopsy (Fig. 90.13): diffuse interstitial infiltrate, severe tubular injury with tubulitis (lymphocytes crossing
   the tubular basement membrane), and eosinophils.

Management of Complications of Acute Kidney Injury
 • Commonest life-threatening complications: hyperkalaemia, acidaemia, volume overload and uraemia,
   arising while the cause is being treated; hyperkalaemia and acidaemia are covered under electrolyte and
   acid–base disorders.
Volume Overload
 • Urgent only when causing respiratory distress (or wound-healing problems from oedema); oedema or
   effusion alone does not demand urgent action.
 • Medical management: sodium and fluid restriction plus intravenous diuretics.
 • Diuretic rules in AKI: higher doses are needed → escalate the dose if the previous one failed → redose
   according to the drug's half-life for further removal → loop diuretics first line, adding a thiazide
   (metolazone, intravenous chlorothiazide) or carbonic anhydrase inhibitor (acetazolamide) for sequential
   blockade of sodium reabsorption.
 • Continuous infusion versus scheduled boluses give similar 24-hour net diuresis; choice rests on logistics
   — reassessment, redosing and the higher level of care an infusion requires.

Uraemia
 • Symptoms (subjective): malaise, poor appetite, dysgeusia, tremor — often confounded by other causes in
   hospital.
 • Signs: increasing somnolence, confusion, asterixis, myoclonus, coarse tremor; a pericardial rub from
   uraemic pericarditis is rare in AKI; prolonged uraemia → obtundation and aspiration.
 • No medical therapy reverses the neurocognitive effects.
 • Uraemic platelet dysfunction and bleeding → desmopressin (1-deamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin,

 • Indication: progressive uraemia, hyperkalaemia, acidaemia or volume overload refractory to medical

   temporary dual-lumen 10–12 French catheter in the jugular or femoral vein; dialysate runs

Management of CKD Complications
Anaemia
 • Erythropoietin (EPO) production falls with advancing CKD, particularly below GFR 30 mL/min/1.73 m² →
   progressive normocytic, normochromic anaemia.
 • Treatment sequence: ensure iron repletion and exclude other causes → EPO or equivalent, subcutaneous
   or intravenous.
 • Target haemoglobin 10–11.5 g/dL in non-dialysis CKD and ESKD (KDIGO CKD 2023); randomised trials
   showed harm when anaemia was corrected above 13 g/dL.
Bone and Mineral Metabolism
 • Secondary hyperparathyroidism (HPT) develops progressively: reduced phosphate excretion, reduced
   1,25-vitamin D production and hypocalcaemia drive a normal parathyroid gland to respond.
 • Biochemical phenotype: low to low-normal calcium, high-normal to high phosphate, raised intact
   parathyroid hormone (PTH).
 • Unchecked → bone disease and vascular calcification, contributing to excess cardiovascular risk and
   death.
 • Treatment reverses the drivers: dietary phosphate restriction, phosphate binders taken with meals to
   chelate gut phosphate, and 1,25-vitamin D.

Hyperkalaemia
 • Renal potassium clearance needs two things: sodium delivery to the distal nephron and aldosterone to

 • Advanced CKD reduces distal sodium delivery; RAAS blockade creates a low-aldosterone state.
 • Dietary potassium restriction limits fresh fruit and vegetables → preferred strategy is potassium binders
   and diuretics, preserving beneficial RAAS blockers and a healthy diet.
Hypertension
 • Hypertension is both a cause and a consequence of CKD; worsening function impairs sodium handling
   and causes volume retention.
 • Regimens need scheduled diuretics to address volume excess. Loop diuretics were traditional in late CKD,
   but thiazide or thiazide-like diuretics, particularly chlorthalidone, are effective and well tolerated in
   advanced CKD.
Sexual Dysfunction
 • Sexual dysfunction worsens as GFR declines. Up to half of men with CKD or ESKD report reduced libido
   and erectile dysfunction, with less frequent intercourse and poorer quality of life — independent of other
   chronic comorbidity.
 • Women: amenorrhoea and infertility as GFR falls; pregnancy rates markedly reduced in advanced CKD and
   ESKD.
 • Putative mechanisms: peripheral vascular disease, autonomic dysfunction, peripheral neuropathy,
   hypogonadism, mood disorders, hyperprolactinaemia, medication effects.
 • Reversibility: variable improvement with dialysis, more reliable improvement after transplantation —
   but not normalisation.
 • Phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) inhibitors may be effective in men.
 • Under-addressed by providers, possibly from discomfort with the topic.
Progression of Chronic Kidney Disease and Cardiovascular Risk
 • First principle: manage the underlying disorder. Foundations: glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) <7.5% and
   blood pressure <130/80 mm Hg.
 • Blood pressure control matters more than agent choice, except in proteinuric disease (with or without
   diabetes), where RAAS blockade slows progression and lowers cardiovascular risk.
 • Finerenone — a non-steroidal selective mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist — reduces CKD progression
   and cardiovascular events in CKD with type 2 diabetes.
 • Sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors give reno- and cardioprotection and a mortality
   benefit in large randomised trials, in diabetic and non-diabetic patients alike.
 • Progression is non-linear: long plateaus punctuated by rapid decline, accelerated by non-adherence, poor
   diabetes and blood pressure control, nephrotoxins, sepsis and surgical complications.
 • Risk calculators predict progression to dialysis after nephrectomy or partial nephrectomy, using baseline
   kidney function — the answer to the fearful patient in the case.

remains, albuminuria how actively it is being damaged. Each complication — anaemia, bone disease,
acidosis, hyperkalaemia, hypertension — is simply the loss of one of the homeostatic functions described in

COMMON ELECTROLYTE AND ACID–BASE ABNORMALITIES
Sodium Disorders
Illustrative case. After 4 days of watery diarrhoea, drinking only water, a hypotensive, tachycardic woman is
confused with sodium 122 mmol/L, potassium 3.0 and bicarbonate 20 mmol/L.

 KEY POINTS: SODIUM DISORDERS
   • Sodium disorders are largely disorders of water balance, not sodium balance.
   • Low serum sodium reflects water excess relative to sodium; high serum sodium reflects water deficit
     relative to sodium.
   • If onset was more than 48 hours ago or is unknown, correct very slowly, with frequent sodium checks
     and adjustment for rapid change, to prevent irreversible neurological injury.

 • Five organising principles:
    1. Volume status and water balance are separate entities.
    2. Volume status is a clinical diagnosis — profuse diarrhoea, hypotension, tachycardia and orthostasis
       indicate hypovolaemia; dyspnoea, orthopnoea, jugular venous distension and oedema with cardiac
       dysfunction indicate hypervolaemia.
    3. Sodium is the currency of volume, so urine sodium is an adjunct to clinical assessment — low urine
       sodium suggests a low-volume state.
    4. Sodium disorders are disorders of water balance.
    5. Water balance is a biochemical diagnosis — low sodium means water excess, high sodium means
       water deficit.
 • Sites: most sodium and water is handled by the proximal tubule, then loop of Henle and distal convoluted
   tubule, but the principal cells of the distal nephron are the key site of water regulation.
 • Requirement 1 — concentrated medullary interstitium: the ascending limbs are the only segments that
   absorb sodium while impermeable to water, building the osmotic gradient that later draws water out of
   the collecting duct (reabsorbed water = concentrated urine = high urine osmolality).
 • Requirement 2 — apical aquaporin 2 (AQP2): distal water reabsorption is impossible unless AQP2 is
   translocated to the apical membrane of principal cells, under ADH (vasopressin) acting on the
   basolateral vasopressin type 2 (V2) receptor.
 • ADH (Fig. 90.17): made in the hypothalamus, secreted from the posterior pituitary; released in response
   to raised plasma osmolality, low mean arterial pressure or low blood volume — the most potent stimuli
   are volume depletion and hyperosmolarity; hypothalamic osmoreceptors also drive thirst; V2 action in
   the medullary and papillary collecting ducts upregulates AQP2 production and insertion.
 • Serum osmolality = 2 × Na + glucose/18 + BUN/2.8 (glucose and BUN in mg/dL) — sodium is the
   dominant contributor.
 • Feedback: high sodium → thirst and ADH → water retained if available and the medulla is concentrated;
   hypo-osmolality → thirst and ADH suppressed → water diuresis.
 • Volume depletion overrides hypo-osmolality — the case: diarrhoeal sodium and water loss → volume
   depletion → ADH and water avidity despite falling osmolality → solute-free water intake →
   hyponatraemia.
Water distribution.
 • Total body water (TBW) = 0.6 × weight (kg): intracellular two-thirds, extracellular one-third; extracellular
   = interstitial three-quarters, intravascular one-quarter.

   hypotonic fluid distributes two-thirds to cells.
 • Starling forces govern interstitial ↔ intravascular movement, so whatever the tonicity, only one-quarter
   of the extracellular share stays in vessels. 1 L of free water leaves only about 83 mL intravascular.

       measurement — largely eliminated by newer methods.

 • Severe symptoms (lethargy, confusion, obtundation, seizures) → 3% hypertonic saline to raise sodium 4–

Hypernatraemia
 • Water deficit relative to sodium: lack of water access with renal or extrarenal loss of solute-poor fluid, or
   sodium gain.
 • Diabetes insipidus (DI) pair: central = lack of ADH; nephrogenic = renal ADH resistance; both impair water
   reclamation → water loss and dilute urine.
 • Nephrogenic DI is mostly acquired: drugs (lithium, amphotericin B), electrolyte disturbance
   (hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia) and post-obstruction; genetic causes exist.
 • Three practical rules: intact thirst plus access to water prevents hypernatraemia → so an undiagnosed DI
   patient kept nil by mouth postoperatively can become markedly hypernatraemic → in a volume-
   overloaded hypernatraemic patient, free water adds little to overload because it distributes mainly into
   cells (Fig. 90.18).
 • Treatment: free water, oral or parenteral (free-water content of fluids, Fig. 90.9).
 • Onset >48 hours or unknown → lower sodium no faster than 0.5 mEq/L per hour: brain cells have
   adapted to hyperosmolality, and a rapid fall causes cerebral oedema, irreversible neurological damage
   and possible death.
 • Calculate the free-water deficit to estimate infusion rate, but check sodium very frequently and adjust to
   avoid overcorrection.
 • Governing contrast: overcorrecting hyponatraemia shrinks adapted brain cells (osmotic demyelination);
   overcorrecting hypernatraemia swells them (cerebral oedema).

Potassium Disorders
 • About 98% of the 3500 mmol total body potassium is intracellular; the remaining ~70 mmol sits in
   extracellular fluid at 4–5 mEq/L.
 • The intracellular-to-extracellular ratio sets the resting membrane potential; disruption causes muscular
   and neuronal dysfunction — at the extremes cardiac arrest (hyperkalaemia) or paralysis (hypokalaemia).




                                                  page 21 of 31
Chapter 90 — Fundamentals of Nephrology                                         Common Electrolyte and Acid–Base Abnormalities
 • Two lines of defence: shifts between cells and extracellular fluid (early) and renal and gut excretion
   (slower); so every potassium disorder is a shift or an excretion–intake mismatch.
 • Daily balance: diet about 100 mmol; kidney excretes 90–95% over 6–12 hours; gut 5–10%, a minor role in
   health.
 • Acute load: insulin and β₂-adrenergic stimulation increase Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity, moving potassium
   into cells — the basis of emergency therapy.
 • Renal excretion is distal tubule and collecting duct secretion. Secretion is increased by:
    1. High urine flow — more distal sodium delivery for exchange; low flow reduces excretion.
    2. Greater distal sodium delivery and absorption, which makes the lumen electronegative and favours
       potassium (and hydrogen) secretion — loop and thiazide diuretics cause hypokalaemia; volume
       depletion or potassium-sparing diuretics do the reverse.
    3. Rising aldosterone — increases distal sodium reabsorption and hence potassium excretion.
    4. Rising plasma potassium acting directly on apical secretory channels.
    5. Extracellular pH — metabolic alkalosis enhances, metabolic acidosis impairs secretion.
 • Adaptation: chronic high intake increases the efficiency of all five mechanisms.
Illustrative case. A diabetic man with CKD on a β-blocker, a recently increased angiotensin-converting
enzyme (ACE) inhibitor and insulin has potassium 6.7 mEq/L, glucose 397 mg/dL and peaked T waves;
calcium gluconate, insulin and an intravenous loop diuretic restore 4.9 mEq/L — several simultaneous hits.
Hyperkalaemia
 • Rarely from intake alone while regulation is intact; results from two non-exclusive mechanisms:
     ◦ Shift out of cells — insulin deficiency, cell lysis, systemic acidosis (hydrogen enters cells in exchange for
       potassium).
     ◦ Impaired renal secretion — acute or chronic kidney disease, aldosterone deficiency or resistance,
       reduced distal sodium delivery and urine flow (volume depletion).
 • Urological cause: ureteric diversion into jejunum (much less often ileum) → intestinal reabsorption of
   urinary potassium.
 • Pseudohyperkalaemia — falsely high value with normal true level: traumatic difficult draw, prolonged
   tourniquet, excessive fist clenching; extreme leucocytosis or thrombocytosis. Exclude it first.

Calcium Disorders
Illustrative case. Months of abdominal pain, polyuria, bone ache and low mood, then a first stone; dry and

Acid–Base Disorders
Illustrative case. A stone former with a new mixed calcium oxalate–calcium phosphate stone, recently
started on topiramate: bicarbonate 21 mEq/L with a normal anion gap; 24-hour urine pH 6.81, citrate low,
oxalate and sodium high, calcium phosphate supersaturation high — carbonic anhydrase inhibition (below).
 • Extracellular pH is held at 7.38–7.42 for life-sustaining processes.
 • Volatile acid (carbon dioxide) from carbohydrate and fat metabolism is removed by ventilation; non-
   volatile (fixed) acid from carbohydrate, fat and sulphur- and phosphate-containing amino acids is handled
   by the kidney, which (1) excretes the acid load and (2) reclaims filtered bicarbonate.
 • Disorders arise when ventilation, renal processes or both fail or are overwhelmed.

URINARY TRACT OBSTRUCTION AND OBSTRUCTIVE UROPATHY
Introduction
 • Urinary tract obstruction (UTO): blockage of urinary flow at any level from renal tubule to urethra, arising
   in fetal life, childhood or adulthood; usually shown as dilatation on imaging; congenital or acquired,
   benign or malignant.
 • Impact depends on degree (partial or complete, unilateral or bilateral), chronicity (acute or chronic) and
   pre-existing renal function.

 • Asymptomatic OU may go unnoticed in SCr, but uncorrected unilateral OU causes progressive irreversible
   ipsilateral loss, and uncorrected bilateral OU leads to ESKD.
 • OU is reversible if identified and corrected promptly — one of the few potentially curable forms of
   kidney disease, so consider it in any unexplained acute or chronic kidney disease.
 • Further detail: Chapter 51 and electronic version.

Postobstructive Diuresis
 • A normal physiological event after relief, particularly of prolonged and bilateral obstruction; its size
   reflects the volume overload and the urea and electrolytes retained during obstruction.
 • Usually self-limited; no defining rate, but urine output up to 300 mL/h is common. Polyuria typically
   begins within hours of relief in most patients and lasts about 2 days; fractional sodium excretion may be
   many times normal.
 • Three mechanisms:
    1. Transporter dysregulation — sodium (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase) and water channels (all aquaporins, chiefly
       AQP2) are dysregulated during obstruction. In extreme cases AQP2 suppression lasts days to weeks →
       nephrogenic DI: polyuria, low urine osmolality (usually 300–400 mOsm/kg water) and, if severe, life-
       threatening hypernatraemia from free-water loss; diagnosed by water deprivation or desmopressin
       testing.
    2. Extracellular volume expansion (especially bilateral) → on relief, reduced sympathetic tone, reduced
       aldosterone and increased atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) promote salt and water excretion.
    3. Osmotic diuresis — retained urea enters the filtrate, raising tubular osmotic pressure and reducing
       water reabsorption.
 • Commonest setting: catheterisation for urinary retention. Gradual bladder decompression has no role
   — it does not reduce haematuria or diuresis.
 • First-line: free access to oral fluids, without routine intravenous supplementation; most cases resolve
   with expectant management.
 • Pathological diuresis (rare): hypovolaemia and disturbed sodium, potassium, phosphate, calcium and
   magnesium → metabolic panel every 4–6 hours with careful replacement.

Renal Recovery
 • Recovery depends on both the duration and the degree of obstruction.

 • Discrepancy at 1 week: the two complete-UUO models differ (about 60% versus full recovery); the source
   does not reconcile them — the consistent message is near-full recovery within 1–2 weeks, steep decline
   by 4 weeks, none by 6 weeks.
 • Vascular basis (Fig. 90.25): scanning electron microscopy of glomerular microvascular casts after
   obstruction shows capillary collapse and irregularity against the normal tuft — postobstructive
   dysfunction is driven chiefly by vascular responses.
 • Clinical translation: most stone formers with hydronephrosis from a small ureteral stone causing low-
   grade obstruction may be safely observed for 2–4 weeks after the first colic, from a functional
   standpoint.
 • Beyond 4 weeks, human outcome is poorly defined and unpredictable; full recovery has been reported
   up to 5 months after relief of unilateral obstruction.
 • Factors favouring recovery: lesser degree of obstruction, greater collecting system compliance, more
   pyelolymphatic backflow, younger age, thicker renal cortex.
 • Because these cannot be judged during the acute event, keep a low threshold to treat the obstruction
   or temporise with a stent or nephrostomy, avoiding permanent tubular injury and fibrosis.
Concept — Obstruction injures the kidney through its microvasculature before its tubules, and recovery tracks
how long arteriolar vasoconstriction has lasted; hence the clock — weeks, not the degree of dilatation —
governs when a urologist must intervene.
