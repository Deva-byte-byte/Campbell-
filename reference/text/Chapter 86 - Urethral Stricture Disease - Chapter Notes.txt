GENERALITIES OF RECONSTRUCTIVE SURGICAL TECHNIQUES
 • Governing aim: minimise tissue injury and promote healing. Unlike extirpative surgery, reconstructive
   results depend on methods that minimise tissue damage and maximise wound healing.
 • Visualisation: loupe magnification is used by almost all surgeons in adult and paediatric genital
   reconstruction; a headlight or lighted suction helps in deep perineal surgery.

 • Why bipolar near delicate structures: current is dissipated along conductors — in tissue, the vessels and

 • Instruments: plastic surgery or peripheral vascular tray — fine tenotomy scissors, fine forceps, skin hooks,
   delicate needle holders. Sharp scissors that cut with minimal collateral trauma are essential.
 • Calibration: a set of bougie à boule sizers checks luminal calibre; McCrea sounds supplement van Buren
   sounds, but sounds do not replace bougie à boule calibration.
 • Posterior urethral reconstruction: a sound passed through the cystostomy tract and prostate locates the
   proximal urethral end — Haygrove sound (authors), or cystoscope or van Buren sounds.
 • Suture: absorbable is the rule in urethral surgery — braided absorbable or monofilament absorbable;
   chromic rarely (others superior). Use the smallest calibre that aligns tissue tension-free; there is no
   reason for suture stronger than the tissue.

 KEY POINTS: RECONSTRUCTIVE SURGICAL TECHNIQUES
   • All efforts aim at minimising tissue injury and promoting healing; loupes almost universal; headlight
     or lighted suction for deep exposure; delicate instruments because sutures and needles are small.
   • In urethral surgery absorbable suture is the rule, smallest calibre that aligns tissue tension-free.
   • Positioning is the surgeon's preference. Proper diagnosis and planning are central to success.

Concept — reconstructive outcome is a wound-healing outcome: magnification, confined current, the finest
absorbable suture and a taper needle all serve to limit collateral injury, because the scar the surgeon creates
is the next stricture.

 • Meatotomy if narrow and symptomatic. Observe voiding: the meatus should open with a full forceful

   meatus opening at the dorsal lip.
 • Ammoniacal meatitis: meatal dilation with 0.05% clobetasol cream — abates within a week; anecdotally

Adult
 • Ventral meatotomy in adolescents and adults usually needs three sutures (apex and each side)
   approximating mucosa to skin for haemostasis; a meatal dilator (adult 6–34 Fr, paediatric 6–10 Fr) keeps it
   open.
 • Dorsal meatotomy is sometimes needed — a Y-V-plasty after excising any scarred ridge of neourethra;
   effective but cosmetically suboptimal meatus.
Concept — in the child, meatal stenosis is a thin post-circumcision membrane cured by meatotomy and
dilation; in the adult it is usually the visible tip of a fossa navicularis stricture and is evaluated as stricture
disease.

 KEY POINTS: SELECTED PROCESSES
   • Paediatric meatal stenosis follows circumcision → ammoniacal meatitis → ventral membrane.
     Neonatal circumcision remains controversial; if performed, it must be performed properly.
   • Failed hypospadias repair is complex; many patients are victims of the technology of their era. Any
     urethral involvement is evaluated as urethral stricture disease.
   • Modern exstrophy–epispadias reconstruction gives better function and less secondary surgery;
     secondary reconstruction targets the escutcheon, dorsal penile base, shaft, urethra and penoscrotal
     junction.


URETHRAL STRICTURE DISEASE
 • Urethral stricture = fixed anatomic narrowing such that the lumen will not accept instrumentation
   without disrupting the mucosa.
 • Anterior stricture: within the urethra surrounded by corpus spongiosum; scarring may extend through
   the spongy erectile tissue (spongiofibrosis) and into adjacent tissue. Contraction of this scar reduces the
   lumen.
 • Area falls with the square of diameter: 30 Fr = 10 mm diameter ≈ 78 mm²; 15 Fr = 5 mm ≈ 20 mm².
   Halving the calibre removes about three-quarters of the lumen — so scar contraction can be silent for a
   while, then produce marked symptoms as the lumen narrows further.

Urethral Anatomy
 • Bulbar urethra is eccentric within the corpus spongiosum, much closer to the dorsum; distally the penile
   urethra becomes central.
 • Genital skin: dual (proximal and distal) and bilateral supply → a fasciocutaneous system.
 • Corpus spongiosum: from the common penile artery, terminal branch of the internal pudendal artery.
   Dual supply: proximal (arteries to the bulb) and retrograde through the dorsal arteries arborising in the
   glans.
Concept — the retrograde glanular supply is what allows the bulb to be transected and mobilised; where it is
lost (hypospadias, common penile artery injury), mobilisation becomes ischaemia — the thread that runs
through vessel-sparing excision and pelvic fracture repair failure.

Aetiology
 • Any process that injures the epithelium or underlying spongiosum enough to heal with scar causes
   stricture. Categories: iatrogenic, traumatic, inflammatory, idiopathic.
 • Meta-analysis: idiopathic and iatrogenic are the commonest (roughly equal), followed by post-traumatic,
   then inflammatory.

 • Lichen sclerosus: definite association with inflammatory stricture. Begins as glans inflammation and
   inevitably causes meatal stenosis, if not a fossa navicularis stricture; cause unknown.
 • Proposed progression in LS: high-pressure voiding → intravasation of urine into the glands of Littre →

   may limit obstructive symptoms but do not resolve the stricture.

   limited to anterior strictures found in infants before erect ambulation; so defined, the rarest. The term is
   misused by some for any stricture without identifiable cause.
Concept — aetiology predicts biology: iatrogenic and straddle strictures are focal ischaemic or crush scars
suited to excision, whereas LS is a progressive skin disease that recurs in any skin used to repair it.

Diagnosis and Evaluation
 • Presentation: obstructive voiding symptoms; urinary tract infection (prostatitis, epididymitis); retention
   — on close inquiry, long-tolerated obstructive symptoms preceded retention.
 • Acute retention: catheter attempt fails → define the obstruction by retrograde urethrogram (RUG). Most
   are dilated acutely, often not in the patient's best interest; when in doubt, define the stricture and place a
   selective suprapubic cystostomy to buy planning time.
 • Blind passage of filiforms and blind dilation are condemned. Flexible endoscopy is almost universally
   available: visualise the stricture and pass a guidewire under direct vision.
 • Four determinants: location, length, depth and density of spongiofibrosis. Length and location:
   radiography, urethroscopy, ultrasonography. Depth and density: examination, contrast appearance,
   elasticity on urethroscopy — difficult to measure objectively.

 • Contrast technique: extravasation is possible in an inflamed urethra even with gentle technique → use
   contrast suitable for intravenous injection, neat or diluted per manufacturer. Do not thicken contrast
   with lubricating or anaesthetic gel — problematic, no radiographic gain, no added comfort.

Decision Making
 • First decide palliative versus curative. Requires: full radiological and endoscopic characterisation;
   knowledge of techniques and their limits; patient and surgeon agreement on the desired outcome and
   long-term expectations.
 • Curative procedures when possible and desired; palliation acceptable if it is the recognised goal.

Treatment
 • Progress over the last 50 years allows many complex strictures to be reliably reconstructed in one stage.
 • The reconstructive ladder (simplest procedure first, repeated after failure, before escalating) is archaic.
 • Discuss the goal and anticipated cure: some prefer periodic dilation (office, home or hospital); others
   want cure → surgery. Many procedures give short- and midterm success approaching long-term >90–95%.

Dilation
 • Oldest and simplest; may be curative for an epithelial stricture without spongiofibrosis.
 • Goal: stretch the scar without producing more scar. Bleeding means the stricture was torn, not
   stretched — further injury.
 • Least traumatic: soft techniques over multiple sessions. Safest current method = balloon dilating
   catheters (filiform tip, over a guidewire, or integral Coudé tip); for initial dilation, balloon over a wire
   passed under endoscopic control.

 • In selected patients, short- and midterm efficacy equals internal urethrotomy — but only by
   retrospective comparison; no randomised trial.
 • Optilume drug-coated balloon — Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval 2023 for anterior
   stricture; elutes paclitaxel, inhibiting fibroblast growth and scar formation.
 • ROBUST III (phase III; strictures after multiple failed endoscopic treatments): at 1 year, stricture-free
   74.6% vs 26.8% (P < 0.0001); International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) 9 vs 19.9. An improvement
   over serial endoscopy; role as initial (rather than salvage) therapy and cost-worth undetermined.

Internal Urethrotomy
 • Definition: any procedure opening the stricture by transurethral incision — through scar to healthy
   tissue, releasing scar contracture so the lumen heals enlarged.

 • Predictor: the greater the percentage luminal narrowing, the worse the outcome, cut-off 74%

 • Incision position: the traditional single 12 o'clock cut is questionable — the anterior spongiosum is
   thinnest from 10 to 2 o'clock. In the bulb the urethra–corpora distance is short: a deep 12 o'clock cut
   enters the triangular ligament and intracrural space. Distally the anterior spongiosum is thicker, but a
   deep cut enters the corpora cavernosa → erectile dysfunction (ED) from local cavernosal veno-occlusive
   dysfunction; vigorous 10 and 2 o'clock bulbar cuts carry the same risk.
 • Deep spongiofibrosis → cure by urethrotomy impossible, so deep incisions are unnecessary.
 • Complications: commonest = recurrence; bleeding (almost always with erections soon after); irrigant
   extravasation into perispongiosal tissue → use normal saline; deep incisions → spongiosum–cavernosum
   fistula, veno-occlusive dysfunction (now rare).
 • Outcome literature is poorly characterised — stricture features under-reported, and "success" often
   means temporary relief. Long-term actuarial cure is low; no success in non-bulbar strictures; a second
   urethrotomy adds virtually nothing.
 • Prior dilations and urethrotomies diminish the success of later open reconstruction. Urethrotomy
   success does not equal open reconstruction; no cost analysis supports multiple endoscopic
   interventions.

Open Reconstruction
 • Curative intent → anastomotic versus substitution urethroplasty. No single procedure is best for all
   strictures; stratify by length, cause and site.
Excision and reanastomosis (Russell, 1914).
 • Most dependable anterior technique: complete excision of fibrosis with primary anastomosis of normal
   ends. Three technical rules: fibrosis totally excised; anastomosis widely spatulated → large ovoid
   anastomosis; tension-free.
 • Success depends on vigorous mobilisation of the corpus spongiosum, plus dissection of Buck's fascia
   (compliance), development of the intracrural space, and detachment of the bulbospongiosus from the
   perineal body.
 • Length: 1–2 cm easily excised; 3–5 cm in some cases. Very proximal short bulbar strictures: dissect the
   membranous urethra. The closer to the membranous urethra, the longer the excisable stricture.
   Younger patients have more compliant tissue, stretching the limits.
 • Many proximal strictures: single-layer anastomosis preferable. Length precluding total excision → tissue
   transfer.

Staged graft repair.
 • Stage 1: medium-thickness mesh STSG, BMG or Wolfe graft placed on dartos fascia. Placed directly on
   tunica albuginea or corpora it cannot be mobilised, making tubularisation difficult — although a midline
   strip adherent to the corpora is advantageous.
 • Stage 2: tubularisation. Schreiter and Noll (first described mesh STSG) proceeded at 3–4 months; authors
   wait 12 months after STSG.
 • Useful in select cases; in the United States confined to the most difficult cases, single stage for most.
   Staged buccal and posterior auricular grafts succeed in complicated hypospadias; staged buccal grafts in
   LS with midterm follow-up.
 • Figure 86.11 — staged distal repair: tight fossa stenosis extending into pendulous urethra → narrow fossa
   excised, stricturotomy into normal proximal urethra → buccal graft (then bolster) → graft mature at 6
   months → Thiersch tube closed watertight, distal urethra calibrated to about 28 Fr → glans
   reconstruction and shaft closure, covered by a tunica dartos flap carrying a parietal tunica vaginalis
   island from the hemiscrotum.
 • Kulkarni single-stage panurethral repair (2012): perineal incision, penile invagination, dorsal graft from
   proximal bulb to meatus. Good overall success, lower after a previous failed urethroplasty; recurrences
   mostly proximal.
Concept — a graft lives on its bed, so it succeeds where the bed is thick and vascular (bulb, dorsal corpora),
and is staged when the bed — or skin disease such as LS or failed hypospadias — cannot be trusted in one
step.
Flap onlay.
 • Genital skin islands on the dartos fascia of the penis or tunica dartos of the scrotum. Once considered
   separate operations; Quartey's microinjection studies (1983) show them as one concept — the skin
   island is a passenger on a fascial flap, designed as in general flap surgery.
 • Three considerations: nature of flap tissue; flap vasculature; mechanics of transfer. Skin must be non-
   hirsute; for the donor site, use redundant non-hirsute genital skin.

 • Onlay (graft or flap) outperforms tubularised grafts or skin islands — avoid tubes. If unavoidable, limit
   tube length by aggressive mobilisation and excision. Tubularised flaps outperform tubularised grafts.

 • Postoperative ED: overall rate after urethroplasty ≈ that after circumcision; longer reconstructions carry

 • Lichen sclerosus: flaps were embraced → very high recurrence; staged skin grafts also recurred. Because

Perineal urethrostomy (PU).
 • Complex strictures — LS, failed reconstructions, failed hypospadias — have long histories of
   instrumentation and surgery, diminished sexual function and quality of life, and arrive late at referral
   centres.
 • Pananterior multigraft repair (one or more stages) is feasible with fair success but may not be optimal;
   elderly high-risk patients may not warrant it; even in the young, staged success falls after multiple failed
   urethroplasties → PU: simple and effective.
 • Challenges: disease into the proximal bulbar or membranous urethra defeats traditional PU; prior pelvic
   radiation and LS increase PU restricture.
 • BMG PU: brings the urethrostomy to the perineal surface instead of burying skin to the urethral opening;
   a graft in the neomeatus reduces circumferential scarring.

BMG PERINEAL URETHROSTOMY — SEQUENCE
 1. High lithotomy → inverted U incision → bulbospongiosus divided in the midline.
 2. Spongiosum freed from triangular ligament proximally and corpora distally → transected transversely →
    dorsal urethrotomy until healthy mucosa.
 3. Healthy ventral urethra brought to perineal skin (not a channel of inwardly mobilised skin) → proximal
    calibration to 30 Fr bougie à boule → distal urethra closed, allowed to retract.
 4. BMG long enough to reach from dorsal urethrotomy to skin completes the dorsal wall; graft bed =
    corporal bodies; fix graft to corpora so neomeatal skin cannot pull it off its blood supply.
 • Outcome: panurethral strictures of mixed cause (unknown commonest; LS, iatrogenic, failed hypospadias,
   Fournier gangrene, urethral and penile cancer) — most successful at midterm; recurrences managed by
   revision or periodic dilation. Reproducible, viable alternative in selected patients.
Concept — PU trades anatomy for reliability: when the anterior urethra has exhausted its reconstructive
options, the diseased segment is bypassed; the BMG corrects the one weakness of a deep PU —
circumferential skin-to-urethra scarring.

 KEY POINTS: TREATMENT OF URETHRAL STRICTURE
   • Agree the goal of treatment before choosing it.
   • Dilation — oldest, simplest; goal is atraumatic stretching; seldom curative (only the epithelial stricture
     without spongiofibrosis).
   • Internal urethrotomy — reserve for bulbar, <1.5 cm, no dense deep spongiofibrosis; repeated dilation
     or urethrotomy diminishes later open success.
   • Lasers — no advantage over cold knife.
   • Excision and primary anastomosis is the gold standard; once thought limited to <1.5–2 cm, longer
     strictures are now excised with better anatomical understanding.
   • Grafts = flaps in results (meta-analysis); flaps more complex; augmented anastomosis often better
     than pure onlay; all flaps are one operation with multidimensional application.
   • PU is viable in selected patients; BMG helps very proximal bulbar disease.


 KEY POINTS: URETHRAL STRICTURE
   • "Stricture" = anterior disease of epithelium and spongiosum; posterior lesions = pelvic fracture
     urethral injuries (PFUIs); prostatic urethra and bladder neck = contractures or stenoses.
   • Bulbar urethra eccentric (dorsal) in spongiosum; genital skin dual, bilateral, fasciocutaneous supply;
     spongiosum from the common penile artery.
   • Idiopathic and iatrogenic causes predominate, then trauma; gonococcal strictures now rare; LS
     strictures behave as inflammatory.
   • Obstructive symptoms or infection; retention follows long-tolerated symptoms.
   • Define length, location, depth, density — contrast, endoscopy, selective ultrasonography; examine the
     whole urethra; paediatric cystoscope useful.

 • Straddle injury can totally disrupt the urethra but usually involves only the bulbar urethra
   (spongiofibrosis may still obliterate it). Distraction injuries are unique to the membranous urethra.
 • "Apple off its stem" analogy implies injury at the prostatic apex — wrong: the commonest point is the

 • Many injuries are incomplete — a strip of epithelium remains. An aligning catheter (easily placed with
   flexible endoscopy) may let the urethra heal unscarred or with an easily managed stenosis; if complete, it
   aligns the ends and facilitates later reconstruction. Some centres evaluate acutely by endoscopy alone,
   expediting the whole process.
 • An aligning catheter is a guide, not traction on bladder and prostate; it may also drain the liquefying
   pelvic haematoma and hasten its resolution.
 • Close follow-up after the voiding trial — many develop stricture after catheter removal and need
   definitive repair. Primary realignment has been linked to delayed repair and more failed endoscopic
   treatments → some advise against routine use.

Evaluation
 • Define depth, density, length and location. In PFUI depth and density are predictable. Location affects
   continence → a counselling factor, not a determinant of approach. Length must be determined as
   precisely as possible.
 • Contrast studies are first line. Cystogram: bladder outline and rostral displacement of the proximal
   urethra; absent contrast in the posterior urethra says little (inconclusive) about bladder neck integrity.
 • If the patient can relax to void and the cystogram fills the posterior urethra, a simultaneous RUG shows
   the defect length — the exception, not the rule. The RUG's main role is to show whether the anterior
   urethra is normal — if so, successful anastomotic repair is ensured.
 • Primary anastomosis is possible even with some anterior involvement and after a previous failed
   posterior repair (slightly higher failure). Primary anastomosis is the goal in all until proven impossible.
 • Proximal urethra not seen → antegrade flexible endoscopy through the suprapubic tract + RUG: assess
   the bladder neck, advance to the obstruction; the unopacified gap is the defect.
 • Bladder neck appearance on contrast or antegrade endoscopy does not predict its eventual function.
 • MRI: authors' experience minimal; studies show benefit in difficult cases — when the prostatic apex and
   urethral stump lie more cephalad, infrapubectomy is more often needed.

Repair
 • Timing: set by associated injuries — ideally 3–6 months after trauma; lower-limb orthopaedic injuries
   often delay it.
 • Most PFUIs are short → mobilisation of spongiosum + primary anastomosis: spatulated proximal anterior
   urethra to widely spatulated membranous urethra for epithelial apposition.
 • About 10% are complex, may have fistulae (commonest urethrorectal) — technically more demanding.

PERINEAL ANASTOMOTIC REPAIR — SEQUENCE
 1. On-table flexible endoscopy via meatus and suprapubic sinus — excludes bladder stones.
 2. Haygrove or van Buren sound (or cystoscope; hollow Haygrove — Gelman sound — admits a flexible
    scope to view the bladder neck) → feel the proximal stump impulse.
 3. Exaggerated lithotomy (Fig 86.19): Allen or Guardian stirrups; no pressure on lateral legs or calves; hips
    raised by elevating the buttock section; boots set to avoid common peroneal nerve stretch; minimal hip
    and knee flexion.
 4. Incision anterior to the transverse perineal muscles (anterior perineal triangle — versus posterior anal
    triangle for perineal prostatectomy); λ-shaped incision in larger patients → midline muscle fusion over
    the bulb → under the scrotum → ring retractor.
 5. Midline muscle fusion divided, muscle dissected off the bulb → spongiosum detached from triangular
    ligament and corpora; bulb detached from perineal body → dissection to infrapubic space and through
    fibrosis.
 6. Bulbar arteries: sharp-tipped haemostat and monopolar cautery; avoid suture ligature (nerves nearby
    entering the corpora); a vessel-sparing PFUI repair is sometimes possible.
 7. Divide the triangular ligament; develop the intracrural space to the pubis; ligate the dorsal vein; beware
    cavernosal or dorsal arteries displaced into the space by trauma (Doppler if in doubt); periosteal elevator
    on the retropubic surface frees tissues.
 8. Haygrove staff via suprapubic sinus through the bladder neck → palpate → resect fibrosis to normal
    planes (histology) → open epithelium over the staff tip → endoscopy confirms the distal limit of the
    posterior urethra.
 9. If tension: mobilise spongiosum beneath the scrotum as the last manoeuvre (retrograde supply may be
    tenuous); detaching Buck's fascia increases compliance and limits the need.
10. Spatulate proximal urethra to accept ≥30 Fr bougie à boule → 8–10 tagged 4-0 PDS sutures (Heaney and
    Ravitch drivers help) → spatulate distal to 30 Fr → soft silicone ribbed stent under vision → irrigate clot
    → seat anastomosis.

Postoperative Management
 • Small soft silicone stent; urine diverted suprapubically, urethral catheter plugged — a stent only.
   Overnight stay; discharge on an anticholinergic; suppressive antibiotic only if the preoperative culture
   was positive.
 • Contrast voiding trial at 21–28 days: stop oxybutynin 24 hours before; straightforward anastomosis at 21
   days, rostral distraction 3–5 days later. Catheter out → bladder filled with contrast → void; check no
   extravasation, widely patent. No pericatheter RUG.
 • Flexible endoscopy at about 6 months — reconstruction mature and widely patent; endoscopy has
   almost replaced retrograde studies.
 • Curative rates in the high 90s %. In large centres, failures are not technical anastomotic restenosis but
   ischaemia of the proximal spongiosum → stenosis of the mobilised segment, which has become a flap
   on retrograde dorsal arterial flow through the glans.
 • At-risk groups studied by pudendal angiography: dorsal penile nerve injury, failed repair elsewhere,
   lateral-impact and "windswept" pelvic fractures. Many had pudendal lesions; most had vascular
   reconstitution.

Summary
 • Almost all PFUIs can be reconstructed perineally by anastomosis; endoscopic techniques should not be
   used for curative repair.
 • Children: same goals; most reconstructed through the same perineal exposure (harder).
 • Vessel-sparing repair extended to select PFUIs — feasible, good results in small numbers, advantage
   unproven.
Concept — PFUI repair is technically an anastomosis but biologically a flap: failure comes from bulbar
ischaemia, not the suture line, so pudendal inflow is screened before — not after — the spongiosum is
mobilised.

 KEY POINTS: DISTRACTION INJURIES OF THE URETHRA
   • PFUIs follow blunt pelvic trauma, in about 10% of pelvic fractures; often incomplete, with an epithelial
     strip left.
   • Acute aligning catheter is controversial, but at worst it facilitates later reconstruction.
   • Define anatomy with contrast + endoscopy ± selective MRI; bladder neck appearance does not predict
     function; simultaneous bladder neck and posterior urethral reconstruction is not usually undertaken.


VESICOURETHRAL ANASTOMOTIC STENOSIS
 • Robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) has reduced vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis (VUAS)
   — fewer leaks, better mucosal apposition, running anastomosis under magnification. Some still develop
   an obliterative distraction defect or severe stenosis.
 • Length: simultaneous cystogram + RUG, and/or RUG + antegrade endoscopy through the suprapubic
   tube.
 • Patients often comorbid, with thick small bladders and adverse body habitus, both contributing to the
   original difficulty. An indwelling suprapubic tube is always an option, including long term.
 • Endoscopic (short distractions): aggressive incisions at 3 and 9 o'clock, repeated at about 3 weeks;
   holmium laser versus cold knife debatable; hot knife unnecessary. If continuity must be "cored through",
   endoscopy is inappropriate. Radial urethrotomy + intralesional mitomycin C: good initial success in
   recalcitrant stenosis.
 • Continent catheterisable augmentation may be better than aggressive functional reconstruction (channel
   difficult in obesity); diversion is a primary option when functional reconstruction is not obvious.
 • Omentum interposition has been used for distraction defects and complex fistulae, allowing safe
   mobilisation of the rectum from the scar or fistula.
