Chapter 140 — Retroperitoneal Tumours
OVERVIEW
 • The retroperitoneum (RP) is a common site of pathology relevant to urologists; its tumours frequently
   involve the urinary tract, retroperitoneal vasculature and lymphatics.
 • Tumours may be primary to the RP or metastatic deposits that use it as a landing zone; distinguishing
   malignant from benign and primary from metastatic drives management.
 • Conditions unique to the kidney and adrenal are covered in their own chapters.

ANATOMIC CONSIDERATIONS OF THE RETROPERITONEUM
 • Borders: diaphragm superiorly; inlet of the true pelvis inferiorly; peritoneal contents anteriorly;
   posterior body wall posteriorly; flank musculature laterally.
 • Contents: upper urinary tract (kidneys, renal pelvis, proximal and mid ureter); adrenals; great vessels
   (infradiaphragmatic aorta and vena cava, common iliac vessels, lumbar branches); RP lymphatics and
   connective tissue (RP fat, Gerota's fat and fascia); sensory, motor and autonomic nerves (sympathetic
   chain of ganglia, postganglionic sympathetic and parasympathetic fibres).
 • Adjacent peritoneal organs — second and third parts of the duodenum, ascending and descending
   colon, pancreas, splenic hilum — demand attention because RP tumours are expansile and distort or
   invade their neighbours.

 • Genitofemoral nerve injury → transient or permanent paraesthesia of the anterior and medial thigh.
 • Psoas and femoral nerve injury → motor weakness of hip flexion and knee extension, with functional

 • Preoperative anticipation of involved structures gives a safer operation; consult hepatobiliary, vascular,
   thoracic and/or general surgeons as the tumour's location requires, and set realistic expectations of
   convalescence.

 KEY POINTS
  • The RP is a discrete compartment that houses or borders parts of the urinary, vascular,
    hepatobiliary, gastrointestinal, neurological and lymphatic systems.
  • Its tumours therefore often involve several organ systems and require careful planning before any
    intervention.

Concept — The RP is defined by what surrounds it; because its tumours grow silently and expansively, the
operative problem is usually the neighbouring structure, not the tumour itself.

   • Whether found incidentally or through symptoms, the next steps follow the same sequence: imaging →
     serology → tissue.

 Laboratory Testing
   • Young man with an RP mass → alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) and human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG):
     critical for diagnosis, prognosis and management of germ cell tumours (GCTs).
   • Blood counts identify dyscrasias suggesting lymphoproliferative disease; metabolic panels assess renal
     and hepatic function.

 Tissue Sampling
   • Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) or core-needle biopsy gives histological subtype and grade, and matters
     most where it changes the sequence of therapy.
   • Lymphoma: initial therapy is systemic chemotherapy in all cases, regimen by subtype — biopsy is
     essential.
   • Sarcoma: surgery was historically first, so biopsy played little role; high-grade tumours are now often
     treated multimodally, so biopsy selects patients for up-front chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted
     agents or radiotherapy.
   • Route (transperitoneal vs retroperitoneal) and type (FNA vs core) follow presentation and access; open
     or laparoscopic biopsy when larger samples are needed, usually in advanced or unresectable disease.
   • Core (14- or 16-gauge coaxial) over FNA: more adequate tissue and preserved cellular architecture.
   • Exception — liposarcoma: biopsy is often non-diagnostic and of limited value in guiding treatment.

   KEY POINTS
    • Many RP tumours grow silently over months or years and present large, with adjacent organ
      involvement.

 DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS
   • RP tumours fall into primary and metastatic tumours of germ cell/gonadal, mesodermal, neurogenic
     and haematological origin; management differs widely between groups.

 Germ Cell/Gonadal Origins
   • Primary in the RP or metastatic to it. Uncommon overall, but an RP mass in a young man must
     immediately raise the question of GCT.
   • Sex cord stromal tumours of the testis also use the RP as a predictable landing zone. Seminoma,
     nonseminoma and the sex cord stromal tumours (Leydig, Sertoli, granulosa) each have distinct biology
     dictating management.
 Germ Cell Tumours
   • Covered fully in Chapters 79 and 80; relevant here for their propensity to metastasise to the RP and
     occasional primary RP origin.
   • Incidence rising, most rapidly in Hispanic men. Usually present as an enlarging scrotal mass; in the RP,
     as heterogeneous, circumscribed masses along the RP lymph node chains.

 Mesodermal Origins
   • Soft-tissue tumours. Sarcomas: commonest liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, malignant fibrous
     histiocytoma (MFH); less commonly angiosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, myxosarcoma. Benign: lipoma,
     leiomyoma, fibroma, haemangiopericytoma, perivascular epithelioid cell tumour (PEComa), chordoma,
     myelolipoma, xanthogranuloma, myxoma, gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST).

 Liposarcomas
   • Malignant, locally aggressive tumours of adipocytes with nuclear atypia and hyperchromasia. Well-
     differentiated (WD) versus de-differentiated (DD) depends on the presence of a higher-grade, non-
     lipogenic component.
   • Both present as painless, often incidental masses; DD tumours grow rapidly, signalling the more
     aggressive phenotype.
   • CT: low-attenuation (fat-density) mass with mass effect on surrounding structures; internal higher-
     density areas indicate de-differentiated components — a single tumour may contain both.
   • WD histological types: adipocytic (lipoma-like — scant atypical hyperchromatic nuclei among fat
     lobules, so core biopsies are often inconclusive); sclerosing (hyalinised stroma with scattered atypical
     adipocytes); inflammatory (lymphoid aggregates may mimic primary lymphoproliferative malignancy).
     Diagnosis usually needs histology plus clinical and anatomical context.
   • DD histology: arises from WD components — atypical adipocytes surrounding fleshy non-lipogenic
     areas resembling undifferentiated spindle cell sarcoma, myxoid tumour, meningothelial or pleomorphic
     patterns; heterologous osteosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma or small round cell
     elements occur.
   • Molecular: both show overexpression and/or amplification of 12q genes MDM2 and CDK4 (with S100
     positivity); fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) or IHC for MDM2/CDK4 confirms the diagnosis in
     limited samples or where stroma or inflammation obscures it.

 Pleomorphic Liposarcomas
   • Very rare; adults; extremities more than RP; also spermatic cord. Fast growing.
   • Pleomorphic lipoblasts that do not overexpress MDM2 or CDK4; frequent mitoses; variable spindle
     and epithelioid cells. On needle biopsy they mimic poorly differentiated carcinoma or renal cell
     carcinoma; identifying poorly differentiated lipoblasts is essential.
   • Rapid progression to widespread metastasis, lung commonest.
 Concept — The liposarcoma family sorts on MDM2: amplified in well- and de-differentiated tumours (fatty,
 locally recurrent disease), absent in pleomorphic tumours (systemic disease); within the amplified pair, de-
 differentiation is what adds distant spread.
 Leiomyosarcomas
   • Malignant smooth muscle tumours; second commonest RP sarcoma (~20%). Arise from the media of
     RP blood vessels, the muscularis of the collecting system, or other smooth muscle.
   • Grow silently until mass effect or obstruction impairs venous return, urinary drainage or the
     gastrointestinal tract. In women, many are gynaecological in origin. Often incidental. Rarely arise with
     Epstein-Barr virus infection in immunosuppressed patients.
   • Homogeneous tumours encasing structures with fleshy bundles; occasional necrosis or cysts. Fascicles
     of atypical smooth muscle, atypia graded; de-differentiation uncommon.
   • Benign versus malignant criteria: location (RP vs somatic soft tissue), mitotic rate, nuclear and cellular
     atypia, necrosis → spectrum leiomyoma → smooth muscle tumour of uncertain malignant potential
     (STUMP) → leiomyosarcoma.
   • IHC: SMA, desmin, h-caldesmon positive; MDM2, CD117, HMB-45, DOG1 negative.

   • Local and regional recurrence common; distant spread frequent. Needle biopsy underestimates
     aggressiveness → aggressive multimodal approach; negative margins with adjuvant therapy (for
     distant microscopic disease) give the best prognosis.
 Malignant Fibrous Histiocytoma/Undifferentiated Pleomorphic Sarcoma
   • Fibroblastic and histiocyte-like cells; fifth and sixth decades; about a quarter of soft-tissue sarcomas.
     Often silent and large at detection.
   • Radiology: heterogeneous soft-tissue mass (central necrosis, haemorrhage, myxoid degeneration);
     absence of fat separates it from liposarcoma; vascular invasion rare; peripheral nodular enhancement
     of a lobulated mass; calcification in about 10%.
   • Metastasis to lung, liver and bone.
   • Histology: spindle fibroblasts, round histiocyte-like cells, lymphocytes, foam cells, giant cells;
     pseudoangiomatoid pattern; plasma cell and lymphocytic infiltrate; fibrous pseudocapsule.
   • Types: storiform-pleomorphic and myxoid (high grade) versus giant cell, angiomatoid and inflammatory
     (more often low grade).
   • Treatment is surgical in the absence of metastasis: wide resection with negative margins reduces local
     and distant relapse and improves survival. Adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy is unproven; 5-year
     OS about half.

 Synovial Sarcomas
   • Uncommon in the RP; all ages. Arise from kidney, adrenal or other viscera; nerve origin can mimic
     MPNST.
   • Large and asymptomatic; intratumoral calcification hints at the diagnosis. Rarely follows prior
     radiation.
   • Mostly encapsulated with cystic degeneration; herringbone spindle cell pattern; mitoses vary with
     grade; glandular, cystic or round cell differentiation causes confusion.
   • TLE1 positive in about 90% (not exclusive); t(X;18) in most.
   • Outcome depends on grade; favourable: younger age, diffuse calcification.
 Solitary Fibrous Tumours
   • Formerly haemangiopericytoma; fibroblastic; occur anywhere; mostly younger patients; very large
     before symptoms.
   • Doege-Potter syndrome: rare paraneoplastic hypoglycaemia from oversecretion of insulin-like growth
     factor.
   • "Pattern-less" architecture, sometimes myxoid or fatty; high mitotic index and pleomorphism mark
     aggressive behaviour (about a third). Near-uniform NAB2-STAT6 fusion is unique to this tumour.
   • Mostly indolent, but clinicopathological features predict biology poorly — some benign-looking
     tumours metastasise. Risk factors: advancing age, high mitotic rate, necrosis, size. Overtly malignant
     cases do poorly; 8-year OS about 75%.

   • Complete en bloc resection is standard, preserving adjacent organs where feasible (wide margins not
     routinely needed). Radiosensitive → perioperative radiotherapy as adjunct.
 Ewing and Ewing-Like Sarcomas
   • Primarily bone tumours; malignant small round blue cell tumours, rarely in RP soft tissue. EWSR1
     translocation with the ETS transcription factor family.
   • Young adult White men; neuropathic pain when adjacent to nerves.
   • Infiltrative margins, unlike other sarcomas → surgery alone rarely curative; standard is combination
     chemotherapy within multimodal therapy, cure rates above 50%.
   • Poor prognosis: larger size, metastasis, little necrosis after chemotherapy; in Ewing-like sarcoma, CIC
     rearrangement.
 Desmoplastic Small, Round Cell Tumours
   • Translocation-associated small round cell sarcoma: t(11;22)(p13;q12) WT1-EWSR1; staining for the
     fusion product is diagnostic.
   • Young men; multifocal peritoneal implants; abdominal pain and distension; heterogeneous
     enhancement with central necrosis; ascites typical.
   • Prognosis universally poor despite multimodal therapy.
 Unclassified Sarcomas
   • No defining line of differentiation — diagnosis of exclusion, up to 20% of cases; prior radiotherapy in a
     quarter.
   • Rapid growth, aggressive course, universally poor prognosis. Screen for MDM2 to exclude de-
     differentiated liposarcoma and avoid misclassification.
 Perivascular Epithelioid Cell Tumours
   • Family: renal and extrarenal angiomyolipoma (AML), extrapulmonary clear cell tumour, clear cell
     myomelanocytic tumour, pigmented melanotic tumour, lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM), malignant
     PEComa — hence diverse behaviour.
   • Malignant features: size more than 5 cm; high cellularity and atypia; mitoses more than 1 per 50 HPF;
     necrosis; vascular invasion.
   • Mostly sporadic; tuberous sclerosis accounts for the remainder; TFE3 rearrangements in a subset,
     usually younger patients.
   • Largely chemoresistant → wide local excision when adverse features are present; unresectable or
     metastatic → mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors (temsirolimus, everolimus, sirolimus)
     give responses and prolonged survival.
 Desmoid Tumours
   • Fibroblastic, locally advanced; rapid growth and disruption of fascial planes → ureteric or bowel
     obstruction. Peak third decade. Radiologically heterogeneous, hard to distinguish from sarcoma.
     Spindle cells, extracellular collagen, myxoid matrix.
   • Do not metastasise, but recur locally in up to half despite wide resection.
   • Surgery for resectable disease; observation is appropriate where growth would not change the surgical
     outcome or cause functional limitation.
 Concept — Among RP sarcomas, treatment is dictated less by size than by how the tumour spreads:
 locally recurrent types (liposarcoma, desmoid) are won or lost at the margin, whereas infiltrative or
 systemic types (Ewing, desmoplastic small round cell tumour, pleomorphic liposarcoma) need
 chemotherapy because no margin is achievable.

 HAEMATOLOGICAL CONDITIONS/LYMPHOMAS OF THE RETROPERITONEUM
   • A significant share of RP masses. Lymphoma is commonest; extramedullary myeloma or plasmacytoma
     and other lymphoproliferative diseases also occur.

 Lymphomas
   • Almost a third of primary RP tumours. Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin types, with different prognoses.
   • Circumscribed, confluent, commonly homogeneous masses along the RP and pelvic nodal chains.
   • Key issue: they resemble other RP tumours → FNA and/or core biopsy; they rarely, if ever, need
     surgery and are treated with chemotherapy.

 Plasmacytoma
   • One spectrum: focal = plasmacytoma; diffuse = multiple myeloma. Myeloma is the commonest
     primary bone malignancy in adults; extramedullary disease in <10% of new diagnoses — abdomen/
     pelvis, skin and soft tissue, paraspinal regions; the RP is a rare site.
   • Serology: raised immunoglobulin G (IgG); serum electrophoresis "M spike" — myeloma protein
     impairing immunity, coagulation and renal function.

   • From serous or mucinous secretion, or degeneration after chemotherapy or radiotherapy. History is

 SURGICAL MANAGEMENT OF THE RETROPERITONEAL TUMOUR

 Preoperative Considerations
   • No bowel preparation or dietary modification.
   • GCT: serum tumour markers within 7–10 days of surgery.
   • Extra blood products for complex resections.
   • Sperm banking for men wanting future paternity when RP masses lie in the path of the postganglionic
     sympathetic fibres.
   • Bleomycin: a medical oncology partner assesses toxicity, limits dose and obtains pulmonary function
     tests to reduce postoperative acute respiratory distress syndrome; the anaesthetist must know of
     prior bleomycin — low fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) and conservative fluid resuscitation.
   • Imaging: CT abdomen and pelvis reviewed at consultation and again immediately before surgery;
     current chest CT if prior pulmonary masses, planned thoracic resection, or radiological/serological
     progression. Imaging within 6 weeks of surgery. Careful review usually prevents unplanned
     intraoperative consultations.
   • Total IVC thrombosis identified preoperatively simplifies the operation — the IVC is resected.
     Incomplete occlusion requiring resection may need reconstruction with cadaveric allograft or ringed
     polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) graft, provided there is no bowel contamination.

 Surgical Technique
   • Orogastric tube suffices; nasogastric tube only for duodenal resection/repair or high-volume masses
     needing complete mesenteric mobilisation with bowel on the chest.

   • Four postganglionic efferent sympathetic fibres (L1–L4) mediate antegrade ejaculation; anatomy
     varies widely. L2 and L3 are usually fused. L2–L4 run anteriorly along the aorta and common iliac

     anterior surface immediately caudal to the IMA → Kittner sponge sweeps fat to reveal shiny, off-white

     trace cranially.
   • Right fibres: seen as precaval and interaortocaval tissue is rolled medially off the IVC, running
     obliquely anteriorly and inferiorly to the superior hypogastric plexus → cleared, looped and traced to
     the right trunk.

     released from the body wall. Fibres leave the trunk close to lumbar vessels → at risk when controlling
     lumbar bleeding.
   • After bilateral nerve-sparing, or unilateral nerve-sparing with contralateral complete resection, a
     temporary anejaculatory period may occur — probably neuropraxia.

 AUXILIARY PROCEDURES
   • Relevant to advanced, bulky RP disease; rarely needed in low-volume disease. Nephrectomy is
     commonest, then vascular resection or reconstruction; the likelihood of adjacent-organ resection rises
     with RP tumour volume.

 Nephrectomy
   • Commonest auxiliary procedure at PC-RPLND: 5–31%; in primary RP sarcoma, up to 55%.
   • Risk factors: high-risk settings — salvage, desperation, late-relapse or reoperative RPLND; RP mass
     size (mass >10 cm carries far higher odds than <2 cm); left primary with left para-aortic mass.
   • Renal function declines after nephrectomy, but without need for renal replacement therapy and
     without compromising later chemotherapy; progression to chronic kidney disease depends on age
     and preoperative glomerular filtration rate.

   • Usually bulky disease. Lower-limb oedema and imaging suggesting IVC compression/occlusion →
     cavography, US or MRI to assess flow and guide intraoperative decisions.
   • Why caval ligation is tolerated in testis cancer: slow tumour growth and desmoplastic reaction to
     chemotherapy occlude the IVC gradually, allowing venous collaterals — morbidity is lower than after
     acute occlusion, especially when complete occlusion with collaterals exists preoperatively.

     thrombose in 10–40%; anticoagulation varies — consider at least 3 months.

   • Intraluminal thrombi are infrequent in GCT and sarcoma: vena cava commonest, then renal vein, aorta

     in nearly all.
   • Histology: bland thrombus, necrosis, teratoma, residual cancer — nearly half contain teratoma or
     viable cancer.
 Concept — The chronicity of the obstruction decides reconstruction: a gradually occluded IVC has
 collaterals and can be ligated, whereas the aorta carries obligatory flow and must always be replaced.

 COMPLICATIONS

 Ejaculatory Function
   • Modified unilateral template without nerve-sparing: early series 75–87% antegrade ejaculation;
     contemporary 97%; with ipsilateral nerve-sparing 99% — gains from avoiding contralateral fibres
     caudal to the IMA.
   • Nerve-sparing RPLND: antegrade ejaculation 90–100%.
   • Temporary anejaculation after bilateral templates reflects neuropraxia; it was not seen when unilateral
     templates with ipsilateral nerve-sparing were used.

 Vascular
   • Major vascular resection modestly increases vascular morbidity: after caval reconstruction,
     perioperative thrombosis in about one in five (deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism).
   • Venous thromboembolism after RPLND is low (young, healthy patients): pulmonary embolism <1%
     after primary RPLND, 0.1–3.1% after PC-RPLND; deep vein thrombosis 0–1% (under-reported, often
     asymptomatic).

 Chylous Ascites — Risk Factors
   • Suprahilar resection — disruption of the cisterna chyli (L1–L2, medial to the posterior aorta,
     retrocrural) and its tributaries.
   • IVC resection — raised infracaval venous pressure → capillary leak and third-spacing of lymph.
   • More preoperative chemotherapy cycles, greater blood loss, longer operation.
   • Trap: left suprahilar dissection can injure the pancreatic tail, also causing large-volume ascites → check
     drained fluid for amylase and lipase.
 Chylous Ascites — Graduated Management
   • Paracentesis + low-fat/medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) diet ± intramuscular octreotide →
     reaccumulation: indwelling drain → if dietary measures already in place: nil by mouth + total
     parenteral nutrition (TPN) → persistent drainage >100 mL/24 h (exceedingly rare): continued
     conservative management, peritoneovenous (LeVeen) shunt, or surgical ligation of the leak — the last
     two are last resorts (shunt occlusion or malfunction needing revision, sepsis, fat embolism) → after
     resolution, continue low-fat MCT diet for 1–3 months.
   • Octreotide is unstudied in urology but reduces chyle leak after hepaticopancreaticobiliary surgery.

 Neurological
   • After PC-RPLND, paraplegia is not seen; peripheral nerve injuries arise from positioning and retractor
     placement (femoral neuropraxia) — careful positioning prevents them.
   • Paraplegia occurs after resection of bulky combined mediastinal and RP disease; neurological risk rises
     with the extent of para-aortic resection.
   • Primary RP sarcoma: about 5% need major lumbar nerve resection, usually because the sarcoma
     arises from the nerve root. Commonest: femoral and obturator; sensory: genitofemoral, ilioinguinal.
     Femoral nerve loss → knee extension weakness, sometimes needing a hinged knee brace.

 Postoperative Care
   • Drains not routine; a Jackson-Pratt drain for large-volume RP, retrocrural or duodenal resections,
     removed once on a regular diet with serous drainage <100 mL/24 h.
   • Without bowel repair: ice chips evening of surgery → day 1 unlimited clear fluids, chair and ambulation
     → day 2 regular diet, off intravenous analgesia → discharge days 2–5; longer after larger resections.

   KEY POINTS
    • Surgery for an RP mass requires consideration of all risks and obstacles and a planned approach to
      exposure and mobilisation.
    • Advance preparation and coordination of consultation for possible auxiliary procedures are

 Concept — Most complications of RP surgery are functions of extent: suprahilar and caval work drive
 chylous ascites, bilateral and caudal-to-IMA dissection drives anejaculation, and para-aortic and nerve-
 root resection drive neurological loss.

 Radiation
   • Use of radiotherapy with RP sarcoma resection has grown, aiming to reduce local recurrence,
     extrapolated from extremity sarcoma. Timing, dose and field remain debated; long-term toxicity and
     recurrence patterns need study.

 Chemotherapy and Systemic Therapies
   • Outside platinum chemotherapy for metastatic GCT, data for systemic therapy in localised RP sarcoma
     are limited; the commonest RP sarcomas — liposarcoma and leiomyosarcoma — are largely
     chemoresistant. Some histologies are treated primarily by chemotherapy (lymphoma), so accurate
     characterisation is pivotal.
   • In non-metastatic disease the question is whether neoadjuvant therapy reduces distant spread,
     improves resectability and reduces positive margins.
   • Responsive histologies: myxoid liposarcoma, synovial sarcoma, Ewing sarcoma. Standard neoadjuvant
     chemotherapy is recommended only for Ewing sarcoma.
   • Resectable tumour: neoadjuvant benefit uncertain. Resectability in doubt, or regional/distant spread:
     consider multimodal therapy with induction chemotherapy.
   • Minimally responsive tumours (WD liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma): resect local, regional and/or
     metastatic disease when feasible in selected cases; otherwise clinical trials.
   • Early successes: MDM2 and CDK4 inhibitors in liposarcoma; tyrosine kinase inhibitors in refractory
     soft-tissue sarcoma; immune-modulating therapy.
   • STRASS II (ongoing): neoadjuvant doxorubicin + ifosfamide (leiomyosarcoma: doxorubicin +
     dacarbazine) plus surgery vs surgery alone.

 Concept — Testicular GCT is the model of protocolised multimodal therapy; RP sarcoma is its opposite —

 CONCLUSIONS
   • The RP mass needs careful initial characterisation because some entities follow divergent, non-surgical
     pathways.
   • Metastatic GCT: standardised pathways; refer to experienced centres. RP sarcoma: likewise, given
     rarity, variable behaviour and multidisciplinary needs; the role of neoadjuvant or adjuvant
     chemotherapy or radiotherapy is limited by data.
   • As most RP lesions ultimately need surgery, familiarity with their clinical, radiological and histological
     features and perioperative needs is essential.
