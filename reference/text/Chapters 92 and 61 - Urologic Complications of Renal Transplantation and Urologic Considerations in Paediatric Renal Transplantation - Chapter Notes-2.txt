 Urologic Complications of Renal Transplantation
   • Renal transplantation is the most cost-effective treatment for end-stage renal disease (ESRD),
     improving survival and quality of life.
   • Urologic complications are the commonest surgical adverse event after transplantation, at 8% to
     9.5%, with high morbidity and cost.
   • The majority arise from the transplanted ureter and the ureterovesical anastomosis — the fact that
     organises the whole subject.

 HAEMATURIA
   • Immediate postoperative haematuria comes almost always from the ureterovesical anastomosis,
     resolves over several days, and rarely needs bladder irrigation.
   • It is commoner after the Leadbetter anastomosis than after other types.
   • Persistent haematuria after allograft biopsy means an arteriovenous–collecting system fistula until
     proven otherwise: duplex ultrasound diagnoses, magnetic resonance angiography confirms.
   • Most such fistulae close spontaneously; those that do not need highly selective embolisation.
   • Asymptomatic microscopic haematuria follows American Urological Association guidance, with one
     transplant-specific addition: BK virus — suspected from viral cytopathic changes on cytology,
     confirmed by urinary BK titres.

 URETERAL STENT MANAGEMENT

 Timing of Stent Removal
   • Randomised trials settled that routine stenting across the ureterovesical anastomosis reduces urine
     leak and ureteral stricture.
   • The cost is bacterial colonisation — roughly a quarter of stents, regardless of dwell time. The resulting
     urinary tract infection (UTI) threatens graft infection and graft failure, which is why timing matters at
     all.
   • Meta-analysis shows removal before the third postoperative week reduces UTI without increasing
     leak or stenosis; repeat meta-analysis in 2022 confirmed it and fixed the optimum at 14 to 21 days.
   • The counterweight: removal before 14 days increases urinary leak. Hence a window, not a date.
   • Timing remains individualised — surgeon satisfaction with the anastomosis, and recipient risk factors:
     diabetes mellitus, transplant revision, current UTI.
   • Prophylaxis at removal is unresolved. Patients already on trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for
     Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia appear protected even where resistance is high, and additional
     prophylaxis adds nothing; those not on it may benefit from cover at removal.

 Retained Stent
   • Two mechanisms: the ureteroneocystostomy suture has caught the stent, or encrustation (incidence
     up to 5.7%, a function of prolonged dwell time).
   • A trapped suture leaves two options: wait for suture dissolution, or transect the stitch endoscopically.
   • Allograft denervation abolishes the classic obstructive symptoms. The encrusted stent therefore
     presents as progressive decline in renal function, falling urine output, recurrent UTIs; rarely a vague
     ache over the allograft from peritoneal irritation.

 Prevention
   • Stent encrustation is the textbook illustration of Reason's Swiss cheese model — a chain of latent
     errors: undocumented insertion, poor team communication, and inadequate counselling on the stent's
     temporary function, its complications, and the need for timely removal.
   • Log books, stent cards, computerised and web-based registries and smartphone applications have all
     been studied; none is completely reliable.

 Diagnosis and Treatment
   • Computed tomography (CT) in every case, to define encrustation burden and distribution and thereby
     the approach.
   • First step is urinary diversion by percutaneous nephrostomy — decompression, recovery of function,
     and antegrade access in one move.
   • Clearance then uses percutaneous nephrolithotomy, retrograde intrarenal surgery and extracorporeal
     shock wave lithotripsy in combination.

   KEY POINTS: HAEMATURIA AND URETERAL STENT MANAGEMENT
     • Most postoperative haematuria is self-limiting.
     • Consider duplex ultrasound for severe or persistent haematuria after renal biopsy, to exclude
       arteriovenous fistula.
     • A ureteral stent decreases ureteral stricture and urinary leak.
     • Individualised stent removal around 3 weeks after transplantation.


 URINARY LEAK
   • The commonest early urologic complication, at 1.2% to 8.9%.
   • The dominant mechanism is compromise of the distal ureteric blood supply during procurement,
     causing ureteric necrosis. Risk rises with advanced donor age, extended-criteria donors, multiple
     allograft arteries, and failure to reimplant the lower polar artery.

 BOX 92.1 ADDITIONAL CAUSES OF URINARY LEAK
   • An excessively long ureter
   • Premature removal of bladder and/or ureteric drainage
   • Technical problem — suture dehiscence, ureteric twisting, kinking
   • Acute urinary retention or bladder catheter obstruction
   • Necrosis of renal parenchyma
   • Parenchymal perforation during double-J stent placement

   • The box divides into three mechanisms — ischaemia, technical error, and back-pressure from failed
     drainage. Each is preventable, which is why leak rates are surgeon-dependent.

 Diagnosis
   • With a drain: high drain output, falling urine output, rising serum creatinine.
   • Without one: wound leakage, wound dehiscence, scrotal swelling, pelvic or abdominal pain.
   • Ultrasound or non-enhanced CT finds the collection; a fluid creatinine far above the serum creatinine
     — on aspirate, drain fluid or wound discharge — proves it is urine.

 Treatment
   • The principle is to divert urine away from the extravasation, protect graft function and stabilise the
     patient: Foley catheter plus percutaneous nephrostomy.
   • The nephrostomy is both diagnostic (antegrade nephrostogram) and therapeutic (antegrade stent
     access) where a stent is absent or non-functional.
   • Retrograde stenting is the harder route — angulation to the neo-orifice, orifice oedema, and the risk
     of further disrupting the anastomosis.
   • Percutaneous drainage for a substantial urinoma, obstruction caused by it, or infection.
   • Small anastomotic leaks: contrast studies every 1 to 2 weeks, a Foley catheter to aid healing, and
     removal of catheter and nephrostomy once resolved. Follow diligently — ureteric stenosis follows
     later.
   • Explore early for a proximal large leak, or a leak persisting despite maximal drainage. Four reasons:
     conservative success is low, adhesions have not formed, fibrosis is minimal, and later stenosis becomes
     less likely.
   • Reconstruction: tension-free ureteroneocystostomy if the remnant is well vascularised; otherwise
     ureteroureterostomy to the ipsilateral native ureter, a bladder flap, or pyelovesicostomy.

 URETERAL STRICTURE
   • The predominant cause of ureteral obstruction beyond 3 months, affecting 0.5% to 9%.
   • The primary cause is ischaemia — which is why every risk factor below is a perfusion insult or a healing
     insult.
   • Risk factors: cold ischaemia above 435 min, acute rejection, postoperative urinoma or abscess, delayed
     graft function, multiple allograft arteries, advanced donor age, and — unique to transplantation — BK
     virus and cytomegalovirus.
   • Stricture rates do not differ by anastomotic technique, nor between brain-dead and donation-after-
     cardiac-death donors.

 Diagnosis
   • Suspect on any deterioration of renal function, particularly with hydronephrosis of the transplant.

   • Hydronephrosis may be less than expected, because surrounding fibrosis stops the system expanding
     — hence antegrade nephrostogram or a Whitaker test.

 Treatment
   • The priority is decompression, to let the allograft recover — nephrostomy, nephroureteral stent,
     antegrade or retrograde stent.
   • Antegrade access is preferred: retrograde insertion is hampered by neo-orifice position, stricture
     location and tortuosity, while antegrade nephrostomy gives a detailed nephrostogram and versatile
     instrument access. Once a stent is in, retrograde exchange is straightforward, so long-term
     nephrostomy is unnecessary.
   • Endoscopic versus open depends on performance status, comorbidity, and stricture length and
     location.
   • Endoscopic ranking: antegrade double-J alone performs poorly; balloon dilation is the most studied
     and moderately durable; endoureterotomy does better — holmium laser ahead of the Acucise
     cutting balloon, and both ahead of the resectoscope T-loop.
   • Open repair remains the most effective modality, with superior long-term results. Options by size and
     site: allograft ureteric reimplantation, ureteroureterostomy to the ipsilateral native ureter, Boari flap,
     appendiceal or ileal interposition, pyelovesicostomy.
   • Robot-assisted translation of these repairs gives comparable short-term outcomes; indocyanine green
     identifies the ureter and assesses viability.

   KEY POINTS: URETERAL STRICTURE
     • Strictures are the predominant cause of ureteral obstruction beyond 3 months after transplantation.
     • The primary cause of stricture development is ischaemia.
     • Stricture should be suspected with hydronephrosis and decreased graft function.
     • Endourologic options exist, but open reconstructive surgery offers superior long-term results.


 VESICOURETERAL REFLUX
   • Several anastomotic modifications exist to replicate an antireflux mechanism, yet reflux is
     demonstrable on voiding cystography in 50% to 86%, while clinically significant reflux occurs in under
     1% — and that figure is independent of anastomotic technique. The modifications therefore change
     the radiology, not the clinical problem.
   • Sequelae are unclear: one study found more hypertension at 5 years and a trend to urosepsis.
   • Causes: technical error at ureteroneocystostomy, bladder outlet obstruction, or high-pressure storage
     from detrusor overactivity or poor compliance.

 Diagnosis
   • Recurrent UTI prompts voiding cystography; once reflux is confirmed, exclude bladder outlet
     obstruction and high-pressure storage.

 Treatment
   • Therapy targets the underlying cause, not the reflux, in most cases; outlet obstruction and high-
     pressure storage are treated conventionally.
   • Traditional surgery is revision of the anastomosis or reimplantation into the ipsilateral native ureter.

   • Endoscopic submucosal injection (Teflon or Macroplastique) succeeds in low-grade and fails in high-
     grade reflux, so it is confined to clinically significant grade I to II disease.
   • High-grade reflux, or failed endoscopy with persistent symptoms, goes to reconstructive surgery.

 LYMPHOCELE
   • A pseudocyst of lymph within a hard fibrous capsule around the graft.
   • Radiographically evident in 0.6% to 33.9%; symptomatic in 0.03% to 26%; most develop within 6
     months, peaking at 6 weeks.
   • Mechanism: extensive dissection of lymphatics around the recipient iliac vessels or the donor renal
     vessels, at procurement or on the back table.
   • Associations: obesity, recipient age, dialysis duration, warm ischaemia time, prophylactic low-
     molecular-weight heparin, delayed graft function, acute rejection, re-transplantation, mammalian
     target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors.
   • Most are silent. Large ones compress: unilateral limb oedema, graft dysfunction, bladder
     compression, fever, deep vein thrombosis from external iliac vein compression.

 Diagnosis
   • Ultrasound or CT shows the collection; biochemical and microbiologic analysis of drain fluid or a
     guided aspirate separates lymphocele from urinoma, seroma and abscess.

 Treatment
   • Most resolve spontaneously: fewer than 15% need treatment.
   • Efficacy runs aspiration alone worst, drain placement and sclerotherapy intermediate, laparoscopic
     decortication with peritoneal window best, open surgery close behind.
   • Open drainage for wound complications, and for a small lymphocele adjacent to vital renal structures
     — the two settings where laparoscopy is unsafe.

 NEPHROLITHIASIS
   • Incidence about 1%, mean onset 28 ± 22 months; calcium-based commonest, then struvite, then uric
     acid.
   • Lithogenic factors: secondary hyperparathyroidism, recurrent UTI, and metabolic abnormality —
     hypercalciuria, hyperuricosuria, hypocitraturia, hyperoxaluria.
   • Drug effects on the urinary environment: ciclosporin causes chronic hyperuricaemia; calcineurin
     inhibitors cause hyperoxaluria and hypocitraturia.

 Treatment
   • Follows the native-kidney algorithm. Asymptomatic non-obstructing stones under 4 mm may be
     observed with serial ultrasound and creatinine.
   • Growing or symptomatic: extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy, retrograde intrarenal surgery,
     percutaneous nephrolithotomy, or combinations.
   • Afterwards: watch for silent hydronephrosis following instrumentation — denervation again removes
     the warning — and screen metabolically.

 URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS
   • The commonest infectious complication, causing 30% of sepsis admissions; prevalence 7% to 80%,
     highest in the first 6 months.
   • No sex difference immediately after transplantation, but a significantly higher rate in females by 3
     years.
   • Transplant-specific risks: prolonged stent, reflux, rejection, poor graft function, high prednisolone
     dose.
   • Definitions are unchanged from non-transplant practice: asymptomatic bacteriuria; simple cystitis;
     pyelonephritis or complicated UTI (fever, chills, haemodynamic change, allograft pain, or bacteraemia
     with the urinary pathogen); recurrent UTI as three or more in 12 months.
   • Screening has not been shown to reduce complications beyond 2 months post-transplant, and
     treating asymptomatic bacteriuria is poorly supported — except where creatinine has risen
     unexplained.
   • Acute cystitis: empirical fluoroquinolone, amoxicillin-clavulanate, third-generation cephalosporin or
     nitrofurantoin (creatinine clearance above 30 mL/min), then tailored. 5 to 10 days; avoid shorter
     courses.
   • Complicated UTI or pyelonephritis: 14 to 21 days, allowing for greater drug resistance in recipients,
     and confirm the kidney is draining.
   • Recurrent UTI demands structural evaluation — anatomy, reflux, bladder dysfunction, outflow
     obstruction.
   • Non-antibiotic measures are poorly studied here; vaginal oestrogen is recommended for
     postmenopausal recipients by extrapolation.

 LOWER URINARY TRACT COMPLICATIONS

 Voiding Dysfunction
   • Common after transplantation and concealed before it: oliguria masks outlet obstruction and
     incontinence, so the problem only declares itself once urine output returns.
   • Three interacting contributors: small bladder capacity (under 300 mL), pre-existing anatomic
     abnormality, and a direct effect of immunosuppression reducing bladder mass.
   • Evaluation: urologic and neurologic history, previous urologic surgery, full urologic and gynaecologic
     examination, with International Continence Society questionnaires, the International Prostate
     Symptom Score, and a voiding diary. Uroflowmetry, urodynamics and cystoscopy are used selectively
     by risk and symptoms.

 Small Bladder After Transplant
   • Capacity in anuric recipients grows back to normal after transplantation, and the consequence is
     explicit: pretransplant bladder rehabilitation or surgical augmentation is unnecessary.
   • Where symptoms affect quality of life: anticholinergics, then botulinum toxin for insufficient capacity
     or high storage pressure; augmentation or ileal conduit for refractory cases.

 Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia
   • An age-related disease, therefore commoner in recipients, and hard to detect in anuric patients.
     Three-year incidence 9.7%, associated with increased UTI and graft loss.

   • Where significant obstruction is suspected: α-blocker, aggressive treatment of constipation before
     catheter removal, and in selected cases a suprapubic tube at transplantation.
   • Resecting the prostate before transplantation, while anuric or oliguric, risks bladder neck contracture
     and urethral stricture — healing without urine flow is the mechanism.
   • Transurethral resection or holmium enucleation is safe and effective at 3 weeks or more after
     transplantation.
   • Avoid transurethral resection in the first 2 weeks and with a ureteral stent in place — sepsis and
     death have followed.

 Urinary Incontinence
   • Prevalence among female recipients 28%.
   • Stress incontinence is diagnosed and treated as in non-transplant patients: tension-free vaginal tape,
     transobturator tape, sacral neuromodulation and laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy are all reported as
     feasible and safe.
   • An artificial urinary sphincter after radical prostatectomy is feasible in recipients, on limited evidence.

 ERECTILE DYSFUNCTION AND HYPOGONADISM
   • Erectile dysfunction reaches 87.5% of men with ESRD. Transplantation improves it, yet 20% to 50%
     remain affected, with only a modest gain in International Index of Erectile Function score and no
     significant improvement where pretransplant dysfunction was severe.
   • The mechanism is unclear; earlier transplantation does better, probably by pre-empting penile
     vasculopathy.
   • Treatment is identical to non-transplant practice. Hypogonadism occurs in ESRD and testosterone
     improves after transplantation.

 GENITOURINARY MALIGNANCIES

 Renal Cell Carcinoma
   • Incidence exceeds the general population — 4.6% of post-transplant cancers against 3% of tumours
     generally.
   • Only 10% arise in the graft; the rest are in the native kidneys. Mean time to a de novo graft tumour is
     131 months.
   • Graft tumours are favourable: papillary histology predominates, and most non-papillary graft tumours
     are low grade (Fuhrman 1 and 2).
   • Treatment mirrors the general population. No recommendation exists on modifying
     immunosuppression after a cancer diagnosis, though for metastatic graft-origin disease some propose
     stopping immunosuppression after allograft nephrectomy, so the recipient rejects the donor-derived
     tumour cells.
   • Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) does not endorse screening the native kidneys,
     benefit being unproven against harm. Consider screening high-risk recipients only: previous renal cell
     carcinoma, analgesic nephropathy, tuberous sclerosis, acquired cystic disease.

 Prostate Cancer
   • Incidence matches the general population, though prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing does not:
     PSA is generally part of the older male recipient's pre-transplant workup, yet no national
     recommendation exists for or against screening recipients beyond general-population guidance.
   • Management is constrained by the graft's anatomic proximity to the surgical or radiation field, and by
     immunosuppression raising infection, lymphocele, impaired healing and wound complications.
   • Most treated recipients have organ-confined disease, Gleason score 6 or less. Surgical extirpation is
     commonest, then radiotherapy, then active surveillance — which is underused.
   • Oncologic outcomes across modalities match non-transplant patients.

 Bladder Cancer
   • Risk is roughly threefold (standardised incidence ratio 3.18; P = .008), and the disease is more
     aggressive — higher recurrence, progression and metastasis, so aggressive surveillance is necessary.
     Mean interval from transplantation to diagnosis 2.8 to 4 years.
   • Intravesical bacillus Calmette-Guérin remains usable in selected high-risk non-muscle-invasive
     disease despite immunosuppression.
   • Muscle-invasive disease: chemoradiotherapy, or radical cystoprostatectomy with urinary diversion.
     At cystectomy and node dissection, protect the graft vessels, the ureteric blood supply and the ureter.

   KEY POINTS: GENITOURINARY MALIGNANCIES
     • Most kidney tumours develop in the native kidneys and are low grade.
     • Active surveillance, radiation and surgery are all reasonable for prostate cancer in recipients.
     • Recipients carry a threefold risk of urothelial carcinoma, and it is biologically more aggressive.


 CONCLUSION
   • Recipients face multiple urologic complications, but management largely mirrors the general
     population, with defined special considerations.
   • Diligence in recognition and treatment is what secures survival of graft and host.

 Urologic Considerations in Paediatric Renal
 Transplantation
   • The defining contrast with adults: children needing kidney replacement therapy (KRT) usually have a
     congenital anomaly of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), including obstructive uropathy — not
     hypertension and diabetes.
   • The paediatric urologist's task is to ensure the condition that damaged the native kidneys does not go
     on to damage the transplant.
   • Children and adolescents need a multidisciplinary team, ideally with urological involvement before
     the onset of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD).
   • Five goals: unimpeded drainage from kidney to lower tract; a capacious, low-pressure reservoir
     holding enough for socially acceptable continence; volitional emptying; absence of infection; the
     fewest surgical procedures.
   • A reactive approach — referral only after an allograft is in and already in jeopardy — is suboptimal
     and should be avoided.
   • Early lower tract optimisation makes transplantation smooth; pre- and postsurgical assessment and
     monitoring drive early detection of complications and good long-term outcomes.
   • Loss of function from chronic rejection is unavoidable, but every additional insult accelerates it, and
     diligent post-transplant care limits that.

 PRETRANSPLANT ASSESSMENT
   • An important proportion of children needing KRT have uropathy: congenital obstruction,
     vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), or renal dysplasia, hypoplasia or aplasia.
   • Commonest age at paediatric transplantation is 13 to 17 years. Commonest congenital conditions are
     hypoplasia, aplasia and dysplasia, and obstructive uropathy; the commonest acquired cause is focal
     segmental glomerulosclerosis. Adult series are led instead by hypertension and diabetes, which rarely
     cause ESKD in the young.
   • The age split: younger children have dysplasia or obstructive uropathy — chiefly posterior urethral
     valves (PUVs) in boys — while nephrologic causes and neuropathic bladder dysfunction present in
     older children, adolescents and young adults.
   • Screen all children before transplantation, accepting a low yield where the cause is clearly
     nephrologic. History, renal ultrasound, and uroflow with postvoid residual (if toilet trained) exclude
     most abnormalities.
   • Routine voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) is not necessary. Consider it for neuropathic bladder,
     obstructive uropathy, VUR, prior lower tract surgery, recurrent febrile urinary tract infections (UTIs),
     unexplained worsening hydroureteronephrosis, or difficult-to-treat voiding abnormalities.

 Focused Assessment
   • Assessment is directed by the underlying condition and the status of bladder and kidneys.
   • VCUG shows anatomic change with filling and emptying, the urethra, and the presence and grade of
     reflux. Abnormal dynamics on any test justify urodynamics.
   • Indications for urodynamics: neuropathic bladder; outlet obstruction from PUVs or a large ureterocele;
     and selected cases with recalcitrant voiding dysfunction, high-grade hydronephrosis, or recurrent UTI.

   • Urodynamics assess capacity, compliance, detrusor activity, sensation, emptying and sphincter
     function — to characterise dynamics that place the allograft at risk and define the therapy needed.
   • Therapy that follows: antimuscarinics, α-blockers or β3-adrenoceptor agonists; clean intermittent
     catheterisation (CIC); intradetrusor botulinum toxin; lower tract reconstruction including
     augmentation.
   • Emptying matters as much as storage, because it governs infection risk — and treating poor
     compliance medically can itself impair emptying and force CIC.
   • Where the urethra is sensate, or difficult to access or negotiate, a continent catheterisable stoma is
     the best route to effective CIC.
   • Urodynamics are never a one-off: repeat testing tracks response and change over time.

   KEY POINTS: UROLOGIC EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT
     • Goals: low-pressure, unimpeded drainage into the bladder or reservoir; low-pressure storage for a
       socially acceptable time; volitional emptying without incontinence; absence of infection; fewest
       procedures and least trauma.
     • A large fraction of children needing KRT have uropathy — congenital obstruction, VUR, or renal
       dysplasia.
     • Pretransplant assessment is directed by the underlying condition and the status of bladder and
       kidneys.
     • Urodynamics assess capacity, compliance, emptying and sphincter function.
     • Indications include neuropathic bladder; a history of PUVs; and recalcitrant voiding dysfunction,
       high-grade hydronephrosis or recurrent UTI.


 PRETRANSPLANT PREPARATION
   • The commonest early bladder abnormalities in ESKD are low capacity, detrusor overactivity, and
     poor compliance.
   • This is the typical initial picture in PUVs, but bladder dynamics evolve: from increased contractility,
     through amelioration with better capacity, to insufficient contractility and inefficient emptying —
     myogenic failure.
   • Overactivity and poor compliance are the dangerous patterns, because high pressure reaches the
     kidneys even without reflux. Failure to address them puts the allograft at risk.

 Detrusor Overactivity, Low Capacity and Poor Compliance
   • The goal is to store up to estimated capacity for age at detrusor pressures below 40 cm H₂O during
     filling or at leakage, with social continence.
   • Initial management is medical — antimuscarinics. Surgery, augmentation included, is second line.
   • Medical management often needs CIC; children may try to avoid it by double voiding or Valsalva
     emptying, and α-blockers may aid emptying.
   • Never assume volitional emptying is effective — demonstrate it with repeated low postvoid residuals
     and uroflowmetry.
   • Oxybutynin is titrated upward against side effects, watching catheterised volumes, incontinence and
     hydronephrosis. Tolerability improves with selective antimuscarinics (solifenacin, darifenacin), β3
     agonists (mirabegron), or intradetrusor botulinum toxin.

   • Compliance with pretransplant CIC is an excellent test of adherence to the demands of
     transplantation; a family that cannot manage CIC may not manage the rest of post-transplant care.

 Capacity
   • There has been a trend away from augmentation toward less invasive medical management —
     perhaps to the extreme. Augmentation remains valuable in selected failures of medical
     management.
   • Current indications: a small, non-compliant, high-pressure bladder, and failure of medical therapy
     plus CIC to achieve low-pressure storage for up to 3 to 4 hours. Where pressures are safe only briefly,
     or regularly exceed 40 cm H₂O despite maximal therapy and catheterisation, augment.
   • No compelling evidence that augmentation increases complications during or after transplantation.
   • Segment choice depends on anatomy, prior surgery, and whether a concurrent continent
     catheterisable stoma is planned.
   • Gastric augmentation was popular in the late 1980s and early 1990s on the theoretical grounds of acid
     secretion in an acidotic patient, less infection, fewer bladder stones, but is now rarely offered: it
     causes severe complications, especially in the pretransplant anuric group, through gastric secretion
     injuring the native bladder and producing the haematuria-dysuria syndrome.
   • Ileum and sigmoid are the commonest segments. Against them: reports of malignancy after
     augmentation, particularly in the transplant group — long-term surveillance is essential in all cases.
   • The augment patch rarely obstructs the transplant, but the pedicle's presence, location and
     orientation must be recognised.
   • Continent stomas must be sited clear of the anticipated transplant incision, and nearly always lie
     medial to it.
   • Most allografts go retroperitoneally through a Gibson-type incision, so intraperitoneal dissection is
     rarely needed and stomal position rarely matters. In infants and small children under 15 kg the
     vascular anastomosis may have to be fashioned at the aorta and vena cava, requiring a
     transperitoneal approach — though such children rarely have an augment or channel in place.

 Infections
   • Many of these children have recurrent UTIs. Graft pyelonephritis causes major morbidity in an
     immunocompromised host and can end in graft loss.
   • The usual cause is urinary stasis — inadequate emptying by voiding or catheterisation, persistent
     obstruction, or VUR.
   • UTIs do not improve after transplantation, so recurrent infection must be resolved in advance:
     double and triple voiding, or CIC, for timely complete emptying.
   • Differentiate true UTI from colonisation, which is exceedingly common on CIC.
   • Persistent UTI despite adequate emptying and good CIC technique means an anatomic abnormality
     or chronic colonisation. Colonisation may respond to long-term antibiotics, intravesical
     antimicrobials, or regular bladder irrigation; the anatomic culprits — diverticula and ureteral stumps
     — need targeted imaging and surgery.

 The Defunctionalised Bladder
   • A bladder that lacks cycling because of anuria or supravesical diversion. The difficulty is that its
     response to physiologic urine volumes cannot be predicted, and this is compounded by any associated
     lower tract dysfunction.

 Neuropathic bladder.
   • Becoming rare with better management, but still seen where urologic care has been suboptimal.
     Potential function is hard to predict, and a defunctionalised bladder takes time to reach its maximal
     potential.
   • Rehabilitation is by cycling, as a sequence: catheterise → instil progressively increasing volumes of
     saline → hold for a set dwell time → drain → increase volumes at regular intervals, empirically, from
     the initially tolerated volume.
   • The response indicates the safety of the native bladder as a reservoir and any ability to empty
     spontaneously; adjunctive medication is often needed to increase capacity and compliance.
   • The target is expected capacity for age by any standard formula. Goal volumes may not come
     immediately, but steady increases without discomfort or significant leakage predict further
     expansion.
   • Safe storage pressures, capacity and compliance must be confirmed formally by urodynamics, and
     only then is the child cleared for transplant.
   • A renal allograft must never be used to test whether a bladder works. If adequate storage and
     compliance cannot be reached, augmentation must be considered.
 Non-neuropathic bladder.
   • Seen with PUVs or VUR, and in anuric children without prior urologic problems — including those
     after bilateral nephrectomy.
   • These bladders can often be salvaged for storage and spontaneous voiding, but are approached
     exactly as the neuropathic one, assuming CIC may be needed long term.
   • Spontaneous emptying determines whether a continent stoma is needed — more pressing in children
     with normal sensation, who resist urethral catheterisation.
 Decision to augment.
   • Early proactive bladder management reduces or delays the need to augment. The strategy that
     identifies real candidates is regular CIC, overnight bladder drainage, and maximised medical therapy.
   • Enterocystoplasty remains one of the most effective means of providing adequate storage in
     advanced lower tract dysfunction.
   • Broad indications before transplantation: inability to protect the upper tracts and reach functional
     capacity above 75% of expected for age, at pressures below 40 cm H₂O, while catheterising no more
     often than every 3 to 4 hours. The authors concede these thresholds are somewhat arbitrary and lack
     strong data.
   • Timing is condition-specific: in PUVs, progression to myogenic failure makes it prudent to postpone
     augmentation as long as possible; a neuropathic bladder with borderline urodynamics despite
     maximal therapy may deteriorate rapidly under a robust post-transplant urine output, arguing for
     earlier reconstruction.
   • Complications of enterocystoplasty are time-dependent and accumulate across a transplanted child's
     lifespan, compounded by an immunocompromised state that impairs infection control and healing and
     raises malignancy risk. Recommending it is a major lifelong commitment and an individualised
     decision.
   • Always explore a dilated ureter for ureterocystoplasty. Outcomes are mixed and depend on ureteral
     diameter, bladder compliance and VUR, but it is preferable to enterocystoplasty on mucus, stones,
     infection and metabolic grounds.

   • Hence preserve the ureter — for augmentation, for a continent catheterisable channel, or as the target
     for allograft ureteral anastomosis.

 Cutaneous Stomas
   • The defunctionalised bladder may coexist with ileal and colon conduits, vesicostomies, pyelostomies
     or ureterostomies, and the donor ureter can be drained into these diversions.
   • Contemporary management centres on definitive bladder optimisation before transplantation.
   • Long-term stoma or transplant ureterostomy is reserved for severe neurodevelopmental
     compromise, or inability to tolerate CIC.
   • A functioning ileal loop can serve as the enterocystoplasty patch during refunctionalisation, even in
     an anuric patient. If augmentation proves unnecessary and the child still makes urine, implant the
     native kidney(s) to refunctionalise the bladder and discard the loop.

   KEY POINTS: PRETRANSPLANT PREPARATION
     • The commonest bladder abnormality with uropathy and ESKD is a low-capacity, high-pressure
       bladder with poor compliance.
     • No clear evidence that bladder augmentation increases the risk of transplant.
     • Recurrent pyelonephritis is a hazard for the transplant and is associated with graft loss.
     • Initiating CIC before transplantation serves assessment as well as emptying.
     • Refunctionalisation is best achieved by cycling, to increase capacity, determine compliance, and
       assess the family's ability to perform CIC.


 RECONSTRUCTION STRATEGIES
   • Factors to weigh before reconstructing: dialysis modality, likely graft location, timing relative to KRT,
     need for bowel surgery, status of the native kidneys.

 Dialysis Issues
   • In a child on peritoneal dialysis (PD), intraperitoneal surgery will usually force temporary or
     permanent transition to haemodialysis.
   • An intraperitoneal procedure can sometimes be done at PD catheter placement, but adding bowel
     surgery raises the risk of catheter infection and removal. Preserving peritoneal integrity is always
     desirable, PD being the best temporary KRT in children.
   • Where KRT is approaching, starting dialysis before reconstruction improves metabolic status —
     promoting healing, reducing complications, and helping the child tolerate the metabolic load of urine
     reabsorption where bowel is used.
   • Against that: extensive intra-abdominal surgery may preclude peritoneal use, and on haemodialysis
     there is risk of catheter infection with postoperative sepsis, and arteriovenous fistula injury from low
     flow or direct pressure.

 Graft Placement
   • Anticipate the future allograft site. In the very small child, where the graft may lie intraperitoneally,
     move mesenteric pedicles away from the midline.
   • Very small children rarely have had prior reconstruction, and most allografts are still accommodated
     extraperitoneally.

   • A prior psoas hitch, or extensive dissection for native ureteral reimplantation, can make ipsilateral
     iliac graft placement difficult.
   • Where alternatives are few, documentation and collaboration with the transplant team are essential:
     a detailed operative report and a diagram are of great value to future surgeons.

 Timing
   • Perform major urologic reconstruction before transplantation.
   • Allow 6 to 12 weeks for healing after enterocystoplasty or continent diversion.
   • Unilateral native nephrectomy at transplantation is reasonable, particularly ipsilateral to the graft
     site.
   • Avoid concurrent procedures in infants and small children — worse outcomes with prolonged
     anaesthesia and haemodynamic instability, and prophylactic anticoagulation against graft thrombosis
     adds bleeding risk.

 Enterocystoplasty
   • Feasible in a dialysis patient or a child approaching ESKD, and done as in any other child.
   • Segment and strategy depend on need for a continent stoma, availability of native bladder tissue, the
     bladder neck and continence mechanism, and bowel function.
   • Where continent diversion is needed for an unsalvageable bladder, there is no reason to defer it until
     after transplant — except in the child transplanted in the first 2 years of life, before continence is
     expected or beneficial.
   • The often-quoted metabolic problems of continent diversion in renal impairment are not an absolute
     contraindication, though careful medical management is needed afterwards.
   • Two directions to hold together: in advanced chronic kidney disease considered for pre-emptive
     transplantation, urine reabsorption through bowel may accelerate the need for dialysis; in less
     marginal cases, better bladder dynamics and less infection may delay progression to ESKD.

 Native Nephrectomy
   • The decision is multidisciplinary and controversial; the default is to leave the native kidneys.
   • Indications for removal before transplantation: difficult-to-control hypertension; profound nephrotic
     syndrome with malnutrition or hypercoagulability from protein loss; massive polyuria; recurrent upper
     tract infection; large stone burden; high malignancy risk; very large kidneys (for allograft space or to
     allow PD); massive VUR.
   • Massive VUR is relative — but with stasis and infection in an immunosuppressed child, removal may
     be safer than reimplantation or conservative management.
   • The approach is decided by whether the child is on PD.
       – On PD, entering the peritoneum, open or laparoscopically, requires temporary haemodialysis, and
         open surgery may scar the peritoneal membrane and make later PD ineffective.
       – Retroperitoneal nephrectomy allows immediate PD, though leaks occur if the peritoneum is
         breached.
       – For bilateral nephrectomy, the prone approach avoids repositioning and is optimal with relatively
         small kidneys.
       – On PD with large kidneys: posterior open nephrectomy, or laparoscopic nephrectomy with
         morcellation.

Urologic Considerations in Paediatric Renal Transplantation                                  Reconstruction Strategies

       – Not on PD: any approach is acceptable; laparoscopic adhesions are limited and unlikely to
         compromise future PD, and the catheter can be placed at the same procedure if PD is imminent.
       – In selected high-risk cases, renal embolisation is a reasonable alternative.
   • The case for leaving them: water excretion while on dialysis or if the graft fails, reducing fluid
     restriction; erythropoietin production and vitamin D metabolism; and avoidance of nephrectomy
     complications. Most allografts are transplanted without removal, particularly if the kidneys are small or
     atrophic.

 Limiting the Risk of Infection
   • Prevention must be proactive, not a response to systemic compromise — additive damage to the
     allograft determines long-term outcome.
   • Situations warranting active prevention: high-grade reflux; persisting hydronephrosis with or without
     reflux; dependence on intermittent catheterisation, that group being frequently colonised and more
     susceptible.
   • The non-dilated, non-refluxing native kidney is unlikely to be the source of infection.
   • Risk is multifactorial: discuss the protective value of circumcision with families of boys at risk of ESKD,
     particularly those with valves, considering it early to delay progression to chronic kidney disease and
     reduce post-transplant infection.

 Preserving the Ureter(s)
   • At nephrectomy, transect the ureter as high as possible to minimise adhesions and fibrosis around the
     iliac vessels.
   • A normal ureter should always be left — it limits dissection near the iliac vessels and preserves
     primary or salvage ureteroureterostomy for distal transplant ureteral stenosis.
   • A dilated ureter with high-grade reflux or obstruction should be removed entirely, to limit infection
     risk.
   • If bladder function is abnormal and catheterisation may be needed, preserve the ureter as a possible
     continent stoma — best done pretransplant, though creation at transplantation remains an option.

 Combining Nephrectomy and Transplantation
   • Generally avoided, to limit surgical morbidity and anaesthetic time.
   • Done mainly to create space — small children receiving an adult allograft, or very large native kidneys
     — and is most appropriate as a single ipsilateral nephrectomy through the transplant incision.

   KEY POINTS: PRETRANSPLANT RECONSTRUCTION
     • The ability to empty the bladder spontaneously determines the need for a continent catheterisable
       stoma.
     • Indications for augmentation before transplantation: capacity less than 75% of expected for age;
       detrusor pressures above 40 cm H₂O; catheterisation every 3 hours or less; maximal medical
       therapy.
     • For patients on PD, intraperitoneal surgery will likely require temporary transition to haemodialysis.
     • Any major urologic reconstruction should be undertaken well before anticipated transplantation.
     • Native nephrectomy is indicated for: difficult-to-control hypertension despite multiple medications;
       profound nephrotic syndrome with malnutrition from protein loss; high risk for malignancy;

       recurrent upper tract infections; large kidneys, to make space for the transplant; large stone burden;

 TRANSPLANTATION
   • The vascular component follows adult principles unchanged.
   • For the paediatric urologist not performing the vascular anastomosis, the lower urinary tract
     reconstruction is the focus of intraoperative attention and of urologic complications.

 Ureteral Anastomosis
 Surgical Techniques and Options
   • As with reimplantation for VUR, intravesical and extravesical techniques both exist.
   • The authors favour a non-refluxing anastomosis in most cases, acknowledging divergent views and
     that an antireflux mechanism is not always essential. The argument: screening identifies bladder
     dysfunction and therefore graft-reflux risk, and a non-refluxing reimplantation is simple, effective
     and of minimal morbidity.
   • Extravesical ureteroneocystostomy is preferred, as a sequence: after reperfusion and haemostasis,
     partially fill the bladder with saline or dilute antibiotic solution → clear the anterolateral aspect and
     place traction sutures to mobilise it upward and tension the wall → size the ureter to ensure it reaches
     → incise detrusor to mucosa over 3 to 4 cm in an anatomic direction, avoiding kinks → elevate
     detrusor flaps off the mucosa and excise a small disc of mucosa at the distal trough → drain the
     bladder, traction sutures holding the trough → trim and spatulate the ureter for 4 to 5 mm →
     interrupted mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis with fine absorbable sutures → stitches through
     detrusor and terminal ureteral adventitia to prevent eversion → close the detrusor flaps over the
     ureter, not tightening the entry point.
   • Alternative — the Barry technique: a 4 cm tunnel between parallel incisions through which the ureter
     is passed. A shorter tunnel has been used in some paediatric centres with acceptable outcomes.
   • Ureteroureterostomy to the native ureter is routine in some centres with good results, done side-to-
     side, end-to-side or end-to-end. With the native kidney in place, some ligate the proximal ureteral
     segment, without major complications and with few later nephrectomies.
   • Superiority over ureteroneocystostomy is unproven, but it appeals with a normal non-refluxing native
     ureter, a small bladder, a short donor ureter, or difficult pelvic dissection after previous surgery, and
     should always be considered as salvage for post-transplant ureteral stenosis.

 Ureteral Stenting
   • Routine stenting in paediatric transplantation is highly debated, with no conclusive data for routine
     use.
   • Stent for a difficult reimplant — particularly into an abnormal bladder — or a traumatised or
     devascularised graft ureter.
   • Adult series show stenting reduces ureteral complications from 5.8% to 1.9%, at a higher risk of
     infection — the trade-off in one line.
   • If used: a short double-J stent, removed at 4 to 6 weeks; some advocate early removal at 5 to 7 days,
     securing the stent to the tip of the urethral catheter or using an externalised stent or splint, which may
     reduce stent-related complications and infection.
   • Removing an internal stent needs cystoscopy, and can be coordinated with removal of tunnelled
     intravenous access or a PD catheter.

   KEY POINTS: TRANSPLANT URETERONEOCYSTOSTOMY
     • Extravesical non-refluxing ureteroneocystostomy is the preferred ureteral anastomosis.
     • Transplant to native ureteroureterostomy is an effective option.
     • There are no data to support routine ureteral stenting at transplantation.
     • Early stent removal is not associated with increased complications and may reduce infection.


 COMPLICATIONS
   • The incidence of complications is low and rarely costs the allograft.
   • Thrombosis is the paediatric-specific worry — one of the commonest causes of early graft loss, which
     is why some centres anticoagulate routinely early, accepting more bleeding during reconstruction and
     demanding meticulous haemostasis.
   • Comparison of recipients with and without prior obstructive uropathy or reflux shows an increased risk
     of all urologic complications where uropathy pre-exists.
   • PUVs have been linked to increased graft dysfunction in some series but not others; obstructive
     uropathy carries a higher complication risk in many. The practical conclusion: special vigilance and a
     lower threshold for intervention.

 Urine Leaks
   • Rare — under 5% — and identified early, with wound drainage, increased perirenal drain output, or a
     perinephric collection on ultrasound.
   • Fluid creatinine distinguishes urine from lymph or peritoneal fluid, relevant after a transperitoneal
     approach or inadvertent peritoneal opening.
   • Assessment as a sequence: check all urinary drainage tubes, especially the bladder catheter → if in
     place, irrigate and confirm patency; if removed, replace it → transplant ultrasound for
     hydronephrosis; if present, suspect distal ureteral obstruction and consider percutaneous
     nephrostomy → document stent position → localise the leak with MAG3 (if graft function allows) or
     cystogram, the latter showing a bladder leak at the anastomosis, or a pyelocalyceal leak where a stent
     is in place and contrast refluxes.
   • Indications for intervention are clinical: a limited leak may be observed.
   • In young children with very high urine output, the leak may be driven by poor drainage through a
     small bladder catheter; upsizing can resolve it. Hence the preventive rule: the catheter placed at
     transplantation should be the largest the child's urethra accepts.
   • Persistent worsening or a large leak despite adequate bladder drainage: percutaneous renal drainage
     and/or exploration.
   • Exploration aims to identify the cause, confirm the site, and repair:
       – Bladder-based leak — revise the anastomosis, close the defect, drain adequately.
       – Distal ureteral necrosis — replace the ureter. Short segment: bladder mobilisation and
         reimplantation. Long segment: native ureter, ipsilateral or contralateral. No native ureter: psoas
         hitch or bladder flap.
   • These are the same strategies as for ureteral strictures; careful stenting and drainage are essential.

 Infection
   • Early infections relate to the indwelling catheter, the stent, and instrumentation.

   • Late urinary infection is a relatively frequent delayed complication reflecting bladder function and
     the underlying urologic cause of renal failure in an immunocompromised host.
   • High-grade or worsening hydronephrosis may herald pyelonephritis and worsening function, and
     needs prompt evaluation.
   • Surveillance: routine assessment of bladder emptying, detection of hydronephrosis, selective VCUG.
   • On CIC with no correctable cause: prophylactic antibiotics and bladder irrigation may work. Proactive
     management of bladder dysfunction, identified pretransplant, is essential.
   • Graft VUR with documented recurrent febrile infection warrants surgical correction; reflux without
     infection and with normal bladder function may be observed.

 Reflux
   • VUR into a transplant is entirely distinct from reflux into a native unit, for three reasons: it is a
     reimplanted ureter; pyelonephritis threatens a transplanted kidney more; and the patient is
     immunosuppressed.
   • Routine post-transplant evaluation is controversial but performed in some centres for risk
     stratification. Febrile UTI is a clear indication for cystogram.
   • With optimised bladder dynamics and recurrent febrile UTIs, transplant reflux justifies open or
     robotic reimplantation. Uncorrected, allograft pyelonephritis recurs and graft loss risk rises.
   • Identify simultaneous bladder dysfunction, though it may not be correctable. Asymptomatic reflux —
     no infection, no bladder dysfunction — should be observed.
   • Surgical management of transplant reflux is never routine.
   • Endoscopic therapy has a limited role: resolution rates are modest to poor, and post-injection
     obstruction is commoner than in other indications, with significant morbidity. It suits selected low-
     grade cases; recurrent febrile UTI justifies definitive intervention.
   • Data on intravesical versus extravesical reimplantation are few; the authors prefer intravesical, often
     with extravesical mobilisation. Transtrigonal if the contralateral native ureter can be avoided;
     otherwise advancement or Politano-Leadbetter. Stenting is advisable.
   • Where the transplant ureter was joined to the ipsilateral native ureter, options are similar but response
     to injection and ease of dissection may be better, the patient's own ureterovesical junction being
     preserved.

 Hydronephrosis and Obstruction
   • Hydronephrosis is frequent and indicates ureteral obstruction in as many as 8% of transplants.
   • The transplant kidney is particularly sensitive to obstruction, and the degree of impairment does not
     always correlate with the grade of hydronephrosis. High-grade or worsening dilatation prompts
     further evaluation.
   • Obstruction is often heralded by rising creatinine and cystatin C.
   • More than half of obstructions occur within the first 100 days — hence long-term monitoring with
     regular ultrasound.
   • With normal prior bladder function, this pattern means ureteral obstruction until proven otherwise.
     Where bladder dysfunction is known or suspected, both are evaluated and managed together —
     obstruction is commoner with bladder abnormalities, particularly PUVs.
   • An indwelling catheter is a reasonable first step: improvement in dilatation and function points to the
     bladder.

   • Obstruction and rejection may be intermingled when creatinine rises with hydronephrosis:
       – Mild hydronephrosis with other signs of rejection → biopsy first.
       – No suggestion of rejection and dilatation unimproved by bladder drainage → ureteral stent or
         nephrostomy, with or without biopsy.
       – Mild-to-moderate hydronephrosis, rising creatinine, rejection on biopsy, graft not failing rapidly →
         treat the rejection medically, reserving stenting for non-improvement.
       – Failed retrograde stenting → percutaneous drainage, then antegrade study to localise and
         antegrade stent in the same setting.
   • Isolated VUR is unlikely to cause progressive hydronephrosis and worsening function.
   • Diagnostic studies for obstruction are not completely reliable; keep a low threshold for stenting to
     test the effect on function. Rarely, an acutely failing transplant improves on decompression even
     without rejection.
   • The cause is usually distal — stenosis at the reimplantation site — but may be anywhere along the
     ureter and often includes ischaemia toward the distal donor ureter.
   • Neither implant method nor stent use has been conclusively linked to obstruction risk; bladder
     abnormality, particularly from PUVs, has.
   • Extrinsic causes: lymphocele, or mass effect from post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease (PTLD).
   • Definitive therapy follows the cause: focal narrowing on retrograde imaging → balloon dilation with
     stenting for 4 to 6 weeks; long-term stenting is used in adult series but of uncertain value in children;
     open definitive repair should not be excessively delayed, given the risk to the graft.
   • Reconstruction uses native ureter or bladder flaps; avoid non-urothelial segments such as appendix
     where possible. Pyeloureteral anastomosis is an option if the native ureter is in place and healthy. All
     principles of reoperative reconstruction — well-vascularised functional tissue — apply.

 Bladder Dysfunction
   • Bladder dysfunction increases infection risk and impairs physiologic drainage, adversely affecting
     graft function.
   • Distinguishing it from ureteral obstruction needs sequential diagnostic drainage: if a catheter passes
     easily, continuous drainage for 1 to 2 weeks with recheck of creatinine identifies bladder dysfunction
     when creatinine falls and hydronephrosis improves; if not, combine stent and bladder drainage and
     recheck.
   • Treatment is behavioural and medical, plus instituting or intensifying CIC. Augmentation is rarely
     needed and only after maximal medical management. A continent stoma helps where urethral
     catheterisation is difficult.

 Stones
   • Uncommon — quoted up to 5%, more likely under 1% — yet dangerous for long-term graft survival.
     Across recipients of all ages the 3-year risk is about 2%, higher with a preoperative stone history.
   • With appropriate management, stones do not increase graft loss, but are associated with more UTIs.
   • Reported high incidences of bladder stones largely reflected retained suture material.
   • Some stones are present in the allograft at retrieval; bench flexible ureteroscopy can remove small
     stones during cold preparation. Donor-gifted lithiasis is rarely obstructive or infective in adult data
     and can be safely observed; paediatric data are lacking.
   • A new asymptomatic stone on surveillance prompts close monitoring and a search for a predisposing
     factor.

   • Intervene for stones over 1 cm, growing stones, or stones causing graft dysfunction from obstruction
     or infection.
   • All conventional stone treatments are appropriate, modulated by risk to the graft. Choose the
     modality most likely to succeed at a single intervention, even if more complex — weighing the
     anatomic position of the transplant, stone size and location, and ease of retrograde ureteral access,
     and individualising on presentation, imaging and renal function.

   KEY POINTS: COMPLICATIONS
     • Common complications of paediatric renal transplantation: ureteral stenosis (6%), urinary leak (3%),
       stones (2%), clinically significant reflux (2%).
     • Urine leaks are identified early, with increasing fluid from the wound drains.
     • Urinary infection is a long-term and often delayed complication largely reflecting bladder function.
     • VUR into the transplant is entirely distinct from routine reflux, as the risk to renal function from
       acute pyelonephritis is greater.
     • With a rising creatinine and hydronephrosis, obstruction and rejection may both be present.
     • Ureteral obstruction is usually distal, with stenosis close to the reimplantation site.
     • Bladder dysfunction may produce infection, but may also create an obstructive process impairing
       graft function.


 SUMMARY
   • Management of the paediatric ESKD patient with an underlying urologic condition requires a thorough
     understanding of the patterns of bladder dysfunction and clear strategies for evaluation and
     treatment before transplant.
   • Anticipating the needs and constraints of the transplant procedure, and being involved in all cases,
     gives a smoother procedure and continuity of care.
   • Post-transplant monitoring must catch pathology before irreversible graft damage — a high index of
     suspicion, and a clear sense of who is at high risk.
   • Multidisciplinary collaboration between paediatric nephrology, urology and the transplant surgical
     team is critical to maximise patient and graft survival.
