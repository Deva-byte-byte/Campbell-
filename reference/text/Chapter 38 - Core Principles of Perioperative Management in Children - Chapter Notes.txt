PAEDIATRIC ANAESTHESIA
Perioperative Home
 • The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) defined the patient/family-centred medical home over 20
   years ago: accessible, family-centred, continuous, comprehensive, culturally sensitive care.
 • This led to the perioperative surgical home (PSH), defined by the American Society of Anesthesiologists
   (ASA) as a patient-centred continuity-of-care model built on shared decision making, improving
   experience and outcomes while controlling cost.
 • Driver: the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (PPACA) 2010, which introduced accountable
   care organisations (ACOs) — providers jointly accountable for quality targets and cost reduction.
 • Care is divided into preoperative → intraoperative → postoperative → postdischarge groups;
   multidisciplinary members of each build standardised evidence-based pathways.
 • These include enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS): early mobilisation, multimodal analgesia with
   less opioid, early drain removal, early diet, shorter stay.
 • A paediatric PSH led jointly by surgeons and anaesthesiologists improves resource use and patient and
   provider satisfaction.

Anaesthesia and Potential Neurocognitive Effects
 • US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) statement, 14 December 2016: repeated or lengthy general
   anaesthesia or sedation in children under 3 years or third-trimester pregnancy may affect brain
   development.
 • Basis: animal studies showing neuroapoptosis and cognitive deficit, extrapolated to the third-trimester
   fetus, newborn and child up to 3 years.
 • Many operations cannot be deferred; for non-emergent surgery weigh risk and benefit, especially if
   longer than 3 hours or multiple procedures are needed.
 • Smart TOTs: FDA and International Anesthesia Research Society partnership conducting long-term
   human studies.

   KEY POINTS
    • The FDA warns that repeated or lengthy (more than 3 hours) general anaesthesia or sedation in
      children under 3 or in third-trimester pregnancy may affect brain development.
    • Inform families of FDA recommendations while reassuring them that human studies show no
      difference in neurocognitive deficit.

 Concept — The FDA warning is animal-derived and framed around repeated or lengthy exposure under
 age 3; the human landmark studies address single short exposures and are reassuring. Counselling holds
 both without conflating them.

 PREOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT AND PREPARATION FOR SURGERY
 Anaesthesia Risk
   • Risk-raising conditions: upper respiratory infection, asthma exacerbation, dehydration, uncontrolled
     diabetes, prematurity, febrile illness, comorbidities.
   • Strict adherence to ASA nil per os (NPO) guidelines reduces aspiration.
   • Common risks: postoperative nausea and vomiting, bronchospasm, laryngospasm, stridor, emergence
     delirium, maladaptive behaviour. Rare and severe: drug allergy, anaphylaxis, malignant hyperthermia,
     death.
   • The anaesthesia team usually counsels, but the paediatric urologist must be well versed.

 Preoperative Setting
   • Preparation is the foundation of safety, good outcome and family satisfaction.
   • Pain is shaped by expectation: give realistic expectations in age-appropriate language for a better
     emotional and physical outcome.
   • Certified child life specialists (CCLSs) help children and families through anxiety-provoking care; use
     preoperatively where possible.
   • Information technology devices (computers, smartphones, handheld games) are accessible, cheap and
     reduce distress.
   • Virtual reality-guided theatre tours reduce anxiety and improve compliance at induction.
   • Preoperative anxiety affects up to half of children and predicts higher pain scores, slower recovery
     after discharge and emergence delirium (agitation, crying, thrashing in recovery).

   • New maladaptive behaviours — anxiety, night crying, enuresis, separation anxiety, tantrums — occur
     in up to half after surgery; most improve, some persist.
 Concept — Preoperative anxiety is an upstream causal variable: it drives pain, emergence delirium and
 later behaviour, so reducing child and parental anxiety is a clinical intervention, not a courtesy.

 Special Preoperative Considerations
 Jehovah's Witness
  • Belief: life resides in blood, and transfusion compromises the soul; parents may refuse on the child's
    behalf.
   • The physician's legal and ethical obligation is to the child, not the parents.
   • AAP (2013): respect religion and avoid unnecessary polarisation — but a child will not be allowed to
     die for lack of blood.
   • Authors' institution: separate consent that refusal is overridden in life-threatening instances, with an
     automatic court order. Know state law and institutional policy.
   • Blood-sparing optimisation: correct anaemia and coagulopathy; stop bleeding-promoting drugs and
     herbal supplements → less morbidity, mortality and transfusion.
 Department of Social Services
  • Children in custody need consent from a court-ordered guardian or designee; obtain it beforehand or
    ensure availability on the day.

 Pain Assessment Tools
   • International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP): pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional
     experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage — harder to assess in children with
     limited cognition and communication.
   • Self-report is the gold standard, but is impossible under 3 years.
   • Common self-report scales: Wong-Baker FACES and the numeric rating scale.

   • Prolonged fasting causes hunger, thirst, anxiety, nausea, vomiting, pain, hypoglycaemia, electrolyte
     imbalance, dehydration and hypotension at induction.
   • Children have greater potential for fluid loss than adults.
   • Simple or complex carbohydrate drinks reduce thirst, hunger and irritability → allow up to 2 hours
     before anaesthesia.
   • Outpatients: deficits are minimal when rules are followed, but non-adherence prolongs fasting →
     schedule younger children earliest and educate parents.
   • Inpatients: maintenance intravenous fluid during the fast prevents dehydration.
   • Emergencies: gastric aspiration → pneumonitis; rapid-sequence induction and intubation protects the
     full-stomach airway.
 Special NPO Situations
 Ketogenic diet
  • Commonest indication epilepsy; explored in autism, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, multiple sclerosis,
    Parkinson disease.
   • Stay on the diet, avoid prolonged fasting; standard NPO (light meal 6 h, clears 2 h).
   • Avoid carbohydrate-containing suspensions; check every drug for dextrose, carbohydrate or
     gluconeogenesis induction — mannitol, nitroglycerin, dexamethasone.

   • Consider complete blood count (CBC), electrolytes, albumin, prealbumin; intraoperative glucose and
     electrolytes if surgery exceeds 3 hours.
   • Baseline glucose 50–80 mg/dL; treat only if below 40 mg/dL or symptomatic.
   • Blood products contain variable carbohydrate — avoid where possible.
 Enteral tube feeds
  • Stop formula 6 hours before induction; Pedialyte to 2 hours.
   • Postpyloric feeds carry less reflux and pneumonia risk and some continue them for non-abdominal
     surgery; the authors apply standard NPO regardless, as tube position is uncertain and aspiration
     morbidity high.
 Diabetes and metabolic disorders
  • Fasting volume depletion plus withdrawn diabetic drugs → preoperative hyperglycaemia.
   • Type 1 diabetes affects about 1 in 300 US children.
   • Delay elective surgery until glycaemic control is optimised: no ketonuria, normal electrolytes, glycated
     haemoglobin (HbA1c) near the age-appropriate range; first-start case.
   • Admission is not required; some advise it for major surgery.
   • Never induce without a blood glucose; if surgery is delayed, monitor frequently.

 Preoperative Studies
 Laboratory Tests
  • Routine bloods are not indicated in healthy children for low-risk procedures: low yield, anxiety-
    provoking, not cost-effective.
   • Reserve for complex procedures with haemodynamic shifts or major blood loss, and for premature
     infants, metabolic disorders, personal or family bleeding history, complex conditions.
   • Bleeding screens: HEMSTOP and the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH)
     Bleeding Assessment Tool.
   • Pregnancy testing: ASA — offer to females of childbearing age and whenever the result alters
     management; authors test all females of menstrual age with urine human chorionic gonadotrophin
     (hCG).
   • Trap: false-positive urine hCG after augmentation cystoplasty.
 Chest Radiographs and Electrocardiogram
  • Not routine. A murmur alone does not warrant an electrocardiogram (ECG); pathological murmurs or
    cardiopulmonary symptoms → cardiology.
   • Endocarditis prophylaxis guidance changes often — know and apply the current version.
   • Chest radiograph for lung disease or conditions affecting lung function.
   • Screen all adolescents for vaping: suspected e-cigarette, or vaping, product use-associated lung injury
     (EVALI) → chest radiograph, computed tomography (CT) and/or pulmonology.
 Pulmonary Function Testing
  • No evidence for routine testing to predict respiratory events; a baseline helps in severe respiratory
    disease and severe scoliosis to allow optimisation.
 Urine Evaluation
  • Evaluate urinary symptoms before any urological procedure; suspected infection → urinalysis and
    culture.
   • When the urinary tract is opened, infection risk rises → preoperative culture guides antibiotic choice.
   • Risk factors for a positive intraoperative culture: neurogenic bladder, prior positive culture, female sex,
     constipation, urolithiasis.
   • Treat positive cultures before elective surgery.
   • Asymptomatic bacteriuria matters in endourology (mucosal injury) → treat with culture-specific
     antibiotics.
 Concept — Preoperative testing is indication-driven, not routine: each test must answer a question that
 changes management.

 COMORBIDITIES: PERIOPERATIVE PLANNING AND POSTOPERATIVE
 CONSIDERATIONS
 Premature Infants
   • Premature: born before 37 weeks; micro-preemie: before 26 weeks or under 800 g. Immature organs
     raise anaesthetic risk.
   • Surfactant deficiency, especially below 34 weeks → respiratory distress syndrome; also
     bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) and chronic lung disease.
   • Anaesthesia → low lung volumes and ventilation/perfusion mismatch → hypoxia, oxygen toxicity,
     barotrauma.
   • Postoperative apnoea peaks in the first 12–23 hours and can be fatal.

   • Cardiovascular: poor diastolic function, low left ventricular compliance; small blood volume → small
     losses cause hypotension and shock.
   • Retinopathy of prematurity (especially under 28 weeks) is worsened by hyperoxia → accept saturation
     in the low 90s.
   • Hypothermia: immature skin, little brown fat, high surface-area ratio → warm environment
     mandatory.
   • Large fluid losses from skin and wound evaporation and poor urinary concentration.
   • Surgery in preterm infants is linked to reduced white matter on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
     and lower scores at 2 years → operate only when urgent or emergent.
 Concept — Every organ system in the preterm infant has less reserve — lungs, heart, blood volume, skin,
 kidneys, retina and brain — so each hazard is amplified and elective surgery is best deferred.

 Asthma and Pulmonary Disease
   • Asthma and BPD are the common paediatric lung disorders.
   • Optimise control before elective surgery; continue home medicines on the day.
   • Lung disease raises the risk of bronchospasm, laryngospasm and airway collapse.
   • Inhaled β2 agonist improves lung function and reduces respiratory adverse events.
   • Perioperative steroids are safe but kept for severe disease (wound infection, poor healing); stress-dose
     steroids if systemic steroids have been needed or inhaled steroids are daily.
   • Induction and emergence are the most dangerous times (mechanical stimulation) → do not examine
     or stimulate the patient then.

 Cardiovascular Anomalies
   • Congenital heart disease (CHD), the commonest congenital anomaly (about 1% of newborns), carries
     high perioperative arrest, morbidity and mortality.
   • The Pediatric Perioperative Cardiac Arrest (POCA) Registry is the best risk-stratification source.
   • Highest risk: functional single ventricle, pulmonary hypertension, left ventricular outflow tract
     obstruction, dilated cardiomyopathy.

   • Adverse events associated with higher ASA class, emergency, surgery within 30 days, inotropes,

     surgical need.

 Renal Abnormalities
   • Rare in children but a significant cause of morbidity and mortality.
   • Under 6 years, developmental abnormalities are the commonest cause, then hereditary
     nephropathies; older children mostly have acquired disease.
   • Chronic kidney disease (CKD) predisposes to acute kidney injury (AKI).
   • Haematological: anaemia (less oxygen delivery; may need transfusion); uraemic platelet dysfunction
     → bleeding; uraemia also impairs nutrition and gastric emptying.
   • Contrast: protein-losing nephropathy is hypercoagulable → thrombosis.
   • Cardiac: cardiomyopathy, heart failure, pulmonary oedema. Urine output may be normal, polyuric,
     oliguric or anuric.
   • Electrolytes: hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypocalcaemia, hyperphosphataemia, metabolic acidosis;
     hyperkalaemia → arrhythmia.
   • Work-up: CBC, chemistry, coagulation; chest radiograph and echocardiogram for effusions if heart or
     lung disease suspected; ECG for left ventricular hypertrophy if hypertensive.
   • Continue antihypertensives and immunosuppressants unless advised otherwise.
   • Dialysis preoperatively if indicated; avoid surgery for 4–6 hours after haemodialysis.
   • Analgesia: regional blocks and epidurals if no coagulopathy.

 Haematological Disorders
   • Haemoglobinopathies are commonly inherited: α- and β-thalassaemia and structural variants (HbS,
     sickle cell disease); also von Willebrand disease, factor deficiencies, platelet disorders.
   • Take a thorough family and personal bleeding history; positive family history of excessive bleeding →
     haematology.
   • Sickle cell disease: haemolysis and vaso-occlusion from irreversible sickling; chronic anaemia, pain
     crisis, acute chest syndrome, stroke, splenic sequestration, cardiomyopathy, heart failure, renal disease.
   • Assess end-organ damage; surgeon–anaesthesiologist–haematologist planning; preoperative
     transfusion is controversial; hydroxyurea may help some.
   • Acute chest syndrome is the main cause of death under anaesthesia and may occur postoperatively.
   • Most important single factor: avoid dehydration, especially during NPO → first case, intravenous fluid
     during the fast.
   • Intraoperative: normothermia, hydration, analgesia, no hypoxia or acidosis; avoid tourniquets.

 Pheochromocytoma
  • Rare adrenal medullary neuroendocrine tumours storing noradrenaline and adrenaline.
   • Manipulation or induction → catecholamine storm: hypertensive crisis, arrhythmia, myocardial
     ischaemia, pulmonary oedema, stroke.
   • Pathophysiology: low blood volume with hypertension; high catecholamines → cardiomyopathy. Blood
     pressure control and fluid support are essential.
   • Target blood pressure below the 50th percentile for age and height, with an α-adrenergic antagonist
     before β-blockade to avoid crisis from unopposed α stimulation.
   • Admission for 24 to 26 hours to optimise hydration and blood pressure.
   • Postoperative hypoglycaemia: catecholamine fall → rebound hyperinsulinaemia and peripheral glucose
     uptake → monitor glucose.
 Concept — Pheochromocytoma care follows catecholamine physiology: α-blockade before β-blockade,
 volume before induction, and glucose surveillance once the catecholamine source is removed.

 Spina Bifida
   • Neurological, anatomical and comorbid challenges all shape planning.
   • 80–90% have or develop hydrocephalus; the ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt is the gold standard, or
     endoscopic third ventriculostomy.
   • Shunt function must be adequate; suspected malfunction → reschedule elective surgery.
   • Scoliosis in about half reduces thoracic capacity; lung disease may be silent → preoperative respiratory
     testing helps.

   • Overweight and obesity are common → metabolic syndrome: diabetes, dyslipidaemia, hypertension,
     cardiovascular disease, sleep apnoea, osteoporosis, poor skin integrity.
   • Theatre: know the skeletal anatomy, pad pressure points, position carefully; air mattress for severe
     deformity with long admission.
   • Autonomic dysreflexia: lesions at T6 and above; noxious stimuli or bladder distension trigger
     dysregulated sympathetic discharge → uncontrolled hypertension, bradycardia, stroke, death.
   • Anaphylactic latex allergy: bronchoconstriction, vasodilatation and hypotension, capillary leak,
     coronary vasoconstriction, myocardial depression; fatal without immediate treatment.
   • Analgesia is difficult, especially with shunted hydrocephalus: epidural catheters are safe and effective
     in selected patients; peripheral regional catheters reduce opioid use without raising pain scores after
     major surgery; alternatives patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) and intravenous drugs.

 Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia
   • Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH): inherited inability to make cortisol; 90–99% from 21-hydroxylase
     deficiency (CYP21A2 mutations).
   • Cortisol deficiency ± aldosterone deficiency, plus androgen excess; salt-losing vs non-salt-losing.
   • Salt-wasting: renal hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, acidosis, shock if untreated. Cortisol deficiency →
     poor contractility, low vascular tone.
   • Classic CAH cannot mount a stress cortisol response → stress-dose steroids, preferably intravenous
     with intravenous fluids; dose differs for minor and major surgery.
   • Adrenaline and cortisol deficiency → fasting hypoglycaemia → glucose-containing fluids and
     monitoring.

   KEY POINTS
    • Anaesthesia risks include postoperative nausea and vomiting, bronchospasm, laryngospasm, stridor,
      emergence delirium and maladaptive behaviours.
    • NPO violation significantly increases the risk of gastric aspiration and pneumonitis.
    • Children are at increased risk of dehydration, so families need NPO education.
    • Premature infants have significantly increased perioperative risk.
    • Spina bifida patients have multiple comorbidities to consider perioperatively.
    • Autonomic dysreflexia is life threatening; paediatric urologists should know its signs and treatment.


 Acute Preoperative Events
 Sepsis
  • A leading cause of paediatric morbidity, mortality and cost; mortality varies widely with severity, risk
    factors and geography.
   • Most deaths are from shock or multiple organ dysfunction within 48–72 hours → early recognition
     and aggressive resuscitation.
   • Infection, especially urosepsis, is a common cause.
   • Empirical antimicrobials within 1 hour of recognising septic shock, chosen by history and site.
   • Rapid aggressive fluids ± vasopressors; airway support; oxygen titrated to continuous central venous
     oxygen saturation (ScvO2) in the first hours.

   • If surgery is needed: crossmatch, adequate vascular access, chemistry, CBC, coagulation; septic shock
     → intensive care postoperatively.

 Trauma
  • Trauma kills more children aged 1–18 than all other causes combined.
   • In high-impact blunt trauma the kidney is injured more often than spleen, liver, pancreas or bowel;
     renal injury is usually conservative, but other injuries may force exploration.
   • Focused history and examination; imaging must not delay lifesaving intervention; focused abdominal
     sonography for trauma (FAST) may help.
   • Bloods can be drawn in theatre. Trauma panel: CBC, metabolic panel, coagulation, amylase, lipase, type
     and screen, urinalysis, β-hCG in females of reproductive age.
   • Always a full stomach; rapid-sequence induction is the gold standard.
   • Keep theatre warm: hypothermia → vasoconstriction, low flow, acidosis, consumptive coagulopathy,
     worse outcomes.
   • Untreated pain has lasting psychological and physiological effects: acetaminophen (oral, rectal,
     intravenous), ibuprofen; strong opioids for severe pain unless contraindicated.
 Upper Respiratory Tract Infection
  • Upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) is the commonest non-preventable cause of cancellation; the
    COLDS score grades its severity.
   • A prospective study of children 1–5 years having ilioinguinal surgery with a laryngeal mask airway:
     respiratory adverse events stayed frequent; risk factors were passive smoking and severe URTI in the
     preceding 15 days.
   • Other independent risk factors: endotracheal tube, age under 5, prematurity, asthma, airway surgery.
   • Inflammation leaves a hyperreactive airway for 6–8 weeks → laryngospasm, stridor, bronchospasm,
     cough, breath-holding, desaturation, bradycardia, atelectasis, pneumonia.
   • Laryngeal mask airway (LMA) causes fewer complications than an endotracheal tube.

 Urinary Tract Infection
  • A best-practice statement advises urinalysis, microscopy and culture before any urological procedure
    when urinary tract infection (UTI) is suspected.
   • American Urological Association (AUA) guidelines (2018): eradicate infection before urological
     manipulation with a culture-directed course.
   • Unrecognised bacteriuria → sepsis and surgical site infection (SSI). Risk factors for a positive culture are

 Concept — Acute events divide into two decisions: in sepsis and trauma surgery cannot wait, so physiology

 ERAS PROTOCOLS
   • ERAS is patient-centred multimodal care that optimises recovery and preserves the preoperative
     physical and metabolic state.
   • Paediatric elements: enhanced counselling, shorter starvation, judicious intravenous fluid, no routine
     tubes or drains, adjunctive analgesia.
   • Paediatric urology studies all report at least one benefit — shorter operative time and stay, earlier
     feeding and bowel function — with no rise in complications (some fewer) or readmissions.
   • Poor analgesia prolongs stay; regional anaesthesia adds theatre time but improves pain and shortens
     stay.
   • Barriers: cultural, racial and health-system variation, lack of consensus, need for administrative support
     → impaired standardisation and evaluation.
 Concept — Every ERAS element minimises disturbance of the child's baseline — less starvation, less fluid
 excess, fewer tubes, fewer opioids — and recovery follows from what is not disturbed.

 INTRAOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS
 Preoperative Antibiotics
   • SSIs cause morbidity, mortality, readmission and prolonged stay.
   • Principle (AUA 2018): prophylaxis is justified by host defence, likelihood of bacterial invasion and
     morbidity of infection — benefit must outweigh antibiotic risk.
   • Paediatric practice is extrapolated from adults; AUA gives procedure-specific advice; paediatric urology
     data are limited and practice is changing.
   • Timing for adequate tissue levels: within 60 minutes of incision; vancomycin and fluoroquinolones
     within 120 minutes (long infusion).

 Intraoperative and Perioperative Fluid Management
   • Both restrictive and liberal regimens cause complications → tailor to deficit, maintenance and losses;
     deficit reflects NPO time and any bowel preparation.
   • 4-2-1 rule (Holliday and Segar, 1957): 4 mL/kg/h for the first 10 kg, 2 mL/kg/h for the next 10 kg, 1
     mL/kg/h per kg above 20 kg; 22 kg → 62 mL/h.
   • No definitive crystalloid or colloid preference; some argue lactated Ringer solution beats normal saline
     (NS), which can cause dilutional acidosis.
   • Caveats: dextrose-containing maintenance under 2–4 months (immature liver); no lactate in
     mitochondrial disease; no potassium in dialysed patients.
   • Continue maintenance until oral intake is adequate.

 Anaphylaxis
   • Commoner in adults than children; mainly within minutes of induction; latex now a less common
     cause.
   • Presentation: cardiovascular collapse — tachycardia, hypotension, severe bronchospasm, hives.
   • Management: stop the culprit, 100% oxygen, Trendelenburg, aggressive fluids, albuterol, adrenaline.

 Malignant Hyperthermia
   • Malignant hyperthermia (MH): rare, potentially fatal hypermetabolic disorder.
   • Early signs: unexplained hypercarbia, sinus tachycardia, masseter spasm, hyperthermia. Late signs:
     severe metabolic acidosis, peaked T waves (hyperkalaemia), generalised rigidity, rhabdomyolysis.
   • Susceptible patient → first-start case; activated charcoal filters cut machine preparation from 20–104
     minutes to 90 seconds.

 LAPAROSCOPIC PROCEDURES
   • Introduced to paediatric urology in 1976 as diagnostic laparoscopy for nonpalpable testes; use has
     grown; well tolerated, but paediatric physiology differs.
   • Respiratory: children have high chest wall compliance and low functional residual capacity (FRC);
     insufflation elevates the diaphragm → lower FRC and lung compliance, higher airway resistance,
     ventilation/perfusion mismatch and dead space.
   • Cardiovascular: little effect with a normal heart, but bradyarrhythmias (bradycardia, atrioventricular
     dissociation, nodal rhythm, asystole) at port placement and insufflation.

   • Renal: no proven harm to renal or cerebral perfusion, but pneumoperitoneum compresses parenchyma
     and vessels → higher renovascular resistance, lower renal vein flow → transient oliguria; with impaired
     renal function give generous volume before and during pressure rises.
   • Positioning: pad pressure points; axillary roll in lateral decubitus (brachial plexus); neutral neck;
     dependent leg flexed with a cushion between the knees.
   • Analgesia: multimodal (NSAIDs plus opioids) speeds recovery; consider regional or neuraxial blocks;
     pneumoperitoneum causes diffuse abdominal and shoulder pain.
   • Carbon dioxide (CO2) embolism: large-volume CO2 enters veins during insufflation → right ventricular
     outflow obstruction → sudden desaturation and collapse.
   • Classic: falling end-tidal CO2 with a mill-wheel murmur. Treatment: left lateral decubitus (right side
     up) and Trendelenburg; aspirate via a central line if present.

 Robotic Procedures
   • Robotic pyeloplasty is the commonest; others: heminephrectomy, ureteroureterostomy, ureteral
     reimplantation, urachal cyst excision, bladder diverticulectomy, bladder reconstruction.
   • All laparoscopic considerations apply.
   • Robot location: often in shared adult theatres, away from paediatric experts, equipment and monitors
     → routine simulation; two anaesthesia physicians at induction for high-risk patients.

 Intraoperative Pain Management
   • Agree the plan with the anaesthesiologist; it depends on patient, surgery and anaesthesiologist
     comfort.
   • Epidural analgesia suits open surgery with large incisions; safe from 2 kg in experienced hands.
   • Regional and neuraxial blocks under general anaesthesia are safe and standard.
   • Peripheral: penile ring, dorsal penile nerve blocks. Neuraxial: spinal, caudal, site-specific epidural.

 POSTOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT
 Immediate Postoperative Management
   • Focus on respiratory and cardiovascular status.
   • Respiratory events: hypoxia, obstruction, laryngospasm, bronchospasm, post-obstructive pulmonary
     oedema, aspiration — more frequent if intubated, ASA 3–4, under 1 year, respiratory disease,
     prematurity, current or recent upper respiratory infection.
   • Multiple intubation attempts → airway oedema → stridor.
 Laryngospasm: positive-pressure ventilation → propofol to relax the cords → reintubation if these fail.
 Bronchospasm: albuterol/ipratropium nebulisers → if severe, adrenaline, steroids, magnesium,
 continuous albuterol; admit if needed.
   • CHD raises cardiac event risk. Postoperative arrest in non-cardiac children is rare — fewer than 1 per
     100,000 anaesthetics; most occur under 5 years and most are preventable → constant vigilance in the
     postanaesthesia care unit (PACU).
 Emergence delirium
  • Especially at 2–8 years: restlessness, agitation, combativeness, inconsolability; incidence varies widely.
   • Usually within 30 minutes and self-limited, but distressing and dangerous — self-injury, dislodged
     catheters and lines, wound damage.
   • Exclude pain and normal behaviour (tantrum); the Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium (PAED)
     scale helps.
   • High preoperative anxiety raises risk.
   • Treatment: low-dose propofol, watching the airway; dexmedetomidine, fentanyl, clonidine. Warn
     parents preoperatively; treat pre-emptively in high-risk children.

 Postanaesthesia Care Unit and Pain Management
   • The anaesthesiologist stays until a pain plan is in place; titrate opioids and non-opioids to the
     institutional scale.
   • PCA works as well as in adults; safe from 4 years if cognitively appropriate.
   • PCA by proxy (parent or nurse): set maximum doses, assess pain properly, monitor for respiratory

   • Confirm regional or neuraxial blocks work; no PACU discharge until pain is controlled.

     Anesthetic Discharge Scoring System (PADSS).
   • Postanaesthesia note (respiratory, cardiovascular, mental status, temperature, pain, nausea, vomiting,

 Inpatient and Outpatient Postoperative Pain Management
   • Most surgical patients have postoperative pain, often moderate to severe; in children it is often
     undertreated (hard to assess, fear of opioids).
   • Poor control → complications, slower recovery, morbidity, mortality, anxiety and fear.
   • Discuss expected pain preoperatively.
   • Multimodal: weight-based acetaminophen plus NSAID is the base and substantially reduces opioid
     use.
   • Ketorolac is routine; a Cochrane review found low-quality evidence on its safety and efficacy; bleeding
     remains a concern.
   • Intranasal, rectal and transdermal routes help where venous access is difficult.
   • Opioids for breakthrough pain; side effects nausea, pruritus, constipation, respiratory depression.
   • Codeine: regulators warn against use in children; absolutely contraindicated after tonsillectomy for
     obstructive sleep apnoea.
   • Start with a scheduled regimen; as-needed dosing is inadequate.

 OPIOID USE IN CHILDREN
   • Overdose and misuse risk is high in children, adolescents and young adults: access to unused
     medicines and developing reward and habit-formation centres.
   • No standard paediatric urology prescribing guidelines → overprescription and leftover household
     opioids.
   • Yet untreated pain has lasting psychological and physiological consequences.
   • Prescribing errors above the maximum daily dose occur, concentrated in the youngest → check dosing
     carefully.
   • Major surgery needing opioids: limited discharge supply, e.g. 3–5 days.
   • Teach dose, frequency, storage and disposal: poor storage and undisposed excess are the main sources
     of accidental poisoning in young children and recreational overdose in adolescents; disposal guidance
     on the FDA website.

   KEY POINTS
    • Perioperative complications most often occur in the immediate postoperative period.
    • Team members should know the potential postoperative complications and monitor for them.
    • Postoperative pain control is important for optimal outcomes; a multimodal drug approach is
      recommended.
    • Proper administration and disposal of unused narcotics prevent overdose.

 Concept — The same drug must be given adequately in hospital and removed from the household after
 discharge: undertreatment and leftover supply are the twin failures of paediatric opioid practice.
