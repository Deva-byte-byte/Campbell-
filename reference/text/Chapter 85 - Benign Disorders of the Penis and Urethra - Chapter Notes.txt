OVERVIEW OF RECONSTRUCTIVE SURGICAL TECHNIQUES
 • Goal of genitourinary reconstruction: a safe, functional and aesthetically acceptable urinary tract,
   which requires attention to vascular supply, minimal tissue trauma and the dynamics of urinary flow.
 • Reconstructive versus extirpative surgery: reconstructive results depend on methods that minimise
   tissue damage and maximise wound healing — adequate visualisation, appropriate suture, delicate
   handling, correct positioning and adequate retraction.
Visualisation and cautery.
 • Loupes and headlamps are widely used; avoid non-neutral neck positions, as long-term use is
   associated with work-related musculoskeletal injury.
 • Bipolar cautery on the urethra — active and return electrodes are both in the handpiece, so damage to
   mucosa and surrounding spongiosum is minimised. Monopolar on subcutaneous tissue and muscle.
Instruments.
 • A plastic surgery tray usually suffices: fine tenotomy and Metzenbaum scissors (kept sharp to limit
   collateral trauma), fine forceps, skin hooks, and needle holders delicate enough for fine tissue yet firm
   enough to prevent the needle moving.
 • Toothed forceps on muscle, fascia and subcutaneous fat; non-toothed (DeBakey) forceps on the
   urethra.
 • Bougie à boule sizers — calibration of the urethral lumen (essential). Goodwin sounds — dilation, not
   calibration; safer than van Buren sounds because they carry a lumen for a guidewire. Either may be
   passed through a suprapubic tract into the bladder neck during posterior urethroplasty.
Sutures.
 • Always absorbable in urethral reconstruction — permanent suture is a nidus for stone. Preferred:
   monofilament polydioxanone; braided absorbable is acceptable.
 • Wound support is needed for about 30–60 days; chromic gives only 14 days → not recommended for
   urethral work.
 • Calibre 4-0 to 6-0: 5-0 for most urethral work; 6-0 for single-layer mucosal anastomosis — non-
   transecting excision and primary anastomosis (EPA) with mucosectomy, or the inner ventral layer of a
   transecting EPA. Needle: small tapered (RB-1).
Position and retraction.
 • Lithotomy or high lithotomy when exposure demands it, but limit time → neuropathy and
   compartment syndrome; move to low lithotomy whenever possible (for example during graft
   harvest). Supine for penile surgery when perineal access is not required.
 • Self-retaining perineal Bookwalter retractor (authors prefer a segmented oblong Magrina ring with
   suture slots); alternatives are a Lone Star retractor with stay hooks or a modified Denis-Browne
   retractor slit for stay hooks.

 KEY POINTS: RECONSTRUCTIVE SURGICAL TECHNIQUES
  • Planning: imaging adequate to understand the problem, plus several backup plans if the primary
    approach is not feasible.

 PRINCIPLES OF RECONSTRUCTIVE SURGERY

 Tissue Transfer
   • Tissues used: skin, mucosa, muscle, peritoneum and omentum; chosen by colour, texture, thickness,
     extensibility, innate tension and blood supply, which vary between individuals.
   • Graft versus flap: a graft is separated from its blood supply; a flap is transferred with it. Both require
     knowledge of donor and recipient anatomy.
   • All tissue has extensibility, inherent tension and the viscoelastic properties of stress relaxation and
     creep. These derive mainly from the helical arrangement of collagen with elastin cross-linkages,
     suspended in a mucopolysaccharide matrix that governs viscoelasticity.
   • Buccal mucosa suits the urethra because its thick lamina propria makes it easier to handle, holds
     sutures better and is less prone to sacculation than skin.

 Grafts
   • A graft acquires a new blood supply from the host bed by take (Fig. 85.1), which requires about 96
     hours in two phases, and is impaired by anything that impairs host-bed vascularity.

   • Oral mucosa grafts (OMGs): nonkeratinised epithelium, rich in elastin (pliable, structurally sound), over
     a highly vascular lamina propria that promotes take; panlaminar plexus; accustomed to a wet
     environment and resist infection (Fig. 85.1B).
   • Can be thinned, provided enough deep lamina is kept to preserve physical and vascular characteristics.

   • Buccal onlay grafts are durable into the long term, with failures occurring within the first 12 months.

 Flaps
   • A flap is excised and transferred with its blood supply preserved or surgically re-established; classified
     by composition, blood supply, movement and location (Figs. 85.2–85.4).
   • Axial flap: a specific named vessel (or several named pedicles, as in gracilis) running in the flap's long
     axis; indirect if perforators pass through deeper tissue before the fascia, direct if they pass through
     fascia only. Random flap: no defined cuticular vascular territory.
   • Location: local (defect adjacent to harvest site), regional (same region, not contiguous), distant —
     pedicled (still on native supply) or free (detached and anastomosed to recipient vessels, usually
     microvascular).

   KEY POINTS: PRINCIPLES OF RECONSTRUCTIVE SURGERY
     • Grafts used successfully for primary urethral reconstruction: FTSG, OMG, bladder epithelial graft
       and rectal mucosal graft; the OMG has the most favourable vascular properties.
     • FTSGs and STSGs for penile reconstruction; microvascular free transfer is a mainstay in complex
       cases; dartos-based skin islands for urethral reconstruction; dermal graft for tunical augmentation.

 Concept — A graft must win a new blood supply and a flap brings its own. Every graft property follows
 from one trade-off: dense fine plexus and low mass favour take, while carried dermis or lamina confers
 durability and resists contraction. This explains STSG against FTSG, the failure of extragenital FTSG, the
 Wolfe exception, and why the OMG, with a panlaminar plexus, can be thinned.

 SURGICAL ANATOMY OF THE PENIS AND MALE PERINEUM

 Penile Anatomy
   • The shaft comprises three erectile bodies — two corpora cavernosa and the corpus spongiosum —
     with their fascia, nerves and vessels, covered by skin; all continue into the perineum.
   • Corpora cavernosa: erectile tissue within a dense elastic sheath, the tunica albuginea (outer
     longitudinal and inner circular layers). They form a single space communicating through an
     incompetent midline septum that becomes more complete towards the base; the septum is strands
     interweaving with the tunica dorsally and ventrally (Fig. 85.5). Erectile tissue — arteries, nerves, muscle
     fibres and endothelium-lined venous sinuses (a sponge-like cut surface) — is separated from the tunica
     by thin areolar tissue.
   • Corpus spongiosum: lies in the ventral groove; its tunica is thinner and it holds less erectile tissue;
     carries the urethra, expands distally as the glans (vertical slit meatus on the ventral tip) and broadens
     proximally between the diverging crura as the bulb.
   • The base is supported by two ligaments of mainly elastic fibres continuous with the penile fascia (see
     Colles Fascia).
   • Layers, deep to superficial: tunica albuginea → Buck's fascia → dartos fascia → skin.
   • Buck's fascia — tough, elastic, applied to the tunica. Dorsally it encloses the deep dorsal vein, paired
     dorsal arteries and dorsal nerve branches; ventrally it splits around the spongiosum, and

 Blood Supply
   • Penile skin: left and right superficial external pudendal vessels, from the first part of the femoral
     artery, cross the upper medial femoral triangle and divide into dorsolateral and ventrolateral branches
     with collateralisation across the midline. Fine branches form a rich subdermal plexus that sustains the
     skin after its dartos has been mobilised — the basis of the distal penile circular fasciocutaneous
     (McAninch) flap. Accompanying veins are more prominent than the arteries.
   • Scrotal wall and ventral penile skin: the posterior scrotal artery, a superficial branch of the deep
     internal pudendal artery, arborising in the tunica dartos and Colles fascia (Fig. 85.9B); supports the
     longitudinal ventral penile skin (Turner-Warwick) flap. The perineal artery continues lateral to the
     groin crease onto the thigh.

 Nerve Supply
   • Pudendal nerve (S2–4) → dorsal nerves branch in Alcock canal, pass through the perineal muscles and
     run lateral to the dorsal arteries; sensory to penile skin and glans. The pudendal nerve gives somatic
     motor and sensory innervation.
   • Cavernous nerves: sympathetic (T11–L2) and parasympathetic (S2–4) fibres, with visceral afferents,
     via the inferior hypogastric (pelvic) plexus → along the posterolateral border of the prostate →
     branches to the sphincter → corpora. Parasympathetic is proerectile; sympathetic is inhibitory.

 Perineum
   • Diamond-shaped outlet: anteriorly the pubic arch and arcuate ligament; posteriorly the tip of the
     coccyx; laterally the inferior pubic and ischial rami. A line between the ischial tuberosities divides it
     into an anterior urogenital triangle and a posterior anal triangle (Fig. 85.12).
 Colles Fascia
   • Attached posteriorly to the perineal body, curves below the superficial transverse perineal muscles and
     projects forward as two layers attached laterally to the ischium and inferior pubic ramus.
   • Superficial fatty layer → continuous with scrotal dartos, which contains muscle fibres (scrotal rugae)
     and projects, without muscle, as the scrotal septum. The median raphe runs from scrotum along the
     ventral penis and back along the perineum to the anus.
   • Deep membranous layer → roofs the scrotal cavity, separating it from the superficial perineal pouch;
     joins scrotal dartos anteriorly, folds back beneath bulbospongiosus, and is continuous with penile
     dartos at the base.
   • Suspensory ligaments (thickenings at the base): outer fundiform ligament — from the lower linea
     alba, splits around the penis and unites beneath; inner triangular suspensory ligament — from the
     anterior symphysis, blending with penile dartos.
   • Attachments: anteriorly → Scarpa fascia; laterally → pubic arch and fascia lata; posteriorly → beneath
     the transverse perineal muscles to the posterior perineal membrane. The space beneath is the
     superficial perineal pouch, which confines infection, urinary extravasation and blood after urethral
     trauma.

 Superficial Perineal Space
   • Lies superficial to the perineal membrane and deep to Colles fascia; contains the crura, proximal
     spongiosum and urethra, their muscles, and branches of the internal pudendal vessels and pudendal
     nerves.
   • Ischiocavernosus covers the crura: from the inner ischium and ischial tuberosities, inserting into Buck's
     fascia around the crura below the arcuate ligament.
   • Bulbospongiosus (midline fusion): attached to the perineal body posteriorly and to its fellow in the
     midline, encompassing bulb and crura; confluent laterally with ischiocavernosus and inserting into
     Buck's fascia over the dorsal vessels and nerves.
   • Deep perineal (Gallaudet) fascia covers these muscles and is continuous with Buck's fascia.
 Central Perineal Tendon (Perineal Body)
   • Just anterior to the anus in the plane between the triangles; the interconnection of eight perineal
     muscles: anterior external anal sphincter fibres, superficial transverse perineals (from the ischial
     tuberosities, central insertion), posterior bulbospongiosus fibres, and, on its deep aspect, the deep
     transverse perineals and anterior levator ani.
 Deep Perineal Space
   • Superior to the perineal membrane; contains the urogenital diaphragm and deep transverse perineal
     muscles, external urethral sphincter, bulbourethral (Cowper) glands, vessels and nerves (Fig. 85.12C,
     D).
   • Sphincter fibres arise from the medial inferior pubic rami and meet their fellows; in males they encircle
     the membranous urethra as its somatic sphincter.

   KEY POINTS: ANATOMY OF THE PENIS AND MALE PERINEUM
     • Buck's fascia belongs to the deep structures; the more areolar dartos fascia belongs to the skin and
       its vessels.
     • Genital skin is supplied by the perineal–scrotal branch of the deep internal pudendal artery and by
       the superficial external pudendal vessels from the femoral arteries; deep structures by the
       common penile artery.

 Concept — Two fascial envelopes define two vascular worlds: dartos carries the skin's supply (external
 pudendal and posterior scrotal), so skin flaps are raised on dartos, whereas Buck's fascia encloses the
 erectile bodies fed by the common penile artery. Which envelope is breached determines where blood,
 urine or infection travels.

 SELECTED PROCESSES

 Urethral Hemangioma
   • Rare but usually persistent; presents with haematuria or bloody urethral discharge, occasionally
     obstruction; single or multiple; the meatus is a common site.
   • Cystoscopy diagnoses it (dilated vessels), but the lesion often extends beyond what is seen →
     preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).
   • All reported cases benign → management by size and location: asymptomatic → observe (may regress
     spontaneously); symptomatic → complete excision to prevent recurrence.

 Reactive Arthritis
   • Sterile synovitis after acute gastrointestinal or genitourinary infection; the classic triad — arthritis,
     conjunctivitis, urethritis — occurs in a minority. Formerly Reiter syndrome, renamed because of Hans
     Reiter's association with Nazism.
   • Onset 2–3 weeks after Chlamydia trachomatis, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), Shigella or
     Salmonella infection; HLA-B27 in about two-thirds.
   • Urethral involvement is usually mild and self-limited.
   • Circinate balanitis in up to half: small vesicles or painless hyperkeratotic plaques with serpiginous
     borders on the glans.

 Lichen Sclerosus
   • Lichen sclerosus (LS), formerly balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO): chronic inflammatory skin disorder,
     mainly genital; in males the prepuce, glans and/or urethra.
   • Epidemiology: 1 in 300 to 1 in 1000 in Western populations; more common in females; male peak 30–
     50 years but seen from infancy to old age; commonly found at circumcision for phimosis beyond the
     neonatal period.
   • Lesions: erythematous → pale, hypopigmented, sclerotic plaques; ulceration, peeling, thickening. The
     meatus is most commonly affected and may be the early stage of later, more severe stricture from
     chronic obstruction. Phimosis in the uncircumcised (oedematous, thickened prepuce, possibly
     adherent to glans). Pain, pruritus, burning, obstructive voiding. Chronic and typically progressive.
   • Diagnosis is clinical; biopsy if inconclusive: band-like lymphocytic infiltrate, hyperkeratosis, epidermal
     atrophy, dermal oedema. LS is regarded as possibly premalignant.

 Medical management.
   • Topical steroids benefit many, though efficacy data are largely from females. Common regimen:
     clobetasol propionate or betamethasone 0.05%, once or twice daily for 1–2 months, then alternate
     days for 3–4 months.
   • Urethral involvement: intermittent catheterisation or meatal dilator lubricated with clobetasol — for
     poor surgical candidates, the elderly, and younger patients with stable disease.
   • Phimosis only → circumcision may be curative.
 Meatal and urethral disease.
   • Meatal stenosis: chronic dilation or surgery. Because a proximal stricture often coexists, perform
     retrograde urethrography (RUG) first.
   • Surgery: meatotomy or BMG onlay. Restenosis is commoner than in non-LS stenosis → extended
     meatotomy; ventral or dorsal meatotomy with an inverted-V relaxing incision prevents puckering.
   • Stricture mechanism unclear: repeated instrumentation, or pressure voiding behind meatal stenosis →
     intravasation of urine into the glands of Littre (Fig. 85.14). Urethral LS is limited to the anterior
     urethra.
   • Early meatal and fossa disease → prompt reconstruction may avert panurethral stricture. Longer
     segments are reconstructable but challenging; counsel that recurrence after urethroplasty is higher
     than in idiopathic or traumatic stricture.
   • Staged versus single stage: many advise staged BMG for all LS strictures, but a meta-analysis supports
     single-stage repair for many. Use oral mucosa, never genital skin — LS is a disease of genital skin.
 Buried penis in LS.
   • Shaft skin lost to inflammation traps the penis in the penopubic and scrotal area; patients are often
     overweight with prior circumcision or recircumcision. Management follows the patient's wishes and
     functional need:
   • Perineal urethrostomy — technically straightforward because LS spares the proximal anterior urethra;
     or complete urethral reconstruction; or, for standing voiding and sexual function, formal buried penis
     repair with skin graft.
   • Stricture plus buried penis → staged: limited release and urethroplasty first, formal repair with graft
     later. Urethroplasty via penile invagination avoids complications of penile skin incision. Counsel on
     reburying and persistent glans LS.
   • Malignant transformation in up to 8% of males: squamous cell carcinoma commonest; verrucous
     carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma and melanoma also reported.
 Concept — LS is a progressive disease of genital skin that spares the proximal urethra. That single fact
 explains why the meatus is the sentinel site, why a RUG must precede meatal surgery, why oral mucosa
 rather than skin is used, why recurrence is high and why perineal urethrostomy is easy.

 Amyloidosis
   • Exceedingly rare cause of obstructive voiding; also haematuria, dysuria, discharge or palpable mass —
     consider in any urethral mass. Workup: cystoscopy and biopsy. Management is symptom-directed:
     minimal symptoms → observe; bothersome obstruction → dilation or urethroplasty. Progression and
     recurrence are rare.

 Urethrocutaneous Fistula
   • Epithelium-lined tract from urethra to skin; most commonly iatrogenic — hypospadias repair, excision
     of urethral lesions, urethroplasty in gender-affirming phalloplasty — also piercings and periurethral
     infection with inflammatory stricture. Treat the underlying process as well as the defect.

 Urethral Diverticulum: Male
   • Urothelium-lined outpouching of the urethral lumen. Congenital: all layers of the urethral wall;
     acquired: lacks smooth muscle.
   • Acquired: upstream of a stricture, after urethral surgery or chronic catheterisation, infection or trauma.
     A "congenital" anterior diverticulum may reflect incomplete urethral development or straddle trauma
     with intraspongiosal haematoma.
   • Presentation: asymptomatic, post-void dribbling, recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI), mass, pain,
     stone in the pouch. Workup: cystourethroscopy and RUG.
   • Prostatic urethral diverticulum: a large müllerian remnant with diminished virilisation, or often an
     enlarged utricle in proximal hypospadias. Missed on voiding urethrography; shown by cystoscopy or
     RUG.
   • Consequences: the catheter tip can enter the utricle and drain a little urine, falsely suggesting bladder
     placement; large utricles fill on voiding and empty afterwards → recurrent UTI and "incontinence".

 Paraphimosis, Balanitis, and Phimosis
   • Paraphimosis: painful swelling of foreskin trapped proximal to the corona → venous congestion →
     progressive oedema → glans necrosis — a urological emergency. Typical in hospitalised patients after
     Foley catheterisation when the foreskin is not replaced; also children and adolescents.
   • Reduction: steady pressure for several minutes to express oedema → both thumbs push the glans
     while the fingers pull the foreskin forward. Never a sudden traumatic reduction. Granulated sugar
     (osmotic) has been suggested.
   • Recurrence is common → elective dorsal slit at minimum, or circumcision. Irreducible after hours to
     days → emergency dorsal slit or circumcision; marked postoperative oedema is the rule.
   • Phimosis (Greek "muzzling"): inability to retract the foreskin; physiological in infants, resolving
     between 1 and 8 years. In adults it prevents hygiene → smegma, balanitis, penile cancer. Treatment:
     topical steroid, dorsal slit, circumcision.
   • Balanitis (Greek "acorn"): nonspecific glans inflammation, far commoner in the uncircumcised; from
     inadequate or, rarely, excessive hygiene. Smegma alone inflames or harbours organisms — Candida
     commonest; also Streptococcus, sexually transmitted infection (STI), chemical dermatitis and plasma
     cell (Zoon) balanitis. Balanoposthitis = glans plus foreskin.
   • Treatment: keep clean and dry, exclude or treat STI, topical or oral antifungal for Candida, and check
     blood glucose for undiagnosed or poorly controlled diabetes. Balanitis raises penile cancer risk, so
     prevent recurrence.

 Urethral Meatal Stenosis
   • Children: complication of newborn circumcision (incidence quoted from 0.01% to 20%) — see Chapter
     55.
   • Adults: after instrumentation or catheterisation, or with LS or hypospadias.
   • Simple stenosis → ventral meatotomy with interrupted sutures at the apex and on each side to appose
     tissue and control bleeding. Alternatively chronic self-dilation with topical steroid as lubricant,
     especially in LS. Extension into the fossa → urethroplasty (Chapter 86).

 Circumcision
   • Adult circumcision under local and/or general anaesthesia; penile block along the dorsal nerves at the
     base. For men and older boys the authors favour a sleeve circumcision:
  1. Foreskin retracted → mark an incision leaving a small preputial cuff, straight across the frenular base
     → incise through dartos to the superficial lamina of Buck's fascia.
  2. Foreskin advanced → mark a second incision along the coronal margin and the V of the frenulum.
     Optional frenuloplasty (close the V longitudinally) makes the ventral skin straight, so the incision need
     not follow the V.
  3. Incise, fulgurating vessels with bipolar cautery as the edge is mobilised (adult vessels do not seal with
     compression) → remove the preputial sleeve → haemostasis.
  4. Dartos with interrupted polyglactin (Vicryl) in each quadrant → skin closure.
   • Complications uncommon: temporary glans hyperaesthesia (resolves as glans skin keratinises);
     haematoma or minor dehiscence; minor cosmetic imperfections.
   • Too much skin removed → discomfort with erection and buried penis; too little → adhesions and skin
     bridges. Prevent by marking on undistorted shaft skin, involving the patient in marking, and
     psychosexual counselling.
   • HIV prevention: the landmark sub-Saharan African randomised controlled trials (RCTs) (Auvert 2005,
     Bailey 2007, Gray 2007) showed about 60% reduction in female-to-male HIV acquisition; United States
     retrospective data show a similar reduction among exposed heterosexual men.
   • Also reduces herpes simplex virus type 2, human papillomavirus, genital ulcer disease and some
     bacterial STIs.
   • Men who have sex with men (MSM): meta-analysis shows reduced odds of HIV; protection is greatest
     for predominantly insertive anal intercourse, where an RCT found no seroconversions after
     circumcision against seroconversions in controls.
   • Biological basis: foreskin rich in HIV-susceptible CD4+ T cells, an anaerobic microbiome and
     inflammatory cytokines; removal lessens targets and inflammation. Uncircumcised men have more
     genital ulcers and coital microtears.

     • Urethrocutaneous fistula: treat the underlying process, not only the defect.

 PENILE TRANSPLANTATION

 General
   • After devastating phallic loss (trauma, malignancy, congenital anomaly) the options are phalloplasty
     (now common in masculinising gender-affirmation surgery; Chapter 128) or vascularised composite
     allotransplantation (VCA), which is uncommon and not widely available.
   • VCA advantages: natural appearance, potential erectile function and normal voiding. Challenges:
     lifelong immunosuppression and emotional, cultural, personal and ethical issues — of four successful
     transplants, one was explanted on postoperative day 14 for psychological reasons.

 BURIED PENIS AND FOREIGN BODY INJECTION

 Buried Penis
   • Adult acquired buried penis (AABP): entrapment of the phallus in surrounding skin and/or soft tissue.
     Obesity is the main risk factor; also aggressive circumcision, scrotal lymphoedema, LS.
   • Pathogenesis: excess prepubic tissue (escutcheon) → penis tethered to Scarpa fascia by the suspensory
     ligament → penopubic and penoscrotal angles migrate distally and the phallus telescopes → trapped
     urine and moisture → inflammation and infection → scarring and LS → further trapping.
   • Consequences: impaired voiding and sexual function, UTI, penile cancer, poor mental health,
     avoidance of social and romantic situations. Weight loss generally does not solve it.

 Foreign Body Injection
   • Self-injection for self-perceived inadequate size, across cultures since at least the late 1800s;

     necrotising infection. No infection but wishes normal size → excise all affected tissue and skin, cover
     with scrotoplasty and STSG (Fig. 85.16).

     referral; counsel frankly on realistic outcomes.

     complications (scarring, erosion) possibly under-reported.
