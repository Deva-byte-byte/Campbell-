 • Uncommon but devastating; diagnostically and therapeutically challenging for the urologist.
 • Rare in North America and Europe; a substantial health burden in parts of Africa, South America and Asia.
 • Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is the commonest penile malignancy; premalignant lesions are discussed

   palliative), systemic and multimodal therapy, and a risk-based scheme for the inguinal region built on
   histological and clinical features.


PREMALIGNANT CUTANEOUS LESIONS

Penile Intraepithelial Neoplasia (Formerly Carcinoma in Situ)

Pathological and Clinical Correlates
 • Penile intraepithelial neoplasia (PeIN): dysplastic penile squamous epithelium with an intact basement
   membrane. Like invasive disease, divided into human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated and HPV-
   independent (differentiated) PeIN.
 • HPV-associated PeIN is the commonest form and a precursor of invasive SCC; commonest subtypes basaloid
   and warty.
 • Mechanism of high-risk HPV (hrHPV) transformation: viral E6 binds p53, viral E7 binds retinoblastoma
   protein (Rb) → cell-cycle progression and altered apoptosis.
 • E7 inactivation of Rb → overexpression of p16INK4a (p16) → p16 immunohistochemistry is the surrogate
  marker of hrHPV and the basis of routine classification.
 • HPV-independent (differentiated) PeIN: hyperplastic squamous epithelium, keratin pearls, prominent
   intercellular bridges, atypical basal cells; difficult to distinguish from reactive hyperplasia (squamous or
   pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia).
 • 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification: grading PeIN I/II/III (mild/moderate/severe) is
   discouraged — all PeIN is regarded as high grade. "Low-/high-grade dysplasia" and "squamous carcinoma in
   situ" are to be abandoned as pathological terms.
 • Clinical eponyms — erythroplasia of Queyrat (EQ, glans), Bowen disease (BD) and bowenoid papulosis (BP)
   (shaft) — are useful clinically but have no unique histology and are discouraged as pathological entities; all
   three fall under PeIN.

 • EQ histology: atypical hyperplastic cells with disorientation, vacuolation, multiple hyperchromatic nuclei and
   mitoses at all levels; rete elongated, broadened, bulbous; submucosal capillary proliferation, ectasia and a
   plasma-cell-rich infiltrate — these distinguish EQ from chronic balanitis. Described by Queyrat, 1911.
 • Metastasis from PeIN is extremely rare but reported.

Treatment of PeIN
 • Histological confirmation first: multiple biopsies of adequate depth to exclude invasion.
 • Foreskin lesions: circumcision or excision with a negative margin is adequate.
 • Glans lesions are harder to excise while preserving anatomy → glans resurfacing: epithelium and
   subepithelial tissue dissected off the underlying spongiosum → defect closed with a skin graft; very low early
   local recurrence.
 • Alternatives: topical 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) cream, 5% imiquimod cream, laser ablation (neodymium:yttrium-
   aluminium-garnet [Nd:YAG], potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP) 532 nm, carbon dioxide).

   KEY POINTS: VIRUS-RELATED DERMATOLOGICAL LESIONS
    • Male HPV infection is associated with penile carcinoma and with cervical dysplasia and carcinoma in
      female partners.
    • Imiquimod cream is the standard topical treatment of choice for condyloma.
    • Giant condyloma of Buschke-Löwenstein: locally progressive, HPV related, non-metastatic, but needs
      complete excision and follow-up — coexisting SCC or malignant degeneration occurs.
    • PeIN is an intraepithelial malignant process presenting as three clinical entities: BP, BD, EQ.
    • BP: shaft of young males; does not progress to invasion.
    • Untreated progression: BD about 5%; EQ up to 33%. Metastasis rare.
    • Goal: cancer eradication with organ preservation.
    • Penile Kaposi sarcoma: associated with human herpesvirus 8; prompts investigation for human
      immunodeficiency virus (HIV) or other immunosuppression.

Concept — PeIN is defined by an intact basement membrane, not by a grade: every lesion is high grade, the
eponyms describe only site and appearance, and treatment is therefore organ-preserving eradication tailored to
where the lesion sits.

SQUAMOUS CELL CARCINOMA

Epidemiology
 • 0.4–0.6% of male malignancies in the United States (US) and Europe; up to 10% in parts of Asia, Africa and
   South America.
 • Divergent trends: falling in the US, India, Thailand, Philippines; rising in several European countries (including
   Denmark, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, Scandinavia), China and Israel — attributed to increasing
   HPV exposure and declining childhood circumcision.
 • Global 2020 estimates: age-standardised incidence about 0.8 per 100,000, mortality about 0.3 per 100,000.
 • Disease of older males: abrupt rise in the sixth decade; mean age in the mid-50s. Not unusual in younger
   males — a meaningful minority are under 40, occasionally under 30; reported in children.
 • Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER): US incidence similar in White and Black males but
   substantially higher in Puerto Rican Hispanic males.
 • Racial and ethnic disparity in mortality: independent predictors of worse survival are higher stage, age over
   65, African-American ethnicity and nodal disease; Black patients carry about twice the disease-specific risk of
   death; US Hispanic, Black and Puerto Rican males have higher mortality than White males. Causes unknown
   — biology, access or treatment.

Aetiology and Risk Factors
 • Incidence varies with circumcision practice, hygiene, phimosis, number of sexual partners, HPV, tobacco,
   obesity and other factors.

Phimosis and Circumcision
 • Neonatal circumcision virtually eliminates invasive penile carcinoma by removing the closed preputial
   environment.
 • Smegma (bacterial by-product of desquamated cells) is implicated through chronic irritation; not proven
   carcinogenic in humans, but the association is consistently observed.
 • Cycle: poor hygiene → smegma → inflammation → fibrotic healing → phimosis → perpetuation.
 • Phimosis (circumferential preputial fibrosis preventing retraction) is present in 25–75% of patients and carries
   a seven- to tenfold risk.
 • The risk of non-circumcision is mediated by phimosis: uncircumcised males with phimosis are at markedly
   raised risk; without phimosis the risk is not significantly elevated (underpowered).
 • Rare in Jewish populations (universal neonatal circumcision); less than 1% of US male malignancies; 10–20%
   in non-circumcising African tribes and Asian cultures.
 • Timing matters: rare after neonatal circumcision, more frequent when delayed to puberty; adult circumcision
   offers little or no protection — the critical exposure has occurred by puberty/adulthood (atypia is already
   present in phimotic foreskins).
 • Neonatal circumcision protects against invasive cancer but not against carcinoma in situ (CIS); phimosis
   associates with invasive disease but not CIS.
 • Circumcision protects against HIV-1; no protection against herpes simplex virus type 2, syphilis or
   gonorrhoea.

Human Papillomavirus Infection
 • First clue: wives or ex-wives of penile cancer patients had a threefold risk of cervical carcinoma; male
   partners of females with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia had more PeIN and more HPV.
 • Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and in situ hybridisation detect HPV DNA in penile lesions (benign and
   malignant) but not in normal foreskin. More than 40 types infect genital sites.

 • E6 complexes TP53; E7 binds Rb — via the p14ARF/MDM2/p53 and p16INK4a/cyclin D/Rb pathways — with
   p16 upregulation as the immunohistochemical surrogate of hrHPV.
 • HPV infection correlates with number of lifetime sexual partners, which also tracks penile cancer risk.
 • HPV is not invariable: prevalence in penile cancer is about half → other pathways exist → classification into
   HPV-associated and HPV-independent SCC (2022 WHO).
 • HPV positivity varies by subtype: high in basaloid and warty, low in keratinising (usual) and verrucous.
 • HPV-independent molecular events: CDKN2A silencing by promoter hypermethylation, INK4a/ARF-targeting
   gene expression, TP53 and p14ARF mutations, MDM2 overexpression; somatic variants (amplifications,
   deletions, loss of heterozygosity) and epigenetic change.
 • Inflammation-driven carcinogenesis: lichen sclerosus (LS), phimosis and chronic balanitis → reactive oxygen
   and nitrogen species → DNA damage and genomic instability; cyclo-oxygenase-2 (COX-2) induction →
   prostaglandins and thromboxanes → proliferation, angiogenesis, invasion.

HIV Infection
 • HIV is a risk factor for HPV infection (higher prevalence, multiple genotypes); both HPV and HIV are lower in
   circumcised males (African data).
 • HIV worsens disease: about fourfold risk of death from penile cancer; progression from intraepithelial
   neoplasia occurs about 6 years earlier.

Tobacco Products
 • Smoking raises risk, dose-dependently, independent of phimosis.
 • All forms — cigarettes, chewing tobacco, snuff — are independently associated.
 • Hypothesis: tobacco acts with HPV or inflammation-associated bacteria; shared with other anogenital
   carcinomas.

Penile Trauma and Lichen Sclerosus
 • Trauma: carcinoma in the scarred shaft after mutilating circumcision is a distinct entity; penile tears and
   rashes raise risk; penile injury preceding onset carries a very high odds ratio.
 • Lichen sclerosus (balanitis xerotica obliterans): subsequent cancer in 2.3–9% on long follow-up; LS associates
   specifically with HPV-independent carcinoma.

Obesity
 • Risk factor for invasive cancer via buried penis, inability to examine and clean → smegma and recurrent
   inflammation (functional phimosis); risk rises with each 5-unit increase in body mass index (BMI).
 • No convincing link with occupation, other venereal diseases (gonorrhoea, syphilis, herpes), marijuana or
   alcohol.

Prevention
 • Neonatal circumcision is controversial. American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) evolution: no medical benefit
   (1989) → "potential medical benefits" (1999) → August 2012: benefits outweigh risks, justifying access for
   families who choose it (urinary tract infection, penile cancer, sexually transmitted infections including HIV).
 • Penile carcinoma is the only neoplasm with a predictable, simple prophylaxis that spares the organ at risk.

 • Hygiene may substitute where good: Denmark (circumcision rate very low) saw falling incidence as bathing
   facilities spread — but rates have since risen, probably HPV-driven, so circumcision may still matter in
   developed countries.
 • Modifiable prevention: avoid phimosis (hygiene), avoid HPV, avoid tobacco.
 • HPV vaccination should have an emerging role: bivalent (16/18, Cervarix), quadrivalent (16/18/6/11,
   Gardasil), nonavalent Gardasil 9 (16/18/6/11/31/33/45/52/58); efficacy proven in HPV-negative young
   individuals; wider uptake is the goal.

Natural History
 • Begins as a small lesion → extends over glans, shaft and corpora; papillary/exophytic or flat/ulcerative;
   autoamputation if untreated.
 • Flat ulcerative tumours grow at a similar rate to papillary ones but have earlier nodal metastasis and poorer
   5-year survival.
 • Lesions over 5 cm or covering more than 75% of the shaft associate with more metastases and lower survival
   — not consistently reproduced.
 • Buck fascia is a temporary barrier; penetration of Buck fascia and tunica albuginea → corporal invasion →
   potential vascular dissemination.
 • Earliest dissemination: regional femoral and iliac nodes.

Lymphatic drainage — sequence
  Prepuce and shaft skin → superficial inguinal nodes (external to fascia lata); glans and corpora → collar at
  penile base → superficial nodes → deep inguinal nodes (deep to fascia lata) → pelvic nodes (external iliac,
  internal iliac, obturator).
 • Consistent stepwise pattern on lymphangiography; cross-connections at every level → drainage is bilateral.
 • Nodal enlargement → skin necrosis, chronic infection, death from inanition, sepsis or femoral vessel erosion
   haemorrhage.
 • Clinically detectable distant metastases (lung, liver, bone, brain) are uncommon (1–10%), late, usually after
   local treatment; distant disease without nodal disease is unusual.
 • Relentless course: most untreated patients die within 2 years; no spontaneous remission reported; second
   primary neoplasms in 5–15%.

   KEY POINTS: EPIDEMIOLOGY, AETIOLOGY AND PREVENTION
    • Rare in developed countries; varies with age, circumcision, HPV exposure, lifestyle and hygiene.
    • Disparity in US incidence and outcome, notably for Puerto Rican Hispanic males.
    • Risk factors: no neonatal circumcision, phimosis, HPV and HIV, tobacco, LS, obesity, trauma.
    • Largely preventable: neonatal circumcision, HPV vaccination, behaviour modification.

Concept — Two carcinogenic routes converge on the preputial sac: an HPV-driven route (E6/E7, p16-positive,
basaloid/warty) and an inflammation-driven HPV-independent route (phimosis, smegma, LS, obesity) — which is
why neonatal circumcision, hygiene and vaccination each prevent part of the disease, and adult circumcision
prevents almost none.


MODES OF PRESENTATION

Signs
 • The penile lesion itself usually alerts the patient: subtle induration, small excrescence, papule, pustule, warty
   growth, luxuriant exophytic mass, shallow erosion, or deeply excavated ulcer with rolled edges.

 • Phimosis may conceal the tumour → silent progression → eventually erosion through prepuce, foul odour,
   discharge ± bleeding.
 • Site distribution: glans commonest (about half), then prepuce, glans plus prepuce, coronal sulcus; shaft rare
   (under 2%) — reflecting exposure of glans, sulcus and inner prepuce to irritants in the preputial sac.
 • Rare presentations: inguinal mass, ulceration, suppuration or haemorrhage from nodes of a tumour hidden
   under a phimotic foreskin; urinary retention or urethral fistula.

Symptoms
 • Pain is disproportionately slight and rarely the presenting complaint.
 • Weakness, weight loss, fatigue, malaise from chronic suppuration; occasional significant blood loss from
   primary or nodes.
 • Symptoms of distant metastasis are rare at presentation — local and regional disease is far advanced first.

Diagnosis

Delay
 • Patient delay is characteristic: 15–50% wait more than a year — embarrassment, guilt, fear, ignorance,
   neglect — despite daily handling of the organ.
 • Physician delay: prolonged antibiotic or antifungal courses before biopsy.
 • Survival effect of delay is inconsistent across series, but earlier diagnosis logically improves outcome.

Examination
 • Most lesions are confined to the penis at presentation.
 • Assess the primary: size, location, fixation, corporal involvement; inspect penile base and scrotum.
 • Rectal and bimanual examination: perineal body involvement, pelvic mass.
 • Careful bilateral inguinal palpation is extremely important.

Biopsy
 • Biopsy confirming carcinoma, depth of invasion and grade is mandatory before definitive therapy — it
   guides treatment of the primary and predicts occult nodal risk in cN0 patients.
 • May be separate from definitive surgery; dorsal slit often needed for exposure; alternatively biopsy with
   frozen section → immediate resection or ablation.
 • Superficial biopsies mislead: depth frequently indeterminable, grade discordant (especially verrucous and
   mixed patterns), occasional failure to detect well-differentiated cancer. Adequate specimen cannot be
   overemphasised.

Histological Features
 • Most penile tumours are SCC: keratinisation, epithelial pearls, variable mitoses, disrupted rete pegs,
   basement membrane breached.

 • Pattern: HPV-associated subtypes split into a bulky low-risk (warty) and a highly aggressive (basaloid) form;
   HPV-independent subtypes span the spectrum from non-metastasising (verrucous) to the most lethal
   (sarcomatoid).
Grading
 • Broders classification (1921, cutaneous SCC): keratinisation, pleomorphism, mitoses; four grades, often
   collapsed to three. Low grade (I–II) is 70–80% at diagnosis.
 • Low-grade lesions: cords of atypical cells from a hyperkeratotic epidermis, keratin, intercellular bridges, pearls
   — absent in high grade.
 • Site–grade link: almost half of shaft tumours are poorly differentiated, about 10% of preputial tumours →
   grade and stage correlate.
 • High grade correlates with nodal metastasis; the unmet need is stratifying the majority low/intermediate-
   grade tumours.
 • Current: three-tier WHO/International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grading:
    ◦ Grade I: resembles normal squamous tissue; irregular nests, little stroma.
     ◦ Grade III: small irregular nests, poor keratinisation, pleomorphic hyperchromatic cells, frequent mitoses,
       marked stromal reaction.
     ◦ Any proportion of grade III (anaplastic) features = grade III; the remainder = grade II.
 • Rationale: half of tumours are grade-heterogeneous, and any grade III component associated with nodal
   metastasis.

Invasion variables
 • Vascular invasion predicted nodal metastasis in every study assessing it.
 • Perineural invasion (about one-third of cases) is a strong predictor of nodal metastasis.
 • Synoptic reporting (College of American Pathologists protocol) is standard: core variables for eighth-edition
   tumour-node-metastasis (TNM) staging — grade, structure invaded, lymphovascular and perineural invasion,
   margins — plus optional variables.

   KEY POINTS: NATURAL HISTORY AND PRESENTATION
    • Begins on the glans or prepuce and enlarges progressively.
    • Delay in presentation and in biopsy is common.
    • Examination of primary and groins is critical to planning.
    • Metastasis is by lymphatic embolisation to inguinal nodes; distant metastases occur late.


   KEY POINTS: BIOPSY AND HISTOLOGICAL FEATURES
    • Adequate biopsy is essential.
    • Subtypes now fall into two groups by HPV relationship, with distinct morphology and behaviour.
    • Structure invaded, grade, vascular and perineural invasion predict metastasis and define eighth-edition
      T stage.

Laboratory Studies
 • Often normal; anaemia, leucocytosis, hypoalbuminaemia with chronic suppuration and malnutrition;
   azotaemia from urethral or ureteric obstruction.
 • Hypercalcaemia without bone metastases (about one-fifth in one series): a function of disease bulk, linked
   to inguinal metastases, may resolve after excision of involved nodes; tumour production of parathyroid
   hormone and related substances → osteoclastic resorption.
  Hypercalcaemia management: aggressive saline hydration → diuretic only if volume overloaded →
  bisphosphonate first line (pamidronate, etidronate, zoledronic acid; safer than mithramycin) → for severe
  neurological hypercalcaemia add a calciuric agent such as calcitonin for rapid lowering.

Radiological Studies
Primary penile tumour
 • Physical examination misstages about one-quarter (both under- and overstaging).
 • Ultrasound (7.5 MHz linear): cancer is invariably hypoechoic; underestimates thickness; cannot separate
   glans T1 from spongiosal T2; but tunica albuginea always seen → high sensitivity for cavernosal invasion.
 • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with gadolinium: good soft-tissue detail; not useful for T1. Computed
   tomography (CT): poor soft-tissue resolution, not useful for the primary.
 • Artificial erection (intracorporeal prostaglandin E1) may augment contrast MRI; meta-analysis: MRI
   specificity for corporal invasion is high with or without artificial erection.

Inguinal and pelvic region — clinically node-negative (cN0)
 • Examination reliability varies with primary grade and stage and with body habitus.
 • CT and MRI depend on node enlargement and cannot read internal architecture of normal-sized nodes; CT is
   usually chosen for groins, pelvis and distant disease.
 • CT and lymphangiography add nothing to examination in impalpable groins (low sensitivity, high specificity).
 • Caveat: CT helps in obese patients and after prior inguinal surgery, where examination is unreliable.
 • Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (FDG-PET/CT): SCC is FDG-avid, but sensitivity for
   occult cN0 disease is poor.
 • Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) in cN0 groins also has limited sensitivity.
 • No imaging modality detects microscopic nodal metastasis reliably.
Clinically node-positive (cN+)
 • CT features — central necrosis or irregular nodal border — are highly sensitive and specific for poor-
   prognosis pathology (three or more positive nodes, extranodal extension [ENE], positive pelvic nodes).
 • FDG-PET/CT in cN+ or T3–T4: good sensitivity and negative predictive value for pelvic nodes, high positive
   predictive value for distant lesions.
 • Distant metastases: late, usually with significant inguinal/pelvic disease; commonest sites lung, bone, liver.
   For bulky regional adenopathy: chest, abdominal and pelvic CT, and FDG-PET/CT performs best.

Definition of the primary tumour — changes from the seventh edition
 • Ta: formerly "non-invasive verrucous carcinoma" (misread as covering all verrucous tumours, most of which
   are destructive with a smooth pushing front). Now: pure verrucous carcinoma without overt destructive
   invasion, and non-invasive papillary, warty, basaloid or mixed carcinomas — analogous to pTa urothelial
   tumours.
 • T1 anatomical detail: "subepithelial connective tissue" replaced by site-specific layers (glans, foreskin, shaft).
 • T1a vs T1b validated: pT1b has a markedly higher nodal rate than pT1a; perineural invasion now joins LVI
   and high grade in defining pT1b.
 • T2 vs T3 split: spongiosum (T2) vs cavernosa (T3) — cavernosal invasion carries worse disease-specific
   survival and more nodal metastasis.
 • Urethral invasion removed as a T3 criterion — no independent prognostic value.
 • T4 (scrotum, prostate, pubic bone): uncommon; may need major amputation, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, or
   palliative radiotherapy if unresectable.
Pathological N changes
 • pN1 widened to one or two unilateral nodes without ENE: survival of two nodes equals one node (about
   90% 3-year disease-specific survival) → more patients classed low risk, avoiding adjuvant therapy
   recommended for pN2 (joint European Association of Urology–American Society of Clinical Oncology [EAU-
   ASCO] guideline).
 • pN2 = three or more unilateral or bilateral nodes: clearly worse survival (about 60%).
 • pN3 = ENE or pelvic nodes: ominous (about one-third 3-year survival); adjuvant therapy strongly considered.
 • Stage groups are driven by nodal status — the eighth edition's strength is combining accurate primary
   staging with improved clinical and pathological nodal descriptors.
 • T stage assigned by biopsy, more reliably by complete resection, incorporating grade, LVI/PNI and cavernosal
   involvement. Palpable adenopathy plus primary histology determines further imaging; FNAC of palpable
   nodes or of CT-detected pelvic nodes helps assign N before therapy.

SURGICAL MANAGEMENT OF THE PRIMARY TUMOUR

Penile Preservation
 • Amputation is the oncological gold standard for rapid definitive control: local recurrence 0–8%.
 • Cost: reduced sexual quality of life; negative well-being and psychiatric symptoms in up to half. Relevant
   because more than half of patients are 60 or younger.
 • Favourable histology — Tis, Ta, T1; grade I–II — carries lower metastatic risk and suits organ- or glans-
   sparing procedures.
 • Goals: preserve glans sensation where possible, otherwise maximise shaft length.
 • Options: topical 5-FU or imiquimod (Tis only), radiotherapy, Mohs surgery, limited excision, laser ablation.

Penile-Preserving Strategies

Circumcision and Other Excision Strategies
 • Circumcision, limited glans excision, and glans removal with shaft sparing preserve function and length.
   Historical recurrence 11–50% — but grade, size, location and margins were usually unreported.
 • Intraoperative frozen section makes conservative surgery safe in selected discrete tumours.
 • The 2-cm margin dogma is abandoned:
    ◦ Microscopic proximal spread tracks grade — 5 mm for grade I–II, 10 mm for grade III; no skip lesions.
       ◦ Wide local excision or glans removal with margins well under 2 cm: low local recurrence; larger organ-
         sparing series: modest recurrence that did not affect disease-specific survival.

     ◦ Only margins under 1 mm raised local recurrence → negative margin required; distance should exceed 1

 • Limitations: deeply invasive proximal or distal tumours, high grade, and patients too unfit for salvage.

Glans resurfacing (glans stripping) — for glans CIS
  Document absence of invasion → subdermal dissection of skin and subepithelial connective tissue off the

 • Total resurfacing gives far fewer positive margins than partial; some patients need re-excision for
   unexpected invasion at the margin; clinical recurrence rare.
 • Graft take high; erectile function preserved.

Mohs Micrographic Surgery
 • Layer-by-layer excision with microscopic examination of each undersurface (original fixed-tissue technique)
   → precise margin control with maximal preservation.
 • Original series: high eradication; recurrence linked to size of 3 cm or more, advanced stage, prior failed
   therapy.
 • Not reproduced with modern frozen-section Mohs: recurrence in about one-third (mostly salvaged by repeat
   Mohs) → no clear advantage over excision with frozen-section margins.

TREATMENT OF THE INGUINAL NODES

Overview
 • Presence and extent of inguinal metastasis is the most important prognostic factor — more than grade,
   gross appearance or microscopic pattern of the primary.
 • Lymphadenectomy alone can be curative, unlike most genitourinary cancers — because penile SCC has a
   prolonged locoregional phase before distant spread.
 • Controversies: selecting lymphadenectomy vs observation; low-morbidity staging procedures; multimodal
   therapy for bulky nodes. No randomised trials — retrospective and prospective series only.

Sentinel lymph node biopsy and extended sentinel lymph node dissection
 • Cabanas: lymphangiography showed consistent drainage to a sentinel node superomedial to the

Dynamic sentinel node biopsy
 • Precise sentinel localisation with the lowest morbidity of any surgical staging: vital blue dye or fluorescein
   plus hand-held gamma probe intraoperatively.
 • Netherlands Cancer Institute evolution: initial false-negative rate about 18% → modifications:
    ◦ Pre-operative inguinal ultrasound ± FNAC (subtle architectural change can divert lymph flow; positive
      FNAC → immediate dissection, bypassing DSNB).
     ◦ Serial sectioning and cytokeratin immunohistochemistry of sentinel nodes.
     ◦ Exploration of groins with low or no signal.
 • Two-centre update (Netherlands Cancer Institute and St George's, London): false-negative rate about 7%.
   Systematic review: false-negative 3.5–22%; minor complications 1–20%, major 0–7%.
 • Guidelines: at high-volume centres with a standard protocol, sensitivity is acceptable. National
   Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and EAU-ASCO recommend DSNB; EAU-ASCO names it the

Superficial and fascia-sparing complete inguinal dissection
 • Superficial ILND (SILND): removes nodes superficial to fascia lata; if frozen section positive → complete
   ipsilateral ilioinguinal dissection. Rationale: deep nodes are not involved unless superficial nodes are;
   negative SILND → no recurrence in follow-up → no pelvic dissection needed if negative.
 • Fascia-sparing ILND (FS-ILND): superficial boundaries plus removal of nodes and fascia from the femoral
   vessels within the fossa ovalis; excellent disease-free survival in pN0–pN1, good in pN2 with adjuvant
   therapy.
 • Disadvantage: higher per-groin complications than DSNB (about a quarter vs about one-seventh).
 • Advantages: more information than a single node; no risk of missing the sentinel (all first-echelon nodes
   removed); no specialised equipment.
 • Guideline-endorsed alternatives when DSNB is unavailable or not preferred.
Minimally invasive inguinal lymphadenectomy
 • Laparoscopic and robotic: longer operating time, equivalent nodal yield and recurrence, markedly fewer
   complications (especially skin) than open.
 • Evidence almost entirely in cN0; oncological results in higher-stage disease await validation. EAU-ASCO:
   acceptable for high-risk cN0 when DSNB is not feasible or preferred.

 • In resectable metastatic adenopathy, therapeutic value justifies morbidity. Open complications: wound

 • Assumptions: primary controlled, pathological T stage known, groins examined; CT abdomen/pelvis and chest
   imaging as indicated.

  Impalpable groins → bilateral DSNB (or SILND, FS-ILND or minimally invasive ILND) → both negative: observe;
  either side positive (permanent sections, second sitting): ipsilateral ILND; PLND optional, by pathology (more

Bulky Adenopathy and Fixed Nodal Metastasis (Fig. 82.4C)
 • Survival depends on complete eradication — rarely achievable by any single modality; surgery plus
   chemotherapy shows benefit.
 • Expanded MD Anderson neoadjuvant series (mostly paclitaxel, ifosfamide, cisplatin [TIP]): about two-thirds
   responded; 5-year survival stratified by response — highest with complete/partial remission, intermediate
   with stable disease, lowest with progression; a minority rendered pN0; long-term survival tied to
   chemotherapy response and favourable post-resection pathology.
 • Response improves resectability and survival; surgery in non-responders occasionally cures but usually
   precedes rapid locoregional or distant relapse.
 • Palliative groin dissection rarely palliates; endoluminal vascular stents give transient protection against
   femoral erosion.
 • Chemoradiation for cisplatin-ineligible patients: phase II mitomycin + capecitabine + radiotherapy in stage
   IV M0 → most responded on FDG-PET/CT, over one-third completely.
 • International Penile Advanced Cancer Trial (InPACT; EA 8134; NCT02305654): neoadjuvant chemotherapy →
   ILND vs neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy → ILND in advanced regional disease; will give high-level evidence.
  Fixed nodes, mobile nodes 4 cm or more, or pelvic nodes (after imaging) → cisplatin-eligible? Yes: induction/
  neoadjuvant chemotherapy → responsive or stable → ilioinguinal resection of all residual disease;
  progressive → clinical trial, palliative chemotherapy, radiation to affected side → further progression →
  hospice. No: radiotherapy ± chemotherapy (chemoradiation) → responsive or stable → ilioinguinal resection;
  progressive → clinical trial or palliation → hospice.

   KEY POINTS: TREATMENT OF THE INGUINAL NODES
    • Presence and extent of inguinal metastasis determine survival.
    • Persistent palpable adenopathy → ultrasound- or CT-guided biopsy, then scenario-appropriate
      management.
    • In impalpable groins, risk from the primary (TNM) selects DSNB, superficial/open or minimally invasive
      ILND, or close follow-up.
    • High cure: ≤2 inguinal nodes, unilateral, no ENE, no pelvic nodes; higher volumes → adjuvant or
      neoadjuvant therapy.
    • Morbidity has fallen: fewer radical resections, better peri-operative care.
    • SILND, FS-ILND and minimally invasive ILND reliably detect micrometastasis without special facilities but
      are more morbid than DSNB.
    • Modified DSNB: low morbidity, externally validated at high-volume centres, preferred staging for cN0
      where feasible.
    • Laparoscopic/robotic ILND: open-equivalent yields in cN0; not yet routine for cN+.
    • FS-ILND and R-ILND remain standard for palpable (cN+) disease.
    • PLND when more than two inguinal nodes are positive or ENE is present.

Concept — Because penile SCC lingers in the groin before it spreads, the node is both the prognosis and the
cure: everything in this section trades the survival gain of operating on small-volume nodes early against the
morbidity of operating on negative groins — and TNM risk plus DSNB is the device that lets the surgeon win both.


RADIATION THERAPY

Radiation Therapy of the Penile Primary
 • Curative potential with preservation of form and function; surgical salvage remains curative for recurrence
   → reasonable first strategy in selected patients.

Chapter 82 — Tumours of the Penis                                                                       RADIATION THERAPY


 • External-beam radiotherapy (EBRT) or interstitial brachytherapy.
 • Circumcision before radiotherapy is necessary: exposes the lesion, allows surface infection to settle, prevents
   preputial oedema and later phimosis, and may debulk the tumour.

External-Beam Radiotherapy
 • Advantages: widely available, homogeneous dose, no interventional skill required.
 • Contemporary dose: 60 Gy in 25 fractions (5 weeks) to 74 Gy in 37 fractions (7.5 weeks); older series used
   50–55 Gy.
 • Set-up: supine; penis held vertical in a bivalved wax or Perspex block with a central cylindrical chamber;
   minimal air gaps; larger chamber if oedema develops.
 • Skin dose problem: megavoltage beams spare the skin, but penile cancer is cutaneous → the tissue-
   equivalent block acts as bolus, delivering full dose to the surface.
 • Results: 5-year local control 44–70%; penile preservation about 40–66%; EAU-ASCO pooled 5-year
   recurrence-free rate about 55% → at least an even chance of avoiding amputation; salvage by partial or
   total amputation in most failures; cause-specific survival 58–96% by T and N.
 • Predictors of better control: dose above 60 Gy, overall time 45 days or less, fraction size at least 2 Gy. Worse
   control: T3, over 4 cm, high grade.
 • → Minimum tumour dose 66 Gy in 2-Gy fractions over 6.5 weeks. Hypofractionation (over 2 Gy per fraction)
   may increase toxicity.
 • Used most in patients unfit for surgery (age, comorbidity) or with locoregionally advanced disease (primary
   treated in continuity with both groins and pelvis); may be combined with weekly cisplatin radiosensitisation
   (as in head and neck and cervix).

Brachytherapy
 • Iridium-192 via a temporary parallel array of interstitial needles through and around the tumour.

Low-dose-rate brachytherapy
 • Institut Gustave Roussy (LDR and PDR, over four decades): local recurrence under one-fifth; dose under 62
   Gy → local failure; tumour over 4 cm → worse disease-free survival.
 • Late toxicity drivers: dose over 60 Gy and treatment volume over 25 cc → meatal stenosis; dose over 65 Gy
   and dose rate over 0.43 Gy/hour → painful ulceration.
 • Penile preservation at 5 years about 85–88%, falling to about two-thirds at 10 years.
 • Local failure can be very late — most within 2 years, but some at 3.5–7.5 years and up to 23 years; yet
   salvage penectomy needed in only about 3%.

High-dose-rate interstitial brachytherapy
 • Established in breast, prostate, and essential in cervix; technique as LDR with a catheter scaffold.
 • Limited cumulative experience; penile preservation about 70–86% at 5 years. Largest mature series: median
   about 49 Gy after biopsy (36 Gy after gross excision), 5-year local control about three-quarters, preservation
   about 70% → comparable to LDR/PDR.

Surface Mould Brachytherapy
 • For non-invasive or very superficial tumours: rapid dose fall-off with depth.
 • Careful selection: depth is hard to judge clinically or radiologically, and a dose margin (analogous to a surgical
   margin) is needed.
 • HDR revival: three-dimensional-printed custom moulds or cylindrical applicators; sources set off the skin to
   limit skin dose while reaching depth; suits recurrence after laser or topical therapy.
 • Reaction peaks about 3 weeks, heals over 6–10 weeks; small series only, no long-term data.

Radiation Treatment of Lymph Node Regions
 • Surgical staging, not "prophylactic" groin radiotherapy, is recommended for high-risk cN0 (EAU-ASCO, NCCN)
   → treatment tailored to actual pathology.
 • Surgery for resectable adenopathy also yields node count and ENE.

Inguinal Radiotherapy
 • Adjuvant radiotherapy for node-positive penile SCC is controversial: no level 1 evidence; not recommended
   in prior guidelines; used by extrapolation from head and neck, cervix, vulva and anal canal.
 • Supporting data: National Cancer Database stage III patients after ILND — adjuvant radiotherapy improved
   OS; multicentre series — omitting adjuvant pelvic radiotherapy after positive PLND worsened OS and DSS.
 • More than two positive nodes, negative pelvis: adjuvant radiotherapy gave longer OS than adjuvant
   chemotherapy, with no in-field failures despite frequent ENE — at 54 Gy for ENE, 57–60 Gy for gross
   residual.
 • Dose is critical: at 50 Gy in-field failures were frequent and benefit confined to ENE-negative patients; in pN3,
   in-field failure doubled at 50 Gy or less. Genomic modelling suggests higher doses are needed for primary
   and nodes.
 • HPV-positive patients benefit from adjuvant radiotherapy (OS gain maintained on propensity matching; not
   seen with adjuvant chemotherapy).

Pelvic Radiotherapy
 • PLND recommended for high-risk groin pathology (N2–N3) but not practised everywhere; Danish inguinal-
   pelvic chemoradiotherapy (all ENE, most bilateral) gave encouraging median OS.
 • InPACT randomises high-risk groin pathology to chemoradiotherapy vs PLND then chemoradiotherapy.

Radiation Combined With Sensitising Agent
 • HPV/p16 status may predict chemoradiotherapy responsiveness (as in head and neck): node-positive p16-
   positive patients had much better locoregional control after post-LND chemoradiotherapy; regardless of HPV,
   adjuvant chemoradiotherapy improved 2-year locoregional control.
 • Adjuvant chemoradiotherapy improved cancer-specific survival (CSS) at 1–2 years despite worse selected
   patients (more nodes, pN3, ENE) — OS equalled that of more favourable untreated patients.

    • Radiation is an effective penile-preserving option; brachytherapy for T1–T2 under 4 cm; EBRT also for

    • One in five recurrences occurs after 5 years → continued follow-up; salvage penectomy may cure.

Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy
 • Bulky nodes = extensive lymphatic disease; few are cured by surgery alone. Neoadjuvant therapy treats
   systemic disease early and downsizes nodes → complete resection in responders.
 • Pagliaro et al. (2010) — landmark phase II: four cycles of neoadjuvant TIP for clinical N2–N3: objective
   response rate (ORR) 50%, a few pathological complete responses, median time to progression about 8
   months, median OS 17.1 months; long-term disease-free survival in about two-thirds of responders vs very
   few non-responders. → Neoadjuvant TIP for cisplatin-eligible patients with high-risk inguinal nodes or any
   pelvic nodes.
 • TPF: phase II missed its primary end point with significant toxicity; neoadjuvant TPF gave poor progression-
   free survival and frequent discontinuation for toxicity → may be too difficult for some.
 • Meta-analysis of neoadjuvant studies: pooled ORR about half, overall mortality about half. No standard
   regimen; evidence favours cisplatin-plus-taxane.
 • Summary: neoadjuvant cisplatin-containing chemotherapy is active for unresectable primaries or bulky
   nodes in fit patients, enabling curative resection.

Adjuvant Chemotherapy
 • Historical Milan (Pizzocaro) VBM — vincristine, bleomycin, methotrexate, 12 weekly courses in high-risk
   node-positive patients: very few relapses; adjuvant VBM roughly doubled 5-year survival vs surgery alone.
   Unconfirmed and will not be re-studied because of bleomycin and methotrexate toxicity.
 • Pelvic-node-positive retrospective series (TIP, TPF, bleomycin–methotrexate–cisplatin, VBM): adjuvant
   chemotherapy associated with about a year's survival advantage and lower all-cause mortality —
   confounded; needs prospective confirmation.
 • National Cancer Database: no survival difference after adjustment.
 • Bottom line: no strong evidence that adjuvant chemotherapy improves OS; relapse-free survival poorly
   studied; benefit likely greatest in pN3, especially pelvic node involvement → balanced discussion.

Palliative Systemic Therapy

Chemotherapy
 • Platinum-based first line is preferred; triplet vs doublet and cisplatin vs carboplatin chosen by comorbidity.
 • TPF and TIP each combine two neurotoxic drugs → unsuitable with pre-existing neuropathy or low GFR.
   Paclitaxel–carboplatin extrapolated from other SCC sites.
 • First-line chemotherapy roughly triples median OS versus best supportive care (months, not years).
 • Vinflunine single agent (prospective; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group [ECOG] performance status ≤2,
   GFR ≥60 mL/min): modest response, median OS about 8 months, manageable toxicity.
 • Paclitaxel second line: modest ORR, median OS about 5 months.
 • Second-line chemotherapy is minimally effective: median OS 6 months or less; single-agent vinflunine or
   taxane is an alternative to platinum but less effective.

Immunotherapy
 • Checkpoint blockade, HPV-directed vaccines, adoptive T-cell therapy under study; rationale: relatively high
   PD-L1 expression.
 • Checkpoint inhibitor trials: low response rates (about one in six); basket trials (nivolumab + cabozantinib ±
   ipilimumab; ipilimumab + nivolumab) included few penile patients with occasional partial response or stable
   disease.
 • Routine checkpoint inhibition is not supported; chemotherapy-exposed patients belong in early-phase
   basket trials.

Targeted Therapies
 • Beyond first line, options are limited; actionable alterations are rare.
 • Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) targeting (as in other SCC): sporadic palliative responses to
   cetuximab and panitumumab; dacomitinib (oral pan-HER inhibitor) phase II: response in about one-third,
   one complete response.

NONSQUAMOUS PENILE MALIGNANT NEOPLASMS
 • About 5% of penile neoplasms (SCC 95%); evidence from case reports and small series — incidence,
   pathology, treatment, and parallels to non-genital counterparts.
 • Melanoma and basal cell carcinoma are rare because penile skin is sun-protected.

Basal Cell Carcinoma
 • Very rare on the penis (tiny fraction of male BCC); commonly proximal shaft near the base — contrast with
   distal SCC.
 • Slow growing, long diagnostic delay; local excision virtually always curative; Mohs used; metastasis
   essentially unreported (single case).
 • Fibroepithelioma of Pinkus: premalignant variant on the shaft; diagnosed and cured by excision.
 • No consistent link with immunosuppression, other skin cancers or prior radiation.
 • Mesenchymal (smooth or striated muscle, fibrous, fatty, vascular) malignancies are also rare; treatment from
   case series.

Melanoma
 • More than 150 cases; under 1% of penile cancers.
 • Blue-black or reddish-brown pigmented papule, plaque or ulcer on the glans (prepuce less often). Histology:
   atypical junctional activity with pigmented cells displaced into the dermis.
 • Clark (level) and Breslow (thickness) not prospectively validated on the penis; the AJCC cutaneous
   melanoma system (incorporating both) was used in the largest series.
 • Older series: distant spread in most; contemporary: early-stage node-negative tumours do well.
 • Spread: haematogenous via corporal vasculature; lymphatic permeation to inguinal and pelvic nodes.
 • Surgery is primary; radiotherapy and chemotherapy adjunctive or palliative.
 • Historical: partial or total penectomy with en bloc bilateral ilioinguinal dissection for localised or single-region
   disease.
 • MD Anderson algorithm: foreskin → circumcision; glans → partial penectomy; glans-shaft → partial or total;
   bilateral modified ILND if Breslow ≥1 mm, ulceration, or Clark IV–V. DSNB unproven but reasonable at
   experienced centres.
 • Mucosal-site melanoma (glans, meatus, fossa navicularis, distal urethra) looks more aggressive than
   cutaneous, possibly from diagnostic delay; stage-matched outcomes are similar.
 • Checkpoint immunotherapy has revolutionised recurrent/metastatic melanoma — adjuvant nivolumab after
   resected stage IIIB–IV gave high 12-month recurrence-free survival → melanoma multidisciplinary team or
   expert referral.

Sarcomas
 • Rare; benign and malignant mesenchymal tumours equally common; newborn to eighth decade; same
   presentation for benign and malignant — mass, pain, enlargement, priapism, obstruction; may mimic a
   Peyronie plaque.
 • Malignant: proximal shaft; benign: distal. Commonest malignant: vascular (haemangioendothelioma), then
   neural, myogenic, fibrous; case reports of malignant fibrous histiocytoma, angiosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma,
   epithelioid sarcoma, osteosarcoma.
 • Superficial (integumentary) → wide local excision or partial amputation; deep (corporal) → total amputation.
   Local recurrence is characteristic.
 • Leiomyosarcoma: under 2 cm → local resection; deeper → partial or total amputation; deep lesions at the
   base have the worst prognosis.
 • Regional and distant metastases rare → no node dissection unless palpable; aggressive local treatment aims
   at cure. Radiotherapy and chemotherapy unassessed.

Extramammary Paget Disease
 • Rare; large series from China and Korea. Erythematous, eczematoid, well-demarcated — clinically
   indistinguishable from EQ, BD or CIS; pruritus, discomfort, serosanguineous discharge; penis, scrotum,
   perianal region, axilla — sites of apocrine glands.
 • Paget cells: large, clear, hydropic cells with hypochromatic nuclei; cytokeratin 7, carcinoembryonic antigen
   (CEA), gross cystic disease fluid protein positive; S-100 negative.
 • Behaves as a slow intraepithelial apocrine adenocarcinoma; may invade and spread to nodes via dermal
   lymphatics.
 • Associated genitourinary malignancies (prostate, bladder, kidney) and others — nearly half had another
   cancer, usually diagnosed before the EMPD → screen.
 • Treatment: resect skin and dermis with up to 3 cm gross margin; frozen sections guide extent but false
   negatives are common without dermatopathology.
 • Outpatient mapping biopsies (sequential peripheral biopsies until all negative) → fewer positive margins and
   lower local recurrence; positive margins → further resection; intraepidermal disease with negative margins
   did not recur. Cover with local skin or scrotal flaps.
 • Deep invasion → extended resection; inguinal adenopathy → radical node dissection, poor prognosis.
   Chemotherapy (docetaxel–carboplatin neoadjuvant; cisplatin–epirubicin–paclitaxel for metastatic) shows
   promise. Evidence-based multidisciplinary EMPD guideline now exists (diagnosis, malignancy screening,
   intraepidermal and invasive disease, non-surgical options, metastatic disease, follow-up).

Adenosquamous Carcinoma
 • Glandular plus squamous elements independent of urethral glands; large (5–9 cm) firm grey-white granular
   exophytic mass of distal shaft or glans; mucin- and CEA-positive glands.
 • Case-level: local excision ± limited dissection with long survival; total penectomy with delayed ILND and PLND
   (pT2N3) disease-free at 5 years; one p16/hrHPV-positive case cured by definitive chemoradiation.

Lymphoreticular Malignant Neoplasm
 • "Primary" penile lymphoma is probably a misnomer — usually an early sign of systemic disease; the penis
   has no lymphoid tissue → mandatory search for systemic disease.
 • Leukaemic corporal infiltration → priapism.
 • Commonest: diffuse large B-cell lymphoma; presents as penile lump or priapism; other sites nodes, lung,
   liver, brain.
 • Systemic therapy even if the penis is the only site — commonly rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin,
   vincristine, prednisone (R-CHOP) — treating local disease, occult deposits, and preserving form and function.

Metastases
 • Rare (a few hundred cases) despite rich vascular and lymphatic supply; commonest sources: bladder,
   prostate, rectum; also gastrointestinal tract, testis, kidney.
 • Routes: direct extension, retrograde venous and lymphatic transport, arterial embolism.
 • Commonest sign: priapism (replacement of one or both corpora cavernosa); also swelling, nodularity,
   ulceration, obstruction, haematuria; cutaneous, preputial or glanular deposits less common.
 • Differential: idiopathic priapism, venereal or infective ulcers, tuberculosis, Peyronie plaque, primary tumours.
 • Signals advanced disease: usually appears soon after diagnosis of the primary; most die within a year
   (median survival months). Prostatic-origin metastases (often ductal features) survive longer, treated with
   radiotherapy and androgen deprivation → histology guides therapy and prognosis.
 • Palliation: excision by partial or total amputation for solitary or distal disease; proximal corporal invasion →
   cure unlikely; penectomy for intractable pain; MRI maps proximal extent and satellites; dorsal nerve section
   and suprapubic cystostomy for pain and drainage; systemic therapy for the primary histology, trials or
   hospice.

   KEY POINTS: NONSQUAMOUS MALIGNANT NEOPLASMS
    • BCC: highly curable, low metastatic potential.
    • Sarcomas: prone to local recurrence; metastases rare; superficial lesions treated less radically.
    • Melanoma: aggressive but curable early with appropriate surgery; immunotherapy and targeted therapy
      for advanced disease; multidisciplinary management.
    • EMPD: intraepidermal spread first; wide local excision to negative margins; invasive EMPD can be lethal;
      guideline available.
    • Penile metastases: usually from an obvious existing primary; poor prognosis; therapy directed to primary
      histology and local palliation.

Concept — Each nonsquamous tumour behaves like its counterpart elsewhere, modified by penile anatomy:
sun protection keeps BCC and melanoma rare, corporal vascularity carries melanoma and metastases (hence
priapism), apocrine glands explain EMPD, and absent lymphoid tissue makes penile lymphoma a systemic disease
until proven otherwise.
