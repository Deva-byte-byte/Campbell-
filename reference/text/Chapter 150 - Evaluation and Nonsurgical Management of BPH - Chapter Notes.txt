Chapter 150 — Evaluation and Nonsurgical Management
of Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia
Paolo Capogrosso, Edoardo Pozzi, Andrea Salonia and Francesco Montorsi

 • Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) was historically used for a disease of urinary dysfunction caused by an
   enlarged prostate obstructing micturition. It is now a histological diagnosis: proliferation of smooth
   muscle and epithelial cells within the prostatic transition zone.
 • Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) is the umbrella term for dysfunction of the storage and voiding
   phases of micturition.
 • The causal chain: BPH (histology) → benign prostatic enlargement (BPE) → benign prostatic obstruction
   (BPO) → bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) → LUTS.
 • LUTS are multifactorial and may also arise from:
     ◦ Bladder dysfunction — detrusor overactivity (DO), detrusor underactivity (DUA)
     ◦ Urethral stricture and urinary tract infection (UTI)
     ◦ Non-urological conditions — hormonal, metabolic, cardiovascular and neurological disease
 • A man with bothersome LUTS cannot be assumed to have BPH-related BOO until he has been fully
   assessed. Guidelines therefore use a diagnostic algorithm that grades LUTS severity and identifies the
   pathogenesis, and the pathogenesis then drives management.

DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION
 • LUTS affect up to about half of men, impair health-related quality of life (QoL), and carry a substantial
   social and economic burden.
 • The work-up has two aims: to grade symptom severity and bother, and to identify the underlying
   condition, because the underlying condition determines the correct treatment.
 • Basic evaluation — history, physical examination and urinalysis — excludes most non-prostatic causes.
 • Urinalysis (dipstick or sediment) is mandatory in every man with LUTS. It detects UTI, microhaematuria,
   glycosuria and proteinuria.
 • Additional tests are used to clarify the diagnosis, refine the severity estimate and predict the response to
   treatment.
 • Governing guidelines: American Urological Association (AUA) BPH guideline, updated 2021; European
   Association of Urology (EAU) guideline on non-neurogenic male LUTS, updated 2022.

Medical History
 • A comprehensive history is the first step. Its purpose is to identify potential causes of LUTS so that
   management can be tailored to the patient.
 • Comorbidities associated with LUTS:
     ◦ Diabetes mellitus and diabetes insipidus
     ◦ Renal disease, heart failure and sleep apnoea
     ◦ Neurological disease — Parkinson disease, multiple sclerosis, cerebrovascular disease, spinal cord
       injury, and prolapsed intervertebral disc impinging on the cord
 • Also ask about lower urinary tract (LUT) conditions, gross haematuria, and previous surgery on the
   prostate, bladder or urethra.

 • Medication review:
     ◦ Loop diuretics and β-blockers worsen storage symptoms.
     ◦ Anticholinergics may significantly impair bladder contractility.
 • Lifestyle factors: increased fluid and caffeine intake worsen storage symptoms. LUTS and emotional
   distress are bidirectionally associated.
 • Reassure the patient that LUTS are not associated with prostate cancer (PCa) risk.

 • Grading severity and bother informs how severe the disease is, how it responds to therapy, and the risk
   of progression.
 • Validated patient-reported questionnaires are the gold standard for assessing severity in primary care.
 • AUA Symptom Index (AUASI):
     ◦ Seven questions — frequency, nocturia, weak stream, hesitancy, intermittency, incomplete emptying
       and urgency — each scored 0–5, giving a total of 0–35.
     ◦ Severity bands: mild 0–7, moderate 8–19, severe 20–35.
     ◦ Internally consistent and reliable, correlates with the patient's global rating, and responsive to
       treatment.
 • International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS): the World Health Organization (WHO) adaptation of the
   AUASI (1993). It keeps the seven AUASI questions and adds one bother (QoL) question, and is the
   international standard.
 • IPSS subscales — storage (three questions) and voiding (four questions) — have been proposed but are
   not yet validated.
 • Neither the AUASI nor the IPSS diagnoses BOO. Symptom severity correlates poorly with the underlying
   pathophysiology, and against multichannel urodynamics the IPSS has poor diagnostic accuracy for BOO,
   both above and below a score of 20.

 • Sexual function should be assessed, because LUTS/BPH are strongly linked to sexual dysfunction.

Physical Examination
 • Physical examination is a mandatory part of the basic work-up. Its aim is to identify disorders of the male
   pelvis associated with LUTS.
 • Suprapubic area: look for an overdistended, palpable or percussible bladder.
 • Penis:
     ◦ Meatal stenosis, which can itself cause BOO
     ◦ Phimosis, and malignant lesions of the penile skin or foreskin
     ◦ Ventral palpation for urethral masses, an indirect sign of stricture
     ◦ Urethral discharge, a sign of urethritis
 • Scrotum: look for orchiepididymitis. Testicular size and secondary sex characteristics (body hair, pattern
   of hair loss) may reveal low testosterone, which accompanies central obesity and metabolic syndrome.
 • Metabolic alterations are associated with BPE. Men with raised body mass index or features of metabolic
   syndrome may benefit from lifestyle and dietary intervention.
 • Neurological examination: motor and sensory function of the perineum and lower limbs.
 • Digital rectal examination (DRE) is the last step and has a double aim:
     ◦ A baseline estimate of prostate volume (PV)
     ◦ Exclusion of PCa — nodules or increased consistency
 • DRE has low sensitivity for PCa, with a modest positive predictive value. In the European Randomised
   Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer (ERSPC), DRE combined with prostate-specific antigen (PSA)
   testing reduced overdiagnosis.
 • DRE underestimates PV, especially above 30 mL, because the lateral lobes are partly impalpable. Its
   positive predictive value is nonetheless good at the 30 mL cut-off and near-perfect at 50 mL, and a flat or
   normal prostate on DRE predicts PV <30 mL.
 • PV does not correlate with LUTS severity or degree of obstruction, but the 30 mL and 50 mL cut-offs
   predict treatment response and progression risk.
 • Anal sphincter tone: a flaccid or spastic sphincter suggests neurological disease.

 • Indication: FVCs are recommended in the basic evaluation of predominant storage LUTS or nocturia.
   They are the mainstay of nocturia assessment and are more accurate than recall-based measures such as
   the IPSS.
 • Nocturia (ICS definition) is waking at night one or more times to void. It is a storage symptom.
 • Limitations:
     ◦ Content and format vary widely between diaries.
     ◦ Keeping a diary has a bladder-training effect that biases the measurements. Instructing the patient not
       to change his routine reduces this.
 • Duration is a trade-off: adherence falls with prolonged recording (7 days), while reliability rises with more
   days. The reference duration is 3 days.

Laboratory Tests
 • Laboratory tests serve both as part of the basic evaluation and as secondary investigations in selected
   settings.

Urinalysis
 • Dipstick and/or microscopy in all men with LUTS. It is costless and readily available, aids the differential
   diagnosis, and prompts secondary tests.
 • Pyuria with positive nitrite suggests UTI. However, dipstick and microscopy are insensitive for UTI in
   painless LUTS (reference threshold 105 colony-forming units/mL).
 • Gross haematuria → exclude bladder cancer.
 • Proteinuria, glycosuria or ketonuria → suspect diabetes mellitus.
 • Urine cytology in men with severe storage symptoms and dysuria, especially smokers.
 • No controlled trials exist, but expert consensus holds that the benefit outweighs the cost.

Prostate-Specific Antigen
 • PSA has three roles in LUTS: assessing PCa risk, estimating PV, and predicting BPH outcomes.
 • BPH and PCa frequently coexist at autopsy.
 • A population association between LUTS and later PCa diagnosis is explained by detection bias: men
   treated for LUTS are biopsied more often. In the placebo arm of the Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial
   (PCPT), LUTS were not associated with PCa.
 • Outlet obstruction is a late and, in the modern era, infrequent event in the natural history of PCa.
 • Improving specificity in BPH: PSA velocity, free versus complexed PSA, PSA density, prostate cancer
   antigen 3 (PCA3) and [−2]proPSA (p2PSA).
 • Discuss the benefits and risks of PSA testing with every patient: false-positive and false-negative results,
   and the complications of biopsy.
 • Guideline indication: measure PSA if a PCa diagnosis would change management — that is, not in men
   with a life expectancy under 10 years.
 • PSA as a volume surrogate:
     ◦ PSA predicts PV reasonably well (area under the curve about 0.76–0.78 across volume thresholds).
     ◦ Age-specific PSA cut-offs for PV >40 mL: >1.6 ng/mL in the 50s, >2.0 in the 60s, >2.3 in the 70s.
     ◦ Free PSA gives comparable accuracy, and both forms predict transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) volume to
       within ±20% in most men.
 • PSA as an outcome predictor:
     ◦ Predicts change in LUTS, QoL and maximum flow rate (Qmax).

     ◦ Baseline PSA consistently predicts acute urinary retention (AUR) and BPH surgery in the placebo arms
       of the long-term trials.
     ◦ Total PSA has a moderate positive predictive value for BOO.
 • 5α-Reductase inhibitors (5ARIs) reduce serum PSA by 40–50% after 12 months. A pretreatment baseline
   PSA is therefore essential for interpreting later values.

Renal Function Assessment
 • EAU: measure serum creatinine or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) when hydronephrosis,
   renal insufficiency or urinary retention is suspected.
 • AUA: does not explicitly recommend testing creatinine or eGFR.
 • Renal impairment in LUTS is uncommon. In MTOPS (Medical Therapy of Prostatic Symptoms), under 1%
   of men developed kidney failure over 4 years.
 • Chronic kidney disease in men with LUTS is associated with low Qmax plus hypertension and/or
   diabetes. Qmax correlates with eGFR, whereas PV, postvoid residual (PVR) and IPSS do not.
 • Renal insufficiency increases the risk of complications after BPH surgery.

Instrumental Investigations
 • Some instrumental tests belong to the basic work-up. Others are reserved for selected cases, to guide
   treatment choice and to distinguish BPE from other conditions.

Postvoid Residual Volume
 • Definition: the volume of urine remaining in the bladder at the end of micturition. There is no
   standardised normal value.
 • Practical thresholds:
     ◦ <30 mL is non-significant.
     ◦ Persistently >50 mL is important.
     ◦ >300 mL is significant chronic PVR.
     ◦ 200–300 mL may indicate DUA and predicts a poorer response to treatment (expert opinion).
 • Large within-subject variability — repeated same-day measurements vary widely.
 • ICS Urodynamics Committee guidance (2014; level of evidence 3):
     ◦ Keep a short interval between voiding and measurement.
     ◦ Urethral catheterisation is the gold standard, but it causes discomfort and carries a risk of UTI and
       trauma.
     ◦ Use ultrasound in practice — real-time transabdominal ultrasound or a portable bladder scanner.
 • PVR correlates poorly with BOO: a raised PVR may reflect either BOO or DUA, and its predictive values
   for BOO are modest.
 • PVR predicts symptomatic deterioration but not surgery. A high PVR predicted a ≥4-point IPSS rise in
   MTOPS (>39 mL) and the Alfuzosin Long-Term Efficacy and Safety Study (ALTESS) (>93 mL), but did not
   correlate with the need for BPH surgery.
 • PVR and AUR — conflicting evidence:
     ◦ No association was found in men on α-blockers or watchful waiting (WW), nor in a 10-year cohort with
       BOO.
     ◦ PVR ≥500 mL predicted AUR recurrence.
     ◦ In the MTOPS placebo arm, a rising PVR over time predicted AUR.

 • PVR and UTI: a large or persistent PVR is associated with UTI in at-risk groups (children, neurological
   disease, diabetes), but not in adults without risk factors.
 • Recommendation: measure PVR at baseline and at follow-up. Men with significant PVR who choose non-
   surgical therapy should be monitored closely.

Uroflowmetry
 • A basic non-invasive urodynamic test that electronically records free flow. It integrates bladder function
   and outlet into a single measurement.
 • Main parameters: Qmax (peak flow rate, PFR), voided volume and flow pattern. Average flow rate is less
   accurate than Qmax for detecting BOO.
 • Sources of error:
     ◦ Within-subject variation, both on the same day and between days.
     ◦ A single free flow tends to underestimate the achievable Qmax.
     ◦ Qmax depends on voided volume: readings are inaccurate below 125–150 mL.
     ◦ Voiding position alters the parameters. The ICS advises voiding in the preferred position and
       interpreting only voids the patient considers representative.
 • Repeat the test to improve reliability, especially when the voided volume is <150 mL.
 • Core limitation: uroflowmetry cannot distinguish BOO from DUA, or identify both together. DUA is
   present in 9–48% of men undergoing urodynamics for non-neurogenic LUTS.
 • Qmax cut-offs are a trade-off:
     ◦ 10 mL/s — higher specificity, lower sensitivity.
     ◦ 15 mL/s — higher sensitivity, lower specificity.
 • The EAU systematic review confirmed that uroflowmetry alone cannot detect or quantify BOO.
 • Qmax <15 mL/s is the expert-opinion working definition of outlet obstruction, but it does not separate
   obstruction from bladder decompensation.
 • Guideline status: optional in the initial assessment, but recommended before any active treatment. Also
   valuable for monitoring treatment outcomes.

Filling cystometry
 • Sequence: continuous transurethral filling → simultaneous intravesical and abdominal pressure recording
   → derived detrusor pressure. Sensations recorded: first sensation, first desire and strong desire to void.
 • Main goal in suspected BOO: detect involuntary detrusor contractions (DO).
 • DO is common in suspected BPH and is associated with age and degree of obstruction.
 • Diagnosing DO identifies men likely to benefit from anticholinergics, alone or in combination.

Pressure–flow study
 • Definition: intravesical and abdominal pressure recorded during voiding with a urethral catheter in place,
   plotted as flow (x-axis) against detrusor pressure (y-axis).

 • DUA predicts long-term symptomatic failure and lower flow after BPH surgery.
 • Bladder contractility index neither improves after surgery nor declines with conservatively managed BOO.
   Detrusor contractility is therefore independent of treatment and of the duration of obstruction.
 • A randomised trial found that adding urodynamics to routine non-invasive care did not change IPSS or
   surgery rates at 18 months.
 • Not routine — neither the EAU nor the AUA recommends urodynamics routinely in male LUTS.
 • Indications for PFS before invasive treatment:
     ◦ Previous unsuccessful invasive treatment
     ◦ Unable to void >150 mL
     ◦ PVR >300 mL
     ◦ Age >80 years with predominantly voiding LUTS
     ◦ Age <50 years with predominantly voiding LUTS
 • AUA consensus: PFS may be performed when Qmax >10 mL/s before surgery is considered.
 • Also consider urodynamics in suspected neurological disease or after pelvic surgery.

Imaging
 • Imaging is mainly for surgical candidates, and for selected patients before medical treatment. Novel
   ultrasound parameters of bladder and prostate morphology are under evaluation for diagnosing BOO.

Upper-tract imaging
 • Routine upper-tract ultrasound is not recommended, because hydronephrosis and other renal
   abnormalities are infrequent in LUTS.
 • Indications:
     ◦ Raised creatinine or large PVR
     ◦ History of haematuria, UTI, urolithiasis or previous urinary tract surgery
 • Intravenous urography is obsolete in this setting.

Prostate and bladder imaging
 • Modalities: suprapubic ultrasound or TRUS. TRUS is the method of choice for PV, which drives treatment
   choice and predicts outcome.
 • 5ARI candidates need a PV measurement, because 5ARI outcomes depend on baseline gland volume.

     ◦ TRUS PV predicted progression and invasive treatment in MTOPS.

Cystourethrogram
 • Voiding cystourethrogram gives morphological and functional information: diverticula, vesicoureteric
   reflux and urethral stricture. It is an additional test when stricture or a bladder abnormality is suspected.

Cystourethroscopy
 • What it shows: prostate and bladder-neck morphology, trabeculation and diverticula.
 • It neither diagnoses BOO nor determines the need for treatment. Trabeculation grade (0 none → 3
   severe with pseudodiverticula) correlates with BOO severity, but some obstructed men have a normal
   bladder wall and some with severe trabeculation are unobstructed.
 • Indications:
     ◦ Gross haematuria or a history of bladder cancer
     ◦ Recurrent UTI or urethral injury (to exclude stricture)
     ◦ Previous prostatic or urethral surgery
 • Surgical planning:
     ◦ A median lobe must be assessed before prostatic urethral lift.
     ◦ In young men with a small prostate and obstructive LUTS, exclude bladder-neck contracture before
       surgery.

Micro–Ribonucleic Acid
 • Micro-ribonucleic acids (miRNAs) are small RNAs that act as central regulatory nodes across multiple
   genes. When deregulated they act as oncomiRs or tumour-suppressor miRNAs.
 • They are frequently altered in prostate, kidney and bladder cancer, and PCa-specific signatures have been
   described.
 • miR-221 is a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for early diagnosis and treatment of BPH
   (systematic review and meta-analysis).

Practical Flowchart for the Evaluation of Patients With LUTS
EAU algorithm (men aged ≥40 years)
 • Baseline: history, including sexual function → symptom score → physical examination with DRE (PV;
   excludes locally advanced PCa) → urinalysis → PVR → PSA if life expectancy >10 years and a PCa
   diagnosis would change management.
 • Abnormal DRE, suspected neurological disease, high PSA or abnormal urinalysis → evaluate under the
   relevant guideline, and treat the underlying condition.
 • Symptoms not bothersome and tests normal → no further evaluation or treatment.
 • Significant PVR → renal ultrasound ± renal function.
 • Bothersome symptoms:
        ◦ FVC for predominant storage LUTS or nocturia.
        ◦ Prostate ultrasound and uroflowmetry when medical or surgical treatment is contemplated →
          medical treatment by algorithm.
 • Surgery contemplated: endoscopy if it would alter the surgical choice, and PFS for the specific
   indications. Invasive urodynamics are reserved for suspected DO or DUA, or failed invasive treatment.

AUA algorithm
 • Step 1: history, examination, IPSS and urinalysis. Consider PVR and/or flow if equipment is available. If
   PVR >300 mL, irrespective of symptoms, manage as non-neurogenic chronic urinary retention (AUA white
   paper).

 • Step 2: counsel on options — behavioural and lifestyle change, medical therapy, procedural referral.

 KEY POINTS — DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION
   • Begin with medical history, physical examination including DRE, and urinalysis.
   • Grade symptom severity with a validated questionnaire (IPSS) as the first step of the work-up.
   • Consider PSA when life expectancy is over 10 years.
   • Ultrasound and uroflowmetry for moderate-to-severe symptoms.
   • Invasive urodynamics when a bladder dysfunction (DO, DUA) is suspected.

Concept — Every test in the male LUTS work-up is judged by one question: can it separate outlet obstruction
from bladder dysfunction? Symptoms, flow, PVR, prostate volume and cystoscopy all fail that test, which is
why only the pressure–flow study is the gold standard, and why it is reserved for the men in whom the
answer changes surgery.

MANAGEMENT OF LUTS CAUSED BY BENIGN PROSTATIC ENLARGEMENT
 • Management is driven by three things: the patient's clinical characteristics, the baseline findings, and
   patient preference.
 • Two approaches: conservative management, or active management (medical or surgical).

Conservative Management
 • Indication (AUA and EAU): mild-to-moderate, uncomplicated LUTS with minimal bother.
 • Complicating factors that exclude conservative management: gross haematuria, UTI, raised PSA, raised
   PVR and neurological disease.
 • Conservative management means watchful waiting (WW).

Watchful Waiting
 • Four components: reassurance, education, lifestyle intervention and periodic monitoring.
 • Rationale: many men have indolent, non-progressive disease that never needs active treatment.
 • Definition of BPH progression: deterioration of symptoms or flow, AUR, or the need for surgery.
 • Natural history:
     ◦ In community cohorts, AUR and BPH surgery are uncommon, and about half of men have stable
       symptoms over 15 years.
     ◦ In the placebo arms of the large trials, long-term AUR and surgery rates are in the low single figures to
       about 10%.

 • Veterans Affairs randomised trial — transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) versus WW:
     ◦ Both arms were told to avoid coffee, alcohol and aggravating drugs.
     ◦ TURP was better for symptoms, flow and clinical failure. Clinical failure was defined as death, AUR,
       high PVR, bladder stones, incontinence, renal failure or a high symptom score.
     ◦ Yet most WW patients did not fail. Failures were mainly due to high PVR or symptom score.
     ◦ About a quarter crossed over to surgery, and a high baseline bother score predicted crossover.
     ◦ At 5 years, AUR and renal failure were rare under WW, and symptoms and bother actually fell.
 • Self-management (three small-group sessions of education and lifestyle advice, to consensus standards)
   reduced treatment failure and symptom scores versus standard care in randomised trials. It also reduced
   nocturia, urgency and frequency.
 • Follow-up: review yearly. Symptom progression or complications should prompt more active treatment.

 • Dietary evidence (systematic review): higher intake of fluid, tea and caffeine worsens storage symptoms,
   and lower alcohol intake is associated with less BPH and LUTS.

 KEY POINTS — CONSERVATIVE MANAGEMENT
   • Conservative management is preferred for mild symptoms without complicating factors.
   • WW = education + lifestyle intervention + monitoring.
   • Lifestyle measures: appropriate fluid intake; avoiding or restricting tea, caffeine and alcohol;
     adjusting concurrent medication.
   • Review yearly for worsening symptoms or complications.

Concept — Watchful waiting is justified because the natural history of BPH is mostly benign: complications
are rare, and symptoms often stabilise or improve. The decision to treat is therefore driven by bother and
complicating features, not by symptom score or gland size alone.

Medical Therapy
 • Drug classes: α1-adrenergic blockers, 5ARIs, antimuscarinics, phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors

Impact and Trends of Medical Therapy Use for LUTS
 • Surgery was the mainstay until the 1990s. Medical therapy then expanded, and US Medicare
   prostatectomy numbers fell by about two-thirds between 1987 and 2000 before stabilising. TURP
   numbers fell similarly in Europe.
 • Patients prefer drugs to surgery, and urologists prescribe accordingly. In the European PROBE survey and
   US physician surveys, medical therapy is the commonest treatment, and α1-blockers are the most
   prescribed.
 • Minimally invasive surgical therapies may narrow the gap between drug use and surgery in future.

Clinical Indications
 • Medical therapy is first line for bothersome LUTS without an absolute indication for surgery.
 • Absolute indications for surgery: AUR, recurrent UTI, renal insufficiency, bladder stones and recurrent
   gross haematuria.
 • Tailor the drug to the symptom profile:
     ◦ Predominantly voiding symptoms → α1-blocker, 5ARI or PDE5I, chosen according to severity, PV, PVR
       and preference.
     ◦ Predominantly storage symptoms → antimuscarinic or β3-agonist.

     ◦ Nocturnal polyuria → education, lifestyle change and optional desmopressin, following the nocturia
       guidelines.
 • Nocturnal polyuria (ICS definition, on FVC): nocturnal output >20% of 24-h output in younger men, or
   >33% in men over 65.
 • Discuss the benefits and risks of every class before choosing.

Interpreting the Literature: Bias in Trials of Medical Treatment for LUTS
 • Randomised, placebo-controlled, double-blind trials are the best design, but five caveats apply:
     ◦ Placebo effect: both symptom scores and uroflow improve on placebo. A 4-week placebo run-in is
       ideal.
     ◦ Sample size: too small a trial misses real differences; too large a trial makes clinically trivial differences
       statistically significant. Power the trial at the planning stage.
     ◦ Inclusion and exclusion criteria shape both efficacy and tolerability.
     ◦ Adverse event (AE) ascertainment: checklists yield more AEs than spontaneous reporting, and efficacy
       trials are underpowered for tolerability.
     ◦ Duration: drugs may lose effect over time, and some AEs appear only late.
Concept — Every trial result in this chapter is a change on a symptom scale, a change in Qmax, or a
progression event. Each must be read against the placebo response and against the 3-point, percentage-
based threshold of what the patient can actually perceive.

Rationale
 • Adrenergic receptor (AR) families: α and β, with nine subtypes — α1a, α1b, α1d, α2a, α2b, α2c, β1, β2
   and β3 (Figure 150.4). The α1 subfamily drives LUTS and is the drug target.

 • Prostate and urethral smooth-muscle contraction is α1-mediated. α1a is the dominant prostatic subtype
   (about 70% of α1-ARs).

 • In BPH tissue the balance shifts further to α1a: α1a > α1b > α1d. α1a also predominates in the bladder
   neck and prostatic urethra, and α1-ARs are present on prostatic glandular epithelium.

Review of the Literature on Currently Approved α1-Blockers
 • Level 1a evidence exists for every compound from pooled analyses.
 • Efficacy and tolerability are dose dependent, so trials use two designs: titration to a fixed dose (dose–
   response) or titration to response or maximal dose (practical threshold dose).
 • Phenoxybenzamine, prazosin and indoramin are no longer used and are not reviewed.
 • BOO index (BOOI) meta-analysis (randomised and observational studies): every α1-blocker lowers BOOI,
   and silodosin lowers it most. This is a subgroup analysis, not a head-to-head comparison.

     ◦ AEs were minor and reversible. Postural hypotension was significantly increased at 5 mg. Syncope was
       under 0.5%.

Safety Profile of α1-Blockers in Specific Populations
 • Overall safety is good. Differences follow subtype selectivity and pharmacokinetics. AEs arise from the
   vascular, CNS and urogenital effects of each drug.
 • Commonest AEs across the class: dizziness, asthenia, hypotension and anejaculation.

Haemodynamic side effects
 • Vascular AEs are more frequent with terazosin and doxazosin than with alfuzosin, tamsulosin or
   silodosin, because of their α1b (vascular) affinity. Consequences: falls in systolic or diastolic BP, dizziness,
   asthenia, headache, palpitations and syncope.
 • Orthostatic stress test (the best method): symptomatic hypotension is a standing systolic fall ≥20 mm Hg
   with symptoms. On this test, terazosin caused far more symptomatic postural hypotension than
   tamsulosin (Figure 150.9).
 • "First-dose hypotension" is the reason terazosin and doxazosin must be titrated.
 • Indirect comparison: more dizziness with doxazosin and terazosin than with tamsulosin and alfuzosin.
   Dizziness and asthenia are the main causes of withdrawal.
 • Dizziness and asthenia are partly central, determined by the speed of BP lowering and CNS penetration.
 • Among uroselective agents:
     ◦ Alfuzosin has slightly more cardiovascular effect than tamsulosin. Alfuzosin IR lowered supine and
       standing BP where tamsulosin did not, and tamsulosin caused less symptomatic orthostatic
       hypotension than alfuzosin 5 mg twice daily in men ≥60 (Figure 150.9).
     ◦ Silodosin has no excess hypotension or vascular AEs versus placebo or tamsulosin.

α1-Blocker therapy in patients with cardiovascular disease
 • Doxazosin and terazosin were first developed as antihypertensives. Their BP effect matters only in men
   who are hypertensive at baseline (definitions and BP methods vary between studies).
 • Antihypertensive and Lipid-Lowering Treatment to Prevent Heart Attack Trial (ALLHAT):
     ◦ Hypertensive men with at least one other coronary risk factor were randomised to chlorthalidone,
       doxazosin, amlodipine or lisinopril.
     ◦ The doxazosin arm was stopped because of excess congestive heart failure versus chlorthalidone. It
       was not in excess versus the other agents.
     ◦ This raised concern for doxazosin in cardiovascular disease, but no major cardiovascular AEs occurred
       in the LUTS trials.
 • Interactions with concomitant medication:
     ◦ Alfuzosin → more cardiovascular AEs with antihypertensives or vasodilators.
     ◦ Tamsulosin → no significant interaction with β-blockers, calcium antagonists or angiotensin-converting
       enzyme inhibitors; tolerability unchanged in hypertension or cardiovascular disease.
     ◦ Silodosin → no excess cardiovascular AEs with antihypertensives.
 • Use α1-blockers cautiously with antihypertensives, and never combine two α-blockers.

 • Tolerability matters more in older men. Dizziness and orthostatic hypotension lead to falls, fractures and
   institutionalisation. Older men adapt less to orthostatic stress, have stiffer vessels, and rely more on

 • Erectile function:
     ◦ Any improvement is attributed to LUTS relief and QoL gain, plus cavernosal α-blockade relaxing

     ◦ No effect on desire.

     ◦ Doxazosin and terazosin — no detrimental ejaculatory effect in trials.

Summary — α1-Blockers
 • Efficacy: significant improvement in symptoms and flow, durable in longitudinal trials.
 • Main AEs: asthenia, dizziness, anejaculation and orthostatic hypotension.
 • Superiority of one agent over another is unproven. The distinction is safety: uroselective agents
   (tamsulosin, silodosin) → fewer vascular AEs, more ejaculatory disorders.
 • First line for bothersome moderate-to-severe LUTS. Counsel on sexual and vascular AEs, and monitor BP
   in the first week.
Concept — The α1-blocker class is a single efficacy with three toxicity profiles, and receptor subtype predicts
which: α1b → vascular (terazosin, doxazosin), α1a → ejaculation and IFIS (silodosin, tamsulosin), α1d →
storage benefit (naftopidil). Choosing an α-blocker means choosing the side effect the patient can best
tolerate.

ANDROGEN MANIPULATION
 • Hormonal therapy for BPH has been studied since the 1970s and remains important for LUTS caused by
   BPE.

Rationale for Androgen Manipulation
 • The prostate is androgen dependent. Both testosterone and dihydrotestosterone (DHT) drive BPH.
 • DHT is made from testosterone by two 5α-reductase isoenzymes:
     ◦ Type 2 — prostate and genital tissue.
     ◦ Type 1 — liver, skin and other extraprostatic organs.
 • DHT dissociates from the androgen receptor more slowly and is intrinsically more potent than
   testosterone in the prostate.
 • Castration or drugs that suppress testosterone or DHT reduce PV. This is thought to relieve the static
   component of BOO.
 • First limitation of the hypothesis: LUTS do not depend entirely on prostate size.

Classification of Pharmacological Agents
 • Historical sequence:
     ◦ Surgical castration shrank the enlarged prostate (1890s).


                                                         page 23 of 47
Chapter 150 — Evaluation and Nonsurgical Management of BPH                                           Androgen Manipulation
     ◦ Medical castration (1969): cyproterone acetate, a progestational antiandrogen, caused prostatic
       atrophy by suppressing testicular steroid synthesis without suppressing luteinising hormone.
     ◦ Non-steroidal antiandrogens (flutamide, bicalutamide) block testosterone and DHT binding at the
       androgen receptor.
     ◦ Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues also shrink the gland.
 • Problem with all of these: AEs from disrupting the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis.
 • 5ARIs block testosterone → DHT conversion in prostatic stromal cells without significant interaction with
   the HPG axis.

Interpreting the Literature on Androgen Manipulation
 • Maximal PV reduction takes 6 months, so trial outcomes are reliable only at or after 6 months.
 • Larger prostates benefit most, and most trials enrolled large glands. The results may not generalise to all
   men with BPH.
 • AEs may have been underestimated in trials not designed to capture them.
 • The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) reviewed 5ARI safety in 2011 because of emerging risks of
   depression and sexual dysfunction.
 • Metabolic and cardiovascular safety data are heterogeneous and need cautious interpretation.

Review of the Literature
 • Finasteride and dutasteride are the paradigm of androgen suppression in BPH. The multicentre
   randomised trials follow.

 • Mechanism: selective type 2 5α-reductase inhibitor. It lowers intraprostatic DHT but not to castrate

 • North American Finasteride Trial (first multicentre randomised trial; 1 or 5 mg for 12 months; mean PV

     ◦ 5 mg gave significant symptom relief and a modest Qmax gain.
     ◦ PV fell by about 20% at 12 months.
     ◦ PV change did not track clinical response — the benefit is not purely volumetric.

Zanoterone
 • Steroidal competitive androgen-receptor antagonist.
 • Dose-ranging trial (100–800 mg, 6 months):
     ◦ Flow improved at 200 mg only.
     ◦ No effect on symptoms or PV.
     ◦ Estradiol and testosterone rose, with breast pain and gynaecomastia.
 • Minimal efficacy with excess AEs → trial stopped and development abandoned.

Flutamide
 • Non-steroidal antiandrogen that blocks testosterone and DHT binding to the androgen receptor.
 • Placebo-controlled trials:
     ◦ No benefit in symptoms, PV or flow.
     ◦ High attrition made one trial uninterpretable.
     ◦ Breast tenderness and diarrhoea were frequent.
 • Dose-ranging trial: dose-dependent PV reduction (up to about a quarter at 6 months), but no other
   efficacy benefit. A quarter to over a third stopped because of diarrhoea or breast tenderness.

Cetrorelix
 • GnRH antagonist giving submaximal, non-castrating androgen blockade. The degree of suppression can
   be titrated — relevant if shrinkage and AEs (hot flushes, low libido, impotence) occur at different
   suppression levels.
 • Open-label pilot (loading dose then 1 mg/day for 2 months):
     ◦ Large IPSS fall that continued after stopping, to 18 months.
     ◦ PV fell during treatment, then rose slightly after stopping.
     ◦ Testosterone reached castrate levels after loading, then was only partially suppressed.
     ◦ Most men lost libido and nocturnal tumescence.
 • Randomised dose-regimen trial:
     ◦ Rapid IPSS fall in all arms, still significant 4 months after treatment ended, with sustained flow
       improvement.
     ◦ PV fell persistently only at 10 mg.
     ◦ No clinically significant sexual change — suggesting a possible intermittent regimen.
 • Sustained-release intramuscular formulation: relief lasted up to 6 months after 2 weeks of treatment.
 • Drawbacks: injection and cost. There may be a role if a single injection gives durable relief with minimal

Aromatase Inhibitors
 • Rationale — oestrogens contribute to BPH:
     ◦ They act on stromal oestrogen receptors, affecting proliferation and differentiation; the stroma's role
       is shown in embryonic mouse models.
     ◦ In dogs, androgen plus oestrogen causes earlier and more extensive BPH, and oestrogens potentiate
       androgen-induced BPH.
 • Mechanism: block testosterone → oestrogen conversion.
 • Atamestane (highly selective): lowered serum and intraprostatic oestradiol and oestrone, and modestly
   reduced PV, but gave no symptom or flow benefit. A dose-dependent rise in peripheral androgens may
   explain the failure.
 • Anastrozole in hypogonadal men: no symptom relief.

Selective Oestrogen and Androgen Receptor Modulators
 • Selective oestrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) — tamoxifen, raloxifene, toremifene:
     ◦ Enhance or inhibit oestrogen-receptor activity by receptor type → antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic
       effects on prostatic epithelium and stroma in vitro.
     ◦ Tamoxifen shrank canine prostates. In men it lowered nuclear androgen receptors in resected tissue.
     ◦ Already used for PCa prevention and to mitigate androgen-deprivation AEs (fracture risk, lipids).
     ◦ Possible future role as an adjunct to antiandrogen therapy.
 • Selective androgen receptor modulators (SARMs):
     ◦ Partial antagonists in the prostate; full agonists in muscle and pituitary.
     ◦ Suppress prostate growth while preserving peripheral androgen action.
     ◦ In rats: dose-dependent PV reduction without changes in testosterone or gonadotropins. Human
       trials are needed.

     ◦ In PLESS, withdrawal for AEs was not higher than placebo. REDUCE showed no AE excess.

MUSCARINIC RECEPTOR ANTAGONISTS
 • Indication: BOO with predominant or bothersome storage LUTS.
 • Storage symptoms affect a sizeable minority of men in Europe and North America, and some also have
   BOO.
 • Antimuscarinics are the mainstay of OAB treatment, and their use in selected men with LUTS has grown
   over two decades.

Rationale and Indication in Patients With BPH
 • Innervation: parasympathetic cholinergic fibres act on detrusor muscarinic receptors. These receptors
   drive involuntary contractions and set the sensory threshold.
 • Five subtypes (M1–M5); M2 and M3 predominate in the bladder. Blockade reduces involuntary
   contractions and raises the contraction threshold → less urgency, greater capacity.
 • Antimuscarinics act mainly on sensory (afferent) pathways rather than motor pathways, possibly also via
   the urothelium and CNS.
 • EAU and AUA endorse antimuscarinics for BOO with predominant storage LUTS, used cautiously because
   of the risk of raised PVR and retention.
 • Licensed agents: darifenacin, fesoterodine, oxybutynin, propiverine, solifenacin, tolterodine and
   trospium.

Tolerability and Safety Profile
 • Muscarinic receptors in brain, heart, gut, salivary glands and lacrimal glands cause the class AEs: dry

 • Oxybutynin IR: the first OAB drug, with poor adherence and early discontinuation.
 • Tolterodine ER: better tolerated — dry mouth is the only excess AE. In BPH, dry mouth and constipation
   are commonest.
 • Solifenacin and fesoterodine cause more dry mouth and constipation than tolterodine ER.
 • Combination with α1-blockers or 5ARIs produces only the AEs expected from each drug.

Summary — Antimuscarinics
 • Mainstay of OAB, but few trials exist in BPH with predominant storage symptoms.
 • Safe with low PVR and no history of AUR.
 • Monotherapy efficacy is controversial; there is some benefit for storage symptoms in smaller glands.
 • Guideline indication: moderate-to-severe LUTS, mainly storage symptoms, baseline PVR <200 mL.
Concept — Antimuscarinics treat the bladder, not the outlet. Their use in BPH is therefore governed by one
safety question: how well does the detrusor empty? That is why every recommendation is anchored to a
PVR ceiling of 200 mL.

β3-AGONIST (MIRABEGRON)

Indication, Efficacy and Safety Profile
 • β3 is the predominant β-AR in the bladder. Stimulation increases capacity without changing micturition
   pressure, PVR or the voiding contraction.
 • Mirabegron is the first internationally approved β3-agonist for storage symptoms. It relaxes detrusor
   smooth muscle and promotes storage.
 • Efficacy:
     ◦ Effective for OAB in phase III randomised trials.
     ◦ Meta-analysis (mostly female participants): reduced frequency, urgency and incontinence; increased
       voided volume; and improved nocturia versus both placebo and tolterodine.
 • Urodynamic safety in men with LUTS and BOO (phase II, 50 or 100 mg, 12 weeks):
     ◦ Qmax and detrusor pressure at Qmax unchanged — no impairment of voiding.
     ◦ PVR rose dose-dependently at 100 mg.
     ◦ Frequency and urgency episodes fell, but IPSS storage and voiding subscores did not differ from
       placebo.
     ◦ Supports safety in BOO and a role for storage symptoms.
 • Possible outlet effect (preclinical): urethral relaxation via urethral β3-ARs plus selective α1a/α1d
   blockade; similar prostatic smooth-muscle relaxation.
 • Tolerability versus antimuscarinics:
     ◦ Constipation, dry mouth and neurological impairment are uncommon.
     ◦ No impairment of voiding.
     ◦ Pooled data (over a third with BPH history): AEs equal to placebo; dry mouth less than with
       solifenacin or tolterodine; constipation no different.
     ◦ Commonest AEs: nasopharyngitis, headache and hypertension (hypertension at a rate similar to
       placebo).
     ◦ BP rise of ≤1 mm Hg versus placebo.

 • Novel, potent, highly selective β3-agonist (also known as RVT-901 or KRP-114V).

 • Pharmacokinetics:
     ◦ Half-life 25–38 h → once daily.

Rationale and Efficacy of PDE5 Inhibitors in LUTS
 • PDE5 is expressed throughout the lower urinary tract.
 • Five proposed mechanisms (Figure 150.12):
     ◦ Vascular: PDE5 inhibition in vessel endothelium and smooth muscle → increased bladder and prostate
       oxygenation (vardenafil, tadalafil in rats).
     ◦ Smooth-muscle relaxation of prostate and bladder neck via enhanced nerve-derived nitric oxide
       signalling. In partial obstruction models, urodynamics improved.
     ◦ Antiproliferative: high stromal PDE5 expression. Vardenafil is antiproliferative via cyclic guanosine
       monophosphate (cGMP) and protein kinase G, and PDE5Is block fibroblast → myofibroblast
       transdifferentiation and reverse stromal remodelling.
     ◦ Afferent inhibition: reduced LUT afferent nerve activity via the nitric oxide/cGMP pathway → less
       filling sensation and urgency. Vardenafil improved urodynamics in spinal-cord-injured neurogenic
       bladder.
     ◦ Anti-inflammatory in metabolic syndrome: tadalafil reduced prostatic inflammation in a rabbit
       metabolic-syndrome model.
 • Clinical evidence:
     ◦ First signal: unexpected IPSS improvement in men taking sildenafil for ED.
     ◦ Sildenafil (ED + LUTS, IPSS ≥12, up-titrated to 100 mg for 12 weeks): IPSS improved markedly versus
       placebo, most in severe LUTS. No flow change.
     ◦ Tadalafil (5 → 20 mg): IPSS, QoL and BII improved, with more men achieving a ≥3-point IPSS fall. No
       flow change; dyspepsia, back pain and headache.
     ◦ Vardenafil 10 mg twice daily: IPSS improved in both storage and voiding subscores; headache,
       dyspepsia and flushing.
 • Tadalafil is the only PDE5I licensed for male LUTS, because of its long half-life and convenient 5 mg daily
   dose.
     ◦ Dose-finding trial (2.5–20 mg, 12 weeks): all doses beat placebo with a clear dose–response (Figure
       150.13), and AEs rose with dose. 5 mg had the best benefit–risk.
     ◦ Confirmed by further randomised trials and three meta-analyses.
     ◦ 5 mg daily reduces IPSS by about 6 and QoL by about 1 point (placebo about 3.6 and 0.7).
     ◦ Benefit is independent of baseline ED — it is not a psychological by-product of better erections.
 • Urodynamics and flow: no significant effect in trials and meta-analyses. One single-arm 12-month study
   found a Qmax gain and BOOI fall.
 • Umbrella review of systematic reviews: IPSS reduction without uroflow change; well tolerated; tadalafil
   5 mg also reduces nocturnal voiding.
 • Versus α1-blockers:
     ◦ Tadalafil = tamsulosin for IPSS and QoL (four 3-month trials, plus a newer trial).
     ◦ Tadalafil vs alfuzosin: both effective, alfuzosin greater.
     ◦ Sildenafil vs alfuzosin, doxazosin or tamsulosin: similar pattern.
 • Guidelines (EAU 2022, AUA 2021): PDE5Is are an alternative for moderate-to-severe LUTS, with or
   without ED.
 • Dosing: tadalafil 5 mg once daily at a consistent time, with or without food.

Concept — Combination therapy pairs drugs with non-overlapping targets and time courses. The α-blocker

PHYTOTHERAPY
 • Phytotherapy — the therapeutic use of plants and plant products — has taken a significant place in BPH
   treatment over two decades.

Composition of Phytotherapy Extracts
 • Sources: roots, seeds, bark or fruit.
 • Contents: phytosterols, plant oils, fatty acids and phytoestrogens.
 • Extraction methods vary — method, time, temperature, pressure and solvent. Every extract is therefore
   unique, and extracts from the same plant differ greatly in composition and effect.
 • Combination products (two or more extracts) are marketed for efficacy, marketability and registration.

Mechanism of Action
 • Mechanisms are incompletely understood. Most preclinical work is on Serenoa repens and Pygeum
   africanum.
 • Most studied mechanisms: anti-inflammatory action, 5α-reductase inhibition, and antiproliferative/pro-
   apoptotic effects via growth factors.
 • Anti-inflammatory:
     ◦ Inhibition of cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase reduces prostaglandins and leukotrienes (inflammation
       contributes to BPH). Flavone is a strong cyclooxygenase inhibitor.
     ◦ Serenoa lipidosterolic extract inhibits neutrophil leukotriene production (via its acidic lipophilic
       fraction) and phospholipase A2.

 • Antiandrogenic actions of Serenoa:
     ◦ Inhibits 5α-reductase types I and II (attributed to free fatty acids).
     ◦ Inhibits DHT binding to the cytosolic androgen receptor.
     ◦ Inhibits nuclear oestrogen receptors.
     ◦ Modulates prolactin-induced prostatic growth.
 • But 5α-reductase inhibition is largely in vitro — it is not confirmed in human prostate tissue. In a large
   trial versus finasteride, Serenoa did not lower PSA and barely reduced PV.
 • Animal models: Serenoa blunted hormone-induced prostate growth in castrated rats given oestrogen plus
   testosterone, and in hyperprolactinaemic rats.
 • Antiproliferative and apoptotic effects:
     ◦ Serenoa lowered the proliferative index in BPH tissue.
     ◦ Pygeum and Serenoa inhibited basic fibroblast growth factor- and epidermal growth factor-induced
       fibroblast and BPH-cell proliferation.
     ◦ Serenoa altered proliferation and apoptosis gene expression in BPH cell lines.
 • The link between preclinical mechanisms and clinical effect is unproven.

Current Role of Phytotherapy in LUTS
 • Prescribing:
     ◦ In a four-country European survey, phytotherapy was the second most prescribed BPH therapy after

Serenoa repens (Saw Palmetto Berry)
 • The most popular and most studied phytotherapeutic agent for LUTS.
 • Product heterogeneity:
     ◦ Free fatty acid content differs between brands (Figure 150.19A).
     ◦ Retail samples deviate widely from their labelled content.
     ◦ 5α-reductase inhibition varies between extracts and between batches (Figure 150.19B).
 • Trial extracts: hexane, ethanolic and supercritical CO2; some trials did not specify.

 • Evolution of the evidence:
     ◦ First meta-analysis: Serenoa improved symptoms, nocturia and flow versus placebo, was non-inferior
       to finasteride, and had mild AEs equal to placebo. However, the trials were underpowered, short and
       heterogeneous.
     ◦ Updated Cochrane reviews: no benefit over placebo for symptoms, flow or PV. Non-inferior to
       finasteride and tamsulosin in single trials.
     ◦ Two high-quality long-term trials: supercritical CO2 extract 160 mg twice daily for 1 year, and
       ethanolic extract escalated to 960 mg/day for 72 weeks. Neither differed from placebo for AUASI,
       flow or PV, even at double or triple the dose.
 • Caveat: these negative trials may not apply to every product, because extracts differ.
 • Hexane extract (Permixon):
     ◦ Highest biological activity and the lowest batch-to-batch variability in free fatty acids.
     ◦ Meta-analysis of hexane-only trials: better than placebo for nocturnal voids and Qmax; non-inferior
       to tamsulosin and to short-term finasteride; AEs and withdrawals equal to placebo.
     ◦ Silodosin + Permixon gave more clinically meaningful IPSS responders than silodosin alone.
     ◦ Updated systematic review: fewer nocturnal voids and higher Qmax than placebo; IPSS and Qmax
       similar to α1-blockers; significant IPSS improvement from baseline.

 • Used in Europe since 1969. It also modulates bladder contractility in animals.

Summary — Phytotherapy
 • Worldwide use is rising, but randomised evidence is inconclusive. Large, long-term trials show no
   difference from placebo.
 • Lack of a standard extraction method means biological activity varies and data cannot be compared.

 • Serenoa repens 320 mg daily with food.
Concept — In phytotherapy the extract is the drug. Because composition varies by extraction method and
batch, trial results are specific to a product. That is why the only guideline endorsement is for one
standardised extract (hexane Serenoa), and why the category as a whole carries no recommendation.

PROGNOSIS OF PATIENTS RECEIVING MEDICAL TREATMENT FOR LUTS
 • BPH is a progressive disease.
 • Olmsted County study (12-year community cohort, men 40–79):
     ◦ IPSS rises about 0.18 points per year.
     ◦ Qmax falls about 2% per year.
     ◦ The prostate grows about 1.9% per year.
 • Serious progression events: AUR, detrusor underactivity and hydronephrosis, beyond symptom
   worsening.
 • Medical therapy reduces progression and the need for surgery. Patients prefer conservative, lower-risk
   treatments.
 • MTOPS (placebo arm): 17% progressed over 4 years, mostly symptomatic.
     ◦ Monotherapy reduced risk by 34–39%, and the combination by 66%.
     ◦ AUR and surgery were reduced only by finasteride (alone or in the combination).
     ◦ Similar findings in CombAT and ALTESS.
 • Baseline risk factors for progression: higher PSA, larger prostate, greater PVR. Counsel accordingly.
 • REDUCE placebo arm (post hoc; mild or no symptoms at baseline, IPSS <8):
     ◦ PV 40–80 mL → higher risk of developing LUTS (IPSS ≥14) than PV ≤40 mL.
     ◦ Lower baseline Qmax also predicted incident LUTS.
 • CONDUCT (fixed-dose dutasteride–tamsulosin vs WW): combination beat WW for IPSS at 2 years only in
   men with high baseline bother (BII). Bother may be the better predictor of benefit.
 • Dynamic predictors: worsening symptoms and rising PVR predict AUR. In the MTOPS placebo arm, men
   without AUR had stable PVR.
 • Follow-up schedule on α1-blockers or 5ARIs:
     ◦ 4–12 weeks — initial response (α1-blocker) and AEs.

   • α1-Blockers are first line for moderate-to-severe LUTS.

   • Uroselective agents (tamsulosin, silodosin, alfuzosin) have the best tolerability.

Management of Acute Urinary Retention
 • Sequence: immediate bladder decompression by catheter → trial without catheter (TWOC) after a few
   days (preferred) or immediate surgery.
 • Immediate surgery resolves BOO early and protects the bladder and upper tracts, but emergency surgery
   carries higher morbidity. Practice has therefore shifted to the conservative route.
 • TWOC:
     ◦ Succeeds in about 60%.
     ◦ It is the treatment of choice in most first episodes worldwide.
     ◦ Median catheter duration about 5 days (range 3–8).
     ◦ Second and third attempts still succeed in over a quarter → up to three TWOCs may be tried before
       invasive treatment.
     ◦ However, recurrence after a successful TWOC is common, mostly within 6 months. At that point, PFS
       and surgery are indicated.
 • Predictors of TWOC failure:
     ◦ Advanced age
     ◦ Severe LUTS
     ◦ Drained volume >1 L
     ◦ Spontaneous (not precipitated) AUR
 • Catheter duration: practice is heterogeneous, and no evidence favours short or long catheterisation.
 • Clean intermittent self-catheterisation gave a higher success rate than indwelling catheter plus TWOC in a
   small non-randomised trial, but the groups were younger and had smaller glands.
 • Rationale for α1-blockers: increased sympathetic tone at the bladder neck (the dynamic component)
   contributes to retention.
 • Alfuzosin in Acute Urinary Retention (ALFAUR) study: alfuzosin 10 mg for 3 days after emergency
   catheterisation → TWOC success about 62% vs 48% on placebo.
 • Meta-analysis: α1-blockers before TWOC increase success (relative risk about 1.5) and reduce recurrence.
   Most trials used 1–3 days; the optimal duration is unknown.
