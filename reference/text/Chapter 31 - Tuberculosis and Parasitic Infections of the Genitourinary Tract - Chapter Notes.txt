 Genitourinary Tract
 UROGENITAL TUBERCULOSIS
   • Tuberculosis (TB) can involve any organ, including the urogenital (UG) tract. Untreated UG TB causes
     irreversible tissue destruction (renal failure, infertility), so belongs in the differential of UG disorders.
   • TB is the second "great imitator" (after syphilis); mimicking other diseases delays diagnosis and
     treatment.
   • As TB becomes rarer in industrialised nations, diagnosis depends on deliberate clinical suspicion.
   • Terminology: "urogenital TB" is preferred over "genitourinary TB" because the urinary tract is involved
     far more often than the genital tract.

 History
   • Mycobacterium tuberculosis has co-evolved with humans. A progenitor (M. prototuberculosis) may
     have infected early hominids > 3 million years ago; a Homo erectus skeleton about 500,000 years old
     has TB-consistent bony lesions.
   • Oldest microbiological confirmation: Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex DNA in 9000-year-old
     Neolithic skeletons (Eastern Mediterranean). Tubercle bacilli shown microscopically and molecularly in
     Egyptian mummies (c. 3000 BCE).
   • Written descriptions: ancient East Asia, pre-Columbian Americas, classical Greece and Rome, through
     modern history.
   • Epidemic peak: 18th and 19th centuries in Europe and North America; "consumption" caused up to
     one-quarter of deaths in the Industrial Age.
   • Turning point, 24 March 1882: Robert Koch presented the first isolation and identification of the
     tubercle bacillus; 24 March is World Tuberculosis Day.

 Microbiology
   • TB is caused by the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC): closely related acid-fast bacteria.

     africanum causes a large share.

 Epidemiology
   • Global (World Health Organization, WHO): one-quarter of the world's population has latent MTBC
     infection. 2021: about 10.6 million new active cases and 1.6 million deaths. Deaths have fallen
     steadily since 2000; TB mortality down 45% since 1990.
   • Obstacles to control: the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) epidemic in sub-Saharan Africa, global
     rise in obesity and diabetes, and multidrug and extensive drug resistance.
   • United States: about 8300 active cases in 2022 (2.5 per 100,000), declining steadily since the 1980s
     resurgence (peak 1992). Cases fell during COVID-19 (2020), back to pre-pandemic levels by 2022.
        ◦ Disproportionate burden in people born outside the United States: incidence 16 times that of US-
          born people.
   • UG involvement in pulmonary TB depends on setting:

 Infection, Host Immune Response and Transmission
   • Entry is by inhalation of cough-generated aerosols (direct soft-tissue inoculation is rare) → alveolar
     macrophages phagocytose the bacilli, with three possible outcomes:
        ◦ Cleared: macrophages kill the bacilli; no infection and no adaptive immune response.
        ◦ Established: bacilli escape killing and replicate in macrophages. A cellular immune response may
          take up to 12 weeks to appear; meanwhile bacilli spread via lymphatics to the hilar nodes, then via
          the bloodstream to distant organs — this is how the kidney and epididymis are seeded.
   • Granuloma formation is how the host contains infection:
        ◦ Infected macrophages secrete cytokines: interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-12, IL-1β, tumour necrosis factor-α
          (TNF-α).
        ◦ Structure: a centre of foamy macrophages, epithelioid cells and multinucleated (Langhans) giant
          cells within a lymphocyte cuff.
        ◦ Antigen presentation activates T cells, which secrete IL-2, TNF-α and, most importantly, interferon-
          γ (IFN-γ), which maintains the granuloma and drives killing of infected macrophages and bacilli.

   • Reactivation triggers (balance shifts towards the pathogen): old age, renal failure, diabetes mellitus,
     malnutrition, HIV infection, other immunosuppression.
   • Isolated extrapulmonary (including UG) TB is generally not infectious, except by direct skin
     inoculation or aerosol-generating procedures on infected fluids or tissues — relevant in theatre.
   • The latent/active split is a simplification. TB is a dynamic spectrum — contained infection →
     subclinical disease → progressively infectious disease — and a person can move in either direction as
     host immunity (comorbidity) or the bacilli (treatment of latent infection) change.
 Concept — TB outcome is a balance between host and pathogen, and the granuloma is where that
 balance is held. Latency, reactivation and the whole spectrum are the granuloma holding, failing or re-
 forming as host immunity changes, and IFN-γ is the cytokine that holds it.

 Concept — UG TB is usually a blood-borne disease that lands in the two best-perfused UG organs, the
 kidney and the epididymis, and spreads from there. The distribution of disease in the rest of the tract
 follows from those two starting points.

 Clinical Manifestations and Pathological Features
   • Presentation is non-specific: patients are often repeatedly treated for bacterial infection or
     investigated for malignancy first.

   • Symptoms depend on site and severity. Renal TB can be destructive yet silent until it reaches the
     bladder.
   • Asymptomatic in about 8% in developed countries (earlier presentation).
   • Constitutional symptoms (fever, weight loss, night sweats, malaise) in < 20%; their absence does not
     exclude UG TB.
   • Lower urinary tract symptoms dominate: up to half dysuria alone, about half storage symptoms,
     about one-third haematuria and flank pain.
   • Renal colic in < 10%, from passage of necrotic papillae, clot, stones or caseous debris in severe
     pyelonephritis.
   • Hallmark laboratory finding: sterile pyuria ± haematuria, in > 90% in developing countries.

 Kidney
   • Renal disease is progressive and highly destructive, pathology varying with severity.
   • Kidney as collateral damage of pulmonary or systemic TB (no UG imaging changes):
         ◦ TB granulomatous interstitial nephritis: pulmonary TB + renal failure, ± pyuria, normal UG imaging;
           biopsy: granulomas, sometimes caseating. Treatment can reverse the renal impairment.
         ◦ Immune-complex glomerulonephritis and secondary amyloidosis.

   • Miliary TB (widely disseminated): heavy haematogenous load gives innumerable pale granulomas
     about 3 mm across, like millet seeds, studding cortex and medulla. High mortality, but the renal
     "milia" usually spare renal function.
   • Localised renal TB sequence: bacilli lodge in periglomerular capillaries → parenchymal granulomas
     coalesce → caseation forms cavities → abscess, chronic pyelonephritis, parenchymal and papillary
     necrosis → flank sinus tracts.
        ◦ Examination: costovertebral angle tenderness.
        ◦ With advancing disease the calyces become inflamed, then calcify, causing calyceal distortion,
          dilatation and stenosis.
   • Autonephrectomy (non-functioning kidney) has two types:

   • End-stage renal failure in about 7%.
   • Squamous metaplasia of the renal pelvis can persist after treatment, giving a long-term risk of
     squamous cell carcinoma (SCC).

 Ureter
   • Ureteric TB descends from the kidney: urinary bacilli form mural granulomas, and infected calculi can
     descend and impact.
   • Inflammation → scarring → stricture, commonest distally at the vesicoureteric junction; pan-ureteric
     strictures give a "beaded corkscrew" ureter.
   • A scarred, distorted ureter can both obstruct and reflux. Stricture-related obstruction is an important
     cause of renal failure in UG TB.
   • Spontaneous ureteric perforation above a vesicoureteric junction stricture (single case): stenting,
     medical treatment, reconstruction.

 Bladder
   • Bladder TB descends, beginning around the ureteric orifices and spreading along lymphatics.
     Urothelial implantation causes patchy cystitis; ulcers form where large granulomas coalesce.
   • The dome is most affected; trigone and bladder neck usually spared. Mucosal inflammation, friability
     and haematuria follow.
   • After about a year of chronic inflammation and scarring, the bladder contracts:
        ◦ Capacity < 100 mL → prominent frequency, urgency, pain and dysuria.
        ◦ "Thimble" bladder: capacity < 20 mL.
   • Contracture is late and commoner in the developing world (about 12% vs 4% of UG cases) because
     diagnosis there is made later.
   • Rare: acute abdomen from spontaneous bladder perforation, often recognised only at operation,
     before TB is suspected.

 Epididymis, Vas Deferens, Testes and Scrotum
   • Second commonest site of haematogenous seeding, after the kidney.

   • Disease begins in the more vascular globus minor: epithelial granulomas → fibrosis and luminal
     obliteration → large caseous granulomas → nodular epididymis, swollen or hard on examination.
   • Granulomas can adhere to the skin and ulcerate, forming the posterior scrotal sinus.
   • Vas deferens: thickened and beaded from nodular scarring.
   • Testis: isolated epididymal or testicular TB is rare; epididymal TB usually extends into the testis,
     granulomas replacing seminiferous epithelium and connective tissue with fibrosis to produce the
     tumour-like mass.

 Prostate and Seminal Vesicles
   • The prostate is reached by either route, and the route determines the picture:

     the partial response is the clue.

        ◦ Routes: via the vas (from epididymal or testicular TB), or via the urethra and ejaculatory ducts

        ◦ Features: low-volume ejaculate, oligospermia, azoospermia, haemospermia; rarely, abscess.
        ◦ Examination: enlarged vesicles early; hard nodules late.

 Urethra and Penis
   • Urethral TB usually accompanies prostatic TB; isolated urethral TB is very rare.

 Concept — In UG TB the route of spread predicts the lesion. Haematogenous seeding gives parenchymal
 disease (kidney, epididymis, peripheral prostate). Descending urinary spread gives luminal and orifice
 disease (distal ureteric stricture, cystitis, urethral prostatic disease). Healing by fibrosis turns an infection
 into an obstruction (stricture, contracted bladder, obliterated epididymis, infertility).

 Diagnosis
   • In developed countries the goal is MTBC culture for drug-susceptibility testing. Order of evidence:
        ◦ Culture or DNA positive → diagnosis.
        ◦ Negative → caseating granulomas on tissue in the right clinical context support TB.
        ◦ Also absent → compatible clinical picture + probable exposure + response to empirical treatment.
   • Up to one-fifth of UG TB coexists with pulmonary TB: always look for pulmonary disease.

 Culture
   • Gold standard: urine acid-fast bacilli (AFB) culture.
   • Sampling: first-void (most concentrated) urine, 3–5 samples on consecutive days, cultured
     immediately because prolonged exposure to acidic urine inhibits mycobacterial growth. Culture any
     biopsy or operative tissue too.
   • Sensitivity depends on technique and shedding: up to 80–90% with ideal technique; as low as 10% in
     real-world practice, compounded by intermittent bacillary shedding.
   • Ziehl–Neelsen stain of urine: sensitivity < 50%.

 Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests
   • Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) give results in 1–2 days and detect MTBC when bacillary load
     is too low for culture.
   • Natural amplification inhibitors in urine lower sensitivity in non-respiratory specimens; sensitivity also
     varies with specimen, target sequence and protocol.
   • GeneXpert MTB/RIF (marketed since 2010): self-contained, automated (sputum processing → DNA
     extraction → amplification); detects MTBC and rifampicin resistance simultaneously.
        ◦ Rifampicin resistance is a surrogate for multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB), as > 90% of rifampicin-
          resistant strains are also isoniazid (INH)-resistant.
        ◦ MDR-TB = resistance to both INH and rifampicin.
   • Performance in urine: systematic reviews show good sensitivity and specificity for GeneXpert against
     urine AFB culture, but small, heterogeneous studies make estimates imprecise. NAAT on epididymal
     biopsy tissue performed well in a single study.
   • Role of NAAT:
        ◦ United States: adjunct only; it does not replace culture, and lacking high-quality sensitivity data, a
          negative NAAT does not exclude extrapulmonary TB.
        ◦ WHO: GeneXpert MTB/RIF is part of the initial work-up of all patients with TB; cost and
          equipment are the barriers in developing countries.
        ◦ NAATs cannot monitor treatment response: dead organisms still shed nucleic acid, so the test stays
          positive despite adequate treatment. Culture is used for monitoring.

 Urine Lipoarabinomannan Assay
   • Lipoarabinomannan (LAM) is a mycobacterial cell-wall glycolipid found in urine, sputum and serum.
     Methods range from enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to the point-of-care lateral-flow
     urine assay (LF-LAM): no laboratory equipment, result in < 30 minutes.
   • In people living with HIV (PLHIV): a systematic review showed low sensitivity, high specificity;
     sensitivity somewhat better with TB symptoms. A positive result is useful; a negative result is not
     reassuring.
   • WHO (2019) recommends adding LF-LAM for:
        ◦ PLHIV with TB symptoms
        ◦ People with advanced HIV disease who are unwell
        ◦ PLHIV with a CD4 count < 200 cells/mm³
   • United States: broad access to other diagnostics confines LF-LAM mainly to academic centres.

 Histopathology
   • Biopsy when urine cultures are negative. Mycobacteria are often not seen in tissue, but caseating
     granulomas in the right clinical context support the diagnosis.
   • Histology is consistent with TB in only a minority (about one-third in developed countries, one-fifth
     globally). A negative biopsy does not exclude TB.
   • Epididymal nodules: fine-needle aspiration cytology diagnostic in about two-thirds.
   • Adding a TB NAAT to tissue increases sensitivity.

 Screening Tests
   • The tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon-γ release assays (IGRAs) cannot distinguish latent
     infection from active disease.
        ◦ Both are United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved and widely used but of
          limited value for active disease: a positive test does not rule TB disease in; a negative test does
          not rule it out.
        ◦ Their proper use is screening for TB infection.
   • Preferred screening test: IGRA for persons ≥ 5 years born in TB-endemic countries; TST remains
     acceptable.
 Tuberculin Skin Test (Purified Protein Derivative, Mantoux Test)
   • Principle: detects an existing cellular (delayed-type hypersensitivity) response to MTBC antigens.
   • Technique: tuberculin (sterile protein extract of M. tuberculosis) injected intradermally into the volar
     forearm; induration read at 48–72 hours.
   • Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) cut-offs are risk-stratified:
 TST — POSITIVITY THRESHOLDS BY RISK GROUP (CDC)

  Induration Positive in
  ≥ 5 mm         Recent contact with TB; fibrotic chest radiograph changes consistent with old TB;
                 immunosuppressed
  ≥ 10 mm        Immigrants from high-prevalence countries; residents of or workers in high-risk institutions; people

   • The logic of the table: higher pre-test probability, lower cut-off — a small reaction matters in someone
     likely infected or unable to respond. The trap is one cut-off for everyone.
   • TST advantages: cheap, no laboratory, easy (though staff must be trained to place and read it).
   • TST disadvantages:
        ◦ Not MTBC-specific: BCG vaccination and non-tuberculous mycobacteria can cause positives.
        ◦ Second visit needed for reading.
        ◦ False negatives in 10–25% of active TB.
   • In UG TB the TST is usually positive.
 Interferon-γ Release Assays
   • Principle: blood test measuring IFN-γ released by sensitised T cells to MTBC-specific antigens — an in
     vitro, MTBC-specific TST.
   • Its antigens are absent from all BCG strains and most non-tuberculous mycobacteria, so no BCG false
     positive — the key advantage over TST.

 Radiography
   • Imaging often first suggests TB in a UG disorder. Choose modality by site and clinical picture.
 Plain Radiography (Kidney–Ureter–Bladder Film)

 Intravenous Urography
   • Intravenous urography (IVU), the former gold standard for early renal TB, gave most classic signs. Still
     called the preferred first test in some regions, it is essentially replaced by computed tomography (CT),
     CT urography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasound (US).
   • Early signs: loss of sharpness, irregular urothelial margins; "moth-eaten" calyces.
   • Filling defects from a tuberculoma rupturing into a calyx or papillary necrosis; medullary cavities
     communicating with the collecting system.
   • "Phantom calyx": a stenosed calyx or infundibulum excretes no contrast, so the calyx is absent where
     it should be.
   • Ureter:
        ◦ Pipestem ureter: rigid, calcified, straight, tubular, aperistaltic.
        ◦ Beaded corkscrew ureter: nodular fibrosis along the whole ureter.
        ◦ Both highly suggestive of TB, especially with kidney or bladder changes.
   • Non-functioning kidney (autonephrectomy); fibrosed, contracted bladder (Fig. 31.2).
   • Perinephric abscess suggested by loss of renal respiratory movement or ureteric displacement.
   • Commonest IVU findings are obstructive changes from scarring, not the classic erosive signs:
        ◦ Calyceal obliteration, infundibular narrowing, hydrocalycosis, segmental or total hydronephrosis,
          hydroureter (Figs. 31.3, 31.4)
        ◦ Calyceal dilatation and distortion → "cloverleaf" pattern
        ◦ Vesicoureteric junction obstruction from tuberculous cystitis or distal-third ureteric stricture (Fig.
          31.5)
        ◦ Kerr's kink: "hiked-up" renal pelvis with sharp pelviureteric junction angulation

   • Both replaced by CT urography; useful when IVU or CT is impossible (renal insufficiency, contrast

 Ultrasonography
   • Limited diagnostic role: findings non-specific, visualisation poorer than CT.
   • No radiation and low cost make it the modality for children and pregnancy.
   • Uses:
        ◦ Scrotum: diffusely enlarged testis or epididymis with homogeneous or heterogeneous hypoechoic
          areas, complex collections, hydrocele.
        ◦ Transrectal US: thickened prostate and seminal vesicles.
        ◦ Kidney: abscesses, cavities; septated cystic lesions suggest chronic infection; calcifications
          echogenic with distal acoustic shadowing.
        ◦ Restricted renal respiratory movement suggests perinephric or psoas abscess.
        ◦ Abdomen: ascites, lymphadenopathy, omental caking.
   • Main use: following hydronephrosis during medical treatment, because healing fibrosis can worsen
     obstruction while the infection is cured.

   • MRI is used less than contrast CT; radiation-free, so useful in children and pregnancy. Findings
     resemble CT, but better tissue resolution can show a single granuloma.

        ◦ Small lesions: hypointense on T1 and T2.
        ◦ Larger lesions: centrally T2-hyperintense from central cellularity.
        ◦ Large lesions can mimic malignancy, not always distinguishable.
   • Magnetic resonance urogram (MRU): more sensitive than IVU for urothelial thickening and
     caliectasis.
   • Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI):
        ◦ Distinguishes hydronephrosis from pyonephrosis.

 Treatment
   • Before antimicrobials: sanatorium rest and nutrition; for severe UG disease, extirpative surgery was
     the only hope of cure.
   • Drug milestones: streptomycin 1944 → isoniazid (INH) 1952 → rifamycins 1957, replacing both
     sanatoria and surgery.
   • Today most patients, including MDR-TB, are treated medically as outpatients. Surgery establishes the
     diagnosis or is adjunctive in advanced disease.

 Medical Therapy
   • Multidrug therapy is mandatory, for five reasons:
        ◦ Bacilli live in different micro-environments with different metabolic states and replication rates.
        ◦ Some drugs are bactericidal, others only bacteriostatic.
        ◦ Some act best on rapidly replicating bacilli, others on metabolically quiescent ones.
        ◦ Drugs differ in tissue penetration and optimal pH.
        ◦ Combination therapy prevents emergence of resistance.
   • Start with four drugs: INH, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol (RIPE); first-line combination
     gives the best cure rate in the shortest time.
   • Baseline tests: platelet count, liver and renal function; HIV test; where appropriate, hepatitis B and C
     and diabetes screening.
   • Tailor regimen to susceptibility. Directly observed therapy (DOT) secures adherence and prevents
     resistance.
   • Second-line agents: for intolerance, resistance or failure of first-line treatment; variable tolerability
     and ease of administration.
        ◦ Recent additions:
            ▪ Fluoroquinolones and linezolid, originally developed for other bacterial infections.
            ▪ Bedaquiline, the first new drug in 40 years developed specifically for TB.
            ▪ Pretomanid (FDA-approved 2019), with bedaquiline and linezolid for MDR-TB or extensively
              drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB).

 Surgical Therapy
   • About half (55%) of UG TB patients need surgery, rising with stage.
   • Indications:
        ◦ Relieve obstruction and drain infected material
        ◦ Remove a non-functioning, infected kidney that resists cure
        ◦ Medically resistant hypertension from a functionally excluded kidney
        ◦ Reconstruct the urinary tract
        ◦ Abscesses unresponsive to medical treatment → aspiration, or percutaneous or open drainage
   • More than half of TB operations are now reconstructive.
   • Optimal timing: 4–6 weeks after starting medical therapy, letting inflammation subside, bacillary load
     fall and lesions stabilise.
 Procedures to Relieve Obstruction
   • Uraemia or sepsis → emergency decompression. Renal failure usually reflects bilateral obstruction or
     obstruction of a functionally solitary kidney.
   • Early stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN) for tuberculous ureteric stricture limits loss of
     function and preserves the chance of later reconstruction.
   • Escalation:
        ◦ Retrograde double-J stent (preferred) → succeeds in under half (41%).
        ◦ Fails → antegrade stent, internalised or externalised, via percutaneous puncture.
        ◦ Fails → PCN until definitive treatment; loculation from strictures and scarring may need more than
          one.
   • PCN must be followed by correcting the cause; removal without correction risks a tuberculous
     cutaneous fistula (less likely with effective concurrent chemotherapy).
   • Unsalvageable kidney → nephrectomy.
   • Avoid high contrast-injection pressures during stent or PCN placement, which could disseminate
     infection.
 Nephrectomy
   • The overriding surgical goal is organ preservation.
   • Partial nephrectomy: localised lesion (e.g. polar mass) unresponsive to medical therapy.

 Monitoring for Tuberculosis Relapse
   • Relapse after pulmonary TB: 2–6%, mostly within the first year; needs a second, longer or different
     regimen.
   • Relapse after UG TB is higher: 6.3–22%, even after 12 months of treatment.
        ◦ Reason: an extensively diseased kidney holds innumerable foci that are hard to sterilise; viable
          bacilli have been found after 9 months of treatment.
   • In any recurrence, make a special effort to isolate the organism for susceptibility testing.
   • Follow-up: pulmonary TB 2 years; UG TB, some recommend 10 years, as mean time to relapse is
     about 5 years (5.3).
 Concept — Surgery in UG TB follows chemotherapy. It is timed at 4–6 weeks, aims first to preserve the
 kidney, and is mostly reconstructive. It exists because drugs sterilise but cannot reverse fibrosis. The poorly
 sterilised kidney is also why UG TB relapses more often and needs longer follow-up than pulmonary TB.

   • UG schistosomiasis is the S. haematobium disease: a complex life cycle, many manifestations and
     close ties to the environment.
   • It has co-evolved with humans and non-human primates for millennia, so ancient civilisations
     recognised it.

 History
   • Schistosome antigens in Egyptian mummies (c. 3500 BCE); S. haematobium eggs in later mummies.
   • The Egyptians called it "A-a-a disease"; the hieroglyph shows a penis dripping blood-stained urine.
   • Theodor Bilharz (1852, Cairo autopsies) found worms in the mesenteric veins and linked them to eggs
     in urine and stool — hence "bilharzia".

 Biology and Life Cycle
   • Human cycle:
        ◦ Cercariae penetrate skin (even intact) in infested fresh water. They live about 1 day and lose
          infectivity within hours of leaving the snail.
        ◦ They shed their tails over 90–120 minutes → schistosomulae.
        ◦ Schistosomulae migrate via blood or lymph to the lungs (several weeks) → venous circulation →
          liver sinusoids, where they begin feeding on blood.
        ◦ Adults migrate to the vesical and pelvic venous plexus and live on average 3–5 years. Cercaria to
          adult takes 80–110 days.
        ◦ Pairing: the male holds the female in the gynaecophoric canal, helps her pump blood and
          chemically stimulates egg-laying.
   • Eggs:
        ◦ Fecundity: hundreds to thousands of eggs per female per day.
        ◦ S. haematobium eggs: oval, about 140 µm, terminal spine (Fig. 31.9).
        ◦ Eggs must cross the endothelium and bladder wall to reach urine; some cross the gut wall into
          faeces.
        ◦ Fewer than half are excreted; eggs retained in tissue drive the pathology.
        ◦ Eggs survive about 20 days if kept wet.
   • Environmental cycle:
        ◦ Fresh water and light → eggs hatch → ciliated miracidia (live about 6 hours).
        ◦ Miracidia penetrate Bulinus snails, which prefer slow-flowing water and tolerate low oxygen.
        ◦ Primary sporocyst → 20–40 daughter sporocysts → 200–400 cercariae each, released into water.
        ◦ The prepatent period is temperature-dependent; no cercariae are shed below 15 °C or above 35
          °C.

   • Eggs measure 70–170 µm across species; miracidia hatch within 1–3 hours.

 Epidemiology
   • Distribution follows the snail: most of sub-Saharan Africa, parts of North Africa and the Middle East.
   • Europe — Corsica, 2013: the first autochthonous transmission in Europe for over a century; further
     cases in 2015 suggest a new endemic focus. Some were S. bovis or S. haematobium–S. bovis hybrids.
   • Burden:
        ◦ Mostly the UG form; newer estimates reach 440 million infected.
        ◦ Up to 150,000 deaths a year from S. haematobium obstructive renal failure alone.
        ◦ In sub-Saharan Africa, tens of millions have haematuria and dysuria at any time; millions have major
          bladder pathology and hydronephrosis.
   • Individual risk depends on the type and duration of freshwater contact:
        ◦ Rural women and children: domestic chores (laundry, dishwashing).
        ◦ Children: play and swimming.
        ◦ Adults: fishing, car washing, agriculture with water-intensive crops (rice, sugar cane).
        ◦ Prevalence is highly focal, because transmission depends on water.
   • Dams and irrigation increase transmission by creating year-round slow-flowing snail habitats and
     drawing denser farming populations.

 Pathogenesis and Pathology
   • Cercarial penetration is aided by secreted proteases, which trigger cellular and humoral responses; but
     the parasite modulates host immunity from first contact, so brief penetration causes only local skin
     inflammation. Repeated exposure → hypersensitivity → maculopapular rash.
   • Immune evasion by the tegument: the maturing schistosomulum forms a double lipid bilayer surface
     (the tegument) that lets it survive for years, via host antigen mimicry, continuous membrane
     turnover, immunomodulatory proteins and proteases, evasive biophysical surface properties and
     modulation of surface-antigen expression.
   • Most knowledge comes from autopsy studies and animal models.

 Treatment
 Medical Management
   • Praziquantel (PZQ) is the only WHO-recommended drug. Single-drug dependence risks resistance, but
     PZQ is effective, widely available and of low toxicity, so there is little incentive for alternatives.
   • Dose for S. haematobium: 20 mg/kg twice on the same day, 6–8 hours apart, or 40 mg/kg once.
   • Katayama fever: add corticosteroids.

   • Efficacy (egg reduction and cure rate): 60–90%. Even without cure, worm burden falls substantially,
     reducing further sequelae.
   • Follow-up: urine and stool egg counts and serial UG ultrasound; repeat the course if infection persists.

     spontaneously. Large, bitter tablets are difficult for children; a paediatric formulation is in
     development.
   • Pregnancy: FDA Category B (animal safety data only), so many programmes exclude pregnant and
     lactating women — yet adverse effects are rarely reported despite millions of pregnant women
     treated.
   • Weak activity against schistosomulae means lower cure rates where transmission and reinfection are
     high, and PZQ cannot abort infection soon after exposure. Repeat doses several weeks apart catch
     juveniles missed earlier that have since matured.
   • Resistance: little in endemic areas in large studies; failures reported in returning travellers and
     military personnel. Protection: alternative drugs and environmental control (snail control, sanitation).

 Prevention and Control
   • Travellers: avoid fresh water (streams, rivers, ponds, lakes); fast-flowing water can still transmit.
   • Cercariae are killed by heating water to > 125 °F (about 52 °C) for 5 minutes, chlorination, or standing
     water > 2 days, free of snails.

     simple and cheap, but control remains hard.
   • WHO strategy: periodic, usually annual, targeted MDA with PZQ, aimed at reducing morbidity.
     Without safe water, cycles of infection, treatment and reinfection demand more frequent dosing.
   • Cost: under US$0.50 per person in campaigns, yet at scale this can exceed national budgets; donated
     PZQ from companies and foundations helps.

 Filariasis
   • Filariae are vector-borne tissue nematodes. Human pathogens include the agents of lymphatic
     filariasis (LF), Onchocerca volvulus and Loa loa.
   • LF is caused by the mosquito-borne Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi and Brugia timori, spanning
     acute lymphatic inflammation to chronic lymphatic dilatation, with hydrocele, lymphoedema and
     elephantiasis.

 Organisms
   • Transmission and development:
        ◦ A mosquito bite deposits third-stage (infective) larvae, which migrate to the central lymphatics
          and mature over 6–9 months.
        ◦ Adults (about 20–100 mm × 0.2 mm) live in the afferent lymphatics, especially of the lower limbs
          (inguinal, iliac, para-aortic); W. bancrofti alone also in the male genitalia (epididymis, spermatic
          cord, testis). Adult lifespan 5–7 years.
        ◦ Females release microfilariae (about 200 × 10 µm).
        ◦ Periodicity: microfilaraemia is nocturnal (peaks at midnight) in most areas, matching mosquito
          biting; diurnal in parts of the Pacific.
        ◦ In the mosquito, microfilariae become infective larvae over 10–14 days.
   • Wolbachia: an obligate rickettsia-like endosymbiont of W. bancrofti and Brugia, needed for
     embryogenesis. Doxycycline kills it, reducing microfilarial release and suppressing larval moulting.

 Epidemiology
   • About 68 million people have LF, a large fall since the early 2000s.

 Pathology and Clinical Manifestations
   • Immune phases:
        ◦ Early (larvae, young adults): pro-inflammatory, mixed Th1 and Th2; filaria-specific IgE rises and
          eosinophils kill microfilariae.
        ◦ With microfilaraemia: T-cell responses down-regulated by IL-10, IgG4 blocking antibody and
          antigen-specific suppressor T cells.
        ◦ Some stay uninfected despite long exposure; whether protective immunity develops is unclear.
   • Damage is cumulative (progressive scarring, lymphatic obstruction). Treatment does not readily
     reverse it but can halt progression in active infection.
   • LF is rarely fatal but causes the third-highest loss of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) among
     parasitic infections.
   • How lymphoedema develops:
        ◦ Parasite factors dilate lymphatics → secondary bacterial infection and inflammation against dying
          worms (and pro-inflammatory Wolbachia).
        ◦ Histology: granulomas around worms, ± tissue eosinophils (Fig. 31.15).
        ◦ Vicious cycle: acute attack → worse lymphoedema → more infection → worse lymphoedema.
        ◦ End result: obliterated, scarred lymphatics, with elephantiasis or hydrocele.

   • Palpable, cord-like swelling with oedema. Usually self-limiting, but recurrence and chronic

   • The commonest result of chronic male genital disease; can be very large (Fig. 31.16).

   • Mild scrotal oedema is common early or with hydrocele; penile oedema is unusual.

   • Damaged GU lymphatics leak lymph into urine → heavy fat and protein loss, possible malnutrition.
     Rare.

   • Personal protection: avoid mosquitoes; long-lasting insecticide-treated bednets (LLINs) are proven for
     control and elimination.

     microfilaraemia to transmit. But drugs do not kill all adults, so intermittent dosing must continue for
     years until adults die of senescence. This works for W. bancrofti but is harder for Brugia, which has
     animal reservoirs.
   • MDA: single annual doses of albendazole plus ivermectin or DEC (sustained microfilaricidal effect):
        ◦ Africa: albendazole + ivermectin (DEC avoided because onchocerciasis and Loa are co-endemic).
        ◦ Elsewhere: albendazole + DEC.
        ◦ MDA is the main reason global prevalence has fallen; about 19 million hydroceles prevented since
          2000, halving LF hydroceles.
   • Triple therapy (DEC + albendazole + ivermectin): small studies show deeper, longer microfilarial
     suppression than dual therapy, with no extra serious adverse effects.

 Onchocerciasis
   • River blindness, caused by O. volvulus (vector in the comparison table).
        ◦ 99% of cases in Africa; foci in Brazil, Venezuela and Yemen.
        ◦ About 17 million infected, about half the 2000 figure, thanks to MDA.
        ◦ Transmission is inefficient and focal.
   • Adults live up to 15 years (mean 9–10) in subcutaneous nodules; microfilariae migrate through skin
     and eye.
   • Clinical features: dermatitis and pruritus, keratitis, chorioretinitis → blindness from corneal scarring
     after many years.
   • Urological relevance: late "hanging groin" or scrotal elephantiasis from recurrent lymphadenitis and
     lost skin elasticity. Histology: atrophic, fibrotic inguinal nodes with subcutaneous oedema and fibrosis.
     Occasionally massive inguinal lymphadenopathy.
   • Diagnosis: skin snips for microfilariae; adult worms in nodules; microfilariae in the anterior chamber
     on slit lamp. Serology is less developed than for LF.
   • Onchocerca lupi (a dog parasite): a rare human pathogen — six United States residents since 2013
     (five children), all in the south-west, with nodules containing adult worms (spine, skin and soft tissue,
     extraocular muscle).
   • Treatment:
        ◦ Ivermectin 150 µg/kg once, repeated every 6–12 months until asymptomatic.
        ◦ It acts mainly on microfilariae, but repeated doses shorten adult lifespan by about half or more.
        ◦ Adverse effects: fever, rash, dizziness, pruritus, myalgia, arthralgia, lymphadenopathy, from dying
          filariae and Wolbachia.
        ◦ Caution if Loa co-infection is possible.
        ◦ Then doxycycline 200 mg/day for 6 weeks: kills or shortens the life of most adult females and
          sterilises the rest, clearing or greatly reducing microfilariae.
        ◦ DEC causes blindness and systemic toxicity from inflammation against dying parasites.

   • Loa loa: Central and West Africa, transmitted by Chrysops flies. Adults migrate through subcutaneous
     tissue.
   • Clinical features:
        ◦ Most have asymptomatic eosinophilia.
        ◦ Some have urticaria, migratory subcutaneous swellings and "eye worm" (adult crossing the
          conjunctiva).
        ◦ Haematuria and proteinuria in about 30%; lymphadenitis and hydrocele rare.
   • Treatment:
        ◦ DEC 2–3 mg/kg orally three times daily for 14–21 days is effective; multiple courses may be
          needed.
        ◦ Treatment reactions: pruritus, arthralgia, migratory swellings, fever, eye worms, diarrhoea, renal
          failure.
        ◦ Encephalopathy risk with high microfilaraemia (> 2500–8000 microfilariae/mL), especially with
          ivermectin; pre-treatment apheresis may reduce it.

 encephalopathy in heavy Loa infection. Wolbachia is present in LF and Onchocerca but absent in Loa,
 which is why doxycycline is useful in the first two and not the third.

 Other Non-filarial Genitourinary Parasites
 Echinococcosis
   • Echinococcus granulosus, a cestode (tapeworm), causes cystic echinococcosis (hydatid disease).
   • Transmission: ingested eggs in contaminated food or water, or contact with infected dogs. High
     prevalence in pastoral communities: South America, the Mediterranean littoral, Eastern Europe, the
     Middle East, East Africa, Central Asia, China, Russia, Australia.
   • Cyst sites: usually liver, less often lung; kidney third (see table).
   • Natural history: initially asymptomatic, growing 1–2 cm per year → pain or palpable mass.
     Hydatiduria and renal colic in a minority. Renal function usually preserved.
   • Imaging: thick-walled, fluid-filled spherical cyst, often with a calcified wall; the appearance defines
     the stage, which guides management.
   • Serology is adjunctive only (sensitivity 60–90%).
   • Treatment:
        ◦ Puncture, aspiration, injection, re-aspiration (PAIR) suits liver cysts, not lung or kidney cysts, which
          have only surgical resection or antiparasitic drugs.
        ◦ Albendazole (regimen in table) is the recommended medical treatment; surgical excision for some
          lesions because of size or location.
        ◦ Cyst rupture can cause anaphylaxis.
        ◦ Perioperative PZQ + albendazole may reduce secondary seeding.

 Enterobiasis
   • Enterobius vermicularis (pinworm) occurs worldwide, temperate and tropical.
   • Life cycle: worms live in the proximal colon → migrate to perianal skin to lay eggs, infective after 6
     hours. Spread person to person (faecal–oral, hands, fomites).
   • Clinical features:
        ◦ Usually asymptomatic; perianal pruritus can be severe.
        ◦ Ectopic migration in women via vagina, uterus and tubes into the peritoneum: dead worms and
          eggs cause granulomas and adhesions → vulval and cervical granulomas, salpingitis, oophoritis,
          tubo-ovarian abscess, appendicitis, peritonitis.
        ◦ Men: rare epididymal involvement and inguinal hernia.
   • Treatment: single-dose albendazole or mebendazole (highly effective; doses in table); alternative
     ivermectin 200 µg/kg once. Treat household and close contacts; the 2-week repeat covers reinfection
     and autoinfection.

 Amoebiasis
   • Entamoeba histolytica: a protozoan spread faecal–orally, commonest in the tropics.
   • Most infected are asymptomatic; about 10% develop disease in other organs, including the kidney.
     Cutaneous amoebiasis causes painful perianal and genital ulcers.
   • Treatment:
        ◦ Tissue phase: tinidazole 2 g/day for 3–5 days or metronidazole 750 mg three times daily for 10
          days.
        ◦ Then a luminal agent: paromomycin 8–12 mg/kg three times daily for 7 days or iodoquinol 650 mg
          three times daily for 20 days.

 Trichomoniasis
   • Trichomonas vaginalis, a common sexually transmitted protozoan, is covered in Chapter 29.

     • LF is caused by three species of tissue nematode transmitted by mosquitoes.
     • LF is endemic in much of the tropics but transmitted inefficiently. Clinically apparent disease is
       usually seen only in long-term residents of endemic countries.
     • Diagnosis: blood smears, antibody and antigen assays, imaging and clinical assessment.
