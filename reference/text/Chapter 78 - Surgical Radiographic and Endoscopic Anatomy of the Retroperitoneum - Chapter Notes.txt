Chapter 78 — Surgical, Radiographic, and Endoscopic
Anatomy of the Retroperitoneum
DEFINITION, BOUNDARIES AND ORGANS
 • Why it matters: safe entry into the retroperitoneum underpins open, laparoscopic and robotic surgery
   and percutaneous access for endoscopy.
 • Etymology: retro (“behind”) + peritoneum (the lining of the abdominal cavity), so it is the space behind
   the peritoneum.
 • Boundaries: anterior is the posterior reflection of the peritoneum; posterior is the abdominal wall;
   cranial is the diaphragm; caudal is the extraperitoneal pelvic structures.
 • Boundaries are debated: there is no consensus on whether psoas and quadratus lumborum are
   boundaries or contents.
 • Retroperitoneum ≠ extraperitoneal space. The extraperitoneal space is the retroperitoneum plus the
   space circumferentially surrounding the abdominal cavity.

POSTERIOR ABDOMINAL WALL

Flank Muscles
Superficial → deep:
   External oblique (beneath subcutaneous fascia) arises from ribs 5–12. Fibres run inferomedially to the
   iliac crest and linea alba. The inferior border of its aponeurosis forms the inguinal ligament.
   → Internal oblique arises from the lumbodorsal fascia and iliac crest. Fibres run superomedially to the
   lower ribs and linea alba.
   → Transversus abdominis, named for its transverse fibres.
   → Transversalis fascia crosses the midline anteriorly and fuses with the lumbodorsal fascia posteriorly.
 • Each muscle layer is invested in its own fascia. Together the layers flex, extend and rotate the trunk and
   compress the abdominal contents.
 • Examinable contrast: external oblique fibres run inferomedially (“hands in pockets”), internal oblique
   fibres run superomedially.

Psoas, Iliacus, Quadratus Lumborum and Erector Spinae
 • Posterior compartment: psoas, iliacus and quadratus lumborum are sometimes grouped as the posterior
   compartment of the retroperitoneum.
 • Psoas major: arises from T12–L5, passes along the pelvic brim behind the inguinal ligament to the lesser
   trochanter. It flexes the hip. Nerve supply is the anterior rami of L1–L3.
 • Psoas minor: may be absent. Arises from T12–L1 and inserts at the pelvic brim and iliopubic eminence.
 • Iliacus: arises from the iliac fossa and lateral sacrum and inserts at the lesser trochanter. It flexes the hip
   together with psoas major.
 • Quadratus lumborum: lies posterolateral to psoas. It runs from L5 and the iliac crest to the 12th rib and
   the L1–L4 transverse processes. It stabilises the 12th rib and bends the trunk laterally.
 • Erector spinae (sacrospinalis): a large group of back muscles that extend the spine.

 • What the table is for: it separates the flank layers, which compress the abdomen, from the posterior
   compartment, whose muscles move the hip and spine.
 • Pattern: psoas major and iliacus share the lesser trochanter and act together as hip flexors. Transversus
   is the only flank layer that does not flex the trunk.
 • Trap: psoas minor is inconstant and inserts at the pelvic brim, not the femur.

Spine
 • Vertebral count: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, then sacrum and coccyx.
 • Vertebral structure: each vertebra has a weight-bearing body and a posterior and lateral arch forming the
   vertebral foramen. The spinous process projects posteroinferiorly and the transverse processes
   posterolaterally.
 • Lumbar vertebrae matter most here: they are the largest and have long, thin transverse processes.
 • Vertebral level ≠ spinal cord segmental level: the sacral cord segments begin at vertebral T12–L1 in
   adults. In spinal cord injury, always specify which level is meant.

10th, 11th and 12th Ribs
 • Protective role: the lower ribs shield the retroperitoneum. A lower rib fracture → high suspicion of
   retroperitoneal injury.
 • Shape: lower ribs are shorter and less angulated than the upper ribs.

Concept — The posterior wall is a layered barrier that each approach crosses differently. Flank incisions cut
muscle, while posterior approaches exploit the 12th rib and its ligament. Percutaneous access is limited
above by the pleura and the intercostal nerve, and that is why subcostal access is safer.

LUMBODORSAL FASCIA
 • Structure: the lumbodorsal (thoracolumbar) fascia has three layers investing the posterior wall muscles,
   and they merge into one laterally.
 • Common access point: the tip of the 12th rib, where all three layers have fused. There the single layer
   merges anterolaterally with the transversus abdominis aponeurosis.

Transversalis Fascia and Posterior Pararenal Space
 • Transversalis fascia (outer stratum) lies deep to transversus abdominis and superficial to the
   preperitoneal fat and peritoneum. Behind the kidney it stays anterior to the fascia of quadratus
   lumborum and psoas (Fig. 78.18).
 • Medial fusion with the posterior lamina of Gerota’s fascia is clinically important. This fusion must be
   incised to reach the renal hilum in retroperitoneal dissection.

 • Lateroconal fascia: formed where the two laminae merge laterally. It separates the anterior and

 • Perirenal space contents: adrenal, kidney, ureter, perirenal fat, renal vascular pedicle, gonadal vessels.

PERIRENAL VERSUS PARARENAL FAT

Perirenal (perinephric) fat                               Pararenal (paranephric) fat
Finer, lighter yellow; inside Gerota’s fascia             Coarser, yellow-orange; outside Gerota’s fascia

 • Use of the fat colour: the difference in colour guides the plane during colon mobilisation.
 • Medial extent of the perirenal space: the right and left spaces were once thought separate. In vivo cases
   and cadaveric injection studies suggest communication across the midline below the renal hilum.
 • Caudal extent: once thought closed inferiorly by fusion of Gerota’s fascia. It is in fact cone-shaped and
   open inferiorly into the extraperitoneal pelvis.
 • Clinical consequence: these boundaries contain perinephric collections but let them track caudally:
     ◦ Urine: traumatic or iatrogenic extravasation, or calyceal rupture in obstruction.
     ◦ Blood: traumatic or iatrogenic perinephric haematoma, or ruptured aneurysm.
     ◦ Pus: perinephric abscess or infected urinoma.

Anterior Pararenal Space and Inner Stratum
 • Boundaries: the anterior lamina of the renal fascia behind and the posterior parietal peritoneum in
   front.
 • Surgical use: the space is developed medially from the white line of Toldt to reach the kidney anteriorly.
 • White line of Toldt: the lateral border of the fusion of the colonic mesentery with the posterior
   peritoneum. It forms when the inner stratum fuses with the primary dorsal peritoneum during gut
   rotation and fixation.
 • Contents: the secondarily retroperitoneal organs, namely ascending and descending colon, pancreas, and
   the second and third parts of the duodenum. Each was intraperitoneal until the inner stratum fused
   posteriorly.

The Mesentery as an Organ
 • Current view: the mesentery is now classed as a distinct organ with intestinal, vascular, immunological
   and endocrine functions.

 • History: Leonardo da Vinci drew it as continuous. A fragmented model was adopted in the late 1800s,
   with no mesentery for the ascending or descending colon. An observational cohort of total abdominal
   colectomies re-established continuity from the ileocaecal to the rectosigmoid level.
 • Functions:
    ◦ Scaffold suspending the bowel from the posterior wall.
     ◦ First barrier between gut and body, and a sampling reservoir for systemic immunity. Mesenteric
       nodes regulate cell-mediated responses with the mucosa.
     ◦ Carries postganglionic nerves from the main abdominal ganglia. Its role in the enteric nervous system
       is poorly understood.
 • Urological relevance:
    ◦ Internal hernia through a surgically created mesenteric defect is potentially lethal. Close small
      mesenteric defects primarily after bowel isolation, because small defects carry the higher herniation
      risk.
     ◦ Vascular mesenteropathy can cause loss of the bowel segment used for diversion, ureteric
       interposition or augmentation.

 • What the table is for: it gives the order of origin and the anterior/lateral/posterior pattern.
 • Pattern: unpaired anterior branches supply the gut (foregut → midgut → hindgut). Paired lateral
   branches supply the retroperitoneal organs (adrenals, kidneys, gonads) and share their lymphatic
   drainage. Posterior branches supply the wall.
 • Trap: the gonadal arteries arise anterolaterally below the renals, not laterally. They occasionally arise
   from the renal artery, and then run with the gonadal vein.
 • Adrenal arterial supply has three sources:
    ◦ Superior from the inferior phrenic artery. This supply is constant.
      ◦ Middle and inferior are variable. The commonest pattern is the middle adrenal from the aorta and the
        inferior adrenal from the renal artery.
 • Celiac trunk: short, unpaired and anterior. It supplies the abdominal oesophagus, stomach, duodenum,
   spleen, liver and pancreas. The splenic vessels run along the cephalad edge of the pancreatic body and
   tail, so they are at risk when the inferior pancreatic edge is lifted off the anterior renal fascia in
   transperitoneal adrenal or renal surgery.
 • SMA: branches are the inferior pancreaticoduodenal, ileocolic, right colic and middle colic arteries. SMA
   injury in left-sided surgery → severe bowel ischaemia, despite the collaterals.
 • Marginal artery of Drummond: the middle colic (SMA) ↔ left colic (IMA) anastomosis. It is the SMA-to-
   IMA collateral that lets the IMA be sacrificed without colonic ischaemia.
 • Renal arteries: arise at L1–L2. At least a quarter of people have supernumerary renal arteries. There is
   no consensus on side predominance, and female renal arteries are smaller on CT angiography. Variants
   are covered in Chapter 84.

   right, the IVC) towards the internal inguinal ring.
 • Ovarian arteries: run anterior to the ureter and then medially through the infundibulopelvic
   (suspensory) ligament.
 • Lumbar arteries: a fifth pair sometimes arises from the median sacral artery.

 • Renal veins: lie anterior to the renal arteries and enter the lateral IVC at L1.
 • Left varicocele predominance: the longer left testicular vein and its perpendicular entry into the left

   A plane medial to it risks IVC injury, and lifting the kidney then risks gonadal vein avulsion.
 • Ovarian veins: drain the pampiniform plexus at the ovarian hilum via the infundibulopelvic ligament. The

Portal and Hepatic Venous Drainage
 • Portal system: collects from bowel, spleen, pancreas and gallbladder and drains into the liver (Fig. 78.35).
   It does not mirror the arteries as closely as the systemic veins do.
 • Superior mesenteric vein (SMV): drains the small bowel and colon proximal to the splenic flexure.
   Tributaries are the right gastroepiploic, anterior and posterior inferior pancreaticoduodenal, jejunal, ileal,
   ileocolic, right colic and middle colic veins.
 • Portal vein formation: SMV + splenic vein → portal vein. Splenic tributaries are the inferior mesenteric,
   short gastric, left gastroepiploic and pancreatic veins.
 • Inferior mesenteric vein (IMV): drains the colon distal to the splenic flexure. It joins the SMV rather than
   the splenic vein in 40%. It lies in the mesocolon when the left colon is reflected.
 • Reoperative trap: the IMV can be mistaken for the gonadal vein when the mesentery is stuck to Gerota’s
   fascia.
 • Hepatic outflow: portal vein → right and left branches → hepatic sinusoids → hepatic veins (a larger
   upper and a smaller lower group) → anterior IVC below the diaphragm.
 • Budd–Chiari syndrome: hepatic vein occlusion causing progressive, often rapid, liver failure with
   jaundice, ascites, abdominal pain and hepatomegaly.

LYMPHATIC SYSTEM
 • Node structure: nodes have multiple afferent and a single efferent lymphatic.
 • Direction of flow: lymph flows cephalad and right → left, returning to the left innominate
   (brachiocephalic) vein.
 • Exception to the left-sided return: the head, neck, right thorax, right arm and right heart drain to the
   right innominate vein.
 • Pelvic and lower limb drainage: internal iliac, external iliac, common iliac, obturator and sacral nodes →
   lumbar nodes → lumbar trunks.
 • Why urologists care about lumbar nodes: they are the primary drainage of organs supplied by the
   lateral aortic branches, namely the kidneys, adrenals, ureters and gonads (Fig. 78.36).

Somatic Nervous System
 • Lumbosacral plexus: formed from the anterior rami of the lumbar and sacral nerves plus T12, in the
   retroperitoneum. It supplies the lower abdomen and lower limbs.
 • Relation to psoas: superior branches pierce psoas; inferior branches run medial to it (Fig. 78.40).
 • Subcostal (T12), iliohypogastric (L1) and ilioinguinal (L1): the subcostal runs below the 12th rib. All three
   cross the front of quadratus lumborum, pierce transversus and run deep to internal oblique. They supply
   the abdominal wall muscles and the skin of the posterolateral buttock, upper medial thigh and genitalia.
 • Genitofemoral (L1–L2): runs on the anterior surface of psoas, parallel to it, and divides near the inguinal
   ligament.
      ◦ Femoral branch: passes under the inguinal ligament into the femoral sheath and supplies the upper
        anterior thigh.
     ◦ Genital branch: enters the deep ring. It is motor to cremaster (the efferent limb of the cremasteric
       reflex) and sensory to the anterior scrotum, or to the mons and labium majus.
 • Lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh (L2–L3): sensory to the anterior and lateral thigh.
 • Obturator (L2–L4): arises behind psoas and runs to the obturator canal. It is motor to the thigh
   adductors.
 • Femoral (L2–L4): motor to the anterior thigh (knee extension) and to iliacus and pectineus (hip flexion).
   Sensory to the anteromedial lower limb.
 • Sciatic (L4–S3): most of the motor and sensory supply to the lower limb, including the posterior thigh and
   all leg and foot muscles.
