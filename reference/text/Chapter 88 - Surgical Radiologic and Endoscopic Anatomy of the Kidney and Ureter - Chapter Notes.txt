 Renal Morphology
   • Paired, bean-shaped retroperitoneal organs on the posterior abdominal wall, with a medial concavity
     and lateral convexity; each is roughly the size of a closed fist.
   • The left kidney is slightly larger than the right in length and hilar thickness (mean length about 11 cm).
     Within one kidney the upper pole is wider than the lower pole.
   • Weight 125–170 g in the adult male; 10–15 g less in the female.
   • Upper poles lie slightly medial and posterior to the lower poles; the right kidney lies slightly lower
     than the left because of the liver.

 Surface Anatomy and Relationships
 Kidney Orientation
   • Vertebral level: between the transverse processes of T12 and L3, either side of the vertebral column.
   • Longitudinal axes are oblique because the kidneys lie on the cone-shaped psoas muscles, so the upper
     poles are more medial and posterior than the lower poles. For percutaneous access, the lower pole
     therefore lies lateral and anterior to the upper pole.
   • Angled 30–50° behind the coronal plane: the hilum is rotated anteriorly on psoas, which places the
     lateral borders of both kidneys posteriorly.
   • Descends about 3 cm (one vertebral body) on inspiration and on moving from supine to erect.
     Positions quoted are for supine end-expiration.
   • Right kidney 1–2 cm lower than the left (liver): right from the top of L1 to the bottom of L3; left from
     T12 to L3.

   • The upper poles lie close to the diaphragm and pleura, so breaching the diaphragm while excising a
     large upper-pole mass risks pleural tear and pneumothorax.
   • The posterior pleural reflection reaches the 12th rib, whereas the lowest edge of the lung lies at the
     10th intercostal space (above the 11th rib).
   • Access above the 11th rib (10th intercostal space) carries increased pleural and lung injury; wherever
     possible use subcostal (below the 12th rib) or 11th intercostal space access.
 Liver, spleen and colon.
   • Puncture in mid or full inspiration increases the risk of splenic and hepatic injury, particularly with
     hepatomegaly or splenomegaly.
   • The ascending and descending colon are retroperitoneal and may lie posterolateral to the kidney
     (retrorenal colon), more often at the lower pole; retrorenal colon is considerably more frequent prone
     than supine — a hazard for prone percutaneous access.
 Concept — The kidney's attitude is dictated by the cone-shaped psoas on which it lies; every percutaneous
 hazard is a neighbour whose relation shifts with posture or respiration — pleura above the 11th rib, liver
 and spleen in inspiration, colon when prone.

 Kidney Coverings
   • From inside out: true (fibrous) capsule — smooth, tough, easily stripped in health → perinephric
     (perirenal) fat, extending through the renal hilum into the renal sinus → renal (Gerota) fascia,
     enclosing kidney and adrenal → paranephric fat, of variable thickness.
   • Gerota fascia is closed superiorly and laterally but open inferiorly. Superiorly it is continuous with the
     diaphragmatic fascia; medially it fuses with the contralateral side; inferomedially it continues along the
     ureter as periureteral fascia; inferiorly its anterior and posterior layers are only loosely attached.
       ◦ Closed above and laterally → barrier to tumour spread and container for perinephric collections.
       ◦ Open below → perinephric fluid can track into the pelvis without breaching the fascia.
   • Posterior layer strong and well defined; anterior layer delicate and adherent to peritoneum.
   • Fixation: collagen bundles bind Gerota fascia to the paranephric fat; kidney held by these bundles, the
     fascia and the fat.
 RETROPERITONEAL COMPARTMENTS DEFINED BY THE RENAL (GEROTA) FASCIA
  Compartment                                Contents
  Posterior pararenal space                  Fat only
  Perirenal (intermediate) space             Adrenal glands, kidneys, proximal ureter, perirenal fat
  Anterior pararenal space                   Ascending and descending colon, duodenum, pancreas; crosses the midline

   • Gerota fascia must be opened to reach kidney, adrenal or abdominal ureter.
   • Transperitoneal access: mobilise the colon along the white line of Toldt — the lateral reflection of
     posterior parietal peritoneum over the ascending and descending colon.
   • Right hilum: Kocher manoeuvre (mobilise second part of duodenum and head of pancreas). Left hilum:
     medial mobilisation of pancreatic tail, spleen and splenic vessels.
   • Each perirenal space is separate, so urinoma, haematoma or abscess from endourological
     extravasation rarely reaches the contralateral side.
 Concept — Gerota fascia is a cone closed above and open below: that one shape explains why it contains
 tumour and collections, why fluid descends into the pelvis, and why perirenal disease stays unilateral while
 the anterior pararenal space crosses the midline.

 Intrarenal Anatomy
 Gross and Microscopic Anatomy
   • Cortex: pale outer zone, about 1 cm thick. Medulla: darker inner zone of 8–18 conical renal pyramids.
     Both are distinguishable on imaging, and papillae can be inspected endoscopically.
   • Pyramid base at the corticomedullary junction; apex forms the papilla, each cupped by a minor calyx.
   • Columns of Bertin are cortex extending between pyramids; they carry the interlobar arteries from
     sinus to periphery, which narrow as they pass outward. Hence percutaneous access passes through a
     pyramid into a calyx, avoiding the columns and their larger vessels.
   • Lobe = pyramid plus its cortex; lobes are visible on the fetal surface and may persist for some time
     after birth.
   • Nephron is the functional unit: 0.4–1.2 million per adult kidney (about 2 million glomeruli for the two
     kidneys together).
   • Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) about 125 mL/min — the index of renal function.
   • Filtrate path: glomerulus → Bowman space → proximal convoluted tubule → thin and thick limbs of
     the loop of Henle → macula densa (beside its own glomerulus) → distal convoluted tubule → collecting
     tubules → ducts of Bellini → calyces → pelvis → ureter → bladder. About 99% of filtrate is reabsorbed;
     the remainder is urine.
   • Filtration barrier — three layers: endothelium, glomerular basement membrane, podocytes.
   • Tubules (proximal, distal, loop of Henle): single layer of cuboidal cells. Collecting ducts: cuboidal to
     columnar, more resistant to damage.
   • Calyces to urethra are lined by urothelium (transitional epithelium), from which urothelial (transitional
     cell) carcinoma can arise at any level.
 Figure 88.13 — microanatomy (Netter).
   • Zones: cortex (with subcapsular zone) over medulla; medulla divided into outer zone (outer and inner
     stripes) and inner zone.
   • Cortical corpuscles have short loops of Henle; juxtamedullary corpuscles have long loops descending
     into the inner zone.
   • Segments: proximal (neck, proximal convolution, thick descending limb) → thin segment → distal (thick
     ascending limb, macula densa, distal convolution). Papillary ducts open on the cribriform area of the
     papilla.
 Radiologic Anatomy of the Renal Parenchyma
   • Plain kidney-ureter-bladder (KUB) radiograph: renal shape, margins, size, position; radiopacities,
     calcification and lucencies. Psoas line is lost with retroperitoneal effusion.

   • Corticomedullary differentiation (CMD) matters because cortex and medulla have different functions
     and diseases. It is visible on ultrasonography and T1/T2 MRI; CT needs contrast because both
     compartments share one density.
   • CMD diminishes or disappears in chronic kidney disease (CKD) and acute kidney injury (AKI), without
     clear correlation with serum creatinine, and reappears on MRI as function recovers.

     renal volume correlates with GFR better than bipolar length.
 Congenital anomalies.
   • 20–30% of congenital anomalies in newborns involve the kidneys and ureters: anomalies of number,
     rotation, ascent and fusion.
   • Malrotation: renal pelvis appears to arise centrally rather than medially; calyces medial to the pelvis
     are the hallmark; calyces distorted with or without obstruction.
   • Ectopia: arrest or exaggeration of ascent, usually with malrotation. Ureteral length suits the position,
     but drainage is impaired → stasis, infection, stones. Blood supply is aberrant, from adjacent vessels.
   • Crossed fused ectopia: one kidney crosses and fuses with the other; the ectopic kidney's ureter
     crosses back and inserts normally.
   • Horseshoe kidney: lower poles fused by an isthmus (fibrous band or functioning parenchyma); lies low
     because ascent is arrested by the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA). Associated with ureteropelvic
     junction obstruction (UPJO), vascular anomalies, duplication, stones and urinary tract infection.
     Multiple renal arteries from aorta or iliacs are frequent in horseshoe and pelvic kidneys.

   KEY POINTS: THE KIDNEY AND ITS PARENCHYMA
    • Hilum, anterior to posterior: renal vein, renal artery, pelvis (ureter), posterior segmental artery —
      "VAUA".
    • Papilla → minor calyx; minor calyces join to form a major calyx; major calyces form the pelvis.

 Concept — Intrarenal vessels, not the collecting system, dictate the needle path: interlobar arteries run in
 the columns of Bertin, so the safe route is through pyramid and papilla. On imaging, every normal
 appearance is the cortex–medulla contrast, which disease erases and recovery restores.

 Renal Vasculature
   • Classic pedicle: one artery and one vein through the hilum. Arteries arise from the aorta at the L1–L2
     disc; the longer right renal artery passes behind the inferior vena cava (IVC). Branches supply adrenal,
     pelvis and proximal ureter; capsular and ureteric branches arise from the main artery.
   • Five segmental end arteries (posterior, apical, upper, middle, lower) with no significant anastomosis:
     occlusion causes segmental infarction, but each segment can be resected independently.
   • Anterior division supplies the anterior two-thirds via four segmental branches (apical, upper, middle,
     lower); posterior division supplies the posterior one-third.
   • Posterior segmental artery: first and most constant branch, leaving before the hilum and passing
     behind the pelvis; all others pass in front. If it passes anterior to the ureter, UPJO may result. The
     apical branch usually arises from the anterior division, occasionally from the posterior.
   • Arterial sequence: renal → segmental → lobar (sinus) → interlobar (in columns of Bertin, close to
     minor calyceal infundibula) → arcuate (base of pyramids, corticomedullary junction) → interlobular
     (radial) → afferent arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole → peritubular capillaries (cortex) or vasa
     recta (medulla).
 Arterial variation.
   • Variation in 25–40% of kidneys; supernumerary arteries are the commonest, up to five, especially on
     the left.
   • Early (prehilar) branching must be detected before donor nephrectomy.
   • Accessory renal arteries in 25–28%: from the aorta between T11 and L4, rarely from iliac arteries or
     superior mesenteric artery (SMA). They are the sole supply of their territory — usually lower pole,
     occasionally upper. They may contraindicate laparoscopic donor nephrectomy and bleed severely if
     cut at endopyelotomy for UPJO.
   • In about 5%, main and accessory right renal arteries pass anterior to the IVC.
   • Brödel line: longitudinal avascular plane between anterior and posterior segmental territories, just
     posterior to the lateral border; incision there loses less blood. Its position varies, so delineate it by
     preoperative angiography or intraoperative segmental methylene blue injection. Applications:
     posterior calyces along the Brödel line for percutaneous access; the incision of anatrophic
     nephrolithotomy (Boyce procedure).
 Venous drainage.
   • Veins parallel the arteries but, unlike them, anastomose freely: venous collars around minor calyceal
     infundibula, and a subcapsular stellate plexus linking interlobular with perinephric veins. Segmental
     vein occlusion therefore has little effect on outflow.
   • 3–5 segmental veins form the renal vein; renal veins lie anterior to the arteries and drain to the IVC.
     Renal vein anomalies are less common than arterial.

 Lymphatic Drainage of the Kidney
   • Two networks: superficial capsular and deep hilar. Lymphatics lie in periarterial connective tissue
     along interlobular and arcuate arteries → interlobar → hilar lymphatics, joined by capsular, perinephric,
     pelvic and upper ureteric vessels → nodes at the renal vein. Thereafter drainage differs by side.

 Innervation of the Kidney
   • The kidney functions without neural control — shown by functioning transplants.
   • Sympathetic preganglionic fibres T8–L1, mainly via the coeliac plexus, with lesser contributions from
     the greater splanchnic, intermesenteric and superior hypogastric plexuses. Postganglionic fibres reach
     the kidney through the plexus around the renal artery and follow arteries through cortex and outer
     medulla.
   • Parasympathetic fibres from the vagus travel with them.
   • Sympathetic → vasoconstriction; parasympathetic → vasodilatation.

 Pelvicalyceal System
 Anatomic Classification
   • Knowledge of collecting-system anatomy underpins radiological interpretation and every
     endourological procedure.
   • Calyces: upper pole usually 3 (less often 2); interpolar 3–4; lower pole 2–3. Number, size and shape
     vary with the papillae received (one to three per calyx); compound papillae are commonest at the
     poles.
   • Drainage: upper pole — single midline infundibulum; lower pole — single midline infundibulum or
     paired calyces; hilar region — anterior and posterior rows of paired calyces.
   • Configuration: true pelvis or divided (duplex) pelvis. A true pelvis receives calyces through elongated
     necks; it may be intrarenal (within the sinus) or extrarenal. The pelvis is pyramidal — base to
     parenchyma, apex to ureter — with a capacity of 3–10 mL.

   • Duplex pelvis: divided at the hilum into upper and lower parts and drains more calyces than normal;
     the lower part is shorter but larger and drains hilar and lower-pole calyces. There is no direct
     connection between upper and lower calyces, so a duplex pelvis should be suspected when upper- or
     lower-pole calyces cannot be reached from a given calyceal puncture. It is shown in the excretory
     phase of CT urography and at endoscopy, is easier to recognise at retrograde nephroureteroscopy, and
     can be demonstrated by retrograde pyelography.

 Radiologic Anatomy of the Collecting System
   • Pelvicalyceal anatomy is variable; no simple rule defines calyceal organisation.
   • CT urography (CTU) has replaced IVU; multidetector CT gives ≤1 mm collimation of the whole tract in a
     single breath-hold.
   • Magnetic resonance urography (MRU): static-fluid phase — ideal for the obstructed or dilated
     system; excretory phase — depends on renal function, improved by low-dose furosemide.
   • Congenital pelvicalyceal variants: about 4% of the population. The pelvis may be intrarenal, extrarenal
     or mixed.
   • The calyx is a cup around the papilla whose two side projections are the fornices. Incompletely divided
     calyces form compound calyces at the poles. Average 7–9 calyces per kidney (range 4–19 or more).
   • Duplication spectrum: bifid pelvis → incomplete or complete ureteral duplication (ureteropelvic
     duplication).

   • Obvious on endocasts, hard to see radiographically: on a pyelogram it is superimposed on a major

   • A stone in a perpendicular calyx is difficult for both extracorporeal shock-wave lithotripsy (ESWL) and

 Crossed Calices
   • 17.2%: midzone drained by crossed calyces (type A-II). IPC space shapes: lozenge (commonest), long
     and narrow, small and round.
   • The calyx draining into the inferior group lies ventrally in 87.5% — relevant when entering the pelvis

 Concept — Sampaio's classification asks one question — does the midzone drain independently or through
 the polar groups? — and the endourological traps it reveals (perpendicular calyces, the ventral limb of
 crossed calyces) are precisely what a two-dimensional pyelogram superimposes.

 URETERS
 Course, dimensions and segments.
   • Muscular retroperitoneal ducts continuing from the pelvis at the ureteropelvic junction (UPJ),
     crossing the pelvic brim at the common iliac bifurcation and running on the lateral pelvic wall to the
     bladder.
   • Adult: 22–30 cm long, 1.5–6 mm diameter. Neonate: 6.5–7.0 cm long.
   • Lies just lateral to the tips of the lumbar transverse processes. Surface marking (from behind): a line
     from a point 5 cm lateral to the L1 spinous process to the posterior superior iliac spine. The abdominal
     ureter is adherent to the retroperitoneum throughout.
   • Three physiological narrowings: UPJ, iliac vessel crossing, intramural ureter (ureterovesical junction,
     UVJ) — the sites of stone impaction.

   • Proximity to the uterine vessels causes ureteral injury in gynaecological surgery; in vaginal surgery the

 Intramural ureter and three-dimensional course.
   • Near the bladder the ureter is enveloped by the Waldeyer sheath, then pierces the wall obliquely.
     Intramural length 1.2–2.5 cm in adults; 0.5–0.8 cm in neonates.
   • Waldeyer bundles coalesce with the detrusor, so rising intravesical pressure (micturition) closes the
     tunnel and prevents reflux.
   • At ureteroscopy: approached retrogradely, the ureter runs anterolaterally along the pelvic wall, then
     angulates posteriorly at the pelvic brim. Following this course over a safety guidewire reduces
     perforation, especially with large impacted stones.
 Concept — Down its length the ureter passes beneath a succession of vessels — gonadal, then uterine or
 vas — and over the iliac bifurcation; injury clusters where it is fixed close to peritoneum and vessels (pelvic
 brim, cervix, vaginal fornix).

 Radiologic Anatomy of the Ureter
   • Excretory urography in expiration: inspiration displaces the kidney downward and may kink the ureter.
   • Course and symmetry vary: the ureter may lie lateral to the transverse processes or medial to the renal
     pedicle. A medially placed right ureter can be normal in young Black males. The right ureter may pass
     medially behind the vein at L3 before returning laterally.
   • The whole ureter is rarely seen on one IVU film because of peristalsis; a continuously opacified ureter
     suggests atony or obstruction.
   • Crossing vessels can mimic stricture, so a stricture is not diagnosed from a single film; it requires
     proximal dilatation.

   • Lateral view (now replaced by CT for displacement): the collecting system should not project anterior
     to the spine; the ureter stays behind the anterior vertebral margin down to L4, then lies anterior by

   • In elderly patients with atherosclerosis, the common iliac crossing may indent the ureter posteriorly at
     the pelvic brim, mimicking an extrinsic filling defect; it is distinguished from obstruction by no
     pelvicalyceal dilatation and no delay in emptying on prone or erect films.
   • Benign prostatic hyperplasia elevating the bladder floor curves the intramural ureter upward: "fish

   KEY POINTS: RADIOLOGIC ANATOMY OF THE URETER
    • Have the patient empty the bladder to evaluate the distal ureter properly.

 Concept — The normal ureter is intermittently opacified because it is peristaltic; continuity, a single
 narrowing or displacement is the abnormality, and each apparent lesion is tested against proximal
 dilatation and drainage before it is called obstruction.

 Nerve Supply of the Ureter
   • Autonomic supply from the coeliac, aorticorenal and mesenteric ganglia and the superior and inferior
     hypogastric (pelvic) plexuses.

   • Nerves play a minor role, if any, in ureteral motility: excised ureter keeps contracting, and denervating

 Concept — The ureter is myogenic: its nerves carry pain, not propulsion.

 Microscopic Anatomy of the Ureter
   • Three layers: mucosa, muscularis, adventitia.
   • Mucosa: urothelium of 4–6 cell layers (contracted); junctional complexes with keratin precursors make
     it waterproof; longitudinal folds give the empty ureter a stellate lumen. The lamina propria carries
     vessels and nerves.
   • Muscularis: two longitudinal layers separated by a middle circular layer, often indistinct (especially
     abdominally) and spiral on light microscopy. Distally, inner spirals are steep and outer horizontal —
     appearing as inner longitudinal and outer circular in section. It is continuous with the smooth muscle of
     the minor calyces, the pacemaker site.
   • Adventitia: collagen and elastic fibres, vessels and unmyelinated nerves; continuous with the renal
     capsule proximally; thickened distally into the Waldeyer sheath.
   • The normal UPJ is histologically identical to the pelvis. In UPJO: increased longitudinal muscle,
     increased collagen around muscle fibres and attenuated muscle bundles → functional obstruction.

 Endoscopic Anatomy of the Pelvicalyceal System and Ureter
 Trigone and ureteric orifices.
   • Trigone: raised smooth triangle, apex at the bladder neck, base the interureteric ridge (Mercier bar) —
     more prominent in males — with orifices symmetrical 1–2 cm from the midline. Both orifices are
     rarely seen in a single endoscopic view.
   • The trigone is the most vascular part of the bladder, formed by the ureteric longitudinal muscle
     extending over the detrusor; hence it looks more deeply coloured.
   • Orifice: volcano, horseshoe, or slit that needs meticulous search; pushed laterally as the bladder fills;
     ringed by prominent mucosal vessels.

 Ureter.
   • Intramural ureter is the narrowest segment — mean 3–4 mm; from the orifice it runs about 1.5 cm:
     0.5 cm posterolaterally, then about 1 cm obliquely through the detrusor hiatus. It may need dilatation
     before ureteroscopy.
   • Pelvic brim and UPJ narrowings look stenotic and non-distensible but are wider than the intramural
     segment and pass with irrigation pressure. Iliac pulsation is visible at the brim, where the ureter
     angulates posteriorly.
   • Proximal ureter runs straight up on psoas, stellate when undistended. The UPJ is recognised by its
     opening and closing and merges into the wider, dependent pelvis.
   • Beyond the relatively fixed UPJ, respiratory movement of the kidney (which lies on the diaphragm) is
     visible → reduce tidal volume to limit excursion.
   • Wait for the peristaltic wave to relax before advancing the ureteroscope, to avoid mucosal trauma.
 Pelvis, calyces and papillae.
   • The UPJ is the apex of the funnel-shaped pelvis. Extrarenal pelvis: larger, with longer major calyceal
     infundibula than an intrarenal pelvis.
   • Flexible ureteroscopic sequence: ostia of major calyces (circular openings separated by carinae) → long
     tubular infundibulum → minor calyces.
   • Entering the lower-pole infundibulum requires about 140° deflection (range 104–175°) at the
     ureteroinfundibular angle.
   • Papillae: protruding discs ringed by fornices, paler than the surrounding pink, friable epithelium. A
     circular muscle at the papillary base helps expel urine jets. The ducts of Bellini are minute openings
     that dilate with distal obstruction.
 Infundibulotomy.
   • Segmental and interlobar arteries and major venous tributaries run on the anterior and posterior
     surfaces of major infundibula and minor calyceal necks, so infundibulotomy is made in the superior or
     inferior quadrant, free of major vessels.
   • If several incisions are needed: superior → inferior → between superior and posterior → between
     inferior and posterior → posterior; avoid anterior incisions (large intrarenal vessels, severe bleeding).
 Concept — Intrarenal vessels hug the anterior and posterior faces of the infundibula: the same fact that
 makes the fornix the safe puncture point makes the superior and inferior quadrants the safe incision and
 the anterior face the forbidden one.
