Chapter 48 — Lower Urinary Tract Reconstruction in
Children
John C. Thomas and Douglass B. Clayton

 • Functions a reconstructed lower tract must achieve: protect renal function → avoid urinary tract infection
   (UTI) → eventually provide urinary continence. Operative intervention is minimised, but these functions
   are non-negotiable.
 • Thorough individual evaluation before reconstruction: each child's pathophysiology is understood so that
   techniques are applied thoughtfully, optimising results and minimising morbidity.
 • Scope: augmentation cystoplasty (intestinal segments used more extensively here than anywhere else in
   urology), procedures that raise bladder neck and external sphincter resistance, and catheterisable
   channels.
 • Clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC) — Lapides et al. (1972, 1976), built on Guttmann and Frankel
   (1966) — is the single most important contribution to lower tract reconstruction. Many children do not
   void adequately after reconstruction, so a reliable, easy means of catheterisation is often essential.
 • Primary indication: correction of bladder "hostility" unresponsive to medical management.
 • Spina bifida accounts for many patients; results in the chapter therefore focus on the neurogenic
   population.
 • Most important determinant of outcome: commitment of the patient and family to good care — difficult
   to gauge, never to be underestimated.

"FUNCTIONAL" URINARY TRACT
 • Two dynamic phases, stated as a pair: passive storage — reservoir holding urine at low pressure without
   leakage; active voiding — contraction with efficient elimination.

Dysfunction
Upper Urinary Tract
 • Understand the dynamics of the entire urinary tract before any major reconstruction.
 • Hydronephrosis → exclude upper tract obstruction; obstruction may itself be secondary to severe, long-
   standing hostility (poor compliance and poor emptying).
 • Nuclear renography with a urethral catheter in the bladder rules out primary upper tract obstruction.
 • Obstruction, if present, is corrected at the time of bladder and sphincter reconstruction.
 • Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) with hostility may be primary or secondary and is hard to distinguish; new-
   onset reflux absent on earlier evaluation is likely secondary; in neurogenic children reflux is often
   secondary.
 • Secondary reflux often resolves with augmentation alone once the bladder is adequately managed;
   whether reflux matters at all in a large, compliant bladder is debated.
 • Bacteria ascend with or without reflux after reconstruction; some continent diversions without any
   antireflux mechanism show no increase in pyelonephritis when compliance is adequate.
 • Authors' practice: expect most secondary reflux to resolve, but correct high-grade reflux at
   reconstruction.
 • Upper tract dysfunction presents as hydronephrosis, pyelonephritis or impaired renal function; all are
   addressed before and at reconstruction.

Bladder Dysfunction
 • Assess each component of passive and active function independently.
 • Pathogenic threshold: filling pressure >40 cm H2O reached chronically (McGuire et al., 1981) → impaired
   ureteral drainage → pyelocalyceal change, hydroureteronephrosis, falling glomerular filtration rate (GFR).
   Persistent elevation also causes acquired VUR.
 • Pharmacotherapy plus intermittent catheterisation lowers filling pressure, particularly with overactive
   detrusor contractions, and is effective in neurogenic children.
 • Compliance unresponsive to medical management → augmentation cystoplasty.
 • After reconstruction, efficient detrusor contraction and emptying become less likely → CIC is taught and
   accepted preoperatively. CIC is what permits aggressive correction of storage; spontaneous voiding is a
   goal, not a requirement.
 • Continence requires outflow resistance (bladder neck + external sphincter) greater than bladder
   pressure throughout daily activity; a deficient outlet leaks.
 • α-adrenergic agents can raise outflow resistance, but operative reconstruction is more commonly
   required.
 • Filling-phase incontinence from a poor outlet: evaluate the detrusor as well. Once outlet resistance is
   raised operatively, adverse detrusor behaviour may be unmasked — high-pressure storage or uninhibited
   contractions not previously documented → provocative urodynamics with bladder neck occlusion before
   any bladder neck reconstruction.
 • Normal synergic voiding: bladder neck descends, relaxes and opens → external sphincter relaxes →
   detrusor contracts → low-pressure voiding.
 • Dyssynergia (uncoordinated bladder neck, sphincter and detrusor) → chronic high-pressure voiding that
   damages bladder and upper tracts; a fixed, fibrotic external sphincter behaves the same way. Initial
   treatment: pharmacotherapy and CIC to bypass the abnormal voiding mechanics.
Other Considerations
 • Assess renal function in every patient, particularly with hydronephrosis or severe scarring.
 • Urinary solutes, particularly chloride, are absorbed by small- and large-bowel mucosa.
 • Normal kidneys handle the reabsorbed chloride and acid load; reduced renal function → significant
   metabolic acidosis. Pre-existing acidosis invariably worsens when urine is stored in small or large bowel.
 • Concentrating ability is the first renal function lost after obstruction or infection → very large urine
   volumes; the reconstructed capacity must hold output for 3–4 hours.
 • Oliguria (renal failure) → greater collection and inspissation of mucus in the augmented bladder.
 • Hepatic failure: ammonia reabsorption is dangerous; drugs excreted in urine may be reabsorbed → liver
   function tests and arterial blood gases where appropriate.
 • Take a careful bowel history: short gut is a concern after cloacal exstrophy, bowel resection or significant
   radiation; chronic diarrhoea or faecal incontinence → caution before using the ileocaecal valve.
 • Commitment: incontinence can protect some patients from infection and upper tract deterioration;
   effective storage without regular emptying is dangerous. Everyone must understand the responsibility
   that continence brings.
 • Timing: early when upper tracts or renal function are threatened (high outflow resistance with poor
   compliance) — augmentation may slow decline even with established insufficiency, but prevention is
   preferable; more commonly later, for continence, when incontinence or a temporary stoma becomes
   socially unacceptable (varies by family).

 • Identify all needs and address them in one operation where possible (not always feasible when
   intervening for infection or hydronephrosis): urodynamics decide the need for an outlet procedure;
   preoperative CIC is mandatory — it demonstrates commitment and shows whether a continent
   abdominal wall stoma is needed for reliability and independence; identify neurogenic children who would
   benefit from an antegrade colonic enema for constipation. Sequential procedures add morbidity and
   technical difficulty.
Concept — every reconstructive decision is a storage–emptying trade: raising capacity or outlet resistance
converts a leaking but self-venting system into one that depends entirely on reliable emptying, which is why
CIC and commitment are prerequisites rather than afterthoughts.

PATIENT EVALUATION
 • Upper tract imaging in every patient before reconstruction; most already have surveillance
   ultrasonography.
 • Hydronephrosis → assess for obstruction and reflux: nuclear renography with a bladder catheter for
   primary obstruction; voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) or video urodynamics for reflux.
 • With hydronephrosis: baseline serum electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine; raised
   creatinine or significant hydronephrosis → nephrology evaluation and 24-hour urine for creatinine
   clearance and volume.
 • Prior diversion: permanent intestinal diversion in children is now largely confined to cystectomy for
   cancer; most diversions are temporary. Key to undiversion: understand the original pathology that led to
   diversion.

Urodynamics
Bladder Dynamics: Capacity and Compliance
 • Urodynamic testing is essential; reproducible in children only with meticulous technique, and interrater
   reliability of urodynamic study (UDS) interpretation is variable even across experienced centres.
 • Urologic Management to Preserve Initial Renal function (UMPIRE) protocol: iterative protocol for best
   practice in paediatric neurogenic bladder; its standardisation efforts improved interpretive consensus.
 • Artefact sources:
     ◦ Transurethral catheter size alters leak point pressure, voiding pressure and emptying, especially in
       infants and young males; a suprapubic catheter avoids this but is usually impractical.
     ◦ Medium: body-temperature saline or iodinated contrast gives reproducible results.
     ◦ Filling rate can dramatically change end filling pressure and therefore compliance → fill at ≤10% of
       predicted capacity per minute.
 • Interpretation for planning:
     ◦ Cystometric capacity + fluoroscopic bladder appearance → length of bowel segment.
     ◦ Detrusor leak point pressure + end filling pressure + fluoroscopic bladder neck → need for a bladder
       neck procedure.
     ◦ VUR + history of UTI → ureteral reimplantation at reconstruction.
Sphincter Dynamics: Outflow Resistance
 • Bladder neck and external sphincter act synergically, but only one is required for continence.
 • Neurogenic dysfunction → diminished storage resistance and/or dyssynergic voiding.

 • Electromyography (EMG) of the external sphincter is required to assess coordination and dyssynergia:
   perineal surface electrodes, abdominal sensors, anal plugs, vaginal monitors, wire and concentric needle
   electrodes.
 • Concentric needle or dual wire electrodes through a 25-gauge needle are more accurate in neurogenic
   children; UMPIRE data question the reliability of surface electrodes; few centres offer needle studies; the
   role of sphincter analysis remains unclear.
Bladder Emptying
 • Parameters: synergic sphincter relaxation on EMG, flow rate, postvoid residual.
 • Neurologically intact good emptiers are much more likely to keep emptying after reconstruction than
   neurogenic patients.
 • No test ensures spontaneous voiding after augmentation → every patient must be prepared for CIC.
 • Examine the native urethra for ease of catheterisation; CIC is learnt and practised preoperatively until
   patient, family and surgeon are confident; physical and psychosocial limitations affect independent care.
 • Failure to catheterise reliably → upper tract deterioration, UTI or bladder perforation — despite a
   technically perfect operation.
 • Given the choice, patients overwhelmingly prefer an abdominal wall stoma to the native urethra.
Concept — urodynamics predicts storage but not emptying: capacity, compliance and leak pressures can be
measured and planned for, whereas postoperative voiding cannot, so every plan assumes CIC.

PATIENT PREPARATION
 • Optimise general status: nutrition and hydration corrected; cardiac and pulmonary comorbidity well
   managed.

Preoperative Considerations
 • Bowel preparation has been challenged by enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, adopted
   only recently in neurogenic lower tract surgery; paediatric surgical evidence shows omitting bowel
   preparation does not increase complications or surgical site infection.
 • Hydration is critical (long operations, large fluid shifts) — intravenous fluids on the morning of surgery if
   needed.
 • Oral antibiotic bowel preparation: once dogma, now personal preference.
 • ERAS elements: clear-liquid carbohydrate loading up to 2 hours preoperatively; multi-day clear diet and
   formal mechanical preparation abandoned; ERAS associated with lower 90-day complications and earlier
   bowel function and diet after augmentation.
 • Barriers to ERAS standardisation: clinician unfamiliarity, resources and administrative support, lack of
   consensus.
 • Urine culture several days preoperatively; positive → treat (oral or intravesical antibiotics) and document
   a sterile repeat culture.
 • Ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt (common in spina bifida): with sterile urine and good bowel preparation,
   shunt infection risk should be very low; however, a Pediatric Health Information System (PHIS) database
   analysis showed increased shunt failure after augmentation, cause unclear and likely multifactorial.

 KEY POINTS: EVALUATION AND PREPARATION
   • Evaluate renal function, upper tract obstruction and VUR if hydronephrosis is present, although most
     such changes are secondary to bladder dysfunction.

   • Careful urodynamic evaluation is critical to understand every problem contributing to dysfunction.
   • The patient will likely require CIC after reconstruction and must be capable of performing it reliably.

   • ERAS protocols show promise but need multicentre prospective studies to become standard practice.

ANTIREFLUX
 • Reimplantation into the native bladder, dilated ureter or not, is standard, with good long-term success
   and rare complications — preferable and usually possible during reconstruction.
 • Chronically scarred, short ureters after diversion need special attention; in Hendren's undiversion
   experience most complications arose from those ureters.
Transureteroureterostomy (Figure 48.1, Hendren)
 • Donor ureter crosses the retroperitoneum without tension, sometimes above the inferior mesenteric
   artery; avoid angulation beneath the inferior mesenteric vessels; anastomosis with interrupted sutures,
   knots outside.

Psoas Hitch
 • Purpose: precise control of reimplant length and direction; prevents ureteral angulation with bladder
   filling — particularly obstructive with a dilated, scarred ureter.
 • Nonabsorbable monofilament sutures to psoas muscle and fascia, never entering the bladder lumen —
   otherwise stones form.
 • Broad, shallow purchases hold long term; avoid trapping the femoral and genitofemoral nerves; do not
   tie so tightly that sutures cut through bladder or psoas.
 • Divide the contralateral bladder pedicle when extra mobility and hitch length are needed.
 • Order: Hendren and Hensle (1980) reimplant before hitching; do whichever order allows each step
   precisely with good visualisation. Full technique in Chapter 124.
Technique (Figure 48.2)
Traction sutures; new hiatus as high as possible → two retractors stretch the back wall taut → incise mucosa
and lay back flaps (in a good bladder, a submucosal tunnel without flaps) → ureter widely mobilised with
surrounding tissue, often with gonadal vessels → long-tunnel reimplant (8–10 cm) when the ureter is
abnormal or a tapered bowel segment is implanted → two fingers tent the bladder to the psoas, as high as
possible and lateral to the iliac vessels → 3–5 sutures through bladder muscle, not mucosa, big deep bites
of psoas including tendon, avoiding nerves → tie only after all are placed; surgeon holds bladder against
psoas while assistant ties → hitch lies just lateral to, and immobilises, the new hiatus; bladder elongated and
asymmetric, allowing a very long tunnel.

Antireflux With Intestinal Segments
 • The need to reimplant into bowel may determine the segment chosen for augmentation or replacement.
 • Colon: from ureterosigmoidostomy and colon conduit experience — flap valve by tunnelling the ureter
   beneath a taenia; principles: direct mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis + submucosal tunnel of adequate
   length; durable since the 1950s (Nesbit, 1949; Goodwin et al., 1953; Leadbetter and Clarke, 1954), based
   on Coffey (1911).

 KEY POINTS: ANTIREFLUX
   • Ureteral reimplantation into native bladder is preferable when necessary; the initial bladder incision
     may facilitate it.
   • An antireflux mechanism can be created with any bowel segment but is most challenging with ileum.


Concept — antireflux is a flap-valve problem: any tissue with a supported submucosa — bladder, colon under
a taenia, stomach — accepts a tunnel of adequate length for the ureteral width, whereas ileum cannot and
needs a nipple.

BLADDER NECK RECONSTRUCTION
 • Reliable outflow resistance is one of the greatest technical challenges of bladder reconstruction.
 • Cause neurogenic or anatomic: exstrophy, bilateral ectopic ureters, ureteroceles.
 • Many techniques exist, so no single option is best; thorough knowledge of the patient's physiology is
   required.
 • Technique selection depends on whether a sustained contraction can empty the bladder and whether
   hyperreflexic contractions are absent.
 • Every outlet procedure raises resistance at the expense of detrusor contractility.
 • Raising outlet resistance can create a noncompliant, hostile detrusor for unclear reasons; provocative
   cystometry uncovers only some patients at risk → close postoperative observation is mandatory.
 • Options: flap valve mechanisms, artificial or autologous bulking agents, slings, artificial urinary sphincter
   (AUS) — individualised to pathology, needs, goals and desire for independence.
 • Many repairs prohibit spontaneous voiding; neurogenic children needing augmentation plus an outlet
   procedure need CIC; other diagnoses without augmentation may void.
 • Results derive mostly from spinal dysraphism; every technique has a learning curve requiring forthright
   reporting.
 • Evidence is limited: no consistent definition of "success" or "continence", mixing of patients with and
   without augmentation, small mixed populations.

Young-Dees-Leadbetter Repair
 • One of the most recognised outlet procedures; remains a primary consideration for the exstrophic
   bladder neck.
Technique
Young (1919): excise part of the bladder neck and tighten it over a silver probe → Dees (1949): extend the
excision through the trigone → Leadbetter (1964): elevate the ureters to a cephalad position on the bladder
floor → tubularised trigone and lengthened urethra. Full description in Chapter 43.
Results
 • Minimal success in neurogenic dysfunction, attributed to poor tone and activity of the wrapped
   neurogenic muscle; many early patients were not augmented.
 • Contrast with exstrophy: neurogenic patients mostly need augmentation and CIC, and continence often
   needed a second operation.
 • Largely fallen out of favour in neurogenic dysfunction.
 • Silicone sheath (Mitchell and Rink, 1983): external support and coaptation, plus a plane for a future AUS
   cuff → thick Silastic sheaths eroded into bladder or urethra; thinner sheath with interposed omentum:
   less erosion, better continence, easier later cuff.
 • Largest neurogenic series: continence achievable, but at the cost of near-universal augmentation and
   multiple procedures; sheathed patients initially continent, then erosion; mirrored by other series.

Fascial Sling
 • Mechanism: coaptation and elevation of the bladder neck, raising resistance above resting pressure and
   the pressure of stress or Valsalva.
 • The bladder neck is fixed: a strong contraction may produce flow but rarely adequate emptying in
   anatomic or neurologic disease → prepare for CIC.
 • Sling resistance is overcome by overactivity or poor compliance → simultaneous augmentation is
   reported in most children who become continent.

 • Snodgrass exception: good results without augmentation, but children with sustained pressures >40 cm
   H2O on anticholinergics developed reflux or hydronephrosis; long-term follow-up of the same cohort
   showed frequent further continence procedures, augmentation, upper tract change and chronic kidney
   disease (CKD) — a hostile bladder after raising outlet resistance is a major risk.
 • Isolated outlet procedures in neurogenic patients: a substantial proportion progress to augmentation or
   onabotulinumtoxinA injection, most within 2 years and within 5 years → surveillance for a minimum of 5
   years.
 • Expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (Gore-Tex): early continence not maintained; biodegradable scaffolds:
   good early results.
Technique
Clear fat over the bladder neck and lateral endopelvic fascia → incise endopelvic fascia for about 2 cm →
locate the bladder neck–urethral junction by gentle traction lodging the catheter balloon → blunt plane
between posterior bladder neck and vagina (female) or rectum (male); often easier from the cul-de-sac,
behind bladder and ureters from above → obscured landmarks (secondary repair): open the bladder to avoid
entering urethra or posterior structures; a transvaginal approach avoids both.
 • Fascia: based on McGuire and Lytton (1978); rectus fascia 1 cm wide, harvested vertically or horizontally
   per incision; other sites need a second incision; cadaveric tissue or biodegradable scaffold alternatives;
   secured to anterior rectus fascia on each side; autologous fascia combines compressive wrap with
   suspension.
 • Excess tightness matters less in neurogenic patients because most are managed by CIC.
 • Laparoscopic and robotic placement: robotic operative time longer, but complications, stay and additional
   procedures similar; magnification aids deep pelvic access.
Results
 • Better and more extensive results in females; some recent success in males; long-term success varies
   widely.
 • Circumferential wrap may distribute compression over more bladder neck and posterior urethra, ±
   suspension; variation is too great to credit any modification.
 • Concomitant enterocystoplasty was the only predictor of success — not age, sex, diagnosis, preoperative
   urodynamics or technique.
 • Erosion is rare with fascia (unlike Silastic); revision uncommon; does not preclude later AUS.
 • Reasonable indication: marginally competent bladder neck with deficient external sphincter in a child
   already undergoing augmentation and already on CIC.

Bladder Neck Injection
 • Minimally invasive option for select sphincteric and bladder neck deficiency.
 • Polytetrafluoroethylene (Vorstman et al., 1985) abandoned — regional and distant particle migration.
 • Current agents: polydimethylsiloxane — solid silicone particles in a hydrogel carrier; large particle size
   limits migration; dextranomer/hyaluronic acid — microspheres in hyaluronic acid, favoured in
   contemporary reports.
Technique
 • Retrograde, antegrade or combined endoscopic approach; subepithelial placement ideal — displaces
   epithelium into the lumen; prior bladder neck surgery and scarring make it difficult; circumferential
   injection achieves coaptation.

Results
 • Durability and efficacy lower than formal repairs — the trade-off for minimal invasiveness; worthwhile
   after prior bladder neck surgery or to avoid reconstructive morbidity.
 • Success highly variable (technique, material, definition, pathology, prior repair); long-term success
   modest; continence deteriorates through about 18 months, then stabilises.
 • Antegrade versus retrograde: no significant difference; few become dry; improvement trends higher with
   antegrade.
 • Inferior to fascial sling in augmented intrinsic sphincter deficiency at long follow-up.
 • Injection to rescue a marginal formal repair: no evidence of lasting value or of benefit from repeat
   treatment.
 • Limited role, reserved for a select group whose defining criteria remain unknown.

Artificial Urinary Sphincter
 • Prompt continence while preserving spontaneous voiding. Introduced by Scott et al. (1974).
 • AS800: seamless pressurised balloon reservoir, non-kink tubing, improved cuff for bladder neck and
   proximal urethral coaptation; pump lock allows deactivation and activation without surgery. Alternatives
   have been explored.
Technique
 • Cuff at the bladder neck in all females and prepubertal males; also most effective in pubertal and adult
   neurogenic males. Bulbar urethra is an alternative only in males with mature spongiosum.
 • Children do not outgrow a bladder neck cuff through puberty; routine replacement unnecessary.
 • Cuff around a bowel segment is feasible but more prone to erosion → interpose omentum.
 • Plane as for a fascial sling; cuff snug, not tight.
 • Sterility paramount: preoperative antibiotics and confirmed sterile urine — which then permit opening
   the bladder to aid dissection and placement.
 • Leave deactivated with cuff deflated after placement → pseudocapsule forms → erosion falls; activation
   delayed about 6 weeks. A noncycled AUS occasionally provides enough resistance on its own.
Results
 • Continence must be weighed against cost: mechanical malfunction needing reoperation, and catastrophic
   infection or erosion.
 • Device refinements have dramatically reduced secondary procedures; 10–15-year and longer data show
   high continence with most sphincters functioning; age at implantation does not affect continence.
 • Reference long-term series (Indiana): high overall continence over a decade; malfunction markedly lower
   with AS800 than earlier models; erosion similar across models.
 • CIC still needed in about three-quarters of neurogenic children — and is performed successfully through
   the cuff.
 • Infection without erosion → remove, replace later. Minimise infection: sterile urine, meticulous skin
   preparation, bowel preparation, perioperative parenteral antibiotics, copious antibiotic irrigation.
 • Erosion virtually eliminated when the cuff is the primary bladder neck procedure.
 • Upper tract change and hydronephrosis after AUS, occasionally renal insufficiency: bladder neck
   occlusion unmasks or induces detrusor hostility (falling compliance, rising overactivity); preoperative
   urodynamics identify only some → anticholinergics for hyperreflexia, augmentation for poor compliance;
   close observation always.

 • Timing with augmentation: early data showed far more infection with simultaneous than staged
   procedures; later series showed low infection with simultaneous placement; long-term data show no

   reconstruction.
 • Ultimate benefit: high continence with potential spontaneous voiding. When CIC and augmentation are

Urethral Lengthening
 • Young (1919): excision of anterior bladder neck plus narrowing of the posterior remnant — failed because
   the tubularised segment was unsupported within the bladder; Dees and Leadbetter maximised muscle
   tone and extended the tube through the trigone.
 • Tanagho (1981): cephalad-based anterior detrusor tube, circular fibres acting as sphincter — he cautioned
   against it in the neurogenic population.
 • Tube breakdown and poor results led to the flap-valve concept: Kropp and Angwafo (1986) — anterior
   detrusor tube kept in continuity with the urethra, tubularised and implanted into a submucosal trigonal
   tunnel; effective, but difficult catheterisation is a common, significant problem.
Technique
Foley catheter, bladder filled to capacity → midline or low transverse incision → identify bladder neck by
gentle catheter traction → isolate a 6 × 2 cm rectangular anterior flap based on bladder neck and urethra,
stay sutures, mobilise in continuity with proximal urethra → divide detrusor at the bladder neck (separating
bladder from urethra), or leave muscle intact at 5 and 7 o'clock → expose anterior vaginal wall (female) or
seminal vesicles (male) → tubularise posteriorly around the catheter with continuous absorbable suture,
distal end interrupted so excess can be trimmed → create a capacious submucosal trigonal tunnel between
the orifices (Figure 48.3) → pull the tube through straight, without curve or deviation → lateral anchoring
sutures at the bladder neck eliminate dead space → secure to trigone floor with interrupted absorbable
sutures → close lateral wings incorporating the tube's adventitia for 2–3 cm anteriorly (watertight), often to
the augmentation → the neourethra must reach the true bladder lumen, where pressure makes it a flap
valve.
 • Not all ureters need cephalad reimplantation; in a single-institution comparison, reimplantation was
   needed less often with Kropp than with Salle.
 • Belman and Kaplan (1989) simplification: lateral and posterior muscle not incised, bladder and urethra
   not separated; flap tubularised over an 8 French (Fr) catheter and laid in an incised epithelial trough
   rather than a tunnel; edges secured to the tube; suture line posterior against trigone; remainder covered
   by augmentation.
 • Whatever the modification, urethral catheterisation may remain problematic → prepare for a
   catheterisable abdominal channel.
 • Vesicoscopic lengthening: U-incision lengthening onto the dome, sparing the orifices and usable after
   failed repairs — no long-term benefit.
Results
 • High continence but difficult catheterisation, particularly in males — most catheterised via an abdominal
   stoma. New VUR from lateral ureteric retraction → leave the posterior bladder wall open and flat when
   receiving the augmentation. Kropp reported fewer catheterisation problems and little new reflux.

   reliably. Snodgrass's modification deliberately gave a shorter intravesical tunnel, allowing leakage with

Pippi Salle Procedure
 • Aim: keep the benefits of Kropp while reducing catheterisation problems — an anterior bladder onlay
   flap; the posterior neourethral wall is intact, theoretically less catheter hang-up. Later modifications
   improved flap viability, reduced fistula and extended indications beyond neurogenic bladder.
Technique
Anterior full-thickness bladder wall flap about 1 cm wide × 4–5 cm long mobilised to the bladder neck, 0.1
cm of epithelial edges excised to avoid overlapping suture lines → two parallel mucosal incisions down to
muscle on the trigone from the bladder neck, creating a midline strip → flap sutured to strip (absorbable) to
form the lengthened urethra → muscle beside the strip incised superficially and approximated to flap
muscle as a second layer to prevent fistula (Figure 48.4) → lateral trigonal mucosa mobilised and closed over
the neourethra → distally, bladder neck muscle wrapped tightly around the urethra, incorporating part of
the flap for a watertight seal → proximally, neourethra extends well into the lumen → remaining bladder left
open if augmenting.
Results
 • Early complications: persistent incontinence, urethrovesical fistula at the flap base, partial necrosis of the
   intravesical neourethra → widen the flap base and trim epithelial edges generously.
 • Secondary Salle after prior bladder surgery: lateralise the flap away from the old midline suture line.
 • Catheterisation difficulty less than Kropp and rarely persists; continence lower than Kropp in most

   augmentation if not augmented at the bladder neck procedure; high rate of secondary surgery unrelated
   to the bladder neck in both.

   • Any repair other than AUS, particularly combined with cystoplasty, is likely to make spontaneous

Concept — outlet resistance and detrusor safety are inversely coupled: every increment of continence
gained at the bladder neck is paid for in lost voiding, lost pop-off and risk of an unmasked hostile bladder,
which is why neurogenic outlet repairs succeed mainly with augmentation and all demand years of
surveillance.

BLADDER RECONSTRUCTION
Cutaneous Vesicostomy
 • "Vents" the bladder, sparing the upper tracts from high intravesical pressure.
 • Indications: worsening upper tract dilatation, recurrent febrile UTI with high-grade reflux, high-pressure
   bladder despite maximal medical therapy; temporary or permanent.
 • Typically in young patients; a safe bridge to definitive reconstruction.
 • Disadvantages: stenosis, prolapse, incontinence — harder to manage as the child grows.

Management of the Native Bladder
 • Formerly: supratrigonal excision of the "diseased" bladder leaving a small cuff (Figure 48.6). Now:
   preserve the native bladder but open it widely — a narrow-mouthed anastomosis makes the segment
   behave as a diverticulum (Figure 48.5).
 • Sagittal bivalving incision: from several centimetres cephalad to the bladder neck anteriorly to just above
   the trigone posteriorly — easier anastomosis, native bladder adds capacity; a stellate opening (second,
   transverse incision into both halves) gives greater circumference.
 • Penile or perineal pain in sensate males after preservation has occasionally required secondary bladder
   excision — not frequent enough to justify routine excision.

Management of Intestinal Segments
 • Detubularise on the antimesenteric border and reconfigure into a spherical shape: maximal volume per
   surface area, blunted bowel contractions, improved capacity and compliance.
 • Intact tubular segments generate contractions of 60–100 cm H2O; detubularisation markedly lowers peak
   contractile pressure in both right colon and ileum, more so in ileum; a shorter segment achieves the
   same capacity.
 • Detubularisation and reconfiguration should always be performed.
 • Segment length: mathematical models exist but are cumbersome; 20–40 cm of ileum or about 20 cm of
   colon typically; less bowel for a moderate-volume bladder with high pressure from uninhibited
   contractions than for a tiny one.
 • Err on the side of too large rather than too small; allow for urine output — damaged upper tracts may
   produce huge volumes.

Ileocecal Valve

 • Lemoine (1912) first reported; still used.

Postoperative Management
Early Management
 • Similar regardless of segment.
 • Nasogastric decompression historically routine; may be unnecessary after ileocystoplasty → surgeon
   preference.
 • Fluid and electrolytes: third-space losses may be significant.
 • Continuous suprapubic drainage; mucus may occlude it → irrigate at least three times daily and
   whenever drainage slows.
 • Extravesical drains removed after several days if no urine; earlier with a VP shunt.
 • Static cystogram at about 3 weeks; no leak → clamp the suprapubic tube, remove after a few days of
   successful channel catheterisation.
 • CIC every 2–3 hours by day and once or twice at night initially → interval lengthened over weeks, never
   beyond 4–5 hours by day.
 • Patients without neurologic impairment may later attempt voiding; all check residuals and continue CIC if
   significant.
Late Management
 • Protocols are not well defined.
 • Renal ultrasound at 3 months, 6 months and 1 year; some centres perform urodynamics within 6
   months.
 • Electrolytes, BUN, creatinine and urine culture at 3 and 6 months, then yearly or when symptomatic;
   ultrasound and serum chemistry yearly thereafter.
 • Not every positive culture on CIC needs treatment; treat symptomatic cystitis and urea-splitting
   organisms.
 • Cystoscopic surveillance: no consensus; possibly higher yield in higher-risk groups — valve patients
   immunosuppressed after transplantation, exstrophy (inherent adenocarcinoma risk), gastrocystoplasty;
   not shown to be cost-effective; the Mayo Clinic group considers it unnecessary.

Results and Complications of Augmentation Cystoplasty
 • Judge augmentation two ways: (1) cost to the gastrointestinal tract of removing a segment — more than
   rare gastrointestinal problems would be prohibitive, however good the urinary result; (2) effect on the
   bladder — the primary goal is a compliant reservoir, so the main outcome measures are storage
   pressure and capacity; everything else is a complication arising because bowel is not a perfect
   physiologic substitute for bladder.
 • Further surgery is needed in up to one-third; a modern cohort puts the 10-year risk of additional surgery
   near one-half.
Gastrointestinal Effects
 • Bowel obstruction is uncommon, at a rate equivalent to conduit or continent diversion; prevented by
   delicate handling, closure of mesenteric windows and elimination of internal hernia sites; segment
   differences inconsistent, probably not significant.
 • Chronic diarrhoea after augmentation alone is rare; the ileal lengths used are rarely problematic; colon
   rarely changes bowel function.
 • Removing the ileocaecal valve is most likely to cause diarrhoea: some neurogenic children depend on
   constipation for faecal continence; loss of the valve shortens transit time and allows bacterial backflow

Bladder Compliance After Augmentation
 • Detubularisation and reconfiguration are the defining early lesson.
 • Some experienced surgeons consider ileum superior for compliance (uncontrolled); one report favoured a
   longer U-shaped colon; using a segment well matters more than which segment is chosen.
 • Pressure problems arise from uninhibited contractions, apparently in the bowel segment; failure to
   reach adequate capacity or a flat tonus limb is extremely rare without technical error.
 • Pattern: rhythmic, sinusoidal contractions, sometimes of increasing amplitude (Figure 48.11).
 • Low-amplitude contractions arising late, near capacity, may be insignificant; early high-pressure
   contractions → persistent incontinence, delayed perforation, hydronephrosis, VUR → repeat
   urodynamics; compliance is never assumed after augmentation.
 • Ileum least likely and stomach most likely to show remarkable urodynamic abnormality; colon (caecum
   or sigmoid) still generates more pressure than ileum despite detubularisation, although colonic
   contractions may decrease with time; ileal reservoirs show lower basal pressure and less motor activity;
   significant contractions — above 40 cm H2O at volumes below 200 mL — were not seen after ileal
   augmentation but persisted after some colocystoplasties.
 • No study controlled segment size or technique; a canine model with identically sized segments showed
   no difference between segments.
 • Gastrocystoplasty: rhythmic contractions are frequent; antrum or a larger gastric segment may lessen
   them.
 • Clinically significant contractions affect a small minority; reaugmentation is required in a small minority

Metabolic Complications
Chloride absorption and acidosis
 • First recognised metabolic complication: hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis after
   ureterosigmoidostomy (Ferris and Odel, 1950) — fatigue, weakness, anorexia, polydipsia.
 • Mechanism (Koch and McDougal, 1985): resorption as ammonium → chronic acid load.
 • With normal renal function: a rise in chloride and fall in bicarbonate is near universal, but frank acidosis
   is rare; nonetheless, treatment-requiring acidosis occurs despite normal renal function and is debilitating
   if unrecognised.
 • Increased urinary acid load with wasting of bony buffers, even without frank acidosis → bone
   demineralisation, possibly retarded growth.
 • Treat acidosis with bicarbonate; arterial blood gas base deficit is more sensitive than serum bicarbonate
   or chloride and allows early treatment before demineralisation.

 • Gastrocystoplasty avoids hyperchloraemic acidosis, even with impaired renal function — but its

Calculi
 • Common long-term complication; rates vary widely between series for unclear reasons; catheterisation
   through an abdominal wall stoma carries the highest risk (incomplete emptying).
 • Mostly struvite; bacteriuria is the key risk factor → treat urea-splitting infection.
 • Empty completely at every catheterisation; stones in a voider → re-evaluate emptying; the association
   with stasis is well established.

 • Presentation: ill with abdominal pain, distension, fever; sepsis common; nausea, falling urine output,

   death; gastrocystoplasty patients present promptly because of acid irritation.

   a postdrain view (Figure 48.12); significant false negatives → ultrasonography or computed tomography

Etiology
 • Cause unknown. Traumatic catheterisation proposed (perforation occurs in non-augmented bladders on
   CIC), but sites vary even within one patient and perforation occurs in non-catheterisers → unlikely sole

A Decreasing Necessity?
 • Goal of every paediatric urologist: minimise the number needing cystoplasty — newer medical
   regimens, botulinum toxin A injection, neuromodulation.
 • Earlier, more aggressive treatment limits bladder insult and maximises recovery; early urodynamics in
   posterior urethral valves identifies treatable bladder dysfunction.
 • Kaefer et al. (1999a) — the most compelling evidence: hostile neurogenic bladders treated immediately
   at diagnosis needed augmentation in 17% versus 41% when treated expectantly; no urodynamic data,
   possible lag-time bias.
 • No randomised trials → the benefit of early treatment remains unproven; UMPIRE may clarify. Early
   bladder management initially showed no fall in augmentation; re-evaluation suggests a fall of about a
   quarter over a decade. Improvements will reduce, not abolish, cystoplasty.

Understanding the Patient-Centered Impact of Incontinence and Reconstruction
 • Goals: protect renal function and improve continence — improved health-related quality of life is
   assumed, not demonstrated.
 • Early small series showed no health-related benefit on questionnaires despite good surgical outcomes;
   social impact of a channel versus the urethra is little studied; the literature is surgeon-centred.
 • Emerging data: moderate perceived benefit after reconstruction; faecal incontinence affects quality of
   life more uniformly across childhood and earlier than urinary incontinence; urinary incontinence limits
   social participation; incontinence and its management are distressing and unpredictable.
 • Next step: condition-specific tools, such as web-based goal attainment for catheterisation compliance and
   continence tracking in spina bifida.

Ileovesicostomy
 • Incontinent alternative; counselling of parents and older patients is essential.
 • Candidates: patients mentally, physically or socially unable to catheterise a continent channel; also
   hyperreflexic detrusor with small capacity.
 • Versus vesicostomy: dry per urethra, bag appliance instead of constant leakage and diapers. Versus

Alternatives to Gastrointestinal Cystoplasty
 • Motivation: complications of bowel; efforts span synthetics, autologous grafts, autoaugmentation
   (bladder diverticulum) and neural stimulation — none has stood the test of time against intestinal
   cystoplasty.
 • Ideal augmenting tissue: transitional epithelium (relatively impermeable, no metabolic change), no
   mucus, no added tumour risk.
 • Ureterocystoplasty: transitional epithelium with muscle backing. Autoaugmentation: transitional mucosa
   eventually backed by collagen.
Ureterocystoplasty
(Figure 48.13) Nephrectomy → bladder bivalved, posterior incision carried off the midline into the ureteral
orifice; ureter not detached from the bladder → ureter opened opposite its main blood supply →
reconfigured as for intestinal cystoplasty → anastomosed to the bladder.
Autoaugmentation
(Figure 48.14; Cartwright and Snow, 1989) Detrusor incised → detrusor stripped and excised from mucosa →
detrusor anchored bilaterally (psoas hitch) so that the mucosa bulges as a diverticulum with filling.
Seromuscular Enterocystoplasty
(Figure 48.15; Buson et al., 1994) Detrusor incision as in autoaugmentation → bulging bladder mucosa
covered with a demucosalised sigmoid segment sutured to bladder muscle → space between bladder
mucosa and sigmoid drained.

Bladder Regeneration
 • Nearly two decades of effort (tissue-engineered scaffolds, bioprinting); a bladder with specialised
   urothelium and contractile muscular stroma has not been achieved.
 • Atala et al. (2006): first seeded biodegradable construct for neurogenic augmentation → industry-
   supported multi-institution trial: no clinically or statistically significant improvement in compliance or
   capacity.
 • Remains experimental, not for human use outside well-designed trials.

 KEY POINTS: AUGMENTATION CYSTOPLASTY
   • Any gastrointestinal segment should be reconfigured and widely anastomosed to the bladder to
     maximise capacity and compliance.
   • Failure to achieve adequate compliance is rare and usually related to rhythmic pressure contractions.
   • Gastrointestinal effects are rare, although chronic diarrhoea may follow use of the ileocaecal segment
     in the neurogenic population.
   • Ammonium resorption can cause metabolic acidosis, particularly with renal insufficiency.
   • Bacteriuria is common after cystoplasty and does not always require treatment.
   • Bladder calculi are common after augmentation; the incidence can be reduced with daily irrigation.
   • Aggressive adenocarcinomas have been reported as early as 4 years after augmentation, but not with
     the frequency seen after ureterosigmoidostomy.
   • Delayed perforation of the bowel segment is to be expected in a minority; the reported excess in
     colonic segments has not been consistent across series.
   • Good early care of the underlying bladder dysfunction may lower the need for augmentation.


 KEY POINTS: ALTERNATIVES TO GASTROINTESTINAL CYSTOPLASTY
   • Ureterocystoplasty avoids bowel complications but may not provide adequate volume unless the ureter
     is remarkably dilated.
   • Autoaugmentation seldom adds significant capacity and may not improve compliance long term.
   • Bladder regeneration remains experimental and not ready for human use outside well-designed trials.


Concept — augmentation buys compliance by importing an absorptive, secretory, contractile organ into the
urinary tract: every late complication — acidosis, alkalosis, mucus, stones, infection, tumour, perforation —
follows from bowel not being bladder, and every mitigation (reconfiguration, irrigation, complete emptying,
surveillance) targets one of those properties.

CONTINENT URINARY DIVERSION
 • Frequency depends on definition. Cystectomy for tumour is far rarer than in adults; after cystectomy for
   cancer or severe congenital bladder anomaly, a pure continent diversion (Kock or Indiana pouch) gives
   results equivalent to adults.
 • Orthotopic neobladder is rare in children: neurogenic or anatomic outlet problems prevent spontaneous
   voiding; an occasional neurogenic child is a candidate, but adequate voiding is uncertain.
 • Broader definition: augmentation + continent abdominal stoma + an outlet procedure (bladder neck
   division typically counts) — in complex patients, often after many previous operations.

Considerations
 • Total replacement needs far more bowel than augmentation: about 40 cm of ileum for a Kock reservoir
   versus about 20 cm for augmentation; the entire right colon with hepatic flexure for an Indiana pouch
   versus 15–20 cm for colocystoplasty → the native bladder is used if it provides any significant volume,
   even if the outlet must then be repaired.
 • Imbricated ileocaecal valve and terminal ileum: simple, reliable efferent limb in adults and children; yet,
   despite contrary reports in select patients, concern about faecal incontinence persists in neurogenic
   patients → flap valves (appendix, tapered bowel) are increasingly used.
 • Native urethra is ideal for CIC but not always feasible: catheterisation difficulty after bladder neck
   reconstruction; neurologic limitation of access, especially the wheelchair-bound child; in neurologically
   intact children, urethral sensation causes discomfort that undermines the schedule → a continent
   catheterisable stoma is an adequate and sometimes more reliable alternative.

Continence Mechanisms and Catheterizable Stoma
Ureterosigmoidostomy and Its Variants
 • Advantage: urine evacuated with stool. Complications: hyperchloraemic acidosis, infection,
   hydronephrosis, colonic malignancy → disfavour, particularly in the United States.
 • Rarely performed as originally described; acidosis and upper tract morbidity rise with time; the
   adenocarcinoma risk commits a child with a long life expectancy to lifelong surveillance for an avoidable
   tumour → unlikely to regain acceptance in children.
 • Sigma rectum (Mainz II pouch) — the most basic modification: antimesenteric incision 6 cm proximal and
   distal to the rectosigmoid junction → antirefluxing ureteric implantation → reconfigure and close → low-
   pressure rectosigmoid reservoir. Used in exstrophy; continence good in selected patients; acidosis still
   significant (whole colon exposed); adenocarcinoma risk unaltered; commonest early complication
   ureterocolonic stricture; upper tract protection unproven long term (Chapter 134).
 • Kock colorectal valve: intussuscepted nipple valve stabilised with permanent staples confines urine
   distally; distal rectum opened and patched with ileum → low reservoir pressure; no alkali therapy needed
   at short follow-up; long-term paediatric data awaited.
 • Tumour remains the last major concern: a Boston series of children after ureterosigmoidostomy included
   adenocarcinomas with deaths.
 • Hemi-Kock augmentation of the distal rectum with a colorectal valve: the afferent nipple keeps stool
   away from the ureteroileal anastomoses, perhaps lowering tumour risk; complex, with high early and late
   complications, though children were thought better suited. Others proposed the rectum augmented with
   a nonrefluxing ileocaecal conduit.
 • Prerequisite for any variant: a competent anal sphincter — manometry, EMG and practical retention of
   an oatmeal enema upright without soiling. Neurogenic patients who cannot hold diarrhoea are not
   candidates. Stream-separating procedures within the rectal sphincter are rarely used in children.

Nipple Valves
 • Largest experience: Kock pouch; Skinner's modifications maintain the efferent nipple, yet failure of 15%
   or more is expected and reoperation of about one-third, mostly for the efferent nipple; equivalent results
   in children, with acidosis and new hydronephrosis attributed to patient complexity.
 • Mainz I pouch: nipple evolved — intussuscepted ileum stapled, passed through the intact ileocaecal valve
   and fixed again; low incontinence with the latest modifications, good upper tracts, rare metabolic
   problems in children.
 • Principle (Figure 48.16A): the same pressures that create continence tend to efface nipple valves.
Flap Valves and the Mitrofanoff Principle
 • Mitrofanoff (1980): appendix (and ureter) as a flap valve — any tubular structure can be implanted
   effectively into a low-pressure reservoir; avoids harvesting the ileocaecal valve or other segments.
 • Mechanism: submucosal tunnel for a supple, small-diameter conduit; as the reservoir fills, rising pressure
   transmitted through the epithelium coapts the conduit (Figure 48.16B).
 • Appendix is ideal: removed without significant morbidity; small calibre permits a short tunnel —
   continence with only a 2-cm appendiceal tunnel (Kaefer and Retik, 1997); relatively long in children with
   a thinner abdominal wall.
 • The flap valve is probably the most reliable surgical continence mechanism — some never leak per
   stoma → no pop-off → upper tract deterioration or rupture if catheterisation lapses.
 • Appendix in situ → the reservoir must include right colon; the mesoappendix usually allows
   mobilisation to native bladder or almost any reservoir.
Technique (appendicovesicostomy)
Low midline or transverse incision; if the caecum is high, mobilise ascending colon along the line of Toldt
(laparoscopic assistance possible) → amputate the appendix base with a small caecal cuff (cuff at the stoma
may reduce stenosis); close caecum as for appendicectomy; harvest a flap of caecum in continuity and
tubularise it if appendix length is marginal → choose implantation site by appendix length, bladder mobility
and stoma site; tunnel the distal end posterolaterally (anterior if anatomy suits), bladder opened or not
(Figure 48.17); an extravesical trough covered by laterally mobilised serosa also works → bring the base
(caecal cuff) to the umbilicus or right lower quadrant without tension, without twisting or fascial
compression of the pedicle; umbilicus can hide the stoma → skin flaps against stenosis (a rotational
umbilical flap helps when the mesentery is short) → keep the conduit as short as possible; fix appendix and
bladder to peritoneum beneath fascia to prevent kinking with filling → catheterise after each step; if not
easy, revise the previous step; also at varying volumes. Same principles apply laparoscopically and
robotically.
 • Split appendix for both caecostomy and catheterisable channel: reliable in selected patients with
   meticulous technique; no difference in stomal revision up to 15 years.

Alternatives
 • Ureter (Mitrofanoff): preserve distal blood supply; refluxing ureters usable after extravesical
   reimplantation; more stomal stenosis than appendix (blood supply); catheter distension causes
   discomfort.
 • Fallopian tube (Woodhouse and MacNeily, 1994): catheterisable, but stenosis significant; consider
   fertility when the ipsilateral gonad is normal.
 • Tapered ileum (Adams et al., 1992): narrowed along the mesenteric border with permanent staplers in
   series; easy catheterisation, good continence; long enough to reach skin without tension but short and
   straight — an unnecessarily long, mobile channel kinks, causing difficult catheterisation or perforation.
 • Yang-Monti (Yang, 1993; Monti et al., 1997): 1–2 cm of ileum, harvested independently or adjacent to the
   augmentation segment, opened along the antimesenteric border, closed transversely → circumference
   becomes length; small central mesentery; mesentery-free ends ease tunnelling and exit; an off-centre
   incision makes one limb longer — short limb in the bladder, long limb through a thick abdominal wall;
   long intravesical tunnel unnecessary; tubularised over 12 Fr, two layers, absorbable (Figure 48.18).
     ◦ Efficient use of bowel; possibly easier to catheterise (mucosal folds redirected longitudinally); ileal
       stomas may have less stenosis than appendix.
     ◦ Disadvantage: short — tension in obese patients despite skin flaps → stenosis → double Monti (two
       channels anastomosed) or spiral Monti; similar continence, stenosis and revision, but spiral Monti
       more often needs subfascial revision at the umbilicus.
     ◦ Pouch-like dilatation causing difficult catheterisation is largely avoidable; worsened by excessive
       extravesical length.
Results
 • Continence superb — usually above 95%; inability to use the appendix is rare (other than for antegrade
   enemas).
 • Incontinence is rare → inadequate flap-valve length or persistently high reservoir pressure →
   urodynamics.
 • Stomal stenosis is the commonest complication; also false passage; difficulty may reflect redundant
   extravesical length; early stenosis may need formal revision; "L stent" with topical steroid cream is a
   noninvasive option.
 • Suprafascial or subfascial revision is common; Monti channels are about twice as likely as
   appendicovesicostomy to need revision; age-independent, clustering in the first months to years.
 • Benign fibroepithelial polyps and inflammatory granulation tissue occur in all channel types; many
   symptomatic, often recurrent after resection.
 • Rare stricture and necrosis, particularly of caecal extensions; abdominal stomas carry a higher risk of
   reservoir calculi (incomplete emptying).

 • Yachia (1997): anterior bladder wall tube woven through rectus muscle for compression; complete

Technique
Parallel incisions 3 cm apart in the anterior bladder create a long rectangular full-thickness flap, measured
against the abdominal wall to reach skin without tension → tubularised to the bladder in two layers; muscle
kept broad for blood supply; mucosa trimmed to avoid redundancy → an in-line intravesical mucosal strip 2–
3 cm long, 1.5 cm wide (Figure 48.19: further 2.5 cm) is incised and its edges mobilised — preferably one
edge across to the other, avoiding overlapping suture lines — and tubularised; Casale incised the strip end
transversely; a later modification leaves it intact → bladder mucosa on either side mobilised and closed over
the mucosal tube, forming the flap valve; wider mobilisation of the opposite side avoids overlapping lines
(fistula, incontinence) → bladder closure incorporates the tube to prevent kinking → stent for 3 weeks.
 • Strictures if not catheterised regularly — more so than other channels.

 • Continence good, as with most flap valves; stomal stenosis a significant problem (high at Indiana); skin

Results With Pediatric Continent Diversion
 • The hardest part is the efferent limb: reliable continence plus easy catheterisation.
 • Flap valve most familiar to paediatric urologists: appendix simple, suits most, very good continence;
   tapered intestinal segments when the appendix is absent or used for enemas.
 • Nipple valves: most complex, longest learning curve; continence approaching 85% with stapled nipples
   after extensive experience, versus above 90% approaching 95% with other efferent limbs.
 • Hydronephrosis is rare and not increased versus conduit diversion with good selection and technique —
   but new hydronephrosis when catheterisation is unreliable.
 • Complications will rise with follow-up: the same as augmentation — infection, hydronephrosis, calculi,
   spontaneous perforation, tumour — a function of intestine as reservoir; more bowel than augmentation
   → possibly more complications; raised chloride, lowered bicarbonate and acidosis already noted;
   spontaneous perforation reported but rare.
 • Commonest complication: stomal stenosis, more at the umbilicus with appendix than with tapered
   ileum; skin flaps into the channel lower but do not eliminate it.
 • No efferent limb is superior by consensus → individualise.

 KEY POINTS: CONTINENT URINARY DIVERSION
   • "Pure" continent diversions have been used in children with results similar to adults.
   • Flap valves provide a reliable continence mechanism with appendix or reconfigured intestinal
     segments.
   • The efferent limb should always be kept as short and straight as possible to facilitate catheterisation.
   • Stomal stenosis is the commonest complication of paediatric continent diversion.

Transitional Care Considerations
 • Transitional urologist: emerging field for adults with paediatric reconstructions (Chapter 65).
 • Mortality from medical noncompliance (Husmann, 2017, augmented cohort): spontaneous rupture, with
   a death; contributors substance abuse, mental or physical deficits, catheterisation noncompliance.
 • 240 mL of saline irrigation daily was highly effective in reducing calculi (Husmann, 2016).
 • Augmentation with concomitant bladder neck procedure → new renal scarring and CKD stage 3 or
   higher in a substantial proportion at long follow-up, driven by catheterisation noncompliance.
 • Bladder cancer in spina bifida: about 2–4% overall; most presented with stage III or IV disease; non-
   gastric augmentation did not appear to increase risk → counsel all patients.
 • Lifelong risk: adult providers must assess catheterisation compliance, daily irrigation and upper tract
   deterioration, particularly after augmentation with bladder outlet procedures.
Concept — continence mechanisms divide by what reservoir pressure does to them: pressure seals a flap
valve tighter and effaces a nipple, so flap valves dominate paediatric practice — and their hazard is the mirror
image, no pop-off when catheterisation fails.

SUMMARY
 • Treat bladder dysfunction early and aggressively, whatever the cause, to minimise the number needing
   reconstruction.
 • Evaluate carefully so every problem is identified and addressed; be flexible — fit segment and technique
   to the patient; never force one procedure on every patient.
 • Identify and correct upper tract obstruction or VUR at surgery; low-grade secondary reflux usually
   resolves once bladder dysfunction is corrected.
 • Provide an adequate reservoir holding, at low pressure, the volume produced between voids or
   catheterisations — by augmentation or a continent reservoir, with any gastrointestinal segment.
 • Create outflow resistance at the bladder neck if it is lacking.
 • Every patient must be prepared and able to perform CIC reliably; most need it routinely, particularly
   neurogenic patients.
 • Preserve as much native urinary tract as possible — urothelium avoids intestinal morbidity; bowel is not
   a perfect physiologic substitute.
 • Lifelong follow-up, including true quality of life; motivation of patient and family is the most important
   factor, and its assessment is critical.
