Chapter 16 — Fundamentals of Upper Urinary Tract
Drainage
 • Effective upper tract drainage is a foundation of urologic practice: it facilitates perioperative management
   of many upper tract conditions and is essential in acute situations.

INDICATIONS
Obstruction
 • The commonest indication mandating drainage. Defined as blockage of urine flow through the intrarenal
   collecting system and ureter; acute or chronic, partial or complete.
 • Presentation ranges from silent, long-standing obstruction with loss of function to acute critical illness
   needing urgent intervention.
 Pattern           Causes
 Acute             Stones, blood clots, sloughed papilla, fungal balls
 Insidious         Tumours, ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO), strictures
 From the          Bladder tumour invading or occluding the orifices; outlet obstruction (benign prostatic hyperplasia,
 lower tract       urethral stricture) — lower tract drainage usually corrects it, but upper tract drainage is sometimes
                   required

Diagnostic Studies
 • Whitaker test: percutaneous urodynamic study — irrigant infused at a constant rate into the kidney, renal
   pelvic and bladder pressures measured; a differential above 12 cm water suggests obstruction.
   Distinguishes obstructive from non-obstructive hydronephrosis.
 • Antegrade pyelogram through renal access to evaluate ureteral strictures, often after ureteral rest (time
   for the stricture to mature after manipulation or catheter removal), which improves surgical success.

Perioperative Drainage
 • Preoperative percutaneous access can expedite surgery for large renal stones or intrarenal tumours;
   postoperative percutaneous drainage for patients at higher risk of infection or needing tamponade of
   bleeding.
 • Preoperative ureteral stents passively dilate the ureter before retrograde intrarenal surgery;
   postoperative stents mitigate ureteral oedema.

Mitigating Complications of Trauma
 • Percutaneous or ureteral drainage diverts urine and prevents urinoma; ureteral or nephroureteral tubes
   limit the consequences of ureteral injury and stricture.

Instillation of Therapy
 • Percutaneous access permits instillation of chemotherapy for upper tract urothelial carcinoma (newer
   agents may be chemoablative) and has been used for percutaneous stone dissolution.
Concept — drainage serves five purposes — relieve, diagnose, prepare, protect, treat — but obstruction
dominates, and the choice between retrograde and antegrade is then set by anatomy.

ANATOMIC CONSIDERATIONS
 • Feasibility and success depend on patient-specific anatomy (a pelvic kidney may be unsafe to puncture;
   diversions block retrograde access) and on history — abdominal surgery, prostatic enlargement, urethral
   stricture.

Horseshoe Kidney
 • Commonest renal fusion anomaly. Fusion prevents medial rotation during ascent → renal pelvis and
   ureter lie anteriorly; the high anterosuperior ureteral insertion hampers retrograde access (stenting may
   still be attempted acutely). UPJO coexists in one-third.
 • Percutaneous: upper-pole calyces are easiest — rarely supracostal, and reach the rest of the kidney and
   the ureter (Fig. 16.1); entry is more medial, often through the paraspinous muscles; the lower pole may
   be too distant → extra-long instruments or mid-calyceal access.
 • Axial imaging before access: retrorenal bowel is common. Vessels are aberrant but enter anteromedially
   (except at the isthmus), so direct vascular injury is rare with planned access; pooled series show major
   haemorrhage uncommon and colon injury rare.

Ectopic Kidney
 • Usually pelvic; also intra-abdominal or intrathoracic. Retrograde limited by UPJO or distance; posterior
   access blocked by pelvic bones → supine, transabdominal if no bowel intervenes.
 • Options: computed tomography (CT) guidance; laparoscopic or robotic mobilisation of bowel
   (laparoscopy-assisted percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) in pelvic kidneys uniformly successful
   without complications in reported series); ultrasound to identify bowel; where no bone intervenes,
   posterior access just above the iliac bone or transgluteal through the greater sciatic foramen.

Transplant Kidney
 • Retrograde access is difficult: anterior ureteroneocystostomy and short ureter give poor wire purchase
   → without retrograde opacification, puncture under ultrasound.
 • Pelvic location → anterior approach, supine; extraperitoneal, so bowel rarely intervenes.
 • Dense perinephric fibrosis resists balloon dilation → fascial incising needle with balloon, or telescoping
   metal dilators. Reported series: high technical success, no major complications.

Duplication
 • Complete — separate ureters to the bladder; incomplete — ureters join below the ureteropelvic junction;
   bifid — moieties join at the renal pelvis. Both approaches possible; in complete duplication, confirm the
   target moiety for tube or stent.

Ureteropelvic Junction Obstruction
 • Drain for pain, nausea, vomiting or obstructive pyelonephritis. Antegrade preferred acutely when
   pyeloplasty is planned — spares the ureter inflammation at repair; retrograde stent after
   endopyelotomy for healing.

Infundibular Stenosis/Diverticulum
 • Stenosis (congenital or acquired) → hydrocalycosis; a diverticulum drains through a narrow channel.
   Acutely, antegrade access; retrograde requires dilation or incision of the neck.

Urinary Diversion
 • Retrograde is sometimes possible with a flexible cystoscope to find the ureteroenteric anastomoses, but
   antegrade is more reliable, albeit more morbid. Contrast injected into the diversion opacifies the kidneys
   (most anastomoses reflux); non-refluxing or strictured → ultrasound-guided access.

Body Habitus
 • Lower-limb contractures hinder urethral access and positioning; severe scoliosis displaces perirenal
   organs.

 KEY POINTS
   • Access into a posterior calyx usually allows direct access to the remainder of the kidney.
   • Upper-pole calyces are usually situated in a mediolateral orientation.
   • Cross-sectional imaging is recommended before percutaneous access to evaluate perirenal anatomy.

Concept — anatomy chooses the route: where the ureter is inaccessible go antegrade; where the posterior
approach is blocked go anterior or image-guided; cross-sectional imaging is the constant.

RETROGRADE RENAL ACCESS
Preoperative Considerations
 • Urinalysis for infection. American Urological Association (AUA): antibiotic prophylaxis for all cystoscopy
   with ureteral instrumentation.
 • Critically ill patients at risk of decompensation on induction: stent under local anaesthesia, awake;
   otherwise general anaesthesia or sedation.

Surgical Technique
 • Dorsal lithotomy (flexible cystoscopes allow other positions) → ureteral catheter through the working
   channel into the orifice (a guidewire helps with distal stones, strictures, J-hooked ureters) → retrograde
   pyelography delineates ureter, collecting system and obstruction.

Ureteral Catheters and Guidewires
 • Catheters stabilise the working wire; most are straight, open-ended, 5–6 Fr; angled-tip or torque-
   stabilised angiographic catheters for difficult cases.
 • Guidewires: straight or curved tip; polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), hydrophilic-coated nitinol, or hybrid
   (hydrophilic tip, PTFE body); stiffness from standard to super-stiff. First goal: wire into the collecting
   system.
 • Hydrophilic wires are most dependable for bypassing impacted stones. Stiffer wires need more insertion
   force but prevent stent buckling in a tortuous ureter or long dense stricture: floppy wire and catheter
   navigated bend by bend into the kidney → exchange through a straight catheter for a stiff wire → ureter
   straightened → stent.

Stent Materials
 • Most are polymers (polyurethane, silicone), proprietary blends designed to resist mineral deposition and
   biofilm, i.e. encrustation.
 • Metallic stents resist extrinsic compression (malignant obstruction): Resonance — tightly coiled metal
   alloy, first used in 2006 for retroperitoneal fibrosis; durable patency and acceptable tolerability in benign
   and malignant obstruction.
 • Hybrid stents (hydrophilic polymer reinforced with metal coils — Silhouette, Passage/Snake): high
   compression resistance in vitro, limited clinical data.

Stent Design
 • The ideal stent: easy to insert, memory against migration, drains, radio-opaque, resists encrustation,
   minimal symptoms.
 • Self-retaining double-pigtail stents are most common (Fig. 16.3). Single-pigtail diversion catheters: distal
   end exits through an ostomy. Open-ended, spiral- or whistle-tip ureteral catheters: temporary, to identify
   the ureter during abdominal or pelvic surgery.
 • Novel distal ends to reduce bladder irritation (e.g. Polaris Loop): a randomised trial showed no
   symptomatic benefit over the double-pigtail on a validated questionnaire.

Stent Tolerance
 • Symptoms arise from intravesical stent material irritating the bladder and reflux of urine through the
   stent. Consent should cover urgency, frequency, dysuria, haematuria, urethral, groin and flank pain.
 • Ureteral Stent Symptom Questionnaire (USSQ): validated; six domains, 38 questions — urinary
   symptoms, pain, general health, work performance, sexual function, additional problems. In its founding
   cohort most patients had urinary symptoms and activity-limiting pain, and a substantial minority sexual
   dysfunction and reduced work capacity.

Complications
 • Misplacement: proximal end left below the obstruction; stent pushed submucosally — suspect when
   wire, catheter or stent needs too much force → repeat retrograde pyelogram and renavigate.
   Parenchymal perforation and intravascular placement: very rare case reports.
 • Migration: from anatomy or a too-short stent. Obstructive symptoms with a stent in place → image for
   malposition or failure.
 • Stent failure — multifactorial: extrinsic compression, infection with pseudocapsule, debris, encrustation.
   Highest risk: bilateral obstruction, severe hydronephrosis, recurrent UTI, intrinsic ureteral obstruction,
   prior radiation. Long-term stenting may still be attempted with monitoring.
 • Encrustation: urinary minerals deposit on the stent; risk rises with dwell time, bacterial colonisation and
   patient mineralisation factors → failure, stent fracture, ureteral avulsion on removal. All long-term stents
   monitored — imaging or office cystoscopy.

 KEY POINTS
   • Retrograde pyelography should be used to delineate the collecting system during stent placement.
   • α-Blockers and antimuscarinics can improve ureteral stent tolerability.
   • Obstructive symptoms with an indwelling ureteral stent warrant evaluation for stent failure.
   • Long-term indwelling stents should be monitored for encrustation.

Concept — stent symptoms come mainly from material in the bladder and reflux, so what helps is less
intravesical stent, softer material and bladder-directed drugs — not a thinner stent.

ANTEGRADE RENAL ACCESS
Preoperative Considerations
 • Imaging. Chosen by pathology: cross-sectional imaging for intra- and perirenal anatomy; ultrasound
   suffices for simple decompression of presumed distal obstruction; AUA stone guideline: non-contrast CT
   before PCNL; pyelography for calyceal anatomy; nuclear renogram when ipsilateral function is uncertain.
 • Antimicrobial prophylaxis. Upper tract drainage is clean-contaminated; avoid antibiotic overexposure
   (resistance). Before any procedure: dipstick, with possible urinalysis and conditional culture.
     ◦ Culture required with risk factors: prior UTI, suspected struvite stone, anatomic abnormality (UPJO,
       stricture), indwelling tubes or stents, urinary reconstruction (diversion, augmentation). A culture from
       an externalised drain is sometimes necessary but contamination-prone. Indwelling foreign bodies are
       colonised → higher postoperative infection.

Anesthetic Considerations
 • Set by the anaesthetic team from cardiopulmonary comorbidity and body mass index (BMI). Prone →
   general endotracheal anaesthesia (secure airway; controlled ventilation helps with a mobile kidney);
   supine → laryngeal mask airway possible; spinal anaesthesia is safe in some cases.
 • Postoperative: tract infiltration with local anaesthetic reduces analgesic need and 24-hour pain (meta-
   analysis of randomised trials); intercostal and thoracic paravertebral blocks give similar benefit.

   interventional radiology, agree the approach in advance.
 • Anterior or posterior calyces can be approached from different positions. Never access directly through

Guidewires
 • Goal: secure position for instruments and tubes — ideally down the ureter into the bladder to prevent
   displacement.
 • Safest initial wire: PTFE-coated J- or C-wire — curved tip rarely perforates, but will not easily pass down
   the ureter → floppy-tip PTFE or hydrophilic (straight or angled) wire for that.
 • Upper-pole puncture is in line with the ureter — wire often passes unaided; from a lower pole, or on
   failure, a torque-stabilised angled catheter steers it.
 • Then exchange for a stiff wire (Amplatz Super Stiff) for dilation, and place a second safety wire via a dual-
   lumen or larger coaxial catheter.
 • Single wire acceptable when a second is impossible — complete staghorn, calyceal diverticulum,
   impacted infundibular stone — but keep trying to secure the ureter.

Tract Dilation
 • Incise the skin to the full final sheath size first. Cardinal principle: do not pass the dilator too far —
   perforation of the infundibulum, renal pelvis or ureteropelvic junction; dilate short rather than long — a
   short sheath can be advanced later.

 • Rigid dilators must be advanced in line with the access needle — they kink and displace the wire — under

POSTPROCEDURAL DRAINAGE
General Considerations
 • Nephrostomy tubes give good drainage and control while preserving access; particularly useful with
   obstruction plus bowel diversion, impacted stones precluding a stent, or extrinsic compression causing
   stent failure.
 • Tube size (prospective and randomised trials, 20–24 Fr vs 8–18 Fr): smaller tubes → less pain and less
   leakage, no more bleeding. Larger tubes occasionally bleed immediately on removal → if bleeding is
   feared, remove over a wire or in the radiology suite (replacement, embolisation); most are removed
   safely at the bedside. Clamp before removal after percutaneous surgery to confirm distal patency.
 • Combined nephrostomy + ureteral stent: ureteral injury or oedema (drainage, less stricture); after
   complex reconstruction (leak risk; upper tract control after stent removal); significant urinary tract
   trauma.

Management of Nephrostomy Tube
 • Secure with external sutures or dedicated adhesive dressings against dislodgement. Externalisation →
   colonisation and infection → routine exchange every 3 months; encrustation can block tubes. Flushing
   and hydration maintain patency but risk introducing infection without strict sterile technique.

 • Nephroureteral stent placement: advanced over a wire ending in the bladder → renal coil side holes

   access: wire through the catheter re-establishes through-and-through access (if resistance, advance
   catheter and wire together).

"Tubeless" With Ureteral Stent
 • A nephrostomy is not mandatory. Omitting it reduces pain, analgesic use, hospital stay and cost; a short-
   term ureteral stent (± string) is usually left.
 • Evidence (meta-analyses of randomised trials): shorter stay and operative time tubeless; benefit in stay
   and pain is clear against large tubes (>16 Fr) but not against 8–9 Fr tubes; no difference in fever/
   infection, transfusion or stone-free rates. Safe with supracostal access and bilateral simultaneous
   procedures.
 • Disadvantages: loss of the tract for a second look; stent discomfort — worse Wisconsin Stone Quality of
   Life (WiSQoL) scores than nephrostomy in two randomised trials.
 • Externalised stents (single pigtail through the urethra, or string out through the flank) are removed before
   discharge without a procedure; safe; one trial vs large-bore tube: less narcotic, shorter stay; internal vs
   external stents: equal except more stent symptoms with internal.
 • Keep a nephrostomy when external drainage is needed: pyonephrosis, significant bleeding, significant
   collecting system injury.
Concept — the "tubeless" advantage is really an advantage over large tubes; against a small nephrostomy it
largely disappears, and it trades the tube's discomfort for the stent's.

Complications
 • Percutaneous renal surgery can be morbid and rarely fatal: slightly over 10% have major complications
   (modified Clavien–Dindo).
Acute Hemorrhage
 • The kidney's rich blood supply makes haemorrhage the most significant complication of percutaneous
   access: nephrostomy alone needs transfusion in 0.5–4%.

 • Most intraoperative bleeding is parenchymal and insignificant. Management ladder: venous → pressure
   on the incision → persistent tract bleeding in a stable patient → occlude the nephrostomy so the system

   arterial bleeding: electrocautery sometimes; otherwise angioembolisation.
Delayed Hemorrhage
 • Unrecognised intraparenchymal vascular injury → pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula (AVF);
   treatment needed in under 1% of major percutaneous procedures; bleeding usually at about 1 week.
 • Mechanisms, as a pair: pseudoaneurysm — a clot temporises the arterial injury and ruptures
   intermittently; AVF — adjacent artery and vein heal together, and the thin vein cannot withstand
   arterial pressure.
 • Any bright-red blood in urine or tube after surgery raises suspicion; flank pain from clot obstruction or
   expanding haematoma. Angiography is diagnostic in over 90% and allows selective embolisation in the
   same sitting; may miss bleeds with multiple tracts or intermittent bleeding; non-selective embolisation
   risks partial or total renal loss.
 • Figures: Fig. 16.16 — renal artery injection filling the inferior vena cava early = AVF; Fig. 16.17 — lower-
   pole pseudoaneurysm occluded, but embolisation devascularised much of the lower pole.
Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome and Sepsis
 • Sepsis in 0.5–2.5% after PCNL; much less after uncomplicated drainage alone.
Collecting System Injury
 • Infundibular tears: usually insignificant, may bleed. Renal pelvic injury at access, dilation or manipulation:
   recognised by contrast extravasation, loss of pelvic distension, or direct view (Fig. 16.18) →
   nephrostogram before tube removal to confirm healing.
 • Ureteral injuries mostly heal with a stent. Any injury can leak → infection, ileus; symptomatic urinoma →
   percutaneous drain; intra-abdominal extravasation reported.
Visceral Injury
 • Colon injury about 0.1% in PCNL. Left twice as often as right — colonic apposition greatest at the left
   lower pole. Risk factors: older age, dilated colon, prior colonic surgery or disease, thin habitus,
   horseshoe kidney. Retrorenal colon on CT about 2% supine vs 10% prone → supine may be safer.
 • Management: recognised intraoperatively → separate drainage of colon and collecting system (urinary
   drainage alone reported effective); extraperitoneal → conservative (nil by mouth several days; watch for
   infection or fistula when diet resumes); missed injury — unexplained fever, leucocytosis, prolonged ileus,
   peritonism; intraperitoneal, peritonitis or sepsis → open repair.
 • Small bowel (duodenum): uncommon; peritonitis or nephroenteric fistula; often open repair, though
   conservative management (percutaneous intraduodenal catheter or nasogastric/nasoduodenal drainage,
   upper tract drainage, fasting, parenteral nutrition) has succeeded.
 • Spleen and liver: unlikely unless supracostal access (mostly above the 10th rib; possible above 11th/12th)
   or organomegaly → CT-guided access when imaging shows risk. Conservative: serial examination and full
   blood count; rarely laparotomy and splenectomy; liver bleeds less (deliberate transhepatic PCNL
   reported); tube removal in the interventional suite if bleeding is feared.
 • Gallbladder/biliary injury: rare but needs laparotomy/laparoscopy and cholecystectomy — biliary
   peritonitis carries high mortality. Postoperative pancreatitis without direct injury described.
Pleural Injury
 • Hydrothorax or pneumothorax: supracostal access is the main risk; below the 12th rib rare. Needing
   intervention: above 12th rib about 5% (range 0–18%); above 11th rib about 25% (range 0–100%).

 • Nephropleural fistula: never with subcostal access; about 2% above the 12th and 6% above the 11th;
   persists because of distal ureteral obstruction.
 • Intraoperative detection on fluoroscopy: fluid tracking along the lateral chest wall, compressing the lung
   → small-calibre (8–12 Fr) Cope tube as thoracostomy (Fig. 16.19), placed with the same fluoroscopic
   technique, on a Heimlich valve — water seal unnecessary without lung injury.
 • Postoperative: observe if no respiratory compromise or instability; otherwise small-calibre tube; large-
   bore only for lung injury. Chest radiography is more sensitive, but a clear intraoperative chest fluoroscopy
   reportedly predicts no need for thoracostomy.
Neuromusculoskeletal Complications
 • Pressure on nerves and vessels, from the table or limb position, causes short- or long-term disability:
   support all pressure points; straps must not constrict.
 • Prone carries the highest risk, mostly head and neck: ocular injury with visual loss, facial nerve injury,
   necrosis over facial bones or nose tip; carotid or vertebrobasilar dissection → stroke → head neutral, not
   extended.
 • Limbs: shoulder and elbow abduction ≤90° (brachial plexopathy); pad elbow, forearm and knees; elevate
   ankles off the dorsum of the foot.
Venous Thromboembolism
 • Low incidence (≤3%); the AUA best-practice statement does not list percutaneous renal surgery among
   procedures needing thromboprophylaxis; early ambulation is the best measure.
 • Venous gas embolism: rare, potentially fatal. Air enters veins → right heart → pulmonary circulation,
   blocking right ventricular output → hypoxaemia, hypercapnia, low cardiac output; through a patent
   foramen ovale → arterial embolism, neurological deficit. Associated with air pyelography, with or before
   puncture.
 • Signs: hypoxaemia, pulmonary oedema, raised airway pressure, hypotension, jugular venous distension,
   facial plethora, dysrhythmia, mill-wheel murmur, wide QRS with right heart strain. Most sensitive:
   sudden fall in end-tidal CO2.

 • Treatment: rapid ventilation with 100% oxygen, head down, right side up, general resuscitation.
Concept — bleeding is the signature complication and timing sorts the source: immediate bleeding is
parenchymal or venous and settles with tamponade; bleeding at about a week is arterial (pseudoaneurysm or
AVF) and needs angiography.

 KEY POINTS
   • Antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended for all patients undergoing percutaneous renal surgery.
   • Safe fluoroscopic access requires imaging in two planes to determine the 3D configuration of the
     collecting system.
   • Ultrasound-guided access allows real-time identification of perirenal anatomy.
   • "Tubeless" procedures may improve hospital stay and postoperative pain.
   • Percutaneous access can be morbid; surgeons should understand the commonest complications and
     their management.
