Chapter 24 — Basic Principles of Immunology and
Immunotherapy in Urologic Oncology
 • Immunotherapy is an established modality in bladder, kidney, lung cancer and melanoma; its long
   history in bladder cancer is underappreciated.
 • Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) — a relatively non-specific agent whose mechanism is still incompletely
   understood — is the model framework for genitourinary (GU) immunotherapy.
 • Cancer vaccines aim instead to induce a specific anticancer response (prostate, kidney).
 • Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB): antigen-specific responses are restrained by checkpoint molecules on
   CD4 and CD8 tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), typified by programmed death-1 (PD-1) and
   programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1). Blocking antibodies give objective responses (tumour shrinkage) in
   about 20% of kidney or bladder cancers.
 • ICB is more effective in combination; anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4), which probably
   targets suppressive CD4 cells, is a key combination partner.

INNATE IMMUNE SYSTEM
 • Innate vs adaptive: innate is evolutionarily older (all vertebrates), rapid, pattern-based; adaptive is
   specific and has memory.
 • Innate recognition: pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) bind Toll-like receptors (TLRs)
   (homologues of Drosophila Toll) — the fundamental mechanism by which the organism senses "danger".
 • BCG cell-wall peptidoglycans are canonical PAMPs → bind TLR2 on innate cells in the bladder wall →
   multistep immune response.
 • First responder: the tissue-resident macrophage (makros large, phagos eat) — engulfs pathogens; on
   PAMP recognition secretes cytokines and chemokines that recruit and activate further cells.
 • Neutrophil (polymorphonuclear neutrophil, PMN): most abundant circulating immune cell (about 60%
   of blood leucocytes); half-life hours in blood but days in inflamed tissue; main constituent of pus,
   hallmark of acute inflammation. Emigrates between endothelial cells up a chemokine gradient — the
   hypersegmented nucleus eases deformation. Releases secretory granules on PAMP recognition.

CYTOKINES AND CHEMOKINES
 • Cytokine: any small, immunologically relevant secreted messenger (cyto cell, kinesis movement) — how
   epithelium signals to immune cells and immune cells to one another.
 • Type I interferons (IFN-α, IFN-β): from virally infected or stressed epithelium → make cells more visible to
   adaptive immunity (upregulate class I major histocompatibility complex [MHC] and antigen processing)
   and directly promote epithelial death. Intravesical IFN-α: encouraging but mixed results in bladder
   cancer.
 • Chemokine: cytokine subset whose main role is migration along a concentration gradient. Prototype
   CXCL8 (interleukin-8, IL-8) — secreted by urothelium; powerful neutrophil attractant; probably important
   in the BCG response.
 • Interleukins are numbered by order of discovery, so the name says little about function. IL-1 is really an
   innate cytokine: from stressed epithelium, attracts immune cells, and systemically is a principal mediator
   of fever.
 • Th1 vs Th2 polarisation of CD4 T cells decides the direction of the adaptive response (Table 24.2). In an
   IL-12-rich milieu naive CD4 cells become Th1; with IL-4 they become Th2 (chronic inflammation,
   antibody).
 • Th1 → successful BCG response; Th2 → BCG failure. Cytokines are detectable systemically after BCG;
   raised serum IL-2 after treatment predicts a favourable outcome — BCG effects are not purely local.

ADAPTIVE IMMUNE SYSTEM
 • CD4 helper, CD8 killer and B cells, recruited after innate activation; they confer specificity and memory
   (stronger response on re-encounter).

 • Dendritic cell — the bridge from innate to adaptive. Named for fine processes resembling neurones;
   resident in tissues (e.g. Langerhans cells in skin); at rest samples its surroundings by pinocytosis and stays
   quiescent without a danger signal.
 • DC activation sequence: danger signal (TNF-α from macrophages, or bacterial products via TLRs) → stops
   antigen uptake, retracts dendrites → upregulates MHC (binds 9–12-amino-acid peptides) and
   costimulatory B7-1 and B7-2 → migrates via afferent lymphatics following secondary lymphoid
   chemokine through CCR7 → activates specific CD4, CD8 and B cells in the node.
 • Why migration is needed: resting lymphocytes live in lymph nodes; resting DCs live in tissues.
 • CD4 activation: DC presents cognate peptide on class II MHC. Th1 fully activates CD8 killers; Th2 helps B
   cells mature into plasma cells.
 • Tregs suppress adaptive responses and impede a Th1/CD8 anticancer response. Natural Tregs arise in the
   thymus; induced Tregs form when naive CD4 cells meet antigen in a low-inflammation, transforming
   growth factor-β (TGF-β)-rich milieu. Laboratory data favour natural, thymus-derived Tregs as the critical
   population.
 • CD8 T cell: recognises about 9-amino-acid peptides on class I MHC, present on almost all cells and
   upregulated by inflammation and viral infection. Killing is exquisitely specific (type 1 diabetes: β cells
   lysed, adjacent α cells spared), works by inducing apoptosis in the target, and is serial. CD8 cells are
   activated by vaccines and augmented by checkpoint blockade.

IMMUNE EDITING HYPOTHESIS
 • Apart from virally driven tumours of the immunocompromised, human cancers arise in immunologically
   intact hosts — so host and tumour interact throughout progression.
 • Three phases (the three Es): Elimination (innate macrophages and DCs with adaptive cells destroy small,
   undetectable tumours) → Equilibrium (tumour acquires genetic, epigenetic and stromal changes; growth
   slowed but not eliminated; may last the host's lifetime) → Escape (clinically apparent tumour).
 • Escape mechanisms: downregulation of targeted tumour antigens; downregulation of MHC; induction or
   expansion of Tregs.
 • Subversion of antitumour immunity is now a formal hallmark of cancer.
Concept — A clinically evident cancer is by definition an escaped cancer; immunotherapy works by reversing
the escape mechanism that that tumour happens to depend on.

 KEY POINTS: BASIC IMMUNOLOGY
   • Innate response first (rapid, non-specific) → adaptive (specific, memory).
   • Antitumour response needs Th1: IFN-γ, IL-2, TNF-α.
   • CD4+ Tregs inhibit the adaptive response.
   • Immune editing: early tumours eliminated; clinical tumours must escape.

CHRONIC INFLAMMATION AND THE ENDOGENOUS IMMUNE RESPONSE TO
GENITOURINARY CANCERS
 • Apparent paradox: immune editing says antitumour immunity helps, yet chronic inflammation promotes
   tumours — clearly in bladder cancer, probably in prostate cancer.

Chronic Inflammation and the Immune Response to Bladder Cancer
 • Strongest GU evidence linking inflammation to carcinogenesis.
 • Schistosoma haematobium is an International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) known carcinogen:
   endemic countries have high bladder cancer rates; heavy infestation → squamous cell phenotype.
 • Other inflammatory causes: chronic urinary tract infection, chronic indwelling catheters,
   cyclophosphamide cystitis.
 • Presumed mechanism (as in other cancers): dysfunctional M2 macrophages secreting immunosuppressive
   cytokines; myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs); Th2 and Treg polarisation.
 • Supporting immune editing: CD8 infiltration correlates with better outcome in muscle-invasive bladder
   cancer (MIBC).
 • Escape via checkpoints: checkpoint molecules on tumour-specific T cells bind ligands on tumour cells or
   tumour-associated macrophages → proliferation and effector function strongly inhibited.
 • Bladder tumours express PD-L1; expression rises with grade, was more prognostic than World Health
   Organization (WHO) grade, tracks immune infiltration, and is high in BCG-induced granulomas of
   patients progressing on BCG — a possible escape route.
 • Adaptive immune resistance: tumour mutations → CD8 recognition → CD8 cells infiltrate and secrete
   IFN-γ → IFN-γ induces PD-L1 on tumour, myeloid and epithelial cells → PD-L1 engages PD-1 on the same
   CD8 cells → effector function curtailed. Hence anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies should work — confirmed in
   bladder and kidney cancer.
 • Trap: PD-L1 is largely induced by the immune attack itself — a marker of an ongoing but suppressed
   response, not a fixed tumour trait.

Immune Microenvironment in Kidney Cancer
 • Inflammation–kidney cancer link is weak, suggested only by proinflammatory risk factors (smoking,
   obesity).
 • The renal paradox: in most cancers ↑ CD8 density → better prognosis; in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) ↑
   CD8 density → worse outcome, in primary and metastatic lesions; mechanism unknown.
 • Worst subgroup: high CD8 infiltration + high PD-1 and lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3) + few
   functional antigen-presenting cells.

 • Deep flow-cytometric profiling of primary tumours: marked complexity (17 macrophage and 22 T-cell
   subsets); PD-1 broadly expressed, LAG-3 and TIM-3 narrowly; a CD38-high macrophage population
   travels with PD-1+ T cells → CD38 blockade is a combination candidate (anti-CD38 antibodies are

Chronic Inflammation and the Immune Response to Prostate Cancer
 • Macrophages, T and B cells infiltrate the prostate even without symptoms or cancer; this baseline
   inflammation is probably tumour-promoting.
 • Three lines of evidence: (1) inflammation concentrates in the peripheral zone, where more than 90% of
   tumours arise; (2) Western populations (endemic prostatic inflammation) have higher incidence than
   Asian populations; (3) proliferative inflammatory atrophy (PIA) — flattened but proliferating epithelium
   with inflammatory cells — lies adjacent to high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN), the most
   convincing link.
 • Adaptive environment dominated by Tregs — in the gland, in neoplastic lesions, and as functional Tregs in
   bone marrow.
 • CD8 cells can be suppressive (pro-tumour); regulatory CD8 cells in blood can mask an antigen-specific
   response.
 • Infiltrating CD8 cells are PD-1-positive and clonally restricted.

 KEY POINTS: CHRONIC INFLAMMATION AND THE ENDOGENOUS IMMUNE RESPONSE TO GU CANCERS
   • Bladder cancer is promoted by chronic inflammation from infection or other stimuli.
   • Kidney: an active CD8 response exists, but higher CD8 density → worse prognosis.
   • Prostate cancer may also be driven by chronic inflammation.
   • Checkpoint molecules on TILs attenuate the adaptive response to GU tumours.


IMMUNOTHERAPY FOR GENITOURINARY CANCERS

Bacille Calmette-Guérin in Bladder Cancer
 • BCG sequence: instil 1–5 × 108 viable mycobacteria → most washed out at first void; a fraction adheres
   to urothelium via fibronectin attachment protein → cell-wall PAMPs stimulate urothelium through TLR2
   and TLR4 (direct macrophage/DC activation also likely) → urothelium secretes IL-8 (neutrophil attractant)
   and TNF-α (recruits and activates macrophages) → within hours, a neutrophil wave enters the bladder
   wall, clears bacilli and releases cytokines that activate DCs → over weeks, an adaptive, mainly CD4-driven

   response: DCs prime CD4 cells in draining nodes → Th1 (IL-2, TNF-α, IFN-γ) → consolidation as long-lived
   memory.
 • Memory is unproven in patients but implied by the ~50% of non-muscle-invasive disease remaining in
   remission for years after BCG.
Concept — Successful BCG therapy is a typical immune response: innate (neutrophils, macrophages) first,
then adaptive Th1 CD4, then memory.

Nadofaragene Firadenovec in Bladder Cancer
 • Background: intravesical recombinant IFN-α2b protein is well tolerated and active in non-muscle-invasive
   bladder cancer (NMIBC) after BCG, inducing apoptosis via TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL)
   pathways — but responses are not durable, largely because dwell time is only 1–2 hours.
 • Gene therapy rationale: maximise transgene expression in urothelium, minimise adenovirus outside the
   bladder. Early adenoviral attempts failed against the bladder's antiadherence barrier; the excipient Syn3
   markedly enhances transduction.
 • Product: non-replicating recombinant adenovirus carrying the human IFN-α2b gene + Syn3.
 • Landmark phase III, BCG-unresponsive NMIBC: 75 mL intravesical (3 × 1011 viral particles/mL) every 3
   months for up to 12 months; 1-hour dwell with patient rotating; cytology and cystoscopy (± biopsy)
   every 3 months. Primary endpoint: complete response (CR) in carcinoma in situ (CIS) ± high-grade Ta/T1.
 • Results: CR 53.4% within 3 months (CIS cohort); median CR duration 9.69 months; 24.3% high-grade
   recurrence-free at 12 months. No grade 4–5 drug-related events; commonest: discharge around catheter,
   fatigue, bladder spasm, urgency.
 • US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval 16 December 2022 — first gene therapy for BCG-
   unresponsive NMIBC with CIS ± papillary tumours.

Cancer Vaccines
 • Aim, as with BCG: raise a T-cell response. Two components: an adjuvant (PAMP-like; activates DCs —
   "adds" immunogenicity) and a target antigen overexpressed in tumour relative to normal tissue (choice
   largely empirical).
 • Sequence: intradermal injection → immature DC takes up antigen; adjuvant activates it → DC migrates to
   node, presents peptide on MHC → TCR recognition (signal 1) plus costimulation (signal 2) from a mature
   DC is required → CD8 cells gain lytic function against cells bearing the same MHC–peptide; CD4 cells
   become Th1 → CD8 cells leave the node and traffic widely.

PD-1/PD-L1 Blockade in Kidney Cancer
 • RCC is traditionally immune-sensitive, so single-agent anti-PD-1 responses in phase I were expected.
 • CheckMate 025 (first approval): nivolumab 3 mg/kg vs the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)
   inhibitor everolimus in metastatic RCC (mRCC) progressing on first-line tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI);
   primary endpoint OS → longer OS and fewer grade 3–4 events; updated median OS 25.8 vs 19.7 months.
 • KEYNOTE-564 (adjuvant): 1 year pembrolizumab vs placebo in clear-cell RCC at high risk — pT2 with
   nuclear grade 4 or sarcomatoid differentiation, ≥pT3, N+, or M1 with no evidence of disease.

PD-1/PD-L1 Blockade in Prostate Cancer
 • Phase II pembrolizumab in docetaxel- and endocrine-pretreated mCRPC (PD-L1+, PD-L1− and bone-
   predominant cohorts): poor — PSA response <10%, ORR <5%.
 • Why: prostate cancer cells generally do not express PD-L1, although most infiltrating CD8 cells express
   PD-1.
 • Microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) / mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) tumours carry many
   neoantigens → more immunosensitive. FDA accelerated approval, 23 May 2017: pembrolizumab for
   unresectable or metastatic MSI-H/dMMR solid tumours (adult and paediatric) progressing after prior
   treatment with no satisfactory alternative — the first approval based on a biomarker rather than site of
   origin.
 • National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) prostate guideline: consider MSI-H/dMMR testing;
   pembrolizumab for MSI-H/dMMR mCRPC second line or beyond.
 • MSI-H/dMMR prevalence in prostate cancer 1.2–12%; in one series about one-fifth of these carried a
   germline Lynch syndrome mutation, and nearly half derived durable benefit — in line with other MSI-H
   tumours.

Combination Immunotherapy Regimens in Genitourinary Cancers
CTLA-4 Blockade in Prostate and Kidney Cancer
 • CTLA-4 is relatively overexpressed on FoxP3+ CD4 Tregs, unlike PD-1 → blockade probably attenuates
   Treg function, freeing CD8 TILs.
 • Ipilimumab monotherapy in prostate cancer: PSA response ~15–20%, few radiographic responses; two
   phase III trials (pre- and post-chemotherapy mCRPC) — no OS benefit.
 • Rationale for combination: PD-1 on CD8 killers + CTLA-4 on CD4 Tregs → synergy; the first checkpoint
   combination synergistic in animals.
 • CheckMate 214: nivolumab + ipilimumab vs sunitinib, first-line clear-cell mRCC; co-primary endpoints in
   intermediate/poor risk: median OS not reached vs 26.0 months (HR 0.63, P < .001); ORR 42% vs 27%;
   progression-free survival (PFS) 11.6 vs 8.4 months (P = .03 — not significant against the prespecified .
   009 threshold) → FDA-approved first line for intermediate/poor-risk mRCC.

Combining PD-1/PD-L1 Blockade With Androgen-Deprivation Therapy in Prostate Cancer
 • Androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT) is proinflammatory: the thymus involutes with age; androgen
   ablation restores thymic size and cellularity → more naive T-cell output (aged mice, presumed in
   humans); ADT before prostatectomy → activated CD4+ infiltration of the gland; new antibody
   specificities appear.
 • Enzalutamide raises PD-L1 → phase II adding pembrolizumab on enzalutamide progression: about one in
   five had PSA decline ≥50%; phase III ongoing.

Combining PD-1/PD-L1 Blockade With Chemotherapy in Bladder Cancer
 • Chemotherapy is not always immune-antagonistic: some agents enhance vaccine responses by inducing
   immunogenic cell death — tumour cells die in a way that lets resting DCs take up antigen, traffic to nodes
   and prime tumour-specific T cells.
 • Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs): cytotoxic payload linked to a tumour-specific antibody → less systemic
   toxicity, higher tumour drug concentration.
 • Enfortumab vedotin (EV): anti-nectin-4 antibody (highly expressed in urothelial cancer) + monomethyl
   auristatin E (microtubule disruptor); first ADC FDA-approved for locally advanced/mUC, December
   2019.
 • EV + pembrolizumab: accelerated approval April 2023 for cisplatin-ineligible locally advanced/mUC
   (EV-103/KEYNOTE-869): ORR 68%, CR 12% — the first anti-PD-1 + ADC approval in the USA.
 • Phase III trials adding PD-1/PD-L1 blockade to first-line gemcitabine–cisplatin are ongoing; success would
   make first-line therapy chemo-immunotherapy but leave the best second line uncertain.
Combining PD-1/PD-L1 Blockade With VEGF Inhibition in Kidney Cancer
 • Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signalling drives RCC; VEGF-pathway TKIs have long been a
   mainstay.
 • VEGF is also immunosuppressive: it impairs DC maturation and T-cell entry into tumour (altered
   endothelial trafficking). VEGF inhibition → vascular normalisation → more intratumoural CD8+ cells and
   better DC function → rationale for adding ICB.
 • Four PD-1/PD-L1 + TKI combinations are FDA-approved first line for mRCC with a clear-cell component;
   all beat sunitinib, with striking ORR and PFS gains.

 KEY POINTS: PD-1/PD-L1 BLOCKADE AND COMBINATION REGIMENS IN GU CANCERS
   • Pembrolizumab: BCG-unresponsive CIS (cystectomy-ineligible/declined); first-line mUC if CPS ≥10 and
     cisplatin-ineligible, or platinum-ineligible; second-line mUC; adjuvant high-risk clear-cell RCC; MSI-H/
     dMMR mCRPC second line or beyond.
   • Nivolumab: adjuvant MIUC after radical surgery; second-line mUC; mRCC after TKI.
   • Avelumab: maintenance after first-line platinum; second-line mUC.
   • Nivolumab + ipilimumab: first-line intermediate/poor-risk mRCC.

Concept — Every successful GU immunotherapy either supplies a danger signal that starts a Th1 response
(BCG, vaccine adjuvant, immunogenic cell death, VEGF inhibition) or removes a brake on a response already
under way (PD-1, CTLA-4). Failures cluster where the brake is not the limiting step — PD-L1-negative, Treg-
dominated prostate cancer.

CONCLUSIONS
 • Progress has been especially strong in GU cancers, driven by a better grasp of basic immunology and
   tumour immune resistance.
 • BCG was among the first immune-based treatments in routine practice in any cancer.
 • The future is combinations: PD-1 + CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1 + VEGF inhibitors are approved first line in
   RCC; ADT + ICB (prostate) and chemotherapy + ICB (bladder) are under study.
 • Most patients will still not benefit → resistance mechanisms must be defined in preclinical models and
   patients.
