BONY PELVIS
 • Composition: the sacrum posteriorly and the paired innominate (hip) bones anteriorly and laterally.
 • Each innominate bone is three fused bones — ilium, ischium and pubis — fused at the acetabulum: ilium
   → upper acetabulum; ischium → posteroinferior acetabulum; pubis → anterior acetabulum.
 • Joints: sacroiliac (SI) joints posteriorly; a cartilaginous joint at the pubic symphysis anteriorly.
 • Two strong ligaments fix the sacrum to the hip bones, allowing only limited upward movement of the
   sacrum and so giving the SI joint resilience when the vertebral column takes sudden increases in weight.
   They also create the sciatic foramina:
SACROTUBEROUS VERSUS SACROSPINOUS LIGAMENT

                     Sacrotuberous                                    Sacrospinous
 Position            More posterior                                   More anterior
 Attachments         Sacrum/coccyx → ischial tuberosity               Sacrum/coccyx → ischial spine
 Foramina            Forms the common sciatic foramen                 Bisects it into greater and lesser sciatic foramina

 • Orientation: standing, the pelvis curves obliquely backward relative to the trunk and is tilted forward; the
   plane of the pelvic brim lies at 50–60° to the horizontal.
 • Greater (false) pelvis — the iliac fossae, extending up to the iliac crest; lesser (true) pelvis — contains the
   urogenital organs. They are separated by the arcuate line, running from the sacral promontory to the
   pubis, where it becomes continuous with the pectineal line.
 • Pelvic outlet (inferior pelvic aperture): an incomplete, roughly circular bony line — lower symphysis
   pubis, ischiopubic rami, ischial tuberosities, sacrotuberous ligaments and coccyx.
 • Bony landmarks at urological surgery: pubic arch and symphysis; ischial tuberosities; the iliac crest at the
   level of L4, palpable as it runs posteriorly from the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS).
 • Cooper's (pectineal) ligament overlies the pectineal line of the pubis and takes sutures in inguinal hernia
   repair and urethral suspension procedures.
 • Pelvic radiographs and fluoroscopy show the bony landmarks in high resolution and detail. Figure 102.2
   — an anteroposterior (AP) radiograph (iliac wing, sacrum, sciatic notch, iliopectineal line, obturator
   foramen, ischial tuberosity, a phlebolith) and an oblique voiding cystourethrography spot image showing
   the bladder, verumontanum, prostatic urethra, membranous urethra and an anterior urethral stricture.
Concept — The pelvis is three fused bones braced by two ligaments: the ligaments stabilise the SI joint and,
in doing so, create the sciatic foramina through which pelvic structures leave; the arcuate line then divides
the abdominal (false) pelvis from the surgical (true) pelvis.

 • The umbilicus marks the T10 dermatome and usually lies at the L3 vertebra; its position varies with the
   amount of subcutaneous fat.

 • Colles fascia fuses laterally to the ischiopubic rami and posteriorly to the posterior edge of the perineal

 • These fusions limit the spread of necrotising soft tissue infection of the scrotum, and of urinary

Musculature
 • The lower abdominal wall muscles lie deep to Scarpa's fascia. External oblique, internal oblique and
   transversus abdominis end medially as aponeuroses that fuse in the midline at the linea alba (landmark
   for midline incisions) and form the rectus sheath.

 • The arcuate line lies about one-third of the distance from the umbilicus to the pubic crest. Above it, the
   anterior and posterior layers unite medially to form the linea alba (Figure 102.4).
 • Rectus abdominis: pubis (medial to the pubic tubercle) → xiphoid and adjacent costal cartilages. Fibres

   — each firmly attached to the anterior rectus sheath, so the muscle can be divided transversely without
   significant retraction.
 • Nerve supply: thoracic segmental nerves entering laterally; dividing them in a paramedian incision lateral
   to the rectus can cause rectus atrophy and predispose to ventral hernia.
 • Pyramidalis: small triangular muscle, absent in about 20% of people, anterior to rectus but within its

Concept — Fascial fusions decide where fluid goes: the Scarpa–Colles–dartos continuum is sealed to the
thigh, the ischiopubic rami and the perineal membrane but open superiorly. The rectus is segmented by

lateral paramedian division is not.

 • Shutter mechanism: contraction of internal oblique and transversus closes the roof against the floor,
   preventing herniation of intra-abdominal contents.
 • Direct hernia — medial to the inferior epigastric vessels; indirect hernia — lateral to them. Figure

 • The inner lower abdominal wall is covered by transversalis fascia, a variable amount of extraperitoneal fat
   and parietal peritoneum. Below the umbilicus it shows peritoneal folds, some containing fetal vessel
   remnants, easily identified at transperitoneal laparoscopic and robotic surgery (Figure 102.7).

 KEY POINTS
   • The bony pelvis consists of three fused bones: ilium, ischium and pubis.
   • The true and false pelves are separated by the arcuate line.
   • The inguinal canal transmits the spermatic cord and the ilioinguinal nerve.
   • The medial and median umbilical ligaments are remnant fetal structures and landmarks for access to
     the retropubic space at transperitoneal laparoscopic and robotic surgery.

Concept — The inferior epigastric vessels are the organising landmark of the lower wall: they mark the deep
ring, divide direct from indirect hernia, and form the lateral fold that completes the laparoscopic map —
urachus, umbilical arteries, epigastrics, from the midline outwards.

SOFT TISSUES OF THE PELVIS

Musculature
 • The lateral pelvic sidewalls are padded by obturator internus, which leaves via the lesser sciatic foramen
   to insert on the femur. Its medial surface carries obturator fascia, thickened as a tendinous arch from the
   ischial spine to the lower half of the pubis.
 • This tendinous arch of the levator ani is the origin of the bowl-shaped pelvic diaphragm = levator ani +
   coccygeus (Figure 102.8).

 • Levator ani is under somatic control: a dynamic floor, tonically contracted to support the viscera and
   maintain urinary and faecal continence; it contains both type I (slow-twitch) and type II (fast-twitch)
   fibres.
 • It relaxes to allow defecation and micturition, then contracts to raise the floor; further contraction with
   Valsalva resists raised intra-abdominal pressure that would otherwise drive contents through the pelvic
   outlet.
 • Kegel exercises to regain or maintain continence after pelvic surgery aim to increase the tonic contraction
   of these muscles.

 • Arcus tendineus fascia pelvis: a thick white band of condensed connective tissue on the superomedial
   parietal fascia over levator ani, running from the puboprostatic ligament to the ischial spine. It is the
   junction of parietal and visceral fascia and anchors the visceral condensations that support the urethra
   and bladder.
 • Endopelvic fascia is the conduit for neurovascular structures from sidewall to organs and holds the
   organs in place; its loose tissue extends to the midline, separating bladder, prostate and seminal vesicles
   from the rectum. All pelvic vascular structures and some pelvic nerves travel in it.
Concept — The floor is a somatically controlled, tonically active muscle bowl; the fascia is organised as a
wall lining (parietal) and organ sheaths (visceral) joined at the arcus tendineus — and because the visceral
layer is the neurovascular highway, entering the endopelvic fascia is the gateway to the prostate.

PERINEUM

Perineal Fascia
 • Superficial fatty layer + deep membranous layer (Colles fascia). The fatty layer is greatly diminished in
   males and replaced in penis and scrotum by smooth (dartos) muscle; it is continuous between penis/
   scrotum and thighs with the abdominal subcutaneous fat.
 • Colles fascia attachments: posteriorly the posterior margin of the perineal membrane; laterally the fascia
   lata of the medial thigh; anteriorly continuous with the dartos of penis and scrotum and with Scarpa's
   fascia.
 • Perineal membrane: thin, tough, triangular deep fascia enclosing the urogenital triangle; spans the pubic
   arches (inferior ischiopubic rami) from the pubis to the ischial tuberosities; perforated by the urethra. Its
   anterior aspect supports the urethral sphincter; the external genitalia attach to its inferior surface;
   superficial and deep transverse perineal muscles run along its posterior edge to the perineal body (Figure
   102.10).
 • Perineal body — not a structure but an aggregation of fibromuscular tissue in the midline at the junction
   of urogenital and anal triangles. Anteriorly it blends with the posterior border of the perineal membrane;
   superiorly with the rectovesical septum (contiguous with Denonvilliers fascia).
 • It is the focal point of pelvic support — nearly every pelvic muscle and fascia inserts into it; injury at
   perineal prostatectomy risks urinary and possibly faecal incontinence.
 • Perineum = both an external surface (perineal region) and a shallow compartment limited superiorly by
   the pelvic diaphragm. Osseofibrous boundaries: symphysis anteriorly, ischiopubic rami anterolaterally,
   ischial tuberosities laterally, sacrotuberous ligaments posterolaterally, coccyx posteriorly.

 • A transverse line joining the anterior ends of the ischial tuberosities divides it into the urogenital
   (anterior) and anal (posterior) triangles.

Anal Triangle
 • Contents: anal canal and sphincters; ischiorectal fossa with its nerves and vessels. Lined by superficial and
   deep fascia; perianal subcutaneous fat is continuous with that of the urogenital triangle, buttocks and
   thigh.
 • Ischiorectal fossa: levator ani medially; obturator internus and sacrotuberous ligament laterally; fat-filled;
   extends anteriorly to a recess above the urogenital diaphragm and posteriorly into the pelvis through the
   sciatic foramina → perirectal infection can spread between perineum and pelvic cavity.
 • The anal canal begins where the rectal ampulla abruptly narrows at the puborectalis sling.

Urogenital Triangle
 • Bounded by the scrotum anteriorly and the perineal body posteriorly. Skin and fat overlie Colles fascia,
   which attaches posteriorly to the perineal membrane (perineal body at the centre) → the superficial
   perineal pouch.
 • Root of penis lies deep to this pouch: corpora cavernosa attach to the inferior ischiopubic rami and
   perineal membrane; the corpus spongiosum of the bulbar urethra attaches to the perineal membrane.
 • Bulbospongiosus surrounds the bulb; ischiocavernosus surrounds the corpora cavernosa; their
   contraction compresses the corpora during erection.
 • Internal pudendal artery supplies the perineum: from the internal iliac artery it leaves the pelvis through
   the greater sciatic foramen and enters the perineum through the lesser sciatic foramen, running medial
   to obturator internus in Alcock's (pudendal) canal → branches to the anus → through Colles fascia into
   the superficial pouch and posterior scrotum → terminates as the dorsal artery of the penis (Figure
   102.11).
 • Venous: internal pudendal veins anastomose with the dorsal venous complex via a plexus on the lateral
   prostate → bleeding at prostatic dissection. Inferior rectal veins (from the internal pudendal vein)
   anastomose with the middle and superior rectal veins, which drain to the inferior mesenteric vein.

 KEY POINTS: PERINEUM
   • The perineum is bordered by the symphysis pubis, ischiopubic rami, ischial tuberosities, sacrotuberous
     ligaments and coccyx.
   • It is composed of two triangles: urogenital and anal.
   • Its blood supply is the internal pudendal artery and its branches; its innervation is the pudendal nerve.

Concept — The perineum is a diamond split into two triangles, supplied by one artery and one nerve
(internal pudendal, pudendal) arriving through Alcock's canal; its mechanical keystone is the perineal body,
which is why perineal approaches are defined by how they handle the central tendon and external sphincter.

PELVIC CIRCULATION

Arterial Supply
 • Middle sacral artery: from the aortic bifurcation to the anterior sacrum, supplying rectum and sacral
   foramina; met in presacral dissection.
 • Common iliac arteries lie anterior to the common iliac veins and divide into external and internal iliac
   arteries at the level of the SI joint.
 • External iliac artery: anterolateral to its vein, on iliopsoas; passes under the inguinal ligament to become
   the femoral artery. Branches met at pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND): inferior epigastric (arises
   proximal to the inguinal ligament → rectus), deep circumflex iliac, and pubic branches. The cremasteric
   artery arises from the inferior epigastric and travels with the cord. Accessory obturator vessels branch
   from the inferior epigastric vessels into the obturator fossa — a PLND hazard.
 • Internal iliac (hypogastric) artery runs posteriorly to supply the deep pelvis; it divides into anterior and
   posterior trunks. Anterior trunk, in order: umbilical, obturator, middle rectal and inferior vesical, internal
   pudendal, then inferior gluteal (Figure 102.12).

 • Umbilical artery is obliterated beyond its origin → medial umbilical ligament, the landmark for entering

Venous Drainage
 • A rich venous network. External and internal iliac veins run inferior and medial to their arteries and join
   behind the internal iliac artery — injury here at PLND causes significant bleeding. An accessory obturator
   vein may drain into the external iliac vein — also met at PLND.
 • Dorsal venous complex (DVC): drains the penis; enters the pelvis beneath the pubic symphysis in the
   midline, anterior to the striated sphincter; divides into a central superficial branch and a deeper plexus.
   Often ligated at radical prostatectomy to limit bleeding, but ligation can damage the sphincter,
   particularly laterally.

 • Santorini's (prostatic venous) plexus: drains the prostate and part of the rectum; met on entering the
   lateral prostatic fascia at radical prostatectomy; drains, with several inferior vesical veins, to the internal
   iliac vein.

Pelvic Lymphatics
 • Pelvic viscera drain to the iliac nodes and their tributaries, with frequent cross-drainage between right
   and left.
 • External iliac nodes: from the top of the external iliac artery distally to just over the pelvic brim; drain the
   anterior abdominal wall, urachus and bladder.
 • Node of Cloquet: the most distal node of the packet, where the external iliac vessels leave the pelvis
   beneath the inguinal ligament (in the femoral canal); the distal limit of PLND.
 • Internal iliac nodes: drain prostate and bladder; the presacral and obturator nodes drain into the
   internal iliac chain.
 • External genitalia → inguinal nodes → iliac nodes.

Pelvic Innervation
 • Sensation to pelvis and genitalia comes from the lumbosacral plexus.
 • Iliohypogastric (L1): anterior abdominal wall. Ilioinguinal (L1): through the inguinal canal → anterior
   scrotal skin.
 • Genitofemoral (L1–L2): on the anterior surface of psoas — the lateral landmark/border of PLND; genital
   branch through the inguinal canal → cremaster and anterior scrotum; femoral branch → anterior thigh
   (Figure 102.14).

PELVIC VISCERA

Rectum
 • Begins at S3, follows the sacral curve through the pelvic floor and ends in the perineum as the anus.
 • Anterior rectum is peritoneum-covered up to the rectovesical pouch; incising this peritoneum gives
   access to the seminal vesicles behind the bladder — the posterior, pelvic fascia-sparing approach to
   robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy (Figure 102.17).
 • Beyond the pouch, Denonvilliers fascia separates rectum from prostate.
 • Blood supply: middle rectal artery (internal iliac) and inferior rectal artery (internal pudendal). The pelvic
   plexus lies laterally and provides autonomic innervation.
 • Distal rectum (ampulla) — site of the rectourethralis muscle; lies near the striated urethral sphincter and
   prostate and is the commonest site of rectal injury at prostatectomy.

Bladder
 • Covered by peritoneum superiorly; mostly extraperitoneal. The dome is continuous with the urachus —
   a fibrous embryological structure, typically epithelium-lined but obliterated, running in the midline to the
   umbilicus.
 • The urachus can be a source of bladder adenocarcinoma; persistent communication with the urinary tract
   → fistulae or cysts.
 • Anterior — the retropubic space: a potential extraperitoneal space deep to transversalis fascia, often
   developed surgically to reach bladder or prostate. Lateral — loose connective tissue and fat. Posterior —
   ureters entering alongside the seminal vesicles and vasa (Figure 102.18); the rectum lies behind these.
 • Adult bladder rests in the true pelvis; its neck is firmly attached to the prostatic base, and its urothelium
   is continuous with the prostatic urethra. In infants the bladder is intra-abdominal and migrates into the
   true pelvis during puberty.
 • Wall layers: urothelium (relatively impermeable, multilayered transitional epithelium with superficial
   umbrella cells) → lamina propria with muscularis mucosae → muscularis propria: smooth muscle in
   three layers, inner longitudinal, middle circular, outer longitudinal, forming a network for coordinated
   emptying.
 • At the bladder neck the longitudinal fibres are continuous with the prostatic urethra; the circular fibres
   form the internal urethral sphincter, an important component of male continence — seen in post-
   prostatectomy incontinence when they are damaged.
 • The bladder neck is adrenergically innervated; closure enables antegrade ejaculation.
 • The trigone is the thickest part of the detrusor; outer longitudinal fibres give a strong muscular backing;
   anteriorly the longitudinal fibres are continuous with the puboprostatic ligaments.

Ureterovesical Junction
 • The ureter is encased in fibrous tissue — Waldeyer's sheath — as it enters the trigone (Figure 102.19).
   Entry is cephalad and lateral; the ureter runs 1.5–2 cm to an orifice lying caudal and medial to its point of
   insertion.
 • The intramural ureter is narrow → a site of obstruction by stones or by marked bladder wall thickening.
 • Flap-valve: strong detrusor backing occludes the intramural ureter during filling → one-way flow.
   Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) is thought to arise partly from weak or shortened muscular backing.
 • The ureteric entry point is a weak spot in the detrusor → Hutch diverticulum under raised intravesical
   pressure.

Blood Supply and Lymphatic Drainage of the Bladder
 • Besides the vesical branches, several other hypogastric branches contribute to the bladder pedicles,
   posterior and lateral to the bladder. The veins form a plexus within the pedicles draining to the internal
   iliac vein.
 • Some nodes lie in the perivesical space, but bladder lymphatics begin in the lamina propria and drain
   mainly to the external iliac nodes, also to internal iliac and obturator nodes. Significant cross-drainage to
   both sides of the pelvis.

Innervation of the Bladder
 • Efferents come from the anterior portion of the pelvic plexus.
 • High density of parasympathetic, cholinergic endings; relatively little sympathetic innervation of the
   body.
 • Nonadrenergic noncholinergic (NANC) fibres — probably purinergic; small contribution to normal
   contraction, but potential pharmacological targets in pathological states.
 • Bladder neck: dense α1-adrenergic receptors in males → closure for antegrade ejaculation and
   continence. Nitric oxide synthase-containing neurons in bladder neck and trigone may promote
   relaxation during micturition.
 • Afferents travel with the hypogastric plexus (and pelvic nerves) to the dorsal root ganglia.

 • Detailed ureteric structure is covered in Chapter 88. The pelvic ureter begins where it crosses the
   common iliac artery; proximity to the iliac vessels gives a relative narrowing that can obstruct stone
   passage.
 • Extrinsic obstruction: gravid uterus; pathologically enlarged iliac lymph nodes.

 KEY POINTS: PELVIC VISCERA
   • The rectum is separated from the bladder by a fascial layer (Denonvilliers fascia).
   • The bladder is mostly extraperitoneal except for the dome.
   • The anterior retropubic space is an extraperitoneal access point to bladder and prostate.
   • The ureters are anchored to the strong muscular backing of the trigone, which prevents reflux of urine.

Concept — Bladder outlet competence and ureteric anti-reflux are both muscular-backing mechanisms:
circular fibres under α1 control shut the neck (continence, antegrade ejaculation), and trigonal/detrusor
backing flattens the intramural ureter (anti-reflux). Lose the backing and the result is, respectively,
incontinence or retrograde ejaculation, and reflux or a Hutch diverticulum.

ENDOSCOPIC ANATOMY
 • The male bladder and urethra are readily examined in awake patients under local analgesia. Flexible
   fibreoptic cystoscopy visualises the whole bladder, including ureteric orifices and trigone → identifies
   sources of haematuria and localises bladder tumours.
 • Sequence: survey of bladder → retroflexion at the bladder neck (intravesical prostatic protrusion; small
   tumours hidden just inside the neck) → prostatic urethra: verumontanum posteriorly, ejaculatory ducts
   on its summit → withdraw distally → coaptation of the external sphincter just beyond (distal to) the
   verumontanum → bulbar urethra (Figure 102.20).
 • The verumontanum is the key landmark at transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) for benign
   prostatic enlargement: deep resection beyond it can damage the sphincter → incontinence.
Concept — The verumontanum is the sentinel for the external sphincter: everything proximal belongs to the
prostate and may be resected; the sphincter begins immediately distal to it.

RADIOGRAPHIC IMAGING OF THE PELVIS

Pelvic Radiographs and Fluoroscopy
 • Bony landmarks are shown in high detail, but pelvic organs are poorly depicted because of similar
   densities. A partly filled bladder may appear as a faint line and rounded density above the symphysis;
   rectum and sigmoid are inferred from luminal gas or stool.
 • Calcifications are well seen. A phlebolith (calcification in a pelvic vein lumen) is essentially a normal,
   common finding with no clinical relevance — but it mimics a distal ureteric or bladder stone.
 • Cystogram: the empty bladder lies at or just above the symphysis; on filling it rises into the false pelvis
   and spreads laterally and anteriorly towards the sidewalls (Figure 102.2B). Absent lateral distension → a

Computed Tomography
 • Computed tomography (CT) is acquired as a volumetric data set, reconstructable in any plane (usually
   axial, coronal, sagittal).
 • High spatial resolution but relatively low contrast resolution compared with magnetic resonance imaging
   (MRI), because soft tissues attenuate similarly → most CT needs intravenous contrast. Exception —
   urolithiasis: stones attenuate strongly, so no intravenous contrast is needed.
 • Male pelvic CT uses: pain, infection, stones, postoperative complications, staging of known malignancy.
   Poor for detecting primary urological tumours — most seen only when locally advanced.
 • Spaces: peritoneum reflects over the bladder dome; much of the bladder is surrounded by
   extraperitoneal space. The retropubic space (anterior) communicates with the lateral perivesical spaces
   (fat and vessels) → where urine collects in extraperitoneal bladder rupture and where advanced bladder
   cancer infiltrates.
 • From the dome the peritoneum continues into the retrovesical space as a pelvic reflection of variable
   depth between bladder and rectum; being the most dependent part of the peritoneum, it is a common
   site of fluid collection and postoperative abscess.
