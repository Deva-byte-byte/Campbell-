Chapter 124 — Bladder Surgery for Benign Disease
 • Benign structural and functional bladder disorders — bladder diverticula, distal ureteral strictures,
   neurogenic bladder, patent urachus and bladder stones — can impair quality of life substantially.
 • These conditions demand significant preoperative evaluation and planning before surgery, both to
   confirm the diagnosis and to ensure that the chosen operation will succeed.
 • Correction draws on endoscopic, open, laparoscopic and robotic techniques, often in combination; the
   emphasis throughout is on patient evaluation, indications, postoperative complications and outcomes.

BLADDER ANATOMY AND SURGICAL CONSIDERATIONS
Structure and relations
 • The bladder is a primarily extraperitoneal hollow organ in the anterior compartment of the pelvis,
   composed of an inner mucosa, the lamina propria and layers of smooth detrusor muscle, and surrounded
   by perivesical fat.
 • Anteriorly it is bordered by the pubic bone and the loose areolar tissue of the retropubic space (space of
   Retzius). Dissection of this space gives access to the anterior bladder wall and is also used in operations
   on the prostate and proximal urethra.
 • The transversalis fascia separates the bladder from the musculature of the anterior abdominal wall.
 • Posteriorly the bladder is attached to peritoneum, which carries the umbilical ligaments upward to the
   umbilicus:
     ◦ the medial umbilical ligaments contain the obliterated umbilical arteries, branches of the internal iliac
       artery;
     ◦ the median umbilical ligament is the urachus, the fibrous remnant of the allantois. Developmental
       failure of the urachus produces a patent urachus, urachal sinus or urachal cyst.

 • The peritoneal reflection lies posteroinferiorly — near the prostatorectal junction in men and in the
   vesicouterine space in women. Incising the reflection here opens the space behind the bladder and
   trigone.
 • Directly behind the bladder lies the vagina in women; in men Denonvilliers fascia separates the bladder
   and prostate from the rectum.
 • Laterally the bladder abuts the pelvic sidewall, including the distal obturator space. Laterally and
   posteriorly the round ligament (women) or vas deferens (men) is closely related.
Embryology and the ureter
 • The bladder derives from the urogenital sinus (endoderm); its detrusor consists of longitudinal layers of
   smooth muscle. Developmental defects can produce structural anomalies of the urethra, bladder and
   ureteric insertion.
 • After crossing the common iliac vessels the ureters run lateral to medial and cranial to caudal.
 • The two classic crossings form an examinable pair:

 • The distal ureter passes medial to the medial umbilical ligament and inserts at the trigone, at the bladder
   base on the posteroinferior wall.
Blood supply, lymphatics and innervation
 • Arterial supply is chiefly from the internal iliac artery:
       ◦ the superior vesical artery arises from the umbilical artery;
       ◦ the inferior vesical artery arises directly from the internal iliac (from the vaginal artery in women).
 • The superior and inferior vesical arteries form the bladder pedicles, which enter posterolaterally. Partial
   division of one pedicle aids bladder mobilisation.
 • Venous drainage is via a vesical venous plexus into the internal iliac veins.
 • Lymphatic drainage is mainly to the external iliac nodes, with additional drainage to the obturator,
   internal iliac and common iliac nodes.
 • Innervation: sympathetic T10–L2 (thoracolumbar) and parasympathetic S2–S4 (sacral). Somatic supply to
   the urethral sphincter is via the pudendal nerve, whose motor neurons lie in Onuf's nucleus in the ventral
   horn at S2–S4.
Concept — Nearly every benign bladder operation is organised around three landmarks: the space of Retzius
for access, the peritoneal reflection for exposure of the posterior bladder, and the distal ureter for safety;
knowing where each lies decides whether an operation can stay extraperitoneal.

BLADDER DIVERTICULECTOMY

Description
 • A bladder diverticulum is an outpouching of the bladder wall devoid of a functional muscularis propria.
   Its wall consists of mucosa and lamina propria, with only scattered, architecturally disorganised detrusor
   fibres that cannot contract in a coordinated way. The result is urinary stasis and its sequelae, which
   prompt symptoms and intervention.

Chapter 124 — Bladder Surgery for Benign Disease                                               BLADDER DIVERTICULECTOMY
 • Outside the diverticulum lies the adventitia of the bladder wall, around which a fibrous capsule
   (pseudocapsule) may form; this capsule provides the surgical cleavage plane when the diverticulum is
   resected.
 • Acquired diverticula (adults) arise most often from bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) and the resulting
   high voiding pressure.
        ◦ There is a male predominance because benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) causes the majority;
          urethral stricture can also cause them, and in women pelvic organ prolapse may produce outlet
          obstruction.
        ◦ Neurogenic bladder with poor wall compliance raises intravesical pressure and also produces
          diverticula.
        ◦ Diverticula formed in these settings are frequently multiple and of varying size — factors that strongly
          influence both the need for intervention and the choice of treatment.
 • Most acquired diverticula are asymptomatic and are found incidentally on imaging or cystoscopy
   performed for another reason, often for the underlying condition.
 • Symptoms arise from stasis: urinary tract infection (UTI) and bladder calculi; a large diverticulum may
   also hold enough urine to cause a sensation of incomplete emptying. Less often a large diverticulum
   compresses the ureter extrinsically near its insertion, causing ureteral obstruction and hydronephrosis.
 • An increased risk of bladder cancer within diverticula has been observed, so a thorough evaluation is
   mandatory before any intervention.
 • Congenital diverticula are commoner in children and arise from an intrinsic weakness of the bladder
   musculature, not from outlet obstruction or neurogenic disease. Nevertheless a substantial proportion of
   these patients have voiding dysfunction, so they too require evaluation for outlet obstruction and
   neurogenic dysfunction.
 • Congenital diverticula are usually solitary and posterolateral, close to the ureteric orifice — the Hutch
   diverticulum. Because the herniation disturbs the detrusor at the ureteric hiatus, vesicoureteral reflux
   (VUR) is generally present and may cause hydronephrosis or pyelonephritis; surgical planning should
   therefore include ureteral reimplantation.
 • Less common causes in adults: paediatric neurogenic and obstructive conditions (posterior urethral
   valves, prune-belly syndrome); genetic connective tissue disorders (Ehlers-Danlos and Williams
   syndromes); and iatrogenic disruption of the bladder, most often in women after anti-incontinence
   procedures.

Evaluation and Surgical Indications
 • Cystoscopy is central to the work-up. It allows direct inspection of the diverticulum and, above all,
   evaluation for malignancy, which would change the treatment entirely. Record:
     ◦ the proximity of the diverticular mouth to the ureteric orifices;
     ◦ the number of diverticula, the size of the mouth, and an approximate volume.
 • Every diverticulum should be entered where possible and its wall inspected, because of the reported
   cancer risk. Stones formed by stasis can be treated at the same time as the diverticulectomy.
 • Trabeculation points to outlet obstruction as the cause. An intravesical prostatic lobe, urethral stricture
   or bladder neck contracture should be noted, because each will need treatment before or at the time of
   diverticulum surgery.
 • Voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) defines location, size and number; shows VUR and other anatomical
   abnormalities; and reveals diverticula missed at cystoscopy, particularly amid heavy trabeculation.
     ◦ It reliably shows the relationship of the diverticulum to the ureter and any reflux, which decides
       whether a preoperative ureteral stent (for identification and protection) or a simultaneous ureteral
       reimplantation is needed.
     ◦ It also shows bladder neck obstruction — bladder neck hypertrophy, functional bladder neck
       obstruction, detrusor–bladder neck dyssynergia, posterior urethral valves or urethral stricture.
 • Upper tract imaging:
     ◦ Renal ultrasound detects hydronephrosis but resolves the ureter and calculi poorly.
     ◦ Triphasic computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis with a delayed excretory phase (CT
       urogram) shows hydronephrosis, the course of the ureter and any stones. Hydronephrosis may result
       from direct compression by the diverticulum or from VUR through a weakened detrusor tunnel.
     ◦ Cross-sectional imaging (CT or magnetic resonance imaging, MRI) also resolves the number, size and
       location of diverticula; excretory images can show lateral displacement of the ureter by the
       diverticulum.
 • If contrast cannot be given: ultrasound or non-contrast CT for hydronephrosis, combined with
   intraoperative retrograde pyelography and cystography to define the diverticulum–ureter relationship
   and ureteral obstruction. Magnetic resonance urography may also be considered.
 • Characterise the underlying cause, since it must be treated before or with the diverticulum: in-office flow
   rate or formal urodynamics for detrusor contractility and voiding pressure. Where BPH will be treated
   concurrently, estimate prostate volume by transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS), CT, MRI or cystoscopy.
 • Urine cytology in all patients, because of the cancer association.
 • A negative urine culture before any surgery; treat any preoperative UTI with a culture-directed antibiotic
   course.

 • The table's trap: a diverticulum harbouring cancer is not a diverticulectomy case at all — it becomes a
   cystectomy decision.
Concept — A diverticulum is a marker of the bladder behind it: the operation succeeds only if the outlet or
compliance problem that created it is treated, and only after cancer and ureteric involvement have been
excluded.

Surgical Technique
 • History: diverticulectomy was first described in 1897, and treatment has evolved from open surgery to
   endoscopic, laparoscopic and robotic techniques.
     ◦ Transperitoneal laparoscopic bladder diverticulectomy (LBD) was introduced in 1992; subsequent
       reports confirmed its reproducibility by both transvesical and extravesical routes.
     ◦ Robotic bladder diverticulectomy (RBD) was described in 2007. It mimics the laparoscopic technique,
       helps the surgeon who lacks extensive laparoscopic experience, and gives a comfortable ergonomic
       platform for work in the deep pelvis.
 • Peritoneal access is the governing contrast between open and minimally invasive surgery:

 • Choice of technique depends on the number and location of the diverticula, their proximity to the
   ureter, and the need for concomitant ureteral or outlet surgery.
 • Complex (large, posterior) and multiple diverticula needing ureteral reimplantation have traditionally
   been managed open, although laparoscopic or robotic surgery is reasonable in sufficiently experienced
   hands.
 • Alternative minimally invasive approaches:
     ◦ Transvesical pneumovesicoscopic diverticulectomy — carbon dioxide pneumovesicum established
       cystoscopically, then trocars placed through the abdominal wall directly into the bladder.
     ◦ Laparoendoscopic single-site surgery (LESS) — by transabdominal and by transvesical routes.
     ◦ da Vinci single-port (SP) robot — extravesical and transvesical diverticulectomy for posterior
       diverticula, shown to be technically feasible.
 • The underlying cause must be addressed before or at the time of diverticulectomy — relief of outlet
   obstruction, or augmentation cystoplasty for neurogenic bladder.
     ◦ When BPH is the cause, prostate surgery can be done in the same sitting, immediately before or after
       the resection: transurethral endoscopic procedures, or extirpative surgery such as simple
       prostatectomy, particularly for glands larger than 80 cc. Laparoscopic or robotic simple prostatectomy
       can accompany LBD or RBD.
     ◦ If the outlet is treated endoscopically, do it before the diverticulectomy — otherwise irrigation fluid
       distends the bladder and tensions fresh suture lines, risking perforation.

Open Technique
Intravesical approach
 • Positioning: supine with the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) over the break of the table, and the table
   slightly flexed to open the space of Retzius. The genitalia and infraumbilical abdomen are prepared and
   draped.
 • Bladder preparation: an 18 Fr Foley catheter is placed on the sterile field, the bladder is filled with about
   200 mL of sterile water, and the catheter is plugged.
     ◦ Alternative: flexible cystoscopy at the start; a guidewire is used to place a Councill-tip catheter into the
       diverticulum, so that the diverticulum can be filled selectively for intraoperative localisation (while a
       Foley catheter separately fills the bladder).
     ◦ A ureteral catheter is placed when the diverticulum lies close to the ureter.
 • Access: lower midline incision to the pubic symphysis → subcutaneous tissue divided with electrocautery
   → rectus fascia opened along the linea alba → rectus bellies split → transversalis fascia incised, exposing
   preperitoneal fat above and the space of Retzius below.
     ◦ Bladder distension pushes the peritoneal reflection cephalad, easing entry into the space of Retzius;
       the bladder can be distended further for more displacement.
     ◦ A sponge-stick develops the space of Retzius and the lateral pelvic gutters bluntly, pushing perivesical
       fat medially and sweeping the peritoneal reflection off the dome to avoid inadvertent peritoneal entry.
       A self-retaining retractor is placed.
 • Cystotomy: vertical, in the midline, from the anterior dome to just above the bladder neck, aspirating the
   fluid as it lengthens. 2-0 polyglactin stay sutures at the apices prevent tearing. All fluid is aspirated and
   the mucosa inspected for suspicious lesions; deeper retractor blades (or a hand-held deep blade) expose
   the bladder walls.
 • Identify the ureteric orifices and note their relation to the diverticulum; insert a 5 Fr ureteral catheter if
   there is concern.
 • If the diverticulum can be everted: an Allis clamp is passed through the neck to grasp its fundus and
   evert it into the bladder; the mucosa is incised circumferentially around the neck with a margin of about
   0.5 cm, carried through the submucosal connective tissue to free the sac from the bladder wall.
 • If it cannot be everted: a circumferential mucosal incision is made about 0.5 cm from the mouth into the
   submucosal connective tissue. The plane between the diverticular sac and its fibrous pseudocapsule is
   developed by blunt and sharp dissection, with an Allis clamp on the neck — repositioned around its
   perimeter — providing countertraction, until the sac is freed and removed entire. No drain is needed in
   the remnant capsule.
 • Closure of the defect: two layers of running 2-0 polyglactin, with a secure seromuscular closure to
   minimise recurrence.
 • The catheter is exchanged for a larger Foley (20–22 Fr); the cystotomy is closed in two running layers of
   2-0 polyglactin — mucosa first, then seromuscular.
 • Leak test: the bladder is gently filled with sterile water; any leak is reinforced with figure-of-eight 2-0
   polyglactin.
 • Suprapubic catheter — optional, at the surgeon's discretion to maximise drainage and reduce tension on
   the suture lines: a separate punch cystotomy lateral to the midline cystotomy, the catheter threaded in
   under vision and held with a 2-0 polyglactin purse-string, then brought out through a stab incision lateral
   to the rectus belly and secured with nonabsorbable suture.
 • Pelvic drain: ideally anterior to the cystotomy, curving into the lateral gutter beside the diverticulectomy
   site; out through a separate stab, secured with nonabsorbable suture, on self-suction.

 • Fascia closed (continuous or interrupted); subcutaneous tissue irrigated; skin closed.
Combined intravesical and extravesical approach
 • The opening steps match the intravesical technique: cystotomy, identification of the mouth, and a
   circumferential mucosal incision about 0.5 cm from the mouth into the submucosal connective tissue.
 • A finger or blunt curved instrument (for example a metal suction tip) is placed inside the sac to apply
   anterior traction, bringing its extravesical portion into view. A Kittner sponge teases perivesical fat off the
   external surface, exposing the junction of sac and bladder.
 • With outward traction on the sac, the extravesical surface of the neck is incised circumferentially with
   electrocautery.
 • The extravesical incision must meet the intravesical mucosal incision. Failure to establish this continuity
   creates a new plane and leads to resection of excess bladder wall.
 • The detached sac, still inside its pseudocapsule (which may adhere to perivesical tissue), is dissected free
   bluntly and sharply with Allis countertraction at the neck. The defect is closed intravesically or
   extravesically in two running layers of 2-0 polyglactin.

Laparoscopic and Robotic Techniques
 • Positioning: dorsal lithotomy, arms secured at the sides, pressure points padded ("egg-crate" foam),
   patient taped to the table, optionally on a non-skid pad; then steep Trendelenburg. The abdomen and
   groin are prepared.
 • Cystoscopy before port placement:
     ◦ ureteral catheterisation and retrograde pyelography when needed;
     ◦ selective catheterisation of the diverticulum with a Councill-tip catheter over a guidewire, plus a
       separate Foley catheter in the bladder, for selective filling and identification;
     ◦ or real-time transillumination of the diverticulum with a flexible cystoscope — particularly valuable
       with multiple diverticula, where catheterising each one is impractical;
     ◦ intravesical methylene blue has been used to identify the bladder neck during robotic surgery.
 • Access (transperitoneal LBD and RBD): Veress needle through a periumbilical incision;
   pneumoperitoneum at 15 mm Hg. In patients with previous abdominal surgery a subcostal Veress site
   can be used. Steep Trendelenburg displaces the bowel cephalad.
 • Robotic port placement:
     ◦ Camera port in the midline about 2 cm above the umbilicus — 12 mm for the Si system, 8 mm for the
       Xi system; the abdomen is inspected for injury and the remaining ports placed under vision.
     ◦ Two 8-mm robotic ports on either side, caudal to the camera at umbilical level, about 10 cm lateral to
       it near the lateral border of the rectus.

     ◦ 12-mm assistant port contralateral to the diverticulum, two fingerbreadths above the ASIS — this

     ◦ Optional 5-mm assistant port in the upper abdomen contralaterally, triangulated about 8 cm from the

     ◦ The robot is docked at the side for Xi, between the legs for Si.

   and 5-mm left pararectus working port; an optional 5-mm port about 2 cm cephalad and medial to the
   ASIS.
 • Dissection sequence:
     ◦ incise the peritoneum over the diverticulum, located by selective filling or cystoscopic
       transillumination (the cystoscope is then moved to the chosen entry point anterior to the neck,
       leaving a margin) →
     ◦ for large or multiple diverticula, mobilise the bladder: incise peritoneum lateral to the obliterated
       umbilical ligaments bilaterally, divide the urachus and "drop" the bladder into the space of Retzius →
     ◦ energy: 10-mm LigaSure for LBD; monopolar and bipolar instruments for RBD →
     ◦ divide peridiverticular adhesions down to the neck →
     ◦ circumscribe the extravesical neck (monopolar hook for LBD, monopolar scissors for RBD), beginning
       anteriorly, with traction on the sac (ProGrasp forceps robotically, atraumatic bowel graspers
       laparoscopically) →
     ◦ carry the incision through the bladder wall to excise the diverticulum completely →
     ◦ place the specimen in an Endo Catch bag.
 • Constant vigilance protects the ureter and its orifice; the ureter may need careful dissection off the
   external surface of the sac.
 • Transvesical variant (selected cases): use cautiously if there is ureteric involvement or a planned
   reimplant. After dropping the bladder, open it in the midline; clamp the Foley catheter externally to
   maintain pneumoperitoneum; pull the diverticulum into the bladder (inversion helped by pushing on its
   outer surface while pulling on its mucosa from within); circumscribe the mucosa at the neck and deepen
   the incision to detach it.
 • Closure: the defect (and, for the transvesical route, the cystotomy) in two layers of 2-0 polyglactin; the
   bladder can be hitched anteriorly to expose the defect.
     ◦ Laparoscopically: freehand intracorporeal suturing or the Endo Stitch device.
     ◦ An Endo GIA stapler can excise the diverticulum, but carries a theoretical risk of stone formation on
       the staples.
     ◦ The bladder is filled to confirm a watertight closure.
 • Ureteral reimplantation is added when needed for reflux, obstruction or iatrogenic injury, and is often
   necessary when the diverticulum involves the ureteral hiatus — the neck is amputated, the ureter
   separated from the bladder, the diverticular opening closed, a new anterior-wall opening created, a
   double-J stent placed and the ureter sewn to the bladder.
 • The peritoneum may be closed to separate intraperitoneal and extraperitoneal compartments. A closed-
   suction pelvic drain is brought out through a trocar site; pneumoperitoneum is released; the specimen is
   extracted through an extension of the midline camera-port incision; the extraction-site and 12-mm port
   fascia are closed with figure-of-eight 0-polyglactin.

Postoperative Care and Complications
 • Foley catheter for about 1 week to allow the closure to heal.
 • Pelvic drain: output is monitored — high output suggests a urinary leak. If output is low and catheter
   drainage good, the drain is removed, typically at 24–48 hours. Some surgeons measure drain-fluid
   creatinine to confirm the integrity of the closure.
 • A cystogram before outpatient catheter removal confirms no extravasation; periprocedural antibiotics
   are optional to reduce transient bacteraemia.
 • Complications are similar after open and minimally invasive surgery.
 • Urinary extravasation is the commonest complication. It may appear only as high drain output, or
   symptomatically as abdominal pain and fever from an infected urinoma; after a transperitoneal approach
   it may present with ileus and electrolyte abnormalities.
     ◦ Confirm the leak with drain-fluid creatinine compared with serum creatinine; characterise its site and
       degree with CT cystography.
     ◦ Management ladder: asymptomatic → leave the Foley catheter and drain in place; symptomatic →
       percutaneous drain; before removing drain and catheter → drain study and cystogram; persistent leak
       → nephrostomy tubes versus exploration and closure.
 • Ureteric injury may go unrecognised until after surgery, presenting as acute kidney injury (AKI), features
   of extravasation (fever, abdominal pain, ileus, electrolyte disturbance) or flank pain.
     ◦ Excretory urography or retrograde imaging confirms and characterises the injury, shows
       hydronephrosis and identifies a urinoma, which may need percutaneous drainage.
     ◦ Attempt retrograde stenting first; if it fails, place a nephrostomy and plan delayed repair.
 • Other complications: UTI, urinary fistula, bladder spasm, wound infection and bowel injury.

Concept — Diverticulectomy is a dissection problem, not a reconstruction problem: find the sac (selective

seromuscular defect securely — and fix the outlet first.

 KEY POINTS: BLADDER DIVERTICULECTOMY
   • Success depends on a meticulous preoperative work-up defining the number, size and location of
     diverticula — above all their relationship to the ureter, so that ureteral integrity is preserved.
   • The disease process that formed the diverticulum must be managed before or at the time of repair.
   • The fibrous pseudocapsule is the cleavage plane that frees the diverticulum from surrounding
     extravesical tissue.
   • In minimally invasive surgery, selective catheterisation of the diverticulum with a Councill-tip catheter
     over a guidewire allows selective filling and intraoperative identification.
   • Diverticula may be managed by open, laparoscopic or robotic techniques.


URETERAL REIMPLANTATION

Description
 • In adults, reimplantation may be needed in both acute and elective settings.
 • Urgent indications:
     ◦ distal ureteral trauma — open repair is recommended;
     ◦ iatrogenic injury recognised during pelvic or retroperitoneal surgery — open or minimally invasive
       reconstruction, depending on the approach of the index operation and the magnitude of the injury.
 • Elective indications (commonest): ureteral stricture disease, and unrecognised iatrogenic injury with
   delayed clinical presentation.



                                                      page 10 of 37
Chapter 124 — Bladder Surgery for Benign Disease                                          URETERAL REIMPLANTATION
 • Primary VUR is rarely the indication in adults, in contrast to children.
 • Reimplantation is also done single-stage within another planned operation — bladder diverticulectomy,
   partial cystectomy, or distal ureterectomy for upper tract urothelial carcinoma. The techniques below
   concern non-malignant indications.

Evaluation and Surgical Indications
 • Planning is paramount because success depends on a tension-free anastomosis with the bladder; the
   length and location of the defect must therefore be defined accurately.
 • Acute setting: direct inspection usually defines the injury, so intraoperative imaging may be unnecessary.
     ◦ Attend to the health of the ureteral tissue and its periureteric blood supply.
     ◦ Mechanism matters: after thermal or high-velocity projectile injury, debridement of ureter that looks
       normal may be required.
     ◦ For incomplete transection or contusion, an intraoperative retrograde pyelogram shows the presence,
       extent and site of extravasation; alternatively methylene blue is injected into the ureter proximally.
 • Elective setting: use both antegrade and retrograde imaging to define the length and site of the diseased
   segment.
     ◦ Antegrade imaging is by CT urography, or by antegrade nephrostography if a nephrostomy is present.
     ◦ Note multiple diseased segments — they lengthen the overall defect even when intervening ureter
       looks normal.
 • Stricture causes: an impacted stone, previous pelvic or abdominal radiation, chronic infection or
   inflammation.
 • Exclude malignancy before intervention: urine cytology and, in high-risk patients (smokers, older age),
   ureteroscopy with biopsy; the resected stricture can be sent for intraoperative frozen section.
 • Renal atrophy on imaging, especially with chronic obstruction, calls for nuclear renography to document
   both obstruction and function. With severe obstruction a nephrostomy allows function to be reassessed
   after decompression.
     ◦ Do not reimplant if the ipsilateral kidney functions poorly; perform nephrectomy instead if treatment
       is warranted.
 • Stent already in place for contusion or partial transection: perform retrograde pyelography or CT
   urography before removing the stent; at retrograde study, exchange the stent for a guidewire to keep
   access across the bladder and ureter.
     ◦ Persistent extravasation despite an adequate period of stenting indicates persistent injury and is an
       indication for reimplantation.
     ◦ Define it by passing a ureteral catheter to the renal pelvis over the wire and injecting contrast during
       gradual withdrawal, opacifying the whole ureter.
Concept — The whole work-up measures one distance — from healthy, well-vascularised ureter to bladder —
and makes two exclusions: malignancy, and a kidney not worth saving.

Surgical Technique
 • Because most adult cases are for injury, stricture, or reimplantation during partial cystectomy or
   diverticulectomy, dissection of the intramural ureter is generally unnecessary. The ureter is divided
   proximal to the diseased portion and the distal stump tied.
 • Where ureteral length is inadequate, advancement flaps from the bladder wall achieve a tension-free
   anastomosis.



                                                   page 11 of 37
Chapter 124 — Bladder Surgery for Benign Disease                                               URETERAL REIMPLANTATION
 • A nonrefluxing anastomosis is generally unnecessary in adults, because VUR is rarely the indication.
   Studies show that a refluxing anastomosis does not accelerate renal decline or increase stenosis
   compared with a nonrefluxing one. Infection risk is less studied, but long-term adult data do not suggest
   an increase.

 • Cardinal principles, whatever the technique:
     ◦ Handle the ureter delicately to minimise devascularisation.
     ◦ During extravesical mobilisation, preserve the periureteric fibroadipose tissue and avoid
       skeletonisation, which compromises blood supply.
     ◦ A tension-free ureterovesical anastomosis is paramount. Primary reimplantation is generally feasible
       for distal defects up to 4 cm; for longer defects consider psoas hitch and/or Boari flap. Renal
       mobilisation with downward nephropexy can add up to 5 cm.
     ◦ Avoid excessive angulation at the point of entry into the bladder, which causes postoperative
       obstruction.
     ◦ Insert the ureter along a path directed toward the bladder neck.
 • Choice of approach: open surgery is preferred for trauma, because it allows direct inspection of
   abdomen and pelvis for associated injuries. Iatrogenic injury recognised intraoperatively may be repaired
   by the same approach as the index procedure, depending on the surgeon's comfort and the set-up; in
   robotic or laparoscopic cases, add ports as needed.

Open Technique
Ureteral mobilisation
 • Supine, ASIS over the table break, slight flexion; genitalia and abdomen prepared to just above the
   umbilicus; 16 Fr catheter on the field.
 • Lower midline incision to the symphysis → rectus fascia at the linea alba → recti split → transversalis
   fascia incised into the space of Retzius, taking care not to enter the peritoneum.
 • The bladder is identified by palpating the Foley balloon, and the lateral gutters are developed bluntly with
   a sponge-stick, sweeping perivesical fat medially.
 • Finding the ureter: exposing the great pelvic vessels helps. Trace the external iliac artery proximally to
   the common iliac bifurcation, where the ureter crosses from lateral to medial; alternatively find it
   beneath the obliterated umbilical artery near its origin from the proximal internal iliac artery.
 • Dissect around the ureter with a right-angle clamp and pass a vessel loop; mobilise caudally and cephalad
   without overdissection or devascularisation.
 • For stricture or injury: identify and resect the diseased segment.
     ◦ Clip the distal stump before transection.

Nonrefluxing reimplantation — transvesical approach (modified Politano-Leadbetter)
 • Mobilise the bladder as for the extravesical approach; with the bladder partly distended, make a midline
   anterior cystotomy.
 • Choose the neo-orifice on the posterior wall superior and medial to the native orifice; score the mucosa
   circumferentially to make a defect slightly larger than the ureter (about 1.5 cm).
 • Raise a submucosal tunnel with Metzenbaum scissors starting at the neo-orifice, oriented longitudinally
   toward the bladder neck, three to five times the ureteric lumen in length.
 • Exchange the scissors for a right-angle clamp, beak directed posteriorly, and incise the posterior wall onto
   it — this is the new entry point, cephalad and medial to the native UVJ, which lets the ureter run almost
   longitudinally through the tunnel.
 • Advance the clamp through the wall, grasp the ureteric stay sutures, and draw the ureter through the
   tunnel and out of the neo-orifice.
 • Spatulate and anastomose to the neo-orifice with 4-0 polyglactin and stent as above; large-bore Foley;
   two-layer 2-0 closure of the cystotomy; fill to test; pelvic drain on self-suction; close.
Refluxing reimplantation — extravesical approach
 • Grasp the dome with Allis clamps; make a transmural cystotomy in the posterior dome lateralised to the
   side of pathology, wide enough to take the ureter.
 • Excise the distal ureter, spatulate anteriorly and anastomose as for the nonrefluxing extravesical
   technique.
 • Large-bore Foley and pelvic drain; irrigate to test the anastomosis.
Refluxing reimplantation — transvesical approach
 • With the bladder partly distended, make an anterior midline cystotomy. A Kelly clamp is passed through it
   and pressed against the posterior dome, lateralised to the side of pathology; a cystotomy is made over
   the clamp large enough for the ureter.
 • The clamp is advanced through the defect, grasps the ureteric stay sutures and draws the ureter in as it is
   withdrawn — creating the new UVJ and neo-orifice.
 • The ureter is spatulated and anastomosed to the neo-orifice mucosa; large-bore Foley; two-layer 2-0
   cystotomy closure; fill to test; closed-suction pelvic drain through a separate stab; close.

 • Minimally invasive distal ureteral reconstruction is widely used; accumulated experience now nearly
   replicates open technique.
 • A transperitoneal extravesical approach gives a wide working space, permits both refluxing and
   nonrefluxing anastomoses, and — because psoas hitch and bladder advancement flaps are feasible
   minimally invasively — allows experienced surgeons to manage longer defects.

Postoperative Care and Complications
 • Foley catheter for about 1 week; cystogram before removal.
 • Pelvic drain usually removed after 48 hours once output falls; drain creatinine is optional and must be
   read in the context of output.
 • Ureteral stent for about 6 weeks, removed cystoscopically.
 • Urinary extravasation (open or minimally invasive) presents with fever and abdominal pain, particularly if
   the urinoma is infected; intraperitoneal urinary ascites causes ileus and electrolyte disturbance.
     ◦ Causes: a non-watertight anastomosis, or ischaemia at the anastomosis.
     ◦ Excretory urography defines site and degree and shows a urinoma, which may need percutaneous
       drainage.
     ◦ Management: keep the Foley and the stent for maximal antegrade drainage; if the leak appears after
       stent removal, replace the stent; if refractory, consider a percutaneous nephrostomy.
 • Ureteral obstruction is a late complication, typically after stent removal — found incidentally
   (hydronephrosis on ultrasound) or with flank pain or pyelonephritis.
     ◦ Causes: ureteral ischaemia from excessive skeletonisation, tension on the anastomosis, a narrow
       neo-orifice, inadequate anterior spatulation, or prior radiation; less often, a lateral reimplant site
       that kinks on filling.
     ◦ Define the stricture with antegrade and retrograde imaging. Endoscopic management first, especially
       for short strictures; repeat surgery for high-grade obstruction.
 • Other complications: UTI, urinary fistula, bladder spasm, stent colic, wound infection, bowel injury.

Outcomes
 • Open ureteroneocystostomy remains the gold standard for distal ureteral disease in adults, because of
   its versatility: it can stay entirely extraperitoneal, reimplant extravesically or transvesically, construct a
   refluxing or nonrefluxing anastomosis, and readily incorporate psoas hitch or Boari flap for a tension-free
   repair.
 • Large long-term series exist mainly for open surgery, showing favourable outcomes in the great majority
   — hydronephrosis resolved in most, although a minority still needed long-term stenting, and leak
   requiring prolonged drainage was uncommon.
 • Minimally invasive experience continues to grow; small feasibility studies predominate, but a few larger
   intermediate-term studies support efficacy.

PSOAS HITCH AND BOARI FLAP

Description
 • Minimal distal ureteral loss is managed by simple ureteroneocystostomy; larger gaps need more complex
   reconstruction.
 • Psoas hitch (first described in 1960 and developed in 1969): the bladder is drawn cephalad far enough for
   a tension-free anastomosis with healthy ureter.
 • If the hitch alone is not enough, a Boari flap is added to bridge the gap — typically when disease extends
   up to the mid-ureter.
 • Boari flap history: canine model 1894, humans 1947; it can bridge gaps up to 15 cm. Laparoscopic Boari
   flap 2001, robotic 2008; the robot eases the extensive intracorporeal suturing required.
 • These techniques serve mostly the distal and middle thirds; in selected cases they reach the proximal
   ureter or renal pelvis, although ureteroureterostomy, transureteroureterostomy, renal
   autotransplantation or ileal ureteric substitution should then also be considered.

Evaluation and Surgical Indications
 • Indicated when ureteral loss precludes simple ureteroureterostomy or ureteroneocystostomy.

Open Technique
Psoas hitch
 • Mobilise the bladder on the side of the ureteral pathology as for reimplantation. Draw the ipsilateral
   dome cephalad over the ipsilateral psoas above the common iliac bifurcation; lay the mobilised proximal
   ureter on the superolateral dome to confirm a tension-free anastomosis is possible.
 • Extra bladder mobility: divide the contralateral round ligament (women) or vas deferens (men); free the

   relatively immobile part of the bladder, reducing kinking.

Boari flap
 • Outline an oblique flap across the anterior bladder wall with its base on the side of the pathology.
 • Dimensions:
     ◦ base at least 4 cm wide, wider still for longer flaps, to keep the blood supply;
     ◦ apex at least 3 cm wide;
     ◦ length approximately equal to the ureteral defect, plus usually 3 cm more if a tunnel is planned for a
       nonrefluxing anastomosis;
     ◦ length-to-base ratio must not exceed 3:1 — beyond it the flap becomes ischaemic;
     ◦ measure with the bladder non-distended.
 • Incise the outline with electrocautery and raise the flap up to the ureter; anchor its base to the psoas
   tendon as for a hitch. Combining the flap with a psoas hitch shortens the flap needed and preserves
   bladder capacity.
 • Nonrefluxing anastomosis: allow about 3 cm of overlap between flap and ureter; raise a submucosal
   tunnel about 3 cm long (extra- or intravesically); bring the ureter through the posterior wall of the flap,
   off tension; after anastomosis, tubularise the flap and close in two layers of 2-0 polyglactin; anchor the
   ureteric adventitia to the flap's posterior wall with 4-0 polyglactin.
 • Refluxing anastomosis: sew the spatulated ureter directly to the mucosal edges of the tubularised flap
   with 4-0 poliglecaprone; to avoid stenosis, the flap apex should be about three times the ureteric
   diameter.
 • Close the cystotomy in two layers of 2-0 polyglactin.
 • If the flap still falls short: further ureteral dissection (avoiding overdissection), and mobilising the kidney
   downward — up to 5 cm more.

 • Ligation of the contralateral superior vesical artery plus psoas hitch often gives enough mobility for
   ureteroneocystostomy; if not, a Boari flap or advancement flap is made.
 • The anterior flap is cut with electrosurgical scissors or a 10-mm LigaSure, beginning about 2 cm from the
   bladder neck and extending to the ipsilateral dome; apex about 3 cm, base about 4 cm, checking that
   the base is wide enough for vascularity. The bladder can be distended with methylene blue to show the
   outline.
 • Raise the flap cephalad; suture its base to the psoas tendon (or muscle); spatulate the ureter and
   anastomose it to the flap apex with interrupted 4-0 absorbable sutures (a refluxing anastomosis).
 • Place a 7 Fr double-J stent over a guidewire; close the flap in two running layers of 2-0 polyglactin (or
   with the Endo Stitch device), tubularising it; fill to test; closed-suction drain through a lateral trocar.
Bladder advancement flaps
 • The laparoscopic bladder advancement flap (2005) was introduced as a simplified alternative to the
   Boari flap.
 • The bladder is opened by a transverse incision on the anterior surface one-third of the distance from the
   dome to the bladder neck; as the incision widens, the flap is advanced to the ureter, and the spatulated
   ureter is anastomosed to it as for a Boari flap.
 • Because of its simplicity, versatility, and reach comparable to the Boari flap, an advancement flap may
   be used instead of a Boari flap for longer defects not amenable to primary reimplantation or psoas hitch
   — a large ("mega") advancement flap can reach even a defect extending to the proximal ureter.

Postoperative Care and Complications
 • Management and complications follow those of ureteroneocystostomy.
 • Additional complications:
     ◦ Neurapraxia of the genitofemoral or femoral nerve from hitch sutures — prevented by superficial
       sutures in the tendon, in the direction of the fibres.
     ◦ Iliac vessel injury as the bladder is drawn over the common iliac bifurcation.
     ◦ Ischaemic anastomotic stricture of a Boari flap with a narrow base relative to its length — prevented
       by respecting the 3:1 ratio.
     ◦ Reduced bladder capacity after a Boari flap, with irritative symptoms: urgency, frequency and
       incontinence.

Outcomes
 • Open psoas hitch has been used since the mid-20th century to gain up to 5 cm of length. Long-term
   series, mostly of operative ureteral injury, show high success (no further intervention, symptom relief,
   stable renal function), with no difference between immediate and delayed repair.
 • The Boari flap bridges defects approaching 15 cm, sparing the patient more complex, higher-risk
   procedures such as ileal interposition or autotransplantation; series of mostly gynaecological injuries
   show resolution of obstruction in all, with high success and low morbidity in contemporary series.
 • Laparoscopic psoas hitch reproduces open results — including for ureteral endometriosis and
   gynaecological injury — with no obstruction or reflux at follow-up; proper intracorporeal suturing
   technique is stressed as critical. Contemporary laparoscopic series now report hitch cases together with
   primary reimplants, showing it has entered the routine minimally invasive repertoire.

 • Laparoscopic Boari flap: first series showed feasibility; larger series confirmed high success, with
   complications including anastomotic leak, pulmonary embolism, small-bowel obstruction and

Concept — In distal ureteral reconstruction the bladder is the donor tissue: hitch it first, flap it second — and
every flap rule (base width, the 3:1 ratio, measuring the bladder empty) exists to protect the flap's blood
supply and the bladder's capacity.

 KEY POINTS: PSOAS HITCH AND BOARI FLAP
   • Hitch sutures fixing the bladder to the psoas minor tendon are placed superficially and parallel to the
     muscle fibres to avoid entrapping the femoral and genitofemoral nerves.
   • Reimplant the ureter before tying the psoas anchoring sutures.
   • The Boari flap base must be at least 4 cm, wider for longer flaps; length-to-base ratio not above 3:1.
   • A nonrefluxing anastomosis into a Boari flap needs at least 3 cm of overlap between flap and ureter.
   • Owing to their simplicity and versatility, advancement flaps may replace Boari flaps for longer defects.


ENTEROCYSTOPLASTY

Description
 • In adults, bladder augmentation is most often indicated for neurogenic bladder (congenital or acquired)
   after more conservative attempts to improve compliance have failed. Less often it follows partial
   cystectomy that has substantially reduced capacity.
 • The intrinsic distensibility of bowel lowers intravesical pressure and restores reservoir function — but
   only if the segment is adequately detubularised, which abolishes the coordinated peristaltic contractions
   that would otherwise defeat the purpose.
 • Traditionally open, the operation has gradually moved toward minimally invasive techniques;
   ileocystoplasty has become the most widely used. Laparoscopic, robotic and LESS augmentation are
   reproducible by purely intracorporeal or partly extracorporeal approaches.

Evaluation and Surgical Indications
 • As non-invasive treatment has improved, augmentation for neurogenic bladder has declined. The
   mainstay is clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC), anticholinergics, intravesical botulinum toxin
   injection and sacral neuromodulation.
 • Augment when noncompliance persists despite these measures, particularly with upper tract
   deterioration, hydronephrosis, or incontinence severe enough to cause skin ulceration.
 • Urodynamics: the detrusor leak point pressure is a strong indicator of the bladder's elastic properties and
   of the risk of renal deterioration. Video-urodynamics is preferred — it shows bladder contour, associated
   anomalies (for example diverticula) and VUR at the same time.
 • Ultrasonography routinely: hydronephrosis or parenchymal atrophy indicates poor wall compliance.



                                                   page 21 of 37
Chapter 124 — Bladder Surgery for Benign Disease                                                          ENTEROCYSTOPLASTY
 • After partial cystectomy, frequency, urgency, nocturia or incontinence may signal a significant loss of
   capacity. Conservative measures first, as for neurogenic dysfunction; augment only for refractory
   symptoms. Urodynamics characterises function and response; in men, cystoscopy excludes concomitant
   outlet obstruction from BPH or urethral stricture.
 • Metabolic screening: bowel in the urinary tract can cause metabolic derangement, so obtain a full serum
   chemistry.
     ◦ With ileum, screen carefully for vitamin B12 and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency.
     ◦ Urine–intestinal mucosa interaction disturbs pH, a special concern in renal failure where pH regulation
       is impaired. Because ileum is used most, the principal concern is chronic metabolic acidosis and the
       resulting bone demineralisation.
 • Bowel history:
     ◦ Crohn disease or previous intestinal surgery risks short gut syndrome after further resection.
     ◦ Prior radiation may prevent isolation of a segment with a durable blood supply.
 • Catheterisation remains necessary in most augmented patients, so manual dexterity and anatomical
   ability to catheterise must be confirmed beforehand, especially in neurogenic dysfunction.
 • Concomitant procedures:
     ◦ Stress urinary incontinence (SUI): raise outlet resistance at the same sitting — artificial urinary
       sphincter, urethral sling or bladder neck closure.
     ◦ Continent catheterisable stoma: bladder neck closure may be planned, particularly with incontinence.
     ◦ Nonrefluxing reimplantation for high-grade reflux when there is renal dysfunction and concern that
       intravesical pressure may stay high despite augmentation.

Laparoscopic and Robotic Techniques
 • Cystoscopy with placement of bilateral open-ended or single-J ureteral catheters, secured to a urethral
   catheter; pneumoperitoneum.
 • Three to five trocars according to experience — for example an umbilical port, one at each lateral rectus
   border at umbilical level, and one in the iliac fossa at the level of the ASIS (four-port transperitoneal
   technique).
 • Mobilise the bladder fully — incise peritoneum lateral to both obliterated umbilical ligaments, divide the
   urachus, drop the bladder into the space of Retzius.
 • Choose a 15–20 cm segment with an adequate vascular pedicle that will reach the bladder neck. Bowel
   work is done purely laparoscopically or extracorporeally through an extended umbilical incision.
 • Harvest with the Endo GIA stapler; divide mesentery with further reloads or a 10-mm LigaSure; restore
   continuity by side-to-side stapled anastomosis, completed with a TA stapler (extracorporeal) or Lembert
   sutures (intracorporeal); close the mesenteric window ("trap") to prevent internal herniation.
 • Irrigate, excise the staple lines, open the segment along the antimesenteric border, and form the U with
   running 2-0 polyglactin.
 • Fill the bladder and bivalve it in the midsagittal plane; bring the segment down without tension or
   torsion of its pedicle.
 • Orient the segment with the apex of the U anteriorly at the bladder neck; sew it to the bladder with
   running 2-0 polyglactin, beginning posteriorly and ending anteriorly (continuous full-thickness freehand
   suturing).
 • Confirm integrity by irrigation via the Foley; place a pelvic drain through a port.

Postoperative Care and Complications
 • Foley catheter about 1 week; cystogram before removal. Pelvic drain removed at about 48 hours once
   output falls; drain creatinine optional, read in context of output.
 • Complications shared with diverticulectomy:
     ◦ Urinary extravasation across the suture lines from inadequate suturing or ischaemia of the intestinal
       flap — hence the need to preserve a robust vascular arcade at harvest. Presentation and management
       are as for diverticulectomy.
     ◦ UTI, bowel injury and urinary fistula.
     ◦ Intra-abdominal infection, particularly from irrigation and detubularisation of the bowel if proper
       isolation precautions are not taken.
 • Complications unique to enterocystoplasty: perforation, stones, metabolic derangement and neoplasia.
     ◦ Perforation: regular intermittent catheterisation is imperative — failure to catheterise risks
       perforation. Suspect it with abdominal pain, fever or acute distension, and obtain a CT cystogram
       with cross-sectional imaging; keep a high index of suspicion in neurogenic patients, whose sensory
       deficits may mask it. Perforation is a surgical emergency requiring exploration.
     ◦ Dexterity decides the reconstruction: a patient whose habitus prevents catheterisation at the bladder
       neck may be better served by a continent ileocaecocystoplasty; a patient unable to catheterise, or
       poorly compliant, can have an incontinent ileovesicostomy.
     ◦ Stones are multifactorial: mucus from the bowel, heavier bacterial colonisation and infection, and
       stasis. Irrigation is thought to reduce retained mucus, though its true benefit is debated; treat
       symptomatic infections with antibiotics.
     ◦ Neoplasia: increased cancer risk has been reported in augmented bladders — both adenocarcinoma
       and urothelial carcinoma. Surveillance endoscopy is recommended from 10 years after surgery.
     ◦ Routine blood tests screen for metabolic derangement and vitamin deficiency.

Outcomes
 • Open augmentation is widely used for neurogenic dysfunction; because minimally invasive techniques
   are recent, long-term data come mainly from open surgery.
 • Open series — bottom lines:
     ◦ the earliest ileal and caecal series showed high continence at medium-term follow-up;
     ◦ long-term (over a decade) follow-up after ileocystoplasty for spinal cord injury showed greater
       capacity, lower pressure, very high satisfaction and no change in bowel or sexual function — but
       complications at some point were common, mostly bladder stones;
     ◦ another long-term cohort showed near-universal resolution of incontinence, larger capacity, a
       substantial fall in maximum detrusor pressure and near-universal satisfaction — but about half needed
       further procedures.
 • Verdict: open augmentation is effective for neurogenic patients with refractory symptoms (incontinence,
   overactivity) or at risk of upper tract damage from high pressure; long-term complications are relatively
   frequent but mostly low-morbidity and manageable by secondary intervention.

CONTINENT CATHETERISABLE CHANNEL

Description
 • A continent catheterisable channel made from a harvested bowel segment can be used in children and
   adults to improve bladder dynamics and protect the upper tracts, generally in neuropathic bladder with
   adequate capacity that needs chronic catheterisation.
 • It also provides alternative bladder access, for example in refractory urethral stricture disease.
 • Channels can be combined with augmentation when compliance or capacity is poor.
 • Channel types: appendicovesicostomy (Mitrofanoff) and retubularised ileum (Yang-Monti). Vas
   deferens, colon and stomach have been used, but are technically challenging or associated with poorer
   outcomes.

Evaluation and Surgical Indications
 • Patient selection is paramount. Take a thorough medical and surgical history: inflammatory bowel
   disease and prior radiation; previous small-bowel resection or appendicectomy may limit available
   bowel.
 • Physical examination shows whether the patient has the dexterity to catheterise and whether body
   habitus limits options; understanding the neurological condition also shapes the recommendation.
 • Urinalysis, serum creatinine and upper tract imaging establish baseline renal function and drainage.
 • Video-urodynamics may be needed to confirm adequate capacity and safe storage pressure and to
   detect anatomical abnormalities or VUR.
 • Candidates have usually failed more conservative measures, including clean intermittent urethral
   catheterisation.

Surgical Techniques
 • A channel needs enough length to reach from bladder to stoma site while keeping its blood supply and
   avoiding angulation. Common stoma sites: the right lower quadrant or the umbilicus.
 • Continence depends on submucosal tunnelling of the channel through a sufficient length of detrusor.
 • Every channel operation needs a fallback plan — the appendix may be absent or too short, or the ileal
   segment may not reach the skin.
Appendicovesicostomy
 • First described with a submucosal detrusor tunnel by Mitrofanoff in 1980.
 • The appendix is often ideal: a natural hollow tube of the right calibre for catheterisation, whose use in the
   urinary tract causes no metabolic derangement.
 • Access by lower midline or Pfannenstiel incision. The appendix is checked for adequate length to span
   bladder to stoma; if more is needed, the caecum and ascending colon are mobilised.
 • The appendix is detached proximally with a small cuff of caecum, adding length and a wider stoma; the
   caecal opening is closed.
 • The distal end is opened and spatulated for anastomosis to the bladder; submucosal tunnelling is
   essential for continence and can be done at various sites on the bladder, intravesically or extravesically.
Reconfigured ileum (Yang-Monti channel)
 • Used when the appendix is absent, too short, or needed for another purpose (such as a continence
   enema).
 • A 2–4 cm segment of terminal ileum is isolated and detubularised on its antimesenteric border; the
   ileum is re-anastomosed.
 • The segment is retubularised transversely over a catheter, producing a longer, narrower tube.
 • For more length a second reconfigured segment can be joined — but this is associated with more
   channel diverticula and difficult catheterisation. A spiral Monti also adds length but has a higher revision
   rate than the standard channel.

 • Reporting is sparse; patients are generally admitted. Pelvic drains are monitored and removed before
   discharge; drain creatinine can be checked for high output.

 • Continence rates are high — generally above 95% — regardless of channel type.
 • The commonest complication is stomal stenosis, in both appendicovesicostomy and ileal channels.

PARTIAL CYSTECTOMY AND URACHAL SURGERY

Description
 • Partial cystectomy is used mostly in selected patients with muscle-invasive urothelial carcinoma or
   urachal adenocarcinoma suitable for bladder preservation; it also has several benign indications.
 • Most benign bladder tumours are managed conservatively (removing the offending stimulus) or by
   transurethral resection (TUR). Tumours that grow large, infiltrate locally, or cause refractory local
   symptoms may need resection — most commonly leiomyoma and paraganglioma.
 • Other benign indications: bladder endometriosis, urachal anomalies, and colovesical or vesicovaginal
   fistulae that have failed conservative management.

Evaluation and Surgical Indications
 • Selection criteria are less stringent than for malignancy: tumour multiplicity, premalignant lesions such as
   carcinoma in situ (CIS), and recurrence history matter less.
 • The primary question is whether resection will reduce capacity enough to harm quality of life — so
   avoid partial cystectomy in patients with a small-capacity bladder or severe dysfunctional voiding at
   baseline.
 • Malignancy must be excluded, because the operation differs (for cancer: wide margins and lymph node
   dissection). A cancer-type work-up is therefore usual: cross-sectional excretory-phase imaging, cytology,
   cystoscopy and, in most cases, biopsy for histological diagnosis.
 • Tumour location affects feasibility — lesions near the bladder base are harder; a lesion near the ureteric
   orifice may require simultaneous reimplantation.
Leiomyoma
 • Rare smooth-muscle tumour, reported mainly in women of childbearing age; histologically like the
   uterine fibroid. Aetiology uncertain — hormonal imbalance or chronic detrusor irritation is suspected.
 • Can grow large, causing outlet or ureteral obstruction, irritative voiding symptoms, pelvic pain and
   haematuria.
 • Growth may be predominantly endovesical, intramural or extravesical (a well-circumscribed lesion within
   the detrusor on CT); transvaginal ultrasound, and less often MRI, characterises it.
 • Small endovesical lesions → TUR; large or intramural lesions → wide local resection to minimise
   regrowth.
Paraganglioma
 • An extra-adrenal tumour of sympathetic chromaffin cells, commonest along the sympathetic chain;
   benign bladder tumours occur.
 • Most are functional, releasing catecholamines — episodic hypertension, tachycardia, palpitations,
   sweating or tremor. Unique to bladder paraganglioma: hypotension and syncope with micturition or
   sexual activity.
 • Despite this, they often present non-specifically — painless haematuria, or bladder wall thickening found
   incidentally on cross-sectional imaging.
 • Instrumentation or TUR without preoperative α-adrenergic blockade can provoke a dangerous
   intraoperative catecholamine surge, especially in asymptomatic patients.
 • If suspected: plasma metanephrines and functional imaging — a positive metaiodobenzylguanidine
   (MIBG) scan confirms a functional mass.
 • Cystoscopically: yellow areas of raised mucosa, because the tumour lies within the detrusor.

 • Partial cystectomy is preferred to TUR even for benign lesions, because of a high local recurrence rate
   after incomplete excision.
Endometriosis
 • Bladder endometriosis may be primary or iatrogenic (dissemination at pelvic surgery).
 • Classic symptoms: cyclical haematuria or irritative voiding symptoms coinciding with menses.
 • Involvement is generally extravesical, so cystoscopy is of limited value (it shows only mucosal
   involvement — a bluish submucosal lesion with neovascularisation). Transabdominal ultrasound, often
   with transvaginal ultrasound, gauges size and depth; MRI delineates extent (high signal on T1-weighted
   images).
 • Partial cystectomy is the primary treatment, again because of the extravesical distribution.
Urachal abnormalities
 • The urachus, the embryological remnant of the allantois, provides ligamentous support from dome to
   umbilicus. Urachal disease is commonest in children, but symptoms can appear late in adults.
 • Adult conditions that may warrant partial cystectomy: urachal cyst, vesicourachal diverticulum, patent
   urachus and urachal sinus.
 • Communication with the bladder (vesicourachal diverticulum, patent urachus) causes haematuria,
   mucinuria or urine leakage from the umbilicus. Infection of stagnant urine or fluid causes fever,
   abdominal pain, or a periumbilical or infraumbilical fluctuant mass; a calculus may form.
 • The gravest concern is malignant transformation of remnant urachal tissue.
 • Evaluation: most anomalies are asymptomatic and incidental on cross-sectional imaging. Symptomatic
   ones are assessed by ultrasound or CT; VCUG or sinogram shows communication with the bladder,
   especially with urinary symptoms or skin drainage; the dome and anterior wall are inspected
   cystoscopically for suspicious lesions.
 • Treatment is indicated for symptomatic or infected anomalies:
     ◦ swab the umbilicus and send urine culture before starting antibiotics →
     ◦ oral or intravenous antibiotics →
     ◦ incision and drainage of a fluctuant umbilicus →
     ◦ complete resolution → no further intervention; recurrent infection → surgical excision.
 • Excise the entire urachus with a small cuff of the bladder dome — including a bladder cuff ensures no
   residual tissue near the apex that could cause recurrence or malignant transformation.

Surgical Technique
 • Open partial cystectomy remains the most widely used, given accumulated experience; laparoscopic and
   robotic techniques are gaining ground for benign as well as malignant disease, as a low-morbidity
   alternative for lesions needing more extensive resection than endoscopy can safely achieve.

Open Technique
 • Lesion site decides the route: dome or anterior wall → extraperitoneal; posterior wall →
   intraperitoneal.
Extraperitoneal partial cystectomy
 • Opening steps as for open reimplantation — positioning, preparation, incision and extraperitoneal
   bladder mobilisation. Pelvic lymph node dissection is usually unnecessary for benign disease.
 • With the bladder mobilised bilaterally, intraoperative cystoscopy confirms the site of an intravesical
   lesion; intraoperative ultrasound marks the site and extent of an extravesical or submucosal
   (intradetrusor) lesion.
 • Stay sutures away from the lesion; a longitudinal cystotomy opens the bladder. The bladder may be
   partly distended as it is opened, but the fluid must be aspirated to avoid spillage — even some benign
   processes recur locally. The cystotomy is lengthened for good exposure, and the mucosa is inspected to
   exclude malignancy.
 • Circumferential transmural excision around the lesion with electrocautery. Even for benign disease, a
   margin of normal bladder is necessary because some lesions recur after inadequate resection. Preserve
   the perivesical fat over the lesion. If capacity allows, a 2-cm margin is recommended; biopsies of
   normal-appearing wall confirm adequate resection.
 • If the lesion involves the ureteric orifice, mobilise and reimplant the ureter (refluxing or nonrefluxing).
 • Close the defect in two running layers of 2-0 polyglactin (mucosa first), then the cystotomy likewise.
 • Perivesical drain in the retropubic space, ideally crossing the defect and cystotomy, through a stab lateral
   to the rectus; 22 Fr Foley catheter; irrigate to test; close.
Transperitoneal partial cystectomy
 • Skin, rectus and transversalis fascia opened; peritoneum entered sharply in the midline; self-retaining
   retractor packs the bowel cephalad, exposing the peritoneal fold over the dome; space of Retzius and
   gutters developed bluntly.
 • Ureteral mobilisation is often needed, because resection of the posterior wall may require
   reimplantation: incise the peritoneum over the iliac bifurcation, identify the ureter and dissect it caudally,
   preserving periureteric tissue and its blood supply.
 • Mobilise the posterior bladder enough for resection: in women, incise the peritoneal fold over the
   vesicouterine pouch and develop the pubocervical fascia; in men, incise the rectovesical pouch
   peritoneum to develop Denonvilliers fascia, especially for lesions near the base.
 • Resect as for the extraperitoneal approach.
Urachal surgery (open)
 • Periumbilical incision carried down the midline; after the transversalis fascia, the peritoneum is
   identified.
 • A circumferential incision around the umbilicus is taken down to peritoneum; the peritoneal cavity is
   entered above the umbilicus; with traction on the umbilical segment, the urachus is excised with
   generous peritoneal wings down to the dome.

 • The bladder is distended with sterile water to allow a transmural circumferential incision around the
   urachal insertion. A wide margin is unnecessary for benign disease.
 • Two-layer 2-0 polyglactin closure; large-bore Foley and pelvic drain; irrigate to test.
 • When the remnant is excised for infection or inflammation, the umbilicus need not be removed in the
   absence of malignancy, provided subcutaneous fluid has been properly drained.

Laparoscopic and Robotic Approaches
Transperitoneal partial cystectomy
 • Positioning and ports as for robotic diverticulectomy.
 • Mobilise the bladder off the anterior abdominal wall (incise lateral to the obliterated umbilical ligaments,
   divide the urachus). The bedside assistant performs flexible cystoscopy to transilluminate the bladder
   and show the lesion from the peritoneal side.
 • Posterior-wall lesions: leave the bladder suspended from the anterior wall; cystoscopy identifies the site
   and the overlying peritoneum is incised directly. Intramural or extravesical lesions are characterised with
   a laparoscopic ultrasound probe.
 • Outline the resection on the outer bladder surface with electrocautery to ensure adequate margins;
   remove the cystoscope, place a Foley, clamp it to distend the bladder.
 • Longitudinal cystotomy superior/anterior to the lesion, aspirating the fluid on entry to avoid spillage;
   deflate the balloon; inspect the mucosa, note the lesion and its relation to the ureteric orifices. A
   retrograde ureteral catheter protects the ureter, or reimplant after resection if the ureter is at risk.
 • Full-thickness circumferential excision from inside, preserving perivesical fat and taking an adequate
   normal-mucosal margin; specimen into a 10 or 15 cm EndoCatch bag; frozen sections of the margins
   may be sent.
 • Large-bore Foley; two-layer 2-0 polyglactin closure of defect and cystotomy; fill to test and reinforce with
   full-thickness figure-of-eight 2-0 sutures; drain through the lateral port; extract via an extended camera-
   port incision; close the extraction-site and 12-mm port fascia with figure-of-eight 0-polyglactin.
Urachal surgery (minimally invasive)
 • 12-mm camera port 5 cm above and 2 cm to the left of the umbilicus.
 • Unlike open surgery, the urachus is dissected out last: the bladder is left attached to the anterior
   abdominal wall during the first part of the dissection to maximise exposure; filling the bladder with 200–
   300 mL defines the anatomy.
 • A small rim of bladder around the urachal insertion is scored and taken through all layers except the
   mucosa; the entire urachus with its peritoneal wings is dissected up to the umbilicus and divided there
   (umbilicus removal optional for benign disease).
 • The bladder is then opened and the wall excised circumferentially around the insertion; specimen into an
   EndoCatch bag. Frozen section of the bladder margin is unnecessary for benign disease.
 • Two-layer 2-0 closure; Foley and water test; drain via the lateral port; specimen out through an extended
   camera incision or directly through the 12-mm lateral assistant port; close fascia with figure-of-eight 0-
   polyglactin.

Postoperative Care and Complications
 • Foley catheter about 1 week; cystogram before removal; pelvic drain removed after about 48 hours once
   output falls; drain creatinine optional.
 • Complications are similar for open and minimally invasive surgery and mirror those of diverticulectomy:
   urinary extravasation from the resection or cystotomy site is commonest, followed by ureteric injury —
   presentation and management as described for diverticulectomy.
 • Patient selection and counselling matter, because resecting bladder wall can reduce capacity and cause
   irritative symptoms needing later intervention.
 • Other complications: UTI, bladder spasm, urinary fistula, wound infection, bowel injury.

Outcomes
 • Experience is limited to small case series, given the rarity of these conditions in adults.
 • Nevertheless, partial cystectomy — open or minimally invasive — achieves high cure rates while
   preserving bladder function.
Concept — Benign partial cystectomy borrows the discipline of cancer surgery — exclude malignancy, avoid
spillage, take a margin — because leiomyoma, paraganglioma and endometriosis recur when incompletely
excised; urachal excision for benign disease is the exception, where the whole tract matters and a wide
margin does not.

 KEY POINTS: PARTIAL CYSTECTOMY AND URACHAL SURGERY
   • Confirm adequate bladder capacity and no significant voiding symptoms before partial cystectomy.
   • Benign disease still needs adequate resection margins, given the infiltrative nature of many conditions
     and their tendency to recur after inadequate excision.
   • In minimally invasive partial cystectomy, intraoperative flexible cystoscopy by the bedside assistant
     identifies the site of pathology.
   • Urachal surgery for benign disease does not require a wide margin of dome around the urachal
     insertion.


SIMPLE/SUPRATRIGONAL CYSTECTOMY

Description
 • For neurogenic or end-stage bladder dysfunction refractory to less invasive treatment, a simple
   cystectomy with urinary diversion may be performed; in selected cases a diversion without cystectomy
   is an alternative.
 • Because cystectomy is irreversible and can carry severe complications, it is usually a last resort — to
   preserve renal function or improve quality of life.




                                                     page 32 of 37
Chapter 124 — Bladder Surgery for Benign Disease                                     SIMPLE/SUPRATRIGONAL CYSTECTOMY

Evaluation and Surgical Indications
 • Evaluation is multifaceted and usually follows trials of several non-invasive treatments: thorough history
   and examination, symptom assessment, postvoid residual (PVR), and often urodynamics, cystoscopy and
   imaging.
 • It is essential that the bladder or its outlet is the cause of the problem — patients with bladder pain
   syndrome (BPS) may continue to feel bladder pain after cystectomy.
 • Common benign indications: radiation-induced bladder dysfunction or cystitis; neurogenic bladder from
   spinal cord injury or multiple sclerosis; refractory urinary incontinence; urinary fistula; bladder neck
   contracture; refractory vesicourethral anastomotic stricture.
 • In interstitial cystitis (IC)/BPS, selection is key. Factors associated with success: a small, fibrotic bladder,
   a small bladder capacity under anaesthesia, and Hunner lesions.

Surgical Technique
 • Principles: mobilise the bladder, divide the bladder pedicles, and amputate the bladder near the level of
   the trigone. The bladder is often opened during the operation to identify the trigone and bladder neck.
   The remaining tissue at the base is oversewn or covered with peritoneum. Ileal conduit diversion is
   performed in the usual way.

Open technique
 • Lower midline incision → rectus fascia → space of Retzius → peritoneum opened → ureters identified and

 • The bladder may be filled through the catheter to expose the dome; the detrusor is incised and the

   posteriorly to the cul-de-sac.
 • The lateral segments are amputated with the coagulating instrument; any remaining mucosa is cauterised
   or excised.
 • The posterior peritoneum can be preserved to cover the urethral defect.
Laparoscopic and robotic approaches
 • Described, but reports are limited.
 • Patient secured, pressure points padded; supine in steep Trendelenburg; trocars into the peritoneal cavity
   directed to the pelvis.
 • The dome is identified and bivalved to the bladder neck; the pedicles are divided; mucosa is cauterised or
   excised. Very friable bladder tissue may be removed piecemeal.
 • Diversion is completed intracorporeally or extracorporeally.

Postoperative Care and Complications
 • Care generally mirrors radical cystectomy, depending on the diversion.
 • Closed-suction pelvic drains commonly stay about 5–7 days, removed when output is low and/or drain
   creatinine matches serum creatinine. Uncomplicated stay about 5–7 days; many surgeons await return of
   bowel function before discharge.
 • In patients with refractory benign dysfunction, simple cystectomy with diversion improves long-term
   quality of life.
 • Complication rates are substantial and vary with comorbidity and previous treatment (major
   complications, graded by the Clavien–Dindo system, are common). Commonest: wound infection, UTI,
   blood transfusion and ileus.
 • Diversion without cystectomy risks pyocystis, bleeding, bladder pain and secondary urothelial
   carcinoma; in IC/BPS, a substantial minority later need completion cystectomy.
 • Several reports suggest robot-assisted surgery may reduce complications compared with open or
   laparoscopic approaches.
Concept — Simple cystectomy is chosen, never defaulted to: it is irreversible, so its success depends on
proving the bladder itself is the source — which is why a small, fibrotic bladder with Hunner lesions predicts
success, while pain that is not truly vesical persists after the bladder has gone.

 KEY POINTS: SIMPLE CYSTECTOMY
   • In end-stage bladder dysfunction refractory to conservative measures, simple cystectomy with
     urinary diversion may be offered.
   • Completion cystectomy may be required if diversion is initially performed without cystectomy.


BLADDER STONES, FISTULAE, AND FOREIGN-BODY REMOVAL

Description
 • Bladder calculi are far less common than renal and ureteral stones but can seriously disrupt quality of
   life.
 • The primary cause is urinary stasis; bladder stones therefore occur mostly with bladder outlet
   obstruction — particularly BPH in men — or neurogenic bladder.
 • Less commonly they form after bladder substitution or augmentation with bowel, from incomplete
   emptying and stasis, a high bacterial burden, metabolic changes in urinary composition, and possibly
   increased mucus production.
 • A foreign body in the bladder, usually introduced iatrogenically, can act as a nidus for stone formation.

Evaluation and Surgical Indications
 • Most bladder stones are asymptomatic and are found during work-up of associated conditions.
   Otherwise haematuria, irritative voiding, recurrent UTI, incontinence or urinary retention prompt
   cystoscopy or imaging.
 • Before surgery, evaluate both the stone burden (which decides the management strategy) and the
   concomitant conditions that caused the stone — so that recurrence can be prevented and any
   concomitant procedure planned.




                                                   page 34 of 37
Chapter 124 — Bladder Surgery for Benign Disease                    BLADDER STONES, FISTULAE, AND FOREIGN-BODY REMOVAL
 • Non-contrast CT gives the most reliable measurement of stone size and volume, which guides treatment
   selection. Cross-sectional imaging also shows related anatomy — prostate volume in BPH, or
   hydronephrosis in neurogenic bladder.
 • Cystoscopy before intervention is not necessary unless otherwise indicated — gross haematuria,
   suspected malignancy, or assessment of prostatic lobar hyperplasia and prostatic urethral length.
 • Urodynamics if neurogenic bladder is suspected, or as part of treatment planning in BPH.

Surgical Technique
 • Open cystolithotomy has historically been the gold standard for large bladder calculi. Transurethral and,
   more recently, percutaneous endoscopic techniques have largely replaced it, mainly for cosmesis and
   shorter catheterisation.
 • Open surgery still suits very large stones or a high-volume burden — maximising stone clearance and
   minimising operating time, because prolonged transurethral instrumentation risks urethral stricture.
 • Concomitant procedures often choose the route:
     ◦ a very large prostate unsuitable for transurethral resection or vaporisation → simple prostatectomy,
       ideally transvesical, with cystolithotomy in tandem;
     ◦ a large diverticulum needing diverticulectomy to reduce stasis → cystolithotomy;
     ◦ a poorly compliant neurogenic bladder refractory to anticholinergics or intravesical botulinum toxin
       → enterocystoplasty, with cystolithotomy at the same time.
 • Previous bladder or abdominal surgery may alter pelvic anatomy enough to preclude a percutaneous
   approach, because of the risk of bowel injury at puncture and tract formation — particularly relevant
   with a urinary diversion or augmented bladder.
 • Laparoscopic and robotic cystolithotomy offer a minimally invasive alternative without compromising
   removal of a large burden; growing experience with minimally invasive transvesical simple prostatectomy
   and diverticulectomy makes this attractive when several procedures are needed at one sitting.
 • Percutaneous cystolithotripsy is another minimally invasive option for large calculi:
     ◦ a single small suprapubic incision — better cosmesis than open surgery and lower morbidity, with a
       smaller cystotomy than open or laparoscopic surgery;
     ◦ shorter catheterisation and hospital stay;
     ◦ the nephroscope's larger calibre and wider working channel improve vision and allow a versatile
       range of instruments, giving more efficient fragmentation and shorter operating time than
       transurethral cystoscopic procedures;
     ◦ it virtually eliminates the risk of urethral stricture associated with prolonged transurethral
       cystolitholapaxy.
 • Foreign bodies can be removed by open surgery, laparoscopically (transperitoneal, or transvesical LESS)
   or robotically. Reported objects include surgical materials and mesh, migrated intrauterine devices,
   broken sewing needles and electrical wires.

Open Technique
 • Supine, ASIS over the table break, slight flexion; genitalia and infraumbilical abdomen prepared; 18 Fr
   Foley, bladder filled with about 200 mL of sterile water, catheter clamped.
 • Midline infraumbilical or transverse Pfannenstiel incision — the choice depends partly on whether a
   concomitant procedure (diverticulectomy, simple prostatectomy) is planned.
 • Rectus fascia divided; recti split in the midline; space of Retzius developed bluntly (distension displaces
   the peritoneal reflection cephalad). Extensive lateral mobilisation is unnecessary.
 • Vertical cystotomy across the midline dome toward the bladder neck, aspirating as it lengthens; 2-0
   polyglactin stay sutures at the apices prevent tearing; deep retractor blades for exposure.
 • Remove the stones (or foreign bodies) under direct vision. Inspect the bladder for suspicious mucosal
   lesions and an intravesical prostatic lobe causing obstruction; perform any additional procedure for
   outlet obstruction (suprapubic prostatectomy) or stasis (diverticulectomy) now.
 • Large-bore catheter; two-layer running 2-0 polyglactin closure — mucosa first, then imbricating sutures
   burying the mucosal layer. A suprapubic catheter is optional. Pelvic drain anterior to the cystotomy in the
   retropubic space on self-suction; close fascia and skin.

Laparoscopic and Robotic Approaches
 • The transperitoneal approach is the most straightforward for laparoscopic or robotic cystolithotomy or
   foreign-body excision; positioning and ports as for robotic diverticulectomy; a fourth robotic arm is
   optional.
 • Mobilise the bladder off the anterior wall → anterior vertical or transverse cystotomy → ureteral
   catheterisation if needed → remove stone or excise foreign body → two-layer 2-0 polyglactin closure →
   large-bore Foley and water test → pelvic drain over the cystotomy on self-suction.
 • Transvesical multi-trocar approach (avoids the peritoneal cavity): cystoscopy and saline filling → 5-mm
   trocar placed into the bladder under vision → bladder drained → pneumovesicum at 8–12 mm Hg →
   two further trocars → stone removed or foreign body (for example eroded mesh) excised → bladder
   closed with absorbable suture.
 • Transvesical single-site approach: cystoscopy and saline filling → single-site port deployed directly into
   the bladder → pneumovesicum → foreign body identified and excised → bladder closed; a transurethral
   grasper helps retraction.
 • da Vinci SP robot: not described in isolation, but after transvesical access it can remove stones,
   intravesical mesh and foreign bodies, or repair fistulae. SP transvesical stone removal at the time of
   simple prostatectomy and vesicovaginal fistula repair have been described.

Percutaneous Technique
 • Dorsal lithotomy or supine; perineum and lower abdomen prepared and draped, ideally with a fluid-
   control pouch.
 • Sequence:
     ◦ transurethral Foley; bladder distended with saline →
     ◦ 1-cm skin incision about two fingerbreadths above the pubic symphysis →
     ◦ 18-gauge trocar needle into the bladder; aspirate to confirm — under ultrasound guidance or with
       flexible cystoscopic vision. After previous abdominal surgery, use preoperative cross-sectional
       imaging or real-time ultrasound to exclude interposed bowel →
     ◦ remove the inner obturator; return of urine confirms bladder entry →
     ◦ pass an Amplatz Super Stiff PTFE-coated 0.038-inch guidewire, coiling it in the bladder; remove the
       trocar →
     ◦ Amplatz dilators sequentially to 30 Fr; place a 30 Fr sheath across the tract →
     ◦ insert a 24 or 26 Fr nephroscope; the sheath's wider calibre allows continuous irrigation and clear
       vision.
 • Stones under 1 cm: retrieve intact with rigid grasping forceps. Larger stones: fragment with a pneumatic
   or ultrasonic lithotripter under vision, then retrieve fragments with forceps.
 • When cleared, pass a suprapubic catheter over the guidewire to gravity drainage, or leave a large-bore
   urethral Foley instead.
 • Entrapment-bag technique: a laparoscopic entrapment bag is passed through the 30 Fr sheath; with a
   flexible cystoscope the stone is captured and the bag cinched and exteriorised to skin level (sheath
   removed); the bag is opened, the sheath reintroduced into the bag, and the stone fragmented inside it
   with nephroscope and lithotripter; the bag is then pulled out across the tract, and a suprapubic catheter
   placed over the retained guidewire.

Postoperative Care and Complications
 • Foley catheter about 1 week for healing of the closure; pelvic drain monitored — high output may
   indicate a leak; removed at about 48 hours if output is low and catheter drainage adequate. Drain
   creatinine is not needed unless output is high. Cystogram before catheter removal.
 • After percutaneous cystolithotripsy with a suprapubic tube: clamp the tube for 24–48 hours for a trial of
   voiding, and remove it once the patient voids. A urethral catheter, if used instead, is removed 24–48
   hours after the procedure for a trial of voiding.
Concept — Stone surgery treats the stone and the stasis together: stone burden chooses the route, but the
operation needed for the cause — prostatectomy, diverticulectomy, augmentation — often chooses it first,
and prior abdominal surgery can close the percutaneous door.

 KEY POINTS: BLADDER STONE SURGERY
   • The approach depends on the size and number of calculi and on any associated process causing stone
     formation that must be addressed at the same time.
   • Percutaneous cystolithotripsy is a minimally invasive alternative for large calculi, but percutaneous
     access may be prohibited after prior pelvic or abdominal surgery because of the risk of bowel injury.
