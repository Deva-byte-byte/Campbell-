Chapter 141 — Contemporary Open Surgery of the Kidney
HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE
 • Open renal surgery remains an essential tool despite the shift to minimally invasive techniques: advanced
   kidney cancers, anatomically complex tumours, and kidney pathology in patients with significant prior
   abdominal surgery.
 • Technique has evolved with understanding of surgical anatomy. The thoracoabdominal and dorsal
   lumbotomy approaches are now largely historical because of their morbidity and complexity; they have
   been supplanted by simpler incisions that give more direct access to the kidney and its vasculature.
 • The kidney lies deep in the retroperitoneum beside the aorta and inferior vena cava (IVC), so open
   surgery demands detailed knowledge of both intraperitoneal and retroperitoneal anatomy.
 • The anterior approach requires displacement of the intraperitoneal organs: the surgeon must know their
   vasculature, their attachments to the posterior parietal peritoneum and how they are mobilised, and must
   keep them out of the field with self-retaining retractors.
Concept — open renal surgery has survived as the operation of exposure and vascular control: it is chosen
where the kidney must be reached past mobilised viscera with early command of the great vessels.

PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION AND PREPARATION
Laboratory Studies and Renal Function Assessment
 • The goal of almost every renal operation is preservation of global renal function within the constraints of
   the disease being treated.
 • Baseline function guides three decisions: the operation (partial versus radical nephrectomy), counselling
   on the risk of postoperative renal dysfunction, and planning for temporary or permanent dialysis.
 • Urinalysis and serum creatinine (SCr) are obtained in all patients before renal surgery; the glomerular
   filtration rate (GFR) is then estimated by the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) equation or the
   Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation — CKD-EPI is more accurate and
   now more widely used.

 • Low risk of postoperative dialysis after open renal surgery: no comorbidity affecting renal function
   (hypertension, diabetes) and two normally functioning kidneys.
 • Increased risk: GFR below 60 mL/min or significant proteinuria on urinalysis → perioperative nephrology
   consultation to optimise function before and after surgery.
 • Poorly controlled diabetes or hypertension risks further deterioration even when GFR is 60 mL/min or
   above — record these conditions and ensure they are managed perioperatively.
 • Contemporary open renal surgery is reserved mostly for locally advanced disease (IVC tumour
   thrombus) or metastatic disease in the cytoreductive setting. Complete blood count and comprehensive
   metabolic profile add value for risk stratification — Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC;
   Motzer) and International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium (IMDC) models — and for planning.
 • Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is prone to paraneoplastic phenomena: haemoglobin and other
   haematological aberrations aid prognostic stratification; screen advanced disease for hepatic dysfunction
   (Stauffer syndrome) and associated coagulopathy.
 • Haemoglobin assessment matters because major blood loss is a real risk in advanced disease, especially
   where transfusion is anticipated.
 • Cardiopulmonary assessment warrants particular care because intraoperative positioning, blood loss and
   fluid shifts can cause significant cardiopulmonary compromise.
Concept — renal function assessment is not merely a fitness check: it chooses the operation (partial or
radical) and forecasts the need for dialysis, which is why every patient has creatinine, urinalysis and an
estimated GFR before any renal surgery.

Preoperative Imaging
 • High-quality cross-sectional imaging of the abdomen — computed tomography (CT) or magnetic
   resonance imaging (MRI) — is a prerequisite to any open renal procedure: renal parenchyma, pelvis and
   ureter, and renal vasculature.
 • It maps the neighbouring organs (colon, pleura, pancreas, spleen, liver) that may need mobilisation or
   avoidance or may be involved by disease.
 • Contrast is ideal but may be contraindicated by baseline renal dysfunction. There is no universally
   agreed GFR threshold (it depends on agent, overall health and context), but GFR above 30 mL/min is
   generally acceptable for both iodinated (CT) and gadolinium-based (MRI) contrast.
 • For locally advanced disease with suspected IVC tumour thrombus, MRI (preferably with intravenous
   [IV] contrast) or CT venography is preferred: they best define cephalad and caudal propagation and raise
   suspicion of caval wall infiltration — which determine whether liver mobilisation is needed, the level of
   caval control, and whether a vascular graft or caval resection is required.
 • Thrombus imaging must be current — within 1 month of surgery — because propagation can be rapid.

 • Renal artery embolisation (RAE) deliberately occludes the arterial supply of the diseased kidney with
   embolic agents; it is occasionally used before open surgery, mostly for advanced RCC or large renal
   tumours.

Prophylactic Measures
 • Surgical site infection (American Urological Association [AUA] guideline): a single dose of a first-
   generation cephalosporin (cefazolin) or clindamycin, within 1 hour of incision.
 • Urine culture is not routine; signs of urinary tract infection prompt culture and sensitivity, and active
   infection is treated before surgery.
 • Mechanical bowel preparation is not indicated unless intestinal involvement by the renal process is
   suspected or bowel injury is likely (multiple prior operations, extensive adhesiolysis). If used, weigh
   dehydration and acute kidney injury, especially in older adults.
 • Stress ulcer prophylaxis with a proton pump inhibitor or sucralfate reduces gastric stress ulcers when
   prolonged intensive care is expected.
 • Venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk is heightened by immobilisation and surgical tissue injury, and
   further by the hypercoagulable state of malignancy; intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) devices
   reduce postoperative deep venous thrombosis (DVT).

SURGICAL APPROACHES
 • Adequate exposure is the hallmark of effective open renal surgery. Proper incision and exposure
   minimise the retraction required and thus retractor-related injury.
 • Landmark structures: right kidney — liver, colon, duodenum; left kidney — spleen, tail of pancreas,
   colon.
 • The approach is tailored to the operation, the anatomy on imaging, previous surgery, body habitus, and
   limiting factors such as kyphoscoliosis or pulmonary disease.

Anterior Approaches
Positioning
 • Supine; arms tucked at the side or abducted to 90 degrees on padded arm boards; pad the elbows (ulnar
   nerve) and all pressure points — occiput, dorsal torso, sacrum, dorsal legs, heels — against pressure
   ulcers and nerve palsy (Fig. 141.5A).
 • Hyperlordosis improves exposure: a rolled blanket under the lumbar spine (subcostal or chevron) or
   table flexion with the umbilicus at the break (midline).
 • Excess hyperlordosis tensions the great vessels (reducing flow) and causes postoperative low back pain;
   avoid it in spinal stenosis.
 • Supine hypotension (aortocaval syndrome) occurs if adiposity or abdominal masses compress the great
   vessels; low back pain is common with scoliosis or kyphosis; prosthetic hips and knees may be stressed.

Anterior Midline Approach
 • Efficient access to both kidneys and the great vessels without muscle division → less postoperative pain
   and morbidity.
 • Preferred in renal trauma: rapid control of the major vessels for haemodynamic stability, and survey of
   intraperitoneal contents for associated injuries.
 • The authors' preferred approach for locally advanced tumours, including IVC thrombus: early aortic
   access permits early ligation of the renal artery, reducing blood loss.
 • Technique: supine, bed slightly flexed with the umbilicus at the break → skin incision from xiphoid,
   around the umbilicus, to 2 cm below it (rarely needed to the pubis) → subcutaneous tissue divided with
   cautery → linea alba incised sharply at the midpoint between xiphoid and umbilicus, where
   preperitoneal fat is thinnest → finger admitted into the preperitoneal space, incision extended cephalad

   expose the upper pole.
 • Cattell-Braasch manoeuvre (right medial visceral rotation) — direct access to the central vascular
   compartment (aorta and IVC), especially for right-sided surgery (Fig. 141.7): incise peritoneum lateral to

   colon and mesocolon, duodenum (second to fourth parts), jejunum and ileum with their mesentery.
   Landmarks: the left renal vein (seen as the transverse duodenum is lifted) or the inferior mesenteric vein
   (IMV).
 • Direct infracolic approach to the renal pedicle (trauma, or large tumours obscuring the hilum): shift
   jejunum and ileum aside → vertical incision in the posterior peritoneum below the ligament of Treitz →
   anterior aorta and crossing left renal vein (LRV). The superior mesenteric artery (SMA) arises from the
   anterior aorta about 1–2 cm cephalad to the LRV.
 • The right renal artery can be found in the interaortocaval space at its aortic origin, posterior and inferior
   to the LRV, before its retrocaval course → cephalad retraction of the LRV allows control and ligation —
   valuable for large or medial right tumours that obscure the artery lateral to the IVC.

Anterior Subcostal Approach
 • Supine; rolled blanket under the lumbar spine for hyperlordosis.
 • Incision about two fingerbreadths below the costal margin, from just medial to the tip of the 11th rib to
   the medial border of the rectus abdominis.
 • Technique: subcutaneous tissue divided with cautery → anterior rectus sheath incised, extended laterally
   through external and internal oblique → rectus divided with cautery → posterior rectus sheath divided
   near the midline → laterally, internal oblique and transversus abdominis divided (a right angle may
   replace the finger as guide) → transversalis fascia and parietal peritoneum → peritoneum incised →
   intraperitoneal mobilisation as for the midline approach → layered closure of muscle with absorbable
   sutures.

Chevron Incision (Bilateral Anterior Subcostal Approach)
 • Ideal for renovascular surgery and radical nephrectomy with IVC tumour thrombectomy, with good
   exposure of both renal pedicles and the great vessels.

Flank Approaches
Positioning
 • Lateral decubitus with the table flexed between iliac crest and costal margin; kidney bar raised for
   retroperitoneal exposure — but beware a previously repaired contralateral kidney.
 • Pad all pressure points to prevent rhabdomyolysis of the contralateral flank and hip (Fig. 141.5B).
 • Relative unsuitability: pre-existing cardiopulmonary deficit (exaggerated decubitus impairs pulmonary
   function and venous return) and severe kyphosis (poor exposure, unexpected pressure on flank and
   vertebrae) — the surgeon must command alternative approaches.
 • Full set-up (as for the subcostal flank incision): after induction, endotracheal intubation and Foley
   catheter for urine output → lateral decubitus → head supported against cervical flexion → tip of 12th rib
   over the kidney bar → back supported with rolled blanket or beanbag → dependent leg flexed at hip and
   knee, top leg straight, pillows between the knees → axillary roll just caudal to the axilla → upper foot
   padded → nondependent arm on a padded Mayo stand or pillow, horizontal with slight forward shoulder
   rotation → flex until the flank muscles are stretched; Trendelenburg until the flank is parallel to the floor
   → secure with 2-inch adhesive tape to the mobile part of the table.

Subcostal Flank Incision
 • Excellent exposure of the proximal ureter and parenchyma — suited to the lower pole, pelviureteric
   junction and proximal ureter.
 • Hilar access is suboptimal, limiting it for large masses; not ideal for partial nephrectomy, which needs
   excellent hilar access (Fig. 141.10).
 • Technique: skin incision begins at the costovertebral angle at the lateral border of sacrospinalis, just
   below the 12th rib → one fingerbreadth below and parallel to the 12th rib onto the anterior abdominal
   wall; curve gently downward at the midaxillary line to avoid the subcostal nerve; extend caudally or
   medially to the lateral rectus border if needed → subcutaneous tissue → cautery through latissimus dorsi
   posteriorly and serratus posterior inferior (inserts into the lower four ribs) → external oblique anteriorly
   → exposed fused lumbodorsal fascia (origin of internal oblique and transversus) → lumbodorsal fascia
   and internal oblique divided (Fig. 141.13) → two fingers through an opening at the 12th rib tip sweep the
   peritoneum medially while transversus is split digitally → subcostal nerve identified between internal
   oblique and transversus, and spared (Figs. 141.14–141.15; the subcostal nerve and vessels pierce the
   lumbodorsal fascia posteriorly and run forward on transversus).
 • More posterior exposure: incise the posterior angle of the lumbodorsal fascia (sacrospinalis and
   quadratus lumborum); dividing the costovertebral ligament lets the 12th rib be retracted superiorly. A
   Bookwalter flank retractor holds exposure.

Supracostal Flank Incision
 • Above the 11th or 12th rib; favoured by many open renal surgeons. Turner Warwick (1965) popularised
   it: maximal posterior exposure, simpler wound closure, and less morbidity than a transcostal incision
   requiring rib resection.

 • An 8-cm modified mini-flank supra-11th rib incision is a safe, effective approach to radical or partial
   nephrectomy for renal cortical tumours.
 • Level chosen by anatomy, lesion location and procedure; positioning as for the subcostal flank.
 • Technique: skin along the superior aspect of the 11th or 12th rib, from the lateral border of sacrospinalis
   to the lateral border of the rectus → subcutaneous tissue → latissimus dorsi and serratus posterior
   inferior transected posteriorly, revealing the intercostal muscles → external and internal oblique divided
   → lumbodorsal fascia opened at the rib tip to avoid both peritoneum and pleura → moving medially,
   transversus divided while the peritoneum is swept medially and inferiorly → diaphragm exposed by
   dividing the transversalis layer → pleura identified between divided transversus and diaphragm,
   mobilised superiorly → lateral sacrospinalis incised or retracted to reach the rib neck → intercostal
   muscles divided from the distal rib toward the spine, close to the superior border of the rib to spare the
   neurovascular bundle; the intercostal nerve is identified and spared → extrapleural plane developed by
   entering the investing fascia around the intercostal nerve → diaphragmatic slips on the inferior ribs
   transected.

Transcostal Flank Incision
 • Mirrors the supracostal approach, shifted slightly caudally along the 11th rib toward the umbilicus; the
   palpable 11th rib is the key landmark.
 • Technique: lateral incision directly on the 11th rib → subcutaneous tissue and muscle until the anterior
   surface of the distal 11th rib is seen → periosteum cleared with a periosteal elevator, protecting the
   neurovascular bundle beneath the rib → distal-most rib divided with a bone cutter; fragments removed,
   edges filed with a rongeur, marrow cauterised → remainder as supracostal → retroperitoneum entered at
   the former 11th rib tip, avoiding both peritoneal and pleural entry.
Concept — the flank incisions are all extraperitoneal and are named by their relation to a rib: the higher the
incision, the better the upper-pole and hilar exposure — and the closer the pleura.

SIMPLE NEPHRECTOMY
 • Removal of the kidney within Gerota's fascia, for nonmalignant disease.

Indications
 • Permanent poor function from obstruction, infection, trauma, stones, nephrosclerosis, vesicoureteral
   reflux, polycystic kidney disease or congenital dysplasia.
 • A well-functioning kidney is rarely removed, only for intractable pain, bleeding, renovascular
   hypertension or persistent infection.

Surgical Approach
 • Approach as for radical nephrectomy, but benign disease often distorts anatomy and planes (prior
   instrumentation, chronic infection, aberrant anatomy).
 • Perinephric fat is often adherent and inflamed; avoid entering the renal capsule, which causes bleeding
   and loss of the plane.
 • Adherent to psoas posteriorly → include the psoas fascia in the dissection.
 • Large hydronephrotic kidney → puncture and aspirate the pelvis to decompress and aid mobilisation.
 • Spare the adrenal almost universally by keeping the plane directly on the renal capsule at the upper pole.
 • Partial nephrectomy for benign disease is occasionally indicated — hydronephrosis with focal
   parenchymal atrophy, atrophic pyelonephritis in a duplicated kidney, infected calyceal diverticulum,
   symptomatic benign tumours such as angiomyolipoma — using the same technique as for malignancy.

RADICAL NEPHRECTOMY
 • Classic definition (Robson, 1969): complete removal of the kidney with Gerota's fascia, the ipsilateral
   adrenal and a complete regional lymphadenectomy from the crus of the diaphragm to the aortic
   bifurcation.
 • Modern practice spares the adrenal when technically feasible — removal of a radiographically
   uninvolved adrenal has been associated with worse survival — and reserves extended lymphadenectomy
   for selected cases.

Indications and Workup
 • Radical nephrectomy is reserved for tumours not amenable to partial nephrectomy: tumour in a
   nonfunctioning kidney, large tumours replacing most of the parenchyma, detectable regional
   lymphadenopathy, consolidative cytoreductive surgery for metastatic disease, and renal vein thrombus.
 • Staging of every suspicious renal tumour: abdominopelvic CT or MRI plus chest imaging (Fig. 141.18).
   The authors prefer chest CT to chest radiography — plain films miss subcentimetre nodules and
   mediastinal nodes, RCC favours lung and mediastinum, and missed metastases matter — despite the
   modest extra radiation.
 • Scrutinise imaging for tumour thrombus, enlarged retroperitoneal nodes, and embryological anomalies
   of the collecting system and vasculature.
 • Occult brain metastases occur in a small minority with metastatic RCC; routine brain imaging is not
   recommended without symptoms.
 • Routine bone scan is not recommended; use it for suspicious cross-sectional findings, symptoms,
   pathological fracture or raised bone isoenzyme of alkaline phosphatase.
 • Percutaneous renal biopsy before surgery is considered when:
     ◦ the patient is uncomfortable with uncertainty about histology;
     ◦ the false-negative risk is acceptable and a benign result would change the strategy — a systematic
       review shows that a substantial minority of patients with a benign biopsy who proceed to surgery have
       RCC;
     ◦ the mass appears infiltrative and suspicious for urothelial, metastatic or lymphoid origin;
     ◦ there is metastatic disease to be treated with systemic therapy, where histological subtype directs the
       agent;
     ◦ there are bilateral renal tumours — synchronous bilateral tumours are more often histologically
       discordant than metachronous ones;

 • Large tumours displace organs; distinguishing abutment, displacement and direct invasion (T4) is
   difficult. MRI is suggested when CT suggests invasion.
 • True invasion is likelier when the organ changes in appearance, not merely in contour: tumour
   infiltration, altered enhancement relative to the rest of the organ, bowel wall thickening. Suspected
   involvement of colon, duodenum, liver or spleen → preoperative multidisciplinary planning.
 • The liver is rarely truly invaded because of its bilaminar capsule, despite imaging that suggests it. When
   invasion does occur, depth is usually under 1 cm, and a thin crescent of liver resected with the tumour
   suffices — still performed by a liver surgeon.

Surgical Procedure
 • Incisions: vertical midline, subcostal flank or chevron; retractor Balfour, Bookwalter, Thompson or Omni-
   Tract; ribs retracted laterally and cephalad for upper-quadrant tumours or venous thrombus.
 • Begin with a complete abdominal survey for metastases: entire bowel, spleen, liver surface, and
   palpation of the gastric tube in the decompressed stomach.
 • Right radical nephrectomy. Liver and gallbladder retracted superiorly; if more liver mobilisation is
   needed, the avascular right triangular ligament is incised, usually later in the case. The falciform ligament
   is left intact when liver mobilisation is not required — a falciform flap can buttress a duodenal repair —
   but an intact falciform demands care during retraction because it predisposes to traction lacerations of
   the liver.
 • Sequence: posterior parietal peritoneum incised 2 cm medial to the white line of Toldt, from the pelvis
   (iliac artery) to the right upper quadrant (hepatic flexure) → anterior pararenal space developed between
   the anterior renal fascia and the ascending mesocolon → hepatic flexure mobilised sharply and bluntly →
   second part of the duodenum Kocherised medially (Fig. 141.19) — with extreme care for medial tumours
   → IVC identified posteriorly; dissection anterior to it reveals the renal vein and right gonadal vein →
   vessel loop on the renal vein for gentle traction; renal vein palpated for thrombus → renal artery
   identified posterior to the vein.
 • Finding the plane: mesenteric fat is vibrant yellow or orange; Gerota's fascia is paler and less saturated;
   anterior tension on the colonic mesentery reveals the areolar plane. Large inflammatory masses obscure
   it through neovascularity, lost planes and thickened tissue.
 • Protect the ascending mesocolon — injury to the right colic and ileocolic arteries devitalises colon.
   Bipolar sealing of small neovascular perforators saves time later.
 • Resect the renal fascia in its entirety (best chance of cure, no intra-abdominal tumour spillage), keeping
   the lymph node plane posterior on the downside.
 • If the artery is hard to find: go to the lower pole, identify ureter and gonadal vein; spare the gonadal vein
   if feasible — large tumours often make this unsafe because of avulsion from the IVC (right) or LRV (left)
   → ligate the ureter and lift the kidney from posterior to anterior to display the artery behind it.
 • Interaortocaval option for difficult hilar dissection (Fig. 141.20: the anteromedial IVC leads to the short
   right renal vein; the artery lies deep to the vein): control the right renal artery at its aortic take-off →
   ligate with 0 silk (surgical clip in emergencies) → kidney and tumour shrink and decompress → now-
   flaccid right renal vein examined for thrombus, doubly ligated (0 silk tie + 3-0 Prolene suture ligature)
   and divided → artery lateral to the IVC, now easy to reach, doubly ligated and divided.
 • Lumbar veins. Avulsed lumbar veins → 2-0 or 3-0 silk ties. No clips on the IVC side — they obstruct
   urgent IVC clamping; well-placed clips on the posterior (lumbar) side before release are acceptable.
   Uncontrolled lumbar veins retract lateral to the vertebra and medial to psoas, causing inaccessible
   retroperitoneal bleeding → compress psoas with a sponge stick at the site to slow bleeding and reidentify

 • Left radical nephrectomy. Sequence: white line of Toldt incised from the splenic flexure to the common

Concurrent Adrenalectomy for Renal Cell Carcinoma
 • Consider ipsilateral adrenalectomy for large upper-pole tumours where the kidney–adrenal plane may be
   compromised; it is indicated for diffuse tumour involvement or an adrenal mass on imaging.
 • Otherwise not routine: overall adrenal metastasis is under 5%.
 • CT and MRI miss 20–25% of adrenal metastases, so clinical indicators of adrenal involvement must guide
   practice.
 • Contralateral adrenal metastasis on follow-up is nearly as common as ipsilateral when both glands are
   spared — so prophylactic ipsilateral adrenalectomy exposes the patient to adrenal insufficiency without
   clear benefit.

Regional Lymphadenectomy for Renal Cell Carcinoma
 • Controversial; not required in every radical nephrectomy — overall nodal involvement is about 5%.
 • Retrospective studies suggested benefit in carefully selected patients. The European Organisation for
   Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) randomised trial (772 patients, lymphadenectomy versus
   none) showed no overall survival benefit, but enrolled many small, low-stage tumours unlikely to benefit.
 • Later large cohorts found no survival benefit from staging dissection in the cytoreductive setting or in
   high-risk localised disease; a meta-analysis found no therapeutic benefit → the field has moved away
   from prophylactic staging dissection for most patients.
 • Lymph node dissection does not alter cancer-specific survival, but may improve retroperitoneal
   recurrence-free survival and definitely provides prognostic information → recommended when nodes
   are enlarged on imaging, for diagnosis and prognosis.
 • Probability of node positivity rises with size: maximum short-axis diameter of 1.5 cm or more ≈ 50%
   chance of positive nodes (Fig. 141.25: a sigmoid risk curve against short-axis diameter).
 • Some surgeons perform staging dissection even without clinical lymphadenopathy when node positivity
   is likely: cytoreductive surgery for metastatic disease, tumour over 10 cm, nuclear grade 3 or higher,
   sarcomatoid histology, tumour necrosis on imaging, extrarenal extension, tumour thrombus, and direct
   invasion of adjacent organs.

Wound Closure
 • Check haemostasis and the adjacent organs. Diaphragm and pleura may be injured by retraction.
 • Pleural test: fill the retroperitoneum with saline to the level of the flank incision → anaesthetist gives high
   inspiratory volumes → bubbling indicates pneumothorax.
 • Small pleural injury: running nonabsorbable suture → before completion, tip of a 14-Fr red rubber
   catheter in the pleural cavity, other end under saline → deep inspiratory breath expels air → when
   bubbling ceases, withdraw the catheter and cinch the closure airtight → postoperative chest radiograph
   in every case, even when pneumothorax is not suspected.
 • Fascia in two layers: transversus and internal oblique fasciae together; external oblique separately.
 • Wound infiltration: 1:1 bupivacaine 0.5% and lidocaine 1%. Subcutaneous tissue 3-0 absorbable; skin
   staples or subcuticular 4-0 poliglecaprone 25 (Monocryl).

Vena Caval Tumour Thrombectomy
 • Many retroperitoneal tumours produce venous thrombus. Children: Wilms tumour, clear cell sarcoma of
   the kidney, adrenocortical carcinoma, neuroblastoma. Adults: urothelial carcinoma of the renal pelvis,
   lymphoma, retroperitoneal sarcoma, adrenocortical carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma, angiomyolipoma.
 • RCC is the commonest cause of IVC tumour thrombus; 5–10% of RCCs have venous tumour thrombus.
 • Untreated IVC thrombus carries high mortality — most patients die within a year — so surgery, before
   or after systemic therapy in the metastatic setting, is the backbone of treatment.
 • Venous thrombus marks aggressive disease: regional nodal involvement, metastases and perirenal fat
   invasion are all common.
 • Thrombectomy is usually combined with radical nephrectomy and regional lymph node dissection,
   which also helps expose the IVC and renal artery origin.

Tumour Thrombus Level and Bland Thrombus
 • Thrombus is often an unexpected CT finding with urgent referral; patients are frequently already
   anticoagulated.
 • Best imaging: magnetic resonance venography — better than most CT for cephalad and caudal extent,
   IVC wall invasion and bland thrombus; MRI is independent of contrast timing and so the more reliable
   measure of extent, though well-timed reconstructed CT venograms are also excellent.
 • Two components: tumour thrombus (tumour cells contained within thrombus) versus bland thrombus
   (coagulum without tumour cells). Venous obstruction promotes bland thrombus. Distinguishing them is
   the basis of operative management.

Preoperative Considerations
 • Pulmonary embolism, anticoagulation and IVC filters. Renal tumours raise pulmonary embolism risk
   (malignant hypercoagulability plus thrombus embolism) → screen for concurrent pulmonary embolism
   and lower-limb DVT (Doppler ultrasound).
 • Therapeutic anticoagulation is recommended for: concurrent VTE, existing bland thrombus, and current
   or impending IVC occlusion by bulky thrombus — and should be considered even without these if
   evaluation will be delayed. Evidence is limited; potential benefits are fewer pulmonary emboli, tumour
   thrombus shrinkage, and shrinkage or prevention of bland thrombus.
 • Temporary suprarenal IVC filters are not used: risk of contralateral renal and hepatic vein thrombosis, of
   provoking embolisation, and of obstructing later thrombectomy — thrombus grows onto the device and
   invades the IVC, enlarging the reconstruction required.

 • Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is recommended for level II–IV thrombi because of
   intraoperative detachment and interval growth just before surgery.
 • Angioembolisation for thrombus is seldom used: ischaemic flank pain and little reduction in operative

   from the renal artery and/or aorta, so infarction might shrink a thrombus and avoid bypass or extensive

 • It may be considered when thrombus invades intrahepatic or suprahepatic veins and cannot be excised
   because of Budd–Chiari syndrome, or when the tumour or thrombus is associated with a bleeding
   kidney preoperatively or on systemic therapy. Timing is uncertain, usually within 1 day before surgery;
   the risk of iatrogenic tumour embolism during angiography appears minimal.

Level II Vena Caval Thrombectomy: Left-Sided Tumour
 • Harder, because the IVC is best reached from the right: both colons must be mobilised. Midline or
   chevron incisions give the best access (Fig. 141.36: level II thrombus in a completely duplicated IVC, with
   patch repair and rerouting of the left IVC).
 • Left side first: left colon mobilised, left anterior pararenal space developed → left renal artery ligated
   near its aortic origin → confirm arterial control by palpation or Doppler probe — a persisting pulse
   means an accessory artery or incomplete control (e.g., calcification) → adrenal, lumbar and gonadal
   branches of the LRV ligated and divided (often dilated, friable, occasionally thrombosed) → kidney
   mobilised outside the renal fascia, left adrenal left in situ, left ureter divided with a tie on the stay side.
 • Right side next: right colon and small bowel mobilised → Kocher manoeuvre → right anterior space
   developed, great vessels exposed → IVC dissected to its bifurcation, right gonadal vein ligated on its
   anterior surface → split-and-roll manoeuvre frees the IVC above and below the thrombus with lumbar
   vein ligation → optionally ligate accessory (short) hepatic veins to release the caudate lobe, gaining 2–3
   cm of infrahepatic IVC → sequential control: (1) infrarenal IVC, (2) contralateral (right) renal vein, (3)
   suprarenal IVC.
 • Contralateral renal artery: historically clamped to prevent engorgement, which matters mainly for left
   tumours because the right kidney lacks venous collaterals. The authors no longer clamp the right renal
   artery; engorgement is relieved by periodically releasing the right renal vein clamp — preferable to right
   renal ischaemia.
 • Heparin: some give 0.5 mg/kg IV before clamping; the authors find bleeding, not clotting, is the principal
   problem and do not heparinise routinely.
 • Thrombectomy (Fig. 141.38): LRV ostium circumferentially excised, incision extended superiorly onto the
   anterolateral IVC with Potts scissors → Penfield dissector extracts the thrombus → troublesome lumbar
   veins ligated or sutured; sponge sticks isolate and compress bleeding segments → thrombus and kidney
   removed en bloc → IVC flushed with heparinised saline, intima inspected; suspicious areas biopsied or
   resected → during prolonged clamping, periodically release the contralateral renal vein and inferior IVC
   clamps to clear coagulum.
 • Closure: the IVC lumen may be narrowed safely to about 50% of its preoperative size; greater narrowing
   (residual lumen under 50%) → bovine pericardial patch, smooth side luminal, rough side outward,
   length equal to the defect, width by the Leibovich–Bower equation (see Patch Cavoplasty) → running 4-0
   Prolene → before tying, infrarenal clamp released and 5–10 mL of blood let out to clear air and debris →
   after tying, contralateral renal clamp released, then the suprarenal IVC clamp → regional
   lymphadenectomy; closed suction drain considered; irrigation and closure.

Level III Vena Caval Thrombectomy: Intra-Abdominal Approach
 • Intrahepatic thrombi are very challenging. Theatre set up for possible cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB),
   including deep hypothermic arrest; performed in vascular or cardiac theatres with their anaesthesia
   teams.
 • Intraoperative TEE measures the cranial extent, monitors thrombus fracture and embolisation, and
   assesses cardiac function for haemodynamic management (Fig. 141.39: thrombus in IVC and right atrium).
 • The key decision is made intraoperatively only — after renal artery ligation, liver mobilisation and IVC
   assessment: intra-abdominal extraction with complete hepatic mobilisation, or combined intrathoracic–
   intra-abdominal approach with bypass.
 • Clamping below the hepatic veins is preferred because hepatic venous return is large.
 • Rule of thumb: free-floating, nonocclusive thrombi tolerate suprahepatic clamping poorly and more
   often need bypass; completely occlusive thrombi have developed collateral venous networks and

   tolerate clamping much better (Fig. 141.40: complete IVC occlusion with collateralisation to the azygos
   system on CT and angiography).
 • A level IV thrombus can occasionally be milked into the abdomen through a small diaphragmatic incision
   and treated intra-abdominally.
 • IVC control must never compromise the operation: bleeding and hypotension lead to incomplete tumour
   resection, which is universally fatal.
 • Incision: anterior midline preferred; chevron with sternotomy is an alternative. Right kidney and great
   vessels exposed as for level I; right renal artery ligated interaortocavally; infrahepatic IVC dissected;
   infrarenal IVC and LRV encircled with Rummel tourniquets or clamps.
 • Liver mobilisation (liver-transplant technique; Figs. 141.41–141.42): ligamentum teres (obliterated left
   umbilical vein, at the free border of the falciform) ligated and divided → falciform ligament divided with
   cautery to the upper liver border, where it branches into the coronary ligament (right) and left triangular
   ligament → superior layer of the coronary ligament divided, sparing the liver and the IVC behind it in the
   bare area → continued along the right border to the right triangular ligament (fused superior and inferior
   coronary layers), divided → inferior layer of the coronary ligament (liver to diaphragm) divided up toward
   the IVC → left triangular ligament divided anteriorly, then posteriorly toward the IVC → right lobe rotated
   to the midline, exposing the IVC on the posterior liver. A hepatobiliary surgeon is valuable here.
 • Left-sided tumours: divide the diaphragmatic attachments of the spleen so that it rotates medially with
   the pancreas without trauma (partial Mattox manoeuvre).
 • Retrohepatic plane — the hepatic veins fall into two groups:
     ◦ Upper group — right, middle and left hepatic veins: the principal hepatic outflow, never divided.
       Thrombus may extend into them and must be cleared. Their obstruction produces Budd–Chiari
       syndrome, a contraindication to proceeding with radical nephrectomy and thrombectomy.
     ◦ Lower group — accessory hepatic veins (draining mainly the caudate lobe, a little of the right lobe):
       safely divided, ligated with 3-0 silk on IVC and liver sides.
 • Lumbar veins ligated with 2-0 silk; the plane behind the IVC developed → IVC fully mobilised.
 • Pringle manoeuvre: if the thrombus does not descend after arterial ligation to permit clamping below the
   main hepatic veins, portal inflow must be controlled → window in the lesser omentum → porta hepatis
   (portal triad; hepatic pedicle — portal vein, common hepatic artery, common bile duct) encircled with a
   Rummel tourniquet. Clamping it prevents hepatic engorgement and massive blood loss when the IVC is
   clamped above the major hepatic veins.
 • Total hepatic vascular occlusion = IVC clamped above and below the hepatic veins plus Pringle. If the IVC
   is clamped below the major hepatic veins with accessory veins ligated, Pringle may be unnecessary.
 • Normothermic Pringle limit: up to 60 minutes, but 20 minutes or less preferred — ischaemic liver injury
   and portal vein thrombosis; also splenic engorgement and rupture from backed-up splenic venous
   drainage.
 • Resectability is judged by TEE and intraoperative anatomy: thrombus below, or milkable below, the
   hepatic veins → usually no bypass; thrombus involving the hepatic veins or above the liver → bypass may
   be needed (Fig. 141.44: thrombus to the diaphragm and into the right atrium, eventually requiring CPB
   with deep hypothermic arrest).
 • Clamping trial: occlude the IVC above the liver and thrombus; observe for 2–5 minutes.

 • Minimising Pringle time: isolate the IVC circumferentially above the hepatic veins (initial clamps) and
   below them → after extraction, place an infrahepatic clamp, release the suprahepatic clamp, then
   release the Pringle → close as for level II → tack the hepatic ligaments back to prevent liver torsion.

Level III–IV Vena Caval Thrombectomy: Combined Intra-Abdominal and Intrathoracic Approach
 • For level III thrombi not removable intra-abdominally and nearly all level IV thrombi (Fig. 141.46).
 • Incisions: thoracoabdominal, chevron laparotomy with sternotomy, or anterior midline laparotomy with
   sternotomy (preferred). A cardiothoracic surgeon participates.
 • Sequence: abdominal phase as for the intra-abdominal approach → cardiothoracic surgeon called after
   releasing the caudate lobe but before rotating the liver (mobilisation is easier after sternotomy) →
   median sternotomy, pericardium opened, right heart exposed (Fig. 141.48) → bypass instituted → renal
   vein ostium excised, incision extended cranially on the IVC, thrombus extracted; right atriotomy usually
   needed for suprahepatic thrombus (Fig. 141.52) → atrium and IVC closed → off bypass, chest tubes
   placed → hepatic ligaments tacked back.

Bypass Techniques for Inferior Vena Caval Surgery
 • Bypass complicates and prolongs thrombectomy but is often essential for a safe and complete operation.
 • Consider bypass when: the IVC cross-clamping trial causes significant hypotension; there is preoperative
   cardiac or hepatic dysfunction, contralateral renal dysfunction or portal hypertension; or there is major
   intraoperative bleeding that is difficult to control.

PARTIAL NEPHRECTOMY FOR MALIGNANT DISEASE
 • Partial nephrectomy is the preferred operation for renal neoplasms when technically feasible and when
   preserving renal function is a priority (Fig. 141.56: contrast CT, 3D reconstruction of a large intrarenal
   component, and arterial reconstruction showing a lower-pole artery close to the mass).
 • Historically reserved for imperative indications — bilateral tumours, tumour in a solitary kidney, high
   risk of future renal failure — and small tumours under 4 cm. Indications have broadened to most renal
   masses that can be removed safely and completely, independent of size.

Preoperative Considerations
 • The preoperative considerations for radical nephrectomy apply, plus those unique to partial
   nephrectomy.

Hyperfiltration Injury
 • Mechanism: removal of substantial parenchyma delivers renal blood flow to fewer nephrons → raised
   glomerular capillary perfusion pressure → raised single-nephron GFR (hyperfiltration) → over years to
   decades, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, proteinuria and progressive renal failure.
 • Rare after partial nephrectomy; usually seen when total nephron mass of both kidneys is reduced by
   more than 80%.

Renal Ischaemia and Hypothermia
 • Vascular compression limits blood loss and improves vision: manual compression, a parenchymal
   compression clamp (Kaufmann), selective renal artery clamping (with or without the vein), or en bloc
   clamping of the whole pedicle (Fig. 141.57: Satinsky clamp across the parenchyma of a large polar
   tumour). Leaving the vein unclamped for retrograde perfusion has no proven benefit.
 • Duration of ischaemia and long-term function remain debated. Early landmark studies showed longer
   ischaemia → worse function, and surgeons traditionally aim for under 30 minutes.
 • Once the proportion of healthy parenchyma preserved is accounted for, ischaemia time matters less or
   not at all — but all such studies lay within conventional limits; the effect of prolonged warm ischaemia
   remains unknown. → Judicious, individualised ischaemia, weighing baseline function and expected
   preserved parenchyma.
 • Adjuncts to limit ischaemic injury: renal hypothermia, IV mannitol, IV diuretics.

Multifocality and Tumour Size
 • Multifocality: about 2% in clear cell and chromophobe RCC, about 10% in papillary RCC, and more
   common as tumour size rises (Fig. 141.58: hilar and peripheral tumours on 3D CT).
 • Inspect the entire renal surface at operation to confirm the imaging.
 • Partial nephrectomy remains viable for multifocal tumours if all can be resected safely and the remnant
   reconstructed — including simultaneous bilateral partial nephrectomy for synchronous bilateral tumours
   in selected patients.

Hereditary Renal Malignancy
 • Hereditary tumours are usually multifocal, bilateral and recurrent.
 • Except in hereditary leiomyomatosis and RCC (HLRCC) — treated aggressively with wide excision —
   most hereditary syndromes can be observed safely until tumours reach 3 cm, with little risk of
   metastasis.
 • At partial nephrectomy: preserve perirenal fat and renal fascia, visualise the whole surface, resect all
   visible tumours, and use intraoperative ultrasound to find subcortical tumours.

Surgical Technique
Approach
 • Extraperitoneal (flank incisions) or transperitoneal (midline or subcostal). Surgeons should be
   comfortable with all and individualise by habitus, prior surgery, hilar anatomy, tumour size and location,
   and comorbidity.

Vascular Control and Renal Mobilisation
 • Achieve hilar control early, identifying the main renal artery and vein and any accessory vessels — even
   when ischaemia is not planned, so clamps can be applied promptly if needed.
 • Anticipate anomalies: retroaortic renal vein, precaval renal artery, early or multiple branching,
   accessory vessels.
 • Mobilisation: at minimum, the capsule around the tumour exposed circumferentially to delineate
   borders and permit capsular renorrhaphy; full mobilisation helps deliver the tumour or excise multiple
   tumours.
 • Reapproximate Gerota's fascia at the end to prevent small bowel adhesion to the capsule.

Renal Perfusion During Partial Nephrectomy
 • Traditional technique clamps artery and vein. Modifications: omission of venous clamping; selective
   clamping of tumour-supplying branches; early unclamping after excision or the first renorrhaphy layer;
   fully perfused ("off-clamp") surgery.
 • Verdict (randomised evidence tabulated above): ischaemia within widely accepted limits is safe in
   selected patients; on-clamp versus off-clamp remains the surgeon's individualised choice.

 • Urine leak. Close large collecting system defects primarily with absorbable suture. Leave a closed suction
   drain after open renal surgery, especially after deep resection.
 • Drain rules: output low for 48 hours → leak unlikely → remove the drain by 48 hours. Fluid creatinine is
   measured only if output is high — typically over 50 mL per 24 hours.
 • High output with raised fluid creatinine but no significant symptoms → no cross-sectional imaging
   needed. Symptoms — persistent abdominal pain, fever, ileus, leakage from the incision,
   disproportionate rise in SCr — → CT urography if renal function permits (delayed phase shows
   extravasation and suggests infection or abscess).
 • Treatment sequence: maintain the closed suction drain in the urinoma → percutaneous drain by
   interventional radiology if drainage is inadequate → urinoma unresolved after 48 hours, or symptoms
   persisting beyond 24 hours → retrograde double-J stent plus Foley catheter (antegrade flow); any
   retrograde pyelogram gentle and low-pressure so as not to enlarge the defect → most fistulas resolve in
   4–6 weeks; reoperation is rarely required.
 • Postoperative bleeding. Pseudoaneurysm: persistent communication between a disrupted arterial wall
   and the surrounding tissue, forming a contained haematoma — from injury at excision, suturing or
   thermal energy.
 • Risk rises with tumour complexity (large, endophytic) and postoperative anticoagulation.
 • Mild bleeding in a stable patient: imaging not necessarily required; bed rest, hydration, close monitoring
   and serial blood counts.
 • Significant bleeding (falling haemoglobin, transfusion, instability) → angiography by interventional
   radiology — CT angiography has more false negatives, particularly for pseudoaneurysm, and cannot
   treat → selective embolisation of segmental or subsegmental arteries salvages the kidney.
 • Life-threatening haemorrhage (hypotension refractory to blood-product resuscitation) → reoperative
   exploration.
 • Arteriovenous fistula. Management depends on size, location, symptoms and overall status: observation;
   endovascular coil embolisation or stent; surgery for complex or symptomatic fistulas.
Concept — complications follow anatomy: the right side threatens duodenum and liver, the left side
pancreas, spleen and the SMA, the upper pole the pleura; and after partial nephrectomy, the two late threats
— urine leak and arterial bleeding — are each managed by escalating drainage or selective embolisation
before any reoperation.

   • The right renal artery arises posterior to the LRV and the IVC. In a difficult right radical nephrectomy it
     may be more accessible interaortocavally, posterior and medial to the IVC, near its aortic origin;
     cephalad retraction of the LRV as it crosses the aorta aids exposure.

   • The right gonadal and adrenal veins drain into the IVC; the left gonadal and left adrenal veins drain
     into the LRV.
   • Simple nephrectomy — removal within Gerota's fascia — is for benign disease.
   • Key elements of radical nephrectomy: early ligation of renal artery and vein, and removal outside
     Gerota's fascia; spare a radiographically normal ipsilateral adrenal when feasible.
   • Left radical nephrectomy, especially for upper-pole masses: avoid ligating the SMA, which lies on the
     anterior aorta 1–2 cm cephalad to the LRV.
   • Regional lymphadenectomy from renal hilum to aortic bifurcation is used in selected advanced local
     disease when feasible; its effect on progression-free and overall survival is controversial.
   • Partial nephrectomy is the treatment of choice for stage T1 tumours when technically feasible, even
     without renal insufficiency.
   • Goal of partial nephrectomy: complete excision with negative margins and maximal preservation of
     benign parenchyma — by enucleation or resection.
   • Known IVC thrombus → intraoperative TEE after induction and intubation to define the distal extent
     before starting.
   • Right radical nephrectomy with thrombectomy: the suprarenal IVC can be resected only if the LRV
     has been ligated on the caval side of its tributaries (gonadal, lumbar, adrenal), which then drain the
     left kidney.
   • Left-sided tumour: the right kidney has no such tributaries, so the suprarenal IVC should not be
     resected unless alternative right renal drainage is provided — autotransplantation, or saphenous vein
     graft to the splenic, portal or inferior mesenteric vein.
   • Alternatives to CPB include VVB and extensive liver mobilisation.
   • IVC reconstruction: the lumen can be narrowed by half and closed primarily; larger resections need a
     pericardial patch or PTFE graft.
