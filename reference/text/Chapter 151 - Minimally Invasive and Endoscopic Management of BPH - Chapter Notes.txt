Chapter 151 — Minimally Invasive and Endoscopic
Management of Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia
 • Urinary obstruction in men is ancient: the Chinese catheterised with onion stalks; the Greeks and Romans
   used wooden or metal tubes. The cause of obstruction was not understood.
 • Herophilus (c. 300 BCE) first used the word prostate (reported by Galen). Jean Riolan (1649) first
   suggested that prostatic enlargement causes urinary retention.
 • Morgagni (1760) gave one of the earliest descriptions of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH): an autopsy of
   a 73-year-old man with a thick-walled bladder and an obstructing pear-shaped prostate.
 • 18th-century surgery was blind: punch-type tools to incise and remove tissue; sounds to dilate the
   urethra (some with concealed blades to incise the bladder neck or lobes); lance-shaped devices to
   puncture obstructing tissue.
 • Bozzini's cystoscope (1806) first allowed visualisation of the lower urinary tract.
 • Early 1900s operations: perineal simple prostatectomy (Hugh Hampton Young, 1903); open suprapubic
   prostatectomy (John Thomas Walker, 1919); transurethral incision with Bottini's electric knife. All were
   morbid and unreliable, which drove the search for a better technique.
 • New energy sources and devices (morcellators, prostatic urethral lift, temporary implanted prostatic
   devices) now give more surgical options for lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) attributed to BPH than
   ever. These include the minimally invasive surgical therapies (MISTs).
 • Novel therapies must be judged by consistent, clearly defined criteria. Placebo effects are considerable
   where design lacks rigour (retrospective, single-cohort, uncontrolled). Premature enthusiasm for
   transurethral needle ablation of the prostate (TUNA) produced American Urological Association (AUA)
   guideline recommendations that were later retracted.

Surgical Trends
 • Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) peaked in 1987 (Medicare data). The fall since then is
   attributed to better medical therapy, the rise of MISTs and falling reimbursement. By 1997 the TURP rate
   was less than half its peak.
 • TURP still dominated (about four-fifths of BPH procedures) in 1999, while the total number of BPH
   operations kept falling.
 • Total BPH surgery rose again from 2002, driven entirely by a steep rise in MISTs: transurethral microwave
   therapy (TUMT), TUNA and transurethral laser coagulation or vaporisation. TURP kept declining year on
   year.
 • Total BPH surgery peaked in 2005, then declined again. TURP continued to fall while laser vaporisation
   rose to nearly match it by 2008. Total surgical BPH cases fell further between 2008 and 2014.
 • Operations in the 2023 AUA Guidelines:
     ◦ Transurethral incision of the prostate (TUIP), TURP, and transurethral vaporisation of the prostate
       (TUVP).
     ◦ Photoselective vaporisation of the prostate (PVP) and prostatic urethral lift (PUL; UroLift).
     ◦ Water vapour thermal therapy (WVTT; Rezūm), temporary implanted prostatic devices (TIPD; iTind) and
       robotic waterjet treatment (RWT; Aquablation).
     ◦ Holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP) or thulium laser enucleation of the prostate
       (ThuLEP).
     ◦ Simple prostatectomy (open, robotic or laparoscopic) and prostate artery embolisation (PAE).

   Peer-reviewed comparisons of current trends across all of these are limited.
 • American Board of Urology case logs (2008–2021):
     ◦ TURP remained the most performed BPH procedure and rose year on year.
     ◦ HoLEP use was flat. It was more likely among high-volume surgeons and endourologists.
     ◦ PUL rose steeply after its 2015 introduction, from a small fraction of cases to about one-third by 2020.
Concept — Surgical volume for BPH follows medical therapy, reimbursement and the arrival of new
technology rather than disease burden, which is why TURP falls while MISTs and lasers rise.

PROSTATE SIZE/VOLUME
 • Prostate size increases with age. Community cohorts show median volume rising from the low 20s cc in
   the fifth decade to the low-to-mid 30s cc by the eighth. The Olmsted County study estimated an average
   annual growth of about 1.6%; the Flint Men's Health Study showed the same age association.
 • Growth is faster in men with symptomatic BPH.
     ◦ In the Medical Therapy of Prostatic Symptoms (MTOPS) study, prostate size rose by a median of 24%
       over 4.5 years (about 5.3% per year).
     ◦ The Proscar Long-term Efficacy and Safety Study (PLESS) showed a strong correlation between serum
       prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and prostate size, and 14.1% growth over 4 years (about 3.5% per year).
 • Size alone does not determine symptom severity. Correlation between size and the International Prostate
   Symptom Score (IPSS) is weak or absent.
 • Size does predict the need for surgery.
     ◦ Larger glands carry a higher risk of acute urinary retention (AUR). In Olmsted County, a prostate >30
       cc tripled the risk of AUR.
     ◦ In the placebo arm of the Reduction by Dutasteride of Prostate Cancer Events (REDUCE) trial, a gland
       of 40.1–80 cc carried a 67% higher risk of developing LUTS than a gland ≤40 cc.

 KEY POINTS
   • Medical management and new technologies have changed which surgical treatments are used for LUTS
     secondary to BPH.
   • Medical therapy may defer surgery, so surgical patients are more complex.
   • Larger prostate size is associated with the need for BPH surgery.

Concept — Volume predicts progression (retention, surgery) but not symptoms; size selects the operation,
while symptoms decide whether to operate.

WORKUP
 • Initial evaluation (AUA Statement 1): history, physical examination, IPSS and urinalysis.
 • IPSS (also called the AUA Symptom Score, AUASS) quantifies baseline LUTS and documents response to
   treatment. Severity bands: 0–7 mild, 8–19 moderate, 20–35 severe.
 • Assess comorbidities and medications that affect voiding, to guide behavioural modification and first-line
   medical therapy. Medical management is covered in Chapter 150.
 • Indications for surgery (Statement 26):
     ◦ Renal insufficiency secondary to BPH.
     ◦ Refractory urinary retention secondary to BPH.
     ◦ Recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs).

     ◦ Recurrent bladder stones or gross haematuria secondary to BPH.
     ◦ LUTS refractory to other therapy, or a patient unwilling to use it.
 • Presurgical testing:
     ◦ Should include a post-void residual (PVR) (Statement 6).
     ◦ Consider assessment of prostate size and shape by transrectal or abdominal ultrasound, cystoscopy, or
       cross-sectional imaging such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT)
       (Statement 5).
     ◦ Consider uroflowmetry (Statement 7).
     ◦ Consider pressure-flow studies when there is diagnostic uncertainty (Statement 8).
 • Counselling: warn of possible treatment failure and the need for additional or secondary treatment before
   any surgery or MIST (Statement 9).
 • Units of size: cc, mL and g are used interchangeably because prostate tissue has a specific gravity of about
   1.05 g/mL. Cubic centimetres are technically correct, because volume is calculated with the ellipsoid
   formula π/6 × (L × W × H).
 • Prostate cancer screening belongs in the BPH workup, although the BPH guideline does not address it
   directly.
     ◦ The AUA 2023 Early Detection Guideline recommends shared decision-making, with PSA screening at
       ages 50–69 every 2–4 years (Statements 1, 2 and 6). The interval is then personalised, or screening
       stopped, according to age, comorbidity, PSA, risk, preference and life expectancy (Statement 7).
     ◦ An elevated PSA warrants biopsy before BPH surgery.
     ◦ The European Association of Urology (EAU) recommends prebiopsy MRI: a normal MRI avoids about
       30% of biopsies at the cost of missing some grade group ≥2 cancers.
     ◦ Detecting cancer first matters because clinically significant cancer needs radical prostatectomy, not
       TURP.
 • Summary of counselling: weigh comorbidities, the contribution of BPH to the overall voiding pattern, the
   need for treatment, the probability of success and the risks. The patient's goals guide the choice.

DEFINING OUTCOMES
Subjective
 • Administer the IPSS at baseline (Statement 1) and repeat it after treatment (Statement 3).
 • Other validated instruments:
     ◦ Michigan Incontinence Symptom Index (MISI) for incontinence.
     ◦ Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM) for erectile function.
     ◦ Male Sexual Health Questionnaire Ejaculatory Dysfunction short form (MSHQ-EjD) for ejaculatory and
       orgasmic function.
   Before-and-after use makes subjective outcomes reproducible.
 • The sham effect is large. A systematic review found that sham endoscopic surgery produced a meaningful
   improvement in IPSS and in maximum flow rate (Qmax) at 3 months. This is why sham-controlled trials
   are essential for new devices.

Objective
 • Standard objective end points: AUR occurrence, Qmax, PVR, weight of tissue removed (where available),
   and change in prostate volume and/or PSA.

 • Objective data depend on accurate reporting and are confounded by loss to follow-up and by non-
   responders receiving additional treatment.
Need for Secondary Treatment
 • Treatment failure is an important end point: it guides patient choice and drives cost.
 • It is not clear-cut. The threshold and type of retreatment vary by surgeon, patient, category of failure
   (objective, subjective or both) and initial modality. They also depend on how completely the obstructing
   adenoma was treated.
 • Reported retreatment rates depend on duration and completeness of follow-up. Older trials often
   omitted retreatment with medication; newer trials report it.

Antibiotic Coverage
 • AUA Best Practice Statement (BPS) on antimicrobial prophylaxis, updated 2019:
     ◦ Consider periprocedural systemic prophylaxis for any invasive procedure, in light of patient risk and
       allergy (Statement 3).
     ◦ A single dose is appropriate for most uncomplicated cases (Statement 5) and for clean-contaminated
       genitourinary procedures (Statement 7).
 • Transurethral procedures should cover gram-negative enteric organisms and enterococci. Examples are
   first- or second-generation cephalosporins or trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole.
 • Tailor the choice to the local antibiogram (Statement 12) and to any preoperative urine culture (Statement
   16).
 • Active symptomatic UTI: defer transurethral surgery until treatment is complete and symptoms have
   resolved (Statement 17).

Histologic Specimen
 • Before PSA screening, TURP chips frequently diagnosed prostate cancer: about a quarter of cases were
   incidental cancers. This fell sharply after PSA screening began.
 • With a normal PSA and digital rectal examination, incidental cancer in BPH specimens is uncommon.
   The diagnosis of pT1 and pT1b tumours fell markedly during the PSA era.
 • Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) data: with a PSA cut-off of 4 ng/mL, very few clinically
   significant cancers (Gleason ≥7) would be missed.
 • Therefore, few significant cancers are missed by procedures that yield no specimen: TUVP, PVP, PUL,
   WVTT, PAE and RWT.

Matching the Treatment With the Patient
 • Factors to weigh:
     ◦ The risks, benefits and safety profile of each treatment, and the surgeon's own experience.
     ◦ Prostate size and shape.
     ◦ A history of retention.
     ◦ Inability to stop anticoagulation.
     ◦ Patient preference, for example preserving antegrade ejaculation.
 • The 2021/2023 AUA Guidelines introduced size-based recommendations (Fig. 151.1). Earlier versions had
   none.
 • Cystoscopy is best for identifying a median lobe or excluding a stricture or bladder tumour. It is
   unreliable for sizing. A rule of thumb is that each centimetre of prostatic urethra beyond the normal 2.5
   cm represents about 10 g of extra tissue, but ultrasound, CT and MRI are more accurate.

SPECIFIC TREATMENTS — MINIMALLY INVASIVE SURGICAL THERAPIES
Minimally Invasive Surgical Therapies
 • The ideal MIST:
     ◦ Office-based, in a single session, without general or regional anaesthesia or a hospital stay.
     ◦ Gives better short-term benefit than medication.
     ◦ Avoids the sexual side effects and prolonged recovery of definitive surgery.
     ◦ Removes the need for, and cost of, lifelong daily BPH medication.

Prostatic Urethral Lift
Introduction
 • PUL (UroLift) was developed in 2004 and approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in
   2013. It alters prostatic anatomy without tissue ablation. Its advantages are low complication rates and
   minimal effect on erectile and ejaculatory function.
 • Permanent transprostatic implants are delivered cystoscopically. They compress the parenchyma to open
   the prostatic urethra.
 • AUA: PUL for 30–80 cc without a median lobe. Confirm the absence of a median lobe by cystoscopy or
   ultrasound, and verify the volume.
 • The FDA extended approval to ≤100 cc in 2020. This is not yet in the AUA Guidelines because peer-
   reviewed prospective data are lacking.
Mechanism of Action
 • Performed under local anaesthesia with cystoscopic guidance.
 • Implant design: spring-loaded, with T-shaped ends.
     ◦ Urethral end: stainless steel, which resists encrustation.
     ◦ Capsular tab: nitinol.
     ◦ Connecting suture: permanent monofilament polyethylene terephthalate, held under tension.
 • Deployment: a 19-gauge hollow needle deploys each implant.
     ◦ Glandular tissue is more compliant than the capsule, so implant tension opens the urethra.
     ◦ Typically 4–6 implants are placed anterolaterally from bladder neck to apex (Fig. 151.3). They sit in the
       anterolateral position, between the dorsal veins and the neurovascular bundles.
 • Beyond mechanical compression, canine studies show localised atrophy, fibrosis and scarring within 12
   months.
 • Implants are MRI-conditional. Conditions: ≤3.0 T; maximum spatial field gradient 1500 G/cm; whole-body
   average specific absorption rate 4 W/kg for 15 min. The expected maximum implant heating is 2.4 °C.

Prostatic Urethral Lift for Large Prostates and Those With a Median Lobe
 • The FDA approval for ≤100 cc (2020) rested on a retrospective chart review that has not been made
   public. The AUA found insufficient evidence for 81–100 cc.
 • MedLift: a prospective cohort of men with an obstructing median lobe treated with PUL showed improved
   IPSS, QoL and Qmax. It was excluded by the AUA because it was non-randomised with historical LIFT
   controls.
 • A later retrospective median-lobe series had the same weakness, using historical BPH6/LIFT controls.
Complications
 • Short convalescence: return to preoperative activity in about 8–9 days in LIFT. Because the procedure is
   non-ablative and leaves the mucosa intact, dysuria is minimal.
 • Mild, short-lived early symptoms: dysuria, haematuria, pain and urgency, resolving within 2 weeks.
   Isolated events included clot retention on restarting warfarin and one bladder stone unrelated to the
   implant.
 • Encrustation: a minority had exposed, encrusted implants in the bladder at follow-up cystoscopy.
     ◦ Deploy implants >1.5 cm distal to the bladder neck.
     ◦ Implants placed too proximally can be removed with endoscopic graspers.
 • Network meta-analysis: a lower risk of major adverse events (AEs) than TURP.
 • Rare severe events (Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience, MAUDE, database):
     ◦ Transfusion and clot evacuation.
     ◦ Pelvic or retroperitoneal haematoma; arterial embolisation, orchidectomy and laparotomy in single
       cases.
     ◦ Device malfunction: misfire, needle fragmentation, failed retraction and misplaced implants.
 • No reports of new erectile or ejaculatory dysfunction. The bladder neck is preserved, so antegrade
   ejaculation is maintained.
 • Retreatment risk is higher than after TURP (meta-analysis).
Conclusion
 • PUL: 30–80 cc, no median lobe. It is durable to 5 years and preserves erectile and ejaculatory function. It
   suits men unwilling to take daily medication.

CONVECTIVE RADIOFREQUENCY WATER VAPOUR THERMAL THERAPY
Introduction
 • WVTT (Rezūm) uses convective energy. Heat is transferred through fluid. TUMT and TUNA used
   conductive energy, which is direct transfer of heat.
 • Convection delivers lower energy for a shorter time to cause necrosis.
 • Mechanism: radiofrequency (RF) power generates water vapour, which is injected transurethrally into the
   transition zone.
 • Prostatic collagen contains the thermal energy within the transition zone, preventing off-target ablation.
 • AUA: WVTT for 30–80 cc, and for men who want to preserve erectile and ejaculatory function (Statements
   36 and 37).
 • Unlike PUL, WVTT can treat a median lobe.

Mechanism of Action
 • Equipment: a generator and a handheld delivery device (Fig. 151.4). The device includes a rigid cystoscope
   and an 18-gauge retractable needle with 12 vapour-emitting holes. Saline irrigation provides vision and
   cools the urethra.
 • Sequence: needle penetrates a fixed 10.25 mm → first injection 1 cm distal to the bladder neck at 3 and 9
   o'clock → further injections about 1 cm apart, distally to the proximal edge of the verumontanum →
   contiguous overlapping lesions (Fig. 151.5).
 • Each injection: vapour at 103 °C for 9 seconds, at slightly above interstitial pressure, so that it disperses
   through the interstitium by convection.
 • Condensation releases 540 cal/g onto cell membranes. This causes necrosis and a 1.5–2.0 cm lesion,
   confirmed on MRI and pathology.
 • Energy per procedure: WVTT uses only 4 kJ. Comparators: TUNA 20 kJ, TUMT 86 kJ, ablative lasers 250 kJ.
 • Median lobe: the procedure can be modified to treat it.
 • Outpatient procedure under oral sedation, conscious sedation or prostatic block. Mean treatment time is
   about 5 minutes.

Outcomes
Comparative Studies — Rezūm II
 • Rezūm II: a multicentre RCT, 2:1 WVTT vs sham (rigid cystoscopy with simulated treatment sounds).
     ◦ Inclusion: age ≥50, IPSS ≥13, Qmax 5–15 mL/s, 30–80 cc, PVR ≤250 mL. Median lobes were included.
     ◦ Primary end point: IPSS reduction at 3 months.
 • At 3 months:
     ◦ IPSS fell by 11.2 points with WVTT vs 4.3 with sham.
     ◦ Qmax rose by 6.2 vs 0.5 mL/s, and QoL improved more.
     ◦ No PVR benefit.
     ◦ After unblinding, most sham patients crossed over.

 • Procedure details: injections averaged about 4–5 into the lateral lobes and 1–2 into the median lobe.
     ◦ Pain scores were higher than with sham.
     ◦ Most patients had oral sedation only; a minority had a prostatic block or intravenous sedation.
     ◦ Nearly all were catheterised for a few days, but most catheters were discretionary; only about a third
       followed a failed void trial.
     ◦ Median return to usual activity was 4 days after catheter removal.
 • Durability:
     ◦ Around 50% improvement in IPSS and QoL, and Qmax, at 1 and 2 years, including the crossover group.
     ◦ At 2 years there was no significant loss of erectile function, and ejaculatory bother improved.
 • Five years:
     ◦ IPSS −48%, QoL −45%, Qmax +49%.
     ◦ The responder end point (no retreatment and ≥30% IPSS fall) was met by the majority.
     ◦ No de novo erectile dysfunction (ED).
     ◦ Surgical retreatment 4.4% and medical retreatment 11.1%, most within the first 2 years.
 • Trap — the retreatment denominator: 4.4% was calculated on the original 135 patients, not on the 77
   followed to 5 years, which would give 7.8%. With 58 patients lost to follow-up, true retreatment may be
   higher.

Complications
 • AEs were mild to moderate. Only two were serious: de novo prolonged retention, and nausea or vomiting
   after oral sedation.
 • Non-serious AEs resolved within about 3 weeks: dysuria, haematuria, haematospermia, and urgency or
   frequency.
 • Crossover arm: a bladder neck contracture (BNC) with a bladder stone, and urosepsis after cystoscopy.
 • MAUDE (rare): clot evacuation, transfusion, bladder perforation, epididymitis and infected hydroceles.
 • Rectal safety: never aim the needle directly posterior (the urethral floor within 1 cm of the
   verumontanum) when treating a median lobe. Aim 45° off midline.
 • Sexual function:
     ◦ Temporary reduced ejaculate volume or anejaculation occurs early in a few patients and is almost gone
       by 12 months.
     ◦ No de novo ED at 5 years and no significant loss of IIEF or MSHQ-EjD function.

Conclusion
 • WVTT gives durable 5-year efficacy for 30–80 cc, with or without a median lobe. There is insufficient
   evidence outside that range.
 • It can be done under local anaesthesia as an outpatient, with mild-to-moderate AEs and no obvious harm
   to sexual function.
 • Retreatment is comparable or superior to other MISTs, but probably higher than the reported 4.4%.

 KEY POINTS
   • PUL: a MIST for 30–80 cc without a median lobe.
   • WVTT: a MIST for 30–80 cc with or without a median lobe.
   • Both PUL and WVTT are MISTs for men who want intervention with preservation of erectile and
     ejaculatory function.

RESECTION-BASED TECHNIQUES
Monopolar Transurethral Resection of the Prostate
Introduction
 • TURP is still the gold-standard surgical treatment. It has been performed since the 1940s and has a
   modest learning curve.
 • An electrified wire loop removes transition-zone tissue from the bladder neck to the verumontanum,
   down to the depth of the surgical capsule.
 • AUA: TURP for small and average prostates (Statement 28). There is no preference between monopolar
   and bipolar; choose by expertise (Statement 29).
Mechanism of Action and Technique
 • Monopolar TURP (m-TURP): current passes from the loop through the tissue and the patient to a
   grounding pad.
 • It requires non-ionic irrigation (sterile water, glycine or sorbitol). These fluids are hypo-osmolar.
   Absorption through open sinuses causes acute dilutional hyponatraemia, known as transurethral
   resection (TUR) syndrome. This risk has moved many surgeons to bipolar TURP.
Preoperative considerations.
 • General or spinal anaesthesia. Spinal was historically preferred so that TUR syndrome could be detected
   in the awake patient. Signs include confusion, visual loss, nausea and vomiting, electrocardiogram (ECG)
   changes, coma and death.
 • Positioning: dorsal lithotomy, padded to protect the common peroneal nerve. Put the buttocks at the
   table edge; otherwise the scope collides with the table and the anterior prostate cannot be reached.
 • Baseline abdominal examination so that later prevesical fluid from a perforation can be recognised.

 • Shaving is not required. Prepare the lower abdomen, genitalia and perineum.
 • Grounding pad: on the leg outside the prepared field, on the side opposite any joint replacement.
 • O'Conor rectal shield: historically allowed sterile rectal access to lift the prostate. It is no longer
   commercially available.
 • Irrigation: at body temperature and at the lowest height that gives vision. Raise it only if bleeding

Intraoperative considerations.
 • Before starting:
     ◦ Check the resectoscope assembly. A camera is standard; continuous flow may be passive or active.
     ◦ Use an organised, systematic plan; the best approach is the one the surgeon knows best.
 • Inspect the bladder first for tumour or diverticula. An unsuspected tumour is resected first, and TURP is
   deferred until staging is complete.
 • Note the landmarks and their relation to the adenoma: the bladder neck, trigone, ureteric orifices,
   verumontanum and external sphincter. If the orifices are hard to see, give intravenous indigo carmine or
   methylene blue.
 • If the loop does not cut, check in sequence: scope-to-generator connection → foot pedal → irrigant
   matches the generator technology → patient properly grounded.
 • A median lobe is resected first, then the lateral lobes.
 • Current: mixed (blended) current gives better haemostasis but cuts less well.
 • Approach A — modified Nesbit "encirclement": start at 11–9 and 1–3 o'clock. Expose, but do not resect,
   the bladder neck fibres. Carry the resection to the base of the verumontanum, sparing the external
   sphincter.
 • Fig. 151.6 sequence (anterior start): 12 o'clock, bladder neck and adjacent adenoma in quadrants →
   midportion 12 to 9 o'clock → left midfossa 12 to 3 o'clock → midportion 9 to 6 o'clock → apical tissue
   from beside the verumontanum towards 12 o'clock → residual right side cleared → residual left side

   dorsal junction is over-resected early, the scope moves "uphill" and can detach the trigone from the

   synchronised rocking motion. Short or thin chips are inefficient and leave an irregular bed that hides
   bleeders.

     ◦ The anterior fossa has the least adenoma and perforates easily.
     ◦ The sphincter is tilted, so its anterior part lies more proximally.
     ◦ Emptying the bladder helps to see the anterior area, but keep enough fluid in to avoid perforating the
       bladder with the loop extended.
 • Premature over-resection or capsular perforation opens venous sinuses, causing bleeding and fluid

 • Recovery is usually uneventful: early diet, and walking on the day of surgery if haematuria is mild.

 • A dose-escalating, placebo-controlled trial of a heparin-type drug given at TURP showed dose-

Complications
 • Intraoperative complications are infrequent (transfusion, TUR syndrome, bleeding). Overall morbidity
   approaches about one-fifth when clot retention, recatheterisation, infection, stricture, sexual dysfunction
   and incontinence are included.
Intraoperative and perioperative complications.
 • TUR syndrome — mechanism:
     ◦ Prostatic venous pressure is about 10 mm Hg; irrigation above this pressure is absorbed through
       exposed vessels.
     ◦ Hypo-osmolar fluid causes acute dilutional hyponatraemia. Features: confusion, nausea, vomiting,
       visual changes, hypertension, tachypnoea and bradycardia.
 • Risk factors (Veterans Affairs Cooperative Study): gland >45 cc and resection time >90 minutes. The
   incidence is low and falling in RCT meta-analyses. Isotonic, iso-osmolar irrigation (bipolar) theoretically
   eliminates TUR syndrome.
 • Alternative aetiology — glycine toxicity: absorbed glycine releases ammonia, giving immediate or delayed
   encephalopathy.
 • Irrigation height: 60 cm above the patient (Madsen and Naber) is the minimum for good vision without
   excess absorption. An extra 10 cm more than doubles absorption.
 • Diagnosis: neurological status plus serum sodium. Check sodium postoperatively after long or large
   resections, or intraoperatively if concerned.
     ◦ Na+ <120 mEq/L means significant dilution, with risk of coma, seizures and transtentorial herniation.
     ◦ Transient visual disturbance or blindness indicates central nervous system (CNS) toxicity.
 • Treatment of profound CNS symptoms: judicious hypertonic saline.
    ◦ 3% saline 100 mL bolus raises Na+ by 2–3 mEq/L. Repeat once or twice at 10-minute intervals if still
      symptomatic (maximum 300 mL).
     ◦ Target: raise Na+ by 4–6 mEq/L.
     ◦ A rise of up to 12 mEq/L in the first 24 hours is considered safe for acute post-TURP hyponatraemia.


                                                         page 13 of 35
Chapter 151 — Minimally Invasive and Endoscopic Management of BPH                         Resection-Based Techniques
     ◦ If symptoms present >48 hours after surgery, correct by no more than 8–10 mEq/L per 24 hours.
 • Overly rapid correction risks central pontine myelinolysis. Acute post-TURP hyponatraemia is safer to
   correct than chronic hyponatraemia, because the brain has not yet adapted its organic osmolytes.
 • Ureteric injury is uncommon. Identify the orifices first, helped by the 70° lens and intravenous methylene
   blue or indigo carmine. If they are hidden by a high bladder neck or large median lobe, start in the midline
   and take down the median lobe first; the orifices then appear.
 • Haemorrhage is common. Achieve full haemostasis to avoid a return to theatre. The transfusion risk is low
   but must be counselled.
 • Perforation can occur at the prostatovesical junction, the capsule or the bladder, from resection or from
   overdistension of thinned capsule.
     ◦ Glistening periprostatic or perivesical fat is the telltale sign.
     ◦ If unclear, perform cystography with drainage films.
     ◦ Prostatic extravasation is almost always extraperitoneal.
 • Management of perforation:
     ◦ Dome perforation → cystogram to exclude intraperitoneal rupture, which needs laparotomy and
       cystorrhaphy.
     ◦ Extraperitoneal with limited extravasation → prolonged catheter drainage and observation.
     ◦ Extensive extravasation → percutaneous or open drainage.
 • Intraoperative erection limits scope movement.
     ◦ It often subsides spontaneously; otherwise give intracorporeal phenylephrine and alert anaesthesia to
       possible cardiovascular effects.
     ◦ If injections fail, perineal urethrostomy is a last resort.
Postoperative complications.
 • Bladder neck contracture:
     ◦ Occurs in a small minority. It is caused by over-resection plus excessive fulguration of the bladder
       neck; an undermined trigonal flap may also heal as a membrane.
     ◦ Typical story: excellent early flow that slowly declines, more markedly than with regrowth. Average
       onset is about 6 months (range 3 weeks to 10 years). Serial flow rates decline.
 • BNC management — step-up:
      1. Office cystoscopy to confirm.
        2. Gentle dilation with sounds or a balloon.
        3. Endoscopic incision with a Collins knife, optical urethrotome or laser, over an open-ended catheter
           or wire, until the scar springs open. If the opening is obliterated, intravenous methylene blue with
           suprapubic pressure shows a blue jet, usually anterior. Incise aggressively, down to perivesical fat,
           because after TURP the sphincter is distant; contrast the anastomotic stricture after radical
           prostatectomy, where the sphincter is close.

           0.4 mg/mL).
        5. Intractable BNC → open or robotic Y-V plasty. Endoscopic suturing revision is a newer option.
 • Urethral stricture:
     ◦ Occurs in a small minority, from resectoscope trauma or postoperative infection.
     ◦ Choose an appropriate sheath size. Many men do not calibrate to ≥28 Fr at the meatus and fossa
       navicularis.

     ◦ Calibrate or gently dilate the meatus, inspect on insertion and lubricate generously. The authors instil

 • Incontinence:
     ◦ The internal sphincter is the main male continence mechanism, and TURP resects it or renders it
       incompetent. Continence then depends entirely on the external sphincter; injuring it causes total

     ◦ The verumontanum is the key landmark, both as the distal stop point and for future cystoscopy.

     ◦ The sphincter complex is poorly demarcated, thinnest anteriorly, and tilted so that its anterior part lies

     ◦ Significant early bleeding usually reflects incomplete haemostasis.
     ◦ Delayed bleeding at 1–4 weeks with eschar is common. It becomes less likely with time, and mild
       bleeding settles with rest and fluids.
 • Postoperative retention occurs in a small minority, a proportion unchanged over time. Prolonged
   catheterisation reflects bleeding or surgeon preference.
 • Retreatment:
     ◦ Causes: incomplete resection (apex or anterior), poor selection (gland >80 cc), technical error,
       stricture, BNC, regrowth or residual BPH.
     ◦ Retreatment for BPH is rare. Revision risk rises with gland size and preoperative retention.

   Erectile, orgasmic, desire and satisfaction domains are not worsened by either monopolar or bipolar TURP.
   Discomfort on ejaculation is not increased.

   However, temperatures there stay near body temperature during TURP. There is no conclusive evidence
   that TURP causes ED.
Conclusion
 • m-TURP is durable and effective for IPSS, QoL and objective parameters, and it is the benchmark for
   every new BPH therapy. Its morbidity drove the development of less invasive alternatives.
Concept — Every characteristic hazard of monopolar TURP follows from current returning through the
patient. That forces hypo-osmolar irrigation, which causes TUR syndrome, which rewards speed over
haemostasis. Continence, by contrast, depends on one landmark: stopping at the verumontanum.

BIPOLAR TRANSURETHRAL RESECTION OF THE PROSTATE
Introduction
 • Bipolar TURP (b-TURP) carries the active and return poles on the same electrode, so current stays at the
   resection site and does not pass through the patient to a pad.
 • This allows isotonic, iso-osmolar saline irrigation, eliminating TUR syndrome. Large resections and
   meticulous haemostasis are possible without time pressure.
 • True bipolar: a dual-loop design in which energy arcs between adjacent loops while cutting.




                                                         page 15 of 35
Chapter 151 — Minimally Invasive and Endoscopic Management of BPH           Bipolar Transurethral Resection of the Prostate
 • Transurethral Resection in Saline (TURis) was not truly bipolar: the outer sheath acted as the return
   electrode, exposing the whole urethra and penis to return energy. Strictures and urethrocutaneous
   fistulae resulted, and it fell out of favour.
 • Plasma system (Olympus; common in the US):
     ◦ Energy vaporises the saline around the loop; further energy converts the gas to plasma. Excited sodium
       ions give the orange glow.
     ◦ It resects at lower voltage and temperature while sealing vessels, giving better haemostasis.
     ◦ The loop is platinum–iridium, not tungsten, to withstand thermal and electrical stress.
     ◦ The coagulation setting does not create plasma; it heats tissue to seal vessels.
     ◦ Energy spread is narrower and charring is less, possibly giving fewer storage symptoms.

Technique
 • Essentially identical to m-TURP. It is often quicker, because cutting and sealing are simultaneous.
 • Plasma ignition: older generators struggled to ignite the plasma; newer generators have largely solved
   this. Drag on the loop at first tissue contact means the plasma glow has not ignited.

Outcomes
Comparative Studies
 • Repeated meta-analyses of RCTs: m-TURP and b-TURP have equivalent efficacy for IPSS, QoL and PVR, in
   both the short term and the long term (up to 5 years).
 • Qmax may favour b-TURP slightly. The difference is statistically but not clinically significant, and is skewed
   by a few heavily weighted trials.

Complications
 • The key safety gain comes from isotonic irrigation. The "cut and seal" energy profile and the removal of
   time pressure improve haemostasis.
Perioperative
 • Meta-analyses: b-TURP has fewer overall complications, with lower transfusion, clot retention, TUR
   syndrome (none), immediate reoperation and haemoglobin drop.
 • No difference in UTI or AUR.
 • Number needed to treat with b-TURP: about 50 to prevent one TUR syndrome, and about 20 to prevent
   one clot retention.
Postoperative
 • Urethral stricture, BNC, incontinence, erectile and ejaculatory dysfunction, and BPH retreatment are
   broadly similar between modalities. Some series show slightly less stricture and BNC with bipolar; one
   earlier meta-analysis showed non-significantly more stricture with bipolar.
 • Catheter duration and length of stay (LOS) are shorter with b-TURP.

Conclusion
 • b-TURP is equivalent in efficacy but safer, and is likely to replace m-TURP as the gold standard. Better
   haemostasis and isotonic irrigation allow longer, safer resections.

TRANSURETHRAL INCISION OF THE PROSTATE
Introduction and Concept
 • Incising the prostate or bladder neck dates to Guthrie (1834). TUIP disrupts the prostatic capsule;
   capsular contraction has been proposed as a cause of LUTS.
 • TUIP only for small glands (<30 cc; Statement 31). The ideal patient is a young man with a small prostate
   concerned about ejaculation or fertility.
 • Retrograde ejaculation risk is lower than with TURP, especially with a unilateral incision. If ejaculation is
   paramount, avoid the bladder neck and a complete capsular incision.
 • Because MISTs also preserve ejaculation with minimal morbidity, TUIP may be replaced. It remains
   reasonable where MISTs are unavailable.

Technique
 • Selection is critical: large glands or severe symptoms respond poorly and will need more treatment.
 • A median lobe is a relative contraindication, since most studies excluded it. The EAU recommends TUIP
   for <30 cc without a median lobe.
 • Instruments: a hot (Collins) knife, a thin resectoscope loop, or an end-firing holmium or thulium laser.
 • Sequence (Fig. 151.8): avoid the ureteric orifices → unilateral or bilateral incision at 5 and 7 o'clock →
   start distal to the ureteric orifice → through the bladder neck → end proximal to the verumontanum →
   deepen until the surgical capsule is seen (some go on to periprostatic fat) → haemostasis with cautery.
 • Bleeding should be minimal. The catheter is removed and the patient discharged on day 0–1.
   Convalescence is rapid, with few irritative symptoms.

Outcomes
Comparative Studies — TUIP versus TURP
 • The trials are of poor to moderate methodological quality and are restricted to small glands, which limits
   meta-analysis.
 • TUIP gives shorter operating time, less blood loss and shorter LOS. Catheter duration was longer after
   TUIP in one trial.
 • Symptom scores improve similarly, with a small advantage for TURP. Qmax improves more after TURP.
 • Reoperation is higher after TUIP. Residual obstructing lobes or interlobar adhesions are common at
   follow-up cystoscopy.
 • Holmium laser TUIP in glands <30 cc gives durable improvement to about 5 years.

Complications
Intraoperative and Perioperative
 • Transfusion is rare and much less frequent than after TURP; some TURP comparators had implausibly high
   transfusion rates.
 • Capsular perforation, intended or not, is managed with prolonged catheterisation.
 • Temporary retention occurs in a minority.
Postoperative
 • Retrograde ejaculation is less likely than after TURP (reported 0–37%, probably at the lower end). A
   single incision may carry less risk, but other reports found two incisions did not increase it.
 • No difference from TURP in retention, infection, stricture, incontinence or ED.
 • Reoperation is more likely after TUIP.
 • BNC was not seen after TUIP, whereas it occurred after TURP. Urethral stricture was uncommon.

Conclusion
 • TUIP gives adequate results in well-selected men: a short, simple operation with minimal stay and less
   retrograde ejaculation. Discourage it for moderate or large glands or a median lobe.

 KEY POINTS
   • m-TURP remains an important treatment and the benchmark for new BPH technologies.
   • b-TURP's key advance is resection in isotonic irrigation: no TUR syndrome and better bleeding
     outcomes.
   • AUA 2023: m-TURP or b-TURP for <30 cc and 30–80 cc.
   • TUIP is reasonable in carefully selected men (<30 cc, wishing to preserve antegrade ejaculation); a
     median lobe is a relative contraindication.

Concept — TUIP relieves obstruction by releasing the capsule and bladder neck rather than removing
adenoma. That buys ejaculatory preservation and speed at the cost of durability, and confines it to small
glands without a median lobe.

Robotic Waterjet Therapy
Introduction and Concept
 • RWT (Aquablation) uses a non-thermal, high-velocity saline waterjet to ablate tissue.
     ◦ The surgeon maps the treatment area on real-time transrectal ultrasound (TRUS).
     ◦ A robotically guided transurethral jet then ablates it in minutes.
     ◦ The bladder neck, external sphincter and periprostatic nerves are spared from thermal or anatomical
       injury.
 • Short learning curve, high capital cost. Proponents claim minimal effect on continence, erection and
   antegrade ejaculation.
 • AUA: RWT for 30–80 cc (Statement 39), based on an RCT. A median lobe can be treated.
Technique
 • Aquabeam Robotic System: a console and pump, a robotic transurethral handpiece and a TRUS probe.
   Requires general or spinal anaesthesia.

Chapter 151 — Minimally Invasive and Endoscopic Management of BPH                         Ablation-Based Techniques
 • Sequence: dorsal lithotomy → biplanar TRUS in the rectum → 24 Fr handpiece with integrated cystoscope,
   fixed to a bed-rail arm, placed 1–2 cm past the bladder neck → retract the cystoscope to confirm position
   → sagittal TRUS view capturing prostate, bladder and handpiece → register the handpiece (centred on the
   transverse view, rotated between 3 and 9 o'clock) → plan depth and angle on the console, excluding the
   bladder neck and external sphincter → foot pedal fires the jet in proximal-to-distal sweeps.
 • Waterjet velocity controls ablation depth, to a maximum of 2.4 cm. Treatment is monitored by TRUS and
   cystoscopy.
 • The sphincter is protected because ablation occurs only in front of the cystoscope.
 • Limits:
     ◦ RWT ablates only posterior tissue between 4 and 8 o'clock, so it is unsuitable for substantial anterior

     ◦ No histological specimen.

     ◦ With a resectoscope, bluntly clear the "fluffy" ablated remnant tissue with the loop, then cauterise the
       exposed vessels. Aggressive resection is unnecessary because the tissue is already ablated.
     ◦ This markedly reduced transfusion.
     ◦ The EAU advises informing patients of the bleeding risk.
Outcomes
Comparative studies.
 • WATER: an international, double-blind multicentre RCT of RWT vs TURP, 2:1.
     ◦ Inclusion: age 45–80, 30–80 cc on TRUS, IPSS ≥12, Qmax <15 mL/s, PVR <300 mL.
     ◦ Designed for IPSS non-inferiority, with a margin of 4.7 points.
 • At 6 months RWT was non-inferior. Its mean IPSS improvement was 1.8 points greater, and more so in
   the 50–80 cc subgroup. QoL, Qmax and PVR did not differ.
 • Total operating time was similar, but resection time was far shorter with RWT (about 4 vs 27 min).
 • Durability: equal IPSS gains at 1 year. At 3 and 5 years improvements were durable and equivalent, with
   larger IPSS gains for RWT in 50–80 cc glands.

Perioperative.

   stay and catheter duration were equal.
 • WATER II (large glands, non-comparative): a high 30-day transfusion rate and prolonged catheter traction.
   Focal bladder-neck cautery has since reduced bleeding.
 • Rectal injury is rare but catastrophic; one required a diverting colostomy. Place the TRUS probe carefully
   and consider routine rectoscopy after RWT.
Postoperative.
 • WATER at 3 months: fewer complications with RWT (26% vs 42%), driven by fewer Clavien grade 1
   events.
 • Retrograde ejaculation was lower with RWT (10% vs 36%). This was attributed to the absence of thermal
   energy and to planning that avoids the verumontanum.
 • Trap — the true rate is probably higher. Men with baseline anejaculation, possibly from α-blockers, were

TRANSURETHRAL VAPORISATION OF THE PROSTATE
Introduction and Concept
 • TUVP (Kaplan and Te, 1995) uses TURP equipment with a larger-surface electrode. Higher energy spread
   over a broader contact area vaporises rather than resects, with simultaneous coagulation.
     ◦ This gives better haemostasis and a clearer field.
     ◦ No new capital outlay is needed.
 • VaporTrode (Olympus; Fig. 151.9): a grooved roller electrode, the most-studied monopolar option.
 • TUVP moved to bipolar energy in the late 1990s, allowing isotonic irrigation. AUA: bipolar TUVP for small
   and average glands (Statement 32).

 • Laser stands for light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation. Laser energy is converted to local

 • Advantages: ambulatory (<23-hour observation) or same-day surgery, and minimally invasive versions of

 • Coagulation lasers were abandoned for vaporisation. The laser coagulation code (Current Procedural
   Terminology, CPT 52647) is essentially obsolete compared with laser vaporisation (CPT 52648).

PHOTOSELECTIVE VAPORISATION OF THE PROSTATE
Introduction and Concept
 • PVP uses KTP or LBO lasers, derived from Nd:YAG.
 • Nd:YAG was abandoned: its 1 cm penetration caused massive sloughing, intermittent voiding and
   retention, and it raised safety concerns.
 • Passing the 1064 nm Nd:YAG beam through a KTP or LBO crystal doubles its frequency and halves the
   wavelength to 532 nm. This is absorbed by haemoglobin, an intravascular target, with no energy lost to
   water.
 • Higher energy density gives preferential vaporisation with a thin (0.2 mm) coagulation rim, and so good
   haemostasis.
 • Platforms ("Greenlight" is a trade name):
     ◦ KTP 80 W and 100 W.
     ◦ LBO 120 W High Performance System (HPS) and 180 W (XPS).


                                                         page 22 of 35
Chapter 151 — Minimally Invasive and Endoscopic Management of BPH              Photoselective Vaporisation of the Prostate
 • The 180 W platform has the same power density and the same 1–2 mm vaporisation and coagulation
   depth as 120 W, with a larger beam area (0.28 → 0.44 mm²). The MoXy fibre adds:
     ◦ A metal tip against devitrification.
     ◦ Built-in fluid cooling and automatic overheating protection.
     ◦ Pulsed coagulation.
 • Higher power vaporises more efficiently in animal, ex vivo and clinical studies. Vaporisation and
   coagulation are simultaneous, closing sinuses and preventing fluid absorption.
 • AUA: PVP (120 or 180 W) for <30 cc and 30–80 cc (Statement 33), and an option for higher bleeding risk
   (Statement 43).

Technique
Pre- and Intraoperative
 • Standard workup. PVP is acceptable when anticoagulation cannot be stopped.
 • Equipment: a 23–24 Fr rigid cystoscope. A 600 µm side-firing fibre emits at 70° to the long axis; the 180
   W system uses a 750 µm fibre.
 • Method: sweep the fibre over the parenchyma, vaporising successive superficial layers until capsular
   fibres are seen.
 • Bladder-neck sequence (first step): identify the ureteric orifices → lower power (about 80 W) in this area
   if preferred → one midline incision or incisions at 5 and 7 o'clock to open the bladder neck → vaporise the
   posterior bladder neck until level with the trigone → turn the fibre medially or laterally for vision.
 • Safety at the bladder neck:
     ◦ Aim laterally, never towards either ureteric orifice.
     ◦ Avoid aggressive coagulation at the bladder neck.
     ◦ Some spare an anterior strip of urothelium to prevent BNC.
 • Posterior tissue is harder than at TURP, because the fibre cannot treat tissue directly in line with it; this
   matters most with a high bladder neck. Vaporise posterior tissue in horizontal layers, never aiming
   posteriorly towards the rectum. Move the fibre more than the scope.
 • Working distance:
     ◦ Too close gives contact vaporisation and fibre damage.
     ◦ Too far gives inefficiency and coagulation instead of vaporisation.
     ◦ Use lower power in tight spaces, such as kissing lateral lobes. Prevent tissue build-up on the fibre.
 • Sweep technique:
     ◦ Continuous, even sweeps avoid irregular craters: too fast gives poor vaporisation, too slow creates a
       crater.
     ◦ The most efficient sweep angle is 15–30°, with least coagulation at 30°.
     ◦ Novice rule: sweep 30° in 1–2 seconds.
 • Large bubbles signal efficient vaporisation. Their absence means coagulation or charring, which wastes
   energy and increases dysuria and sloughing.
 • Bleeding hot spots: apex, median lobe, bladder neck and posterolateral prostate, where vessels enter.
     ◦ Moderate bleeding → vaporise the adjacent tissue to control feeding vessels.
     ◦ Pulsatile bleeding → coagulation mode (about 30 W) around and on the vessel. Pinching the vessel
       with the scope or fibre tip before coagulating its edges helps.

 • Finish: re-inspect the bladder for errant laser injury, place a catheter and confirm clear irrigation.

 • Only 120 and 180 W data are relevant to the guideline. PVP is effective and safe.
 • Learning curve: 30–50 cases for familiarity, and more than 100 for efficient vaporisation.
Comparative Studies
 • GOLIATH (landmark): a non-inferiority RCT of 180 W PVP vs TURP at 29 European centres.
     ◦ Men aged 40–80, IPSS ≥12, Qmax ≤15 mL/s, prostate ≤100 cc, American Society of Anesthesiologists

     ◦ PVP non-inferior for IPSS, Qmax and complications at 6 months, 1 year and 2 years. There was no 5-

 • Energy density: reoperation is lower with 180 W than with lower-power platforms. >5 kJ/cc is associated
   with greater PSA decline, a marker of more complete vaporisation.

Complications
Intraoperative and Perioperative
 • No TUR syndrome has ever been reported, because saline is used. Transfusion is very rare, even on
   anticoagulants, and markedly lower than after m-TURP.
 • Capsular perforation is rare (0.2–1%). Maintain an even sweep and deepen circumferentially and

 • Ureteric orifice injury: identify the orifices first, aim away from them, and do not advance the fibre too far
   into the bladder.
Postoperative
 • Dysuria and storage symptoms are common and self-limiting within 3 months. PVP has the highest
   dysuria rate among transurethral techniques. Anti-inflammatories, culture-directed antibiotics or a short
   steroid course help.
 • Infection: UTI and epididymitis are more frequent because the necrotic coagulum harbours bacteria.
   Consider longer antibiotic courses.
 • Stricture: the 24 Fr sheath (vs 26–28 Fr for TURP) may theoretically lower stricture risk. Meta-analysis
   shows no significant difference in stricture or BNC.
 • Erectile function is minimally affected overall. Men with normal or mildly impaired baseline function may
   decline, though none needed ED treatment.
 • Retrograde ejaculation is common, similar to TURP.

 • Vaporisation replaced coagulation. Higher-power platforms improved efficiency and retreatment, and
   532 nm haemoglobin selectivity allows treatment of anticoagulated patients. AUA: <30 cc, 30–80 cc and
   high bleeding risk; not >80 cc, where retreatment is unacceptably high.

   • Laser vaporisation has supplanted laser coagulation.
   • PVP exploits selective absorption of 532 nm by haemoglobin, giving efficient vaporisation and

   • AUA: PVP for patients at higher risk of bleeding.

Concept — Frequency-doubling converts a deep, sloughing Nd:YAG beam into a haemoglobin-seeking 532

Concept and Introduction — Comparison of Thulium and Thulium Fibre Lasers

 • Safe in experienced hands. There was no incomplete morcellation or ureteric injury; morcellator bladder
   injury is uncommon, similar to ThuVEP. Transfusion is uncommon.
Postoperative
 • Early morbidity: symptomatic UTI and early retreatment for residual BPH occur in a minority. Mild storage
   symptoms are frequent but settle within a month. Transient urge incontinence resolves by 1 year.
 • Late complications: BNC and urethral stricture are uncommon at 4–5 years. Retreatment for recurrence is
   low at 5 years.
 • Sexual function: no effect on erectile function (IIEF, IIEF-5); retrograde ejaculation in most (about four-
   fifths) of those with baseline antegrade ejaculation.

Conclusion
 • Thulium 2013 nm (continuous) and TFL 1940 nm (quasi-continuous). Continuous-wave thulium may
   carbonise tissue, which does not impair recovery beyond the learning curve. ThuFLEP is newer but
   promising.
 • ThuLEP is size-independent (Statement 38) and suitable for higher bleeding risk (Statement 43).
 • HoLEP and ThuLEP have the strongest evidence among AEEP energy sources. Long-term follow-up is
   needed to show whether others match HoLEP.

 KEY POINTS
   • HoLEP and ThuLEP are options for patients at increased bleeding risk (Statement 43).
   • HoLEP and ThuLEP are size-independent, with excellent results (Statement 38).
   • HoLEP has long-term outcomes comparable to those historically seen with open prostatectomy.
   • Thulium enucleation was popularised later; longer-term (>15-year) outcomes are still needed.

TRUE MINIMALLY INVASIVE SURGICAL THERAPY CONCEPT
 • MISTs still need rigid cystoscopy (PUL, WVTT), which is uncomfortable.
 • True minimally invasive surgical therapies (TMISTs):
     ◦ Off-the-shelf devices deployed by flexible cystoscopy without general anaesthesia or sedation.
     ◦ Preserve sexual function and improve symptoms.
     ◦ Offer an alternative to lifelong medication.
 • They are deliberately not durable: the target durability is 12–18 months.

TEMPORARY IMPLANTED PROSTATIC DEVICES
Concept and Technique
 • TIPD (iTind; Olympus; originally Medi-Tate, Israel) is FDA-approved. It gives immediate relief without a
   permanent implant.
 • Design:
     ◦ Three elongated nitinol struts at 12, 5 and 7 o'clock.
     ◦ An anchoring leaflet at 6 o'clock against migration.
     ◦ A polyester retrieval suture.
     ◦ Expands to a maximum 33 mm diameter and 50 mm length.
 • Deployment: by flexible or rigid cystoscope, under local anaesthesia or oral or intravenous sedation.
 • Mechanism: left in place for 5–7 days, exerting radial force that remodels the bladder neck and prostatic
   urethra by ischaemic pressure necrosis.
 • Removal: advance an 18 Fr silicone catheter over the suture and pull it taut, so the device collapses into
   the catheter.
 • AUA 2023: TIPD is an option for 25–75 cc without an obstructing median lobe (Statement 41).

Outcomes
 • Pivotal RCT, TIPD vs sham (catheter), 2:1.
     ◦ Inclusion: age ≥50, IPSS ≥10, Qmax ≤12 mL/s, 25–75 cc, no obstructing median lobe.
     ◦ Primary end point: proportion with ≥3-point IPSS improvement at 3 months.
 • Results:
     ◦ Met: a higher proportion of responders with TIPD than sham, including at a ≥7-point threshold. Almost
       all responders remained responders at 12 months.
     ◦ But the absolute IPSS difference from sham at 3 months was not significant, and QoL did not differ.
       Qmax improved.
     ◦ Tolerability was good; AEs were mild and lasted 2–4 weeks.
     ◦ A small minority needed alternative surgery or restarted medication by 12 months.
     ◦ No de novo erectile or ejaculatory dysfunction.
 • Single-arm 2-year study: durable IPSS, QoL, Qmax and PVR with no sexual dysfunction. A median lobe
   (treated despite exclusion) predicted failure.

Conclusion
 • TIPD met its primary end point but not a significant absolute IPSS difference. There are no long-term
   data and no comparative RCT against TURP or other MISTs. The AUA statement rests on expert opinion
   only.

DRUG-COATED BALLOON DILATION
Concept and Technique
 • Optilume BPH Catheter has two balloons:
     ◦ A predilation balloon delivers mechanical force, dilating the channel and causing an anterior
       commissurotomy.
     ◦ A paclitaxel-coated balloon delivers an antiproliferative drug to prevent regrowth and commissural
       scarring. It is inflated for at least 5 minutes.
 • The balloon design anchors force in the prostatic fossa, not the bladder neck. A catheter is left for at least
   48 hours.

Outcomes
 • EVEREST-1: prospective, single-arm, open-label, multicentre (Latin America).
     ◦ Inclusion: age ≥50, IPSS ≥13, Qmax 5–15 mL/s, PVR ≤250 mL, 20–80 cc, prostatic urethral length 35–55
       mm.
     ◦ Excluded: prior transurethral surgery, median lobe >1 cm, stricture and neurogenic bladder.
     ◦ Primary end point: ≥40% IPSS improvement at 3 months.
 • Results:
     ◦ The primary goal was exceeded, with IPSS and QoL improving by about two-thirds to 12 months and a
       meaningful Qmax gain.
     ◦ Complications occurred in a sizeable minority: mainly haematuria, incontinence and UTI. Ejaculatory
       dysfunction occurred in a small minority.
 • Pending: 4-year EVEREST-1 data and the sham-controlled, double-blind PINNACLE RCT. EVEREST-1 was
   done under general or spinal anaesthesia; the aim is office-based use as a TMIST.

PROSTATE ARTERY EMBOLISATION
Introduction and Concept
 • First reported in 2000. Planning may use MRI or CT pelvic angiography.
 • Sequence: femoral arterial access (usually unilateral right femoral) under local anaesthesia → pelvic
   angiography of the iliac tree and prostatic arteries → urethral catheter with the balloon filled with 30%
   contrast and 70% saline as a landmark → 1.9–2.4 Fr microcatheters into the prostatic arteries → embolic
   agent (e.g. alcohol or microspheres) infused until stasis.
 • Bilateral PAE gives better results than unilateral.
 • Contraindications:
     ◦ Acontractile or underactive bladder, or neurogenic bladder.
     ◦ Urethral stricture or BNC.
     ◦ Large bladder diverticula or stones.
     ◦ Contrast allergy and chronic kidney disease.
 • Technically demanding:
     ◦ Tortuosity, atherosclerosis and aberrant anatomy can prevent completion.
     ◦ The prostatic artery arises from five different trunks, most often the internal pudendal artery. There
       are often two prostatic arteries on one side, and anastomoses are common.
     ◦ Procedure and fluoroscopy times are long, giving substantial radiation exposure.
 • Guidelines:
     ◦ EAU: for moderate-to-severe LUTS in men who accept less-than-optimal outcomes compared with
       TURP. Urologists do the workup and follow-up with trained interventional radiologists.
     ◦ AUA 2021: not outside clinical trials.
     ◦ AUA 2023: may be offered, by trained clinicians, after discussion of risks and benefits (Statement 40).

Data
Comparative Studies
 • RCT of PAE vs m-TURP at 2 years: TURP superior for IPSS, QoL, Qmax and PVR, with greater falls in PSA
   and volume.
 • UK Register of Prostate Embolisation (UK-ROPE): a propensity-matched comparison with TURP. PAE was
   not non-inferior (it was inferior) for IPSS and QoL, but LOS was shorter (median 0 vs 2 days).
 • Meta-analyses: TURP gives greater Qmax and volume reduction. IPSS, QoL, IIEF-5 and PVR are similar, and
   PAE has fewer AEs and shorter LOS. A Cochrane review found little or no short-term difference in
   symptoms or QoL.

Complications
Intraoperative
 • Access site: pseudoaneurysm, bleeding and arterial dissection; dissection may delay embolisation but has
   no other sequelae.
 • Non-target embolisation: bladder or rectal ischaemia, usually managed conservatively. Penile
   discoloration and small ulcers can occur through anastomoses.
Postoperative
 • Common minor events: UTI, transient haematuria, urethral burning, rectal bleeding and haematospermia.
 • One RCT vs b-TURP: more complications with PAE, including AUR needing catheterisation in about a
   quarter, post-embolisation syndrome and treatment failure. Another RCT vs m-TURP found fewer
   complications with PAE, with similar severity and none of Clavien grade ≥4.
 • Durability is poor. By 10 years a majority need surgical reintervention (repeat PAE or TURP), and medical
   retreatment is common. About a fifth of trial patients had TURP within 2 years.
 • Sexual function: IIEF is stable or improved, and ejaculatory dysfunction is less than after TURP. The UK-
   ROPE retrograde ejaculation figure could not separate pre-existing from new cases.

Conclusion
 • PAE is low-morbidity, outpatient, and effective but inferior to TURP. There is no size-based
   recommendation. The AUA notes a role in refractory haematuria secondary to BPH. It should be
   performed only by trained clinicians (Statement 40).
Concept — PAE treats the gland without entering the urinary tract. That explains its low morbidity and
preserved ejaculation, and also its dependence on vascular anatomy, its weaker efficacy and its high long-
term reintervention.

CONCLUSION
 • Many MISTs are available and more are coming, differing in invasiveness, morbidity, durability and
   degree of tissue removal. New technologies bring new complications.
 • Scrutinise new studies: designs lacking rigour or sham control may overstate results.
 • The gold-standard operation for a given man is the one that meets his needs and expectations safely. It
   is chosen by shared decision-making around his most bothersome symptoms and goals, patient factors,
   and the surgeon's familiarity with and ability to perform the procedure.
