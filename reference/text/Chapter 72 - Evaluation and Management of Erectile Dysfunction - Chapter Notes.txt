HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE
 • Sexual medicine is an authentic, evidence-based discipline resting on basic science, epidemiology, clinical
   investigation and health services research; the diagnosis and management of erectile dysfunction (ED) is
   its central element.
 • American Urological Association (AUA) definition: the consistent or recurrent inability to attain and/or
   maintain penile erection sufficient for sexual satisfaction.
 • Evolution: 1970s psychoanalysis, sex therapy, aphrodisiacs, herbal and hormonal remedies →
   contemporary structured, balanced, evidence-based evaluation and intervention.
 • Management principles rest on ethics, quality, safety and cost-effectiveness, codified by consensus
   bodies: the AUA ED guideline and Core Curriculum, and the International Consultations on Sexual
   Medicine (ICSM), cosponsored by the World Health Organization (WHO), International Consultation on
   Urological Diseases (ICUD), Société Internationale d'Urologie (SIU) and International Society for Sexual
   Medicine (ISSM).

PUBLIC HEALTH SIGNIFICANCE
 • NIH Consensus Statement (1992): inability to attain and/or maintain erection sufficient for satisfactory
   sexual performance; later opinion accepts sufficiency for sexual satisfaction as the valid purpose.
 • ED is a condition of major health significance: its management affects the individual, the partner and
   society — physical and mental wellness, concurrent disease management and socioeconomic burden.

Epidemiology
 • ED is the most thoroughly studied sexual dysfunction; population-generalisable epidemiology informs its
   nature, cause and prognosis.
 • Overall prevalence in adult men worldwide 10–20%, most studies closer to 20%.
 • Age gradient: under 40 years 1–10%; 40–49 up to 15%; 50–59 up to 30%; 60–69 up to 40%; 70–90 years
   50–100%.
 • Among men seeking first help for new-onset ED, one in four is younger than 40, and nearly half of these
   have severe ED.
 • Global burden: over 152 million men in 1995, projected to ≈322 million by 2025, a trend that holds across
   race, ethnicity and region.
 • Comorbidities raise prevalence: type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), obesity, cardiovascular disease (CVD),
   hypertension, dyslipidaemia, depression, prostate disease/benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) — sharing
   endothelial dysfunction, arterial occlusion and systemic inflammation.
 • Novel disease associations (same mechanisms presumed): epilepsy, sensorineural hearing loss, open-
   angle glaucoma, urinary calculi, psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, chronic periodontitis, viral hepatitis,
   varicocele, gastric ulcer, ankylosing spondylitis, gout, inflammatory bowel disease/ulcerative colitis,
   interstitial cystitis, Parkinson disease, obstructive sleep apnoea, premature ejaculation.
 • Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19): ED diagnoses rose during the pandemic; prior infection carries a
   markedly higher (nearly sixfold) risk of ED, which persists after recovery; mechanism unclear —
   multiorgan vasculogenic, neurogenic and endocrine effects theorised.
 • Opioid use ≈ doubles ED risk, more so in men under 50 → screen for opioid use at initial assessment.
 • Incidence (prospective studies): crude incidence 25.9 cases/1000 man-years in men aged 40–69
   (Massachusetts Male Aging Study cohort); incident ED 57% at 5 years and 65% at 7 years in men ≥55
   (Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial placebo arm).
 • Predictors of incident ED: age, lower education, DM, CVD, hypertension, cigarette and cigar smoking,
   passive smoke exposure, overweight.
 • ED predicts CVD (the reverse direction):
     ◦ Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial (PCPT) placebo arm: ED is a sentinel of cardiovascular events
       comparable with current smoking or a family history of myocardial infarction; men with ED were 45%
       more likely to have a cardiac event at 5 years.
     ◦ Community cohort: ED confers substantially higher coronary artery disease risk at 10 years.
     ◦ Massachusetts Male Aging Study (MMAS), 15-year follow-up: ED is associated with all-cause and CVD
       mortality, comparable to raised body mass index (BMI), DM and hypertension.
     ◦ Younger age at ED onset heightens the cardiovascular risk.

     ◦ ED severity tracks overall health (Charlson Comorbidity Index) independent of cardiovascular (CV) risk

 • Meta-analyses of cohorts confirm a modest but consistent independent excess risk for CVD events,
   coronary heart disease, stroke/cerebrovascular events, myocardial infarction, CV mortality and all-cause
   mortality — largest for cerebrovascular and CVD events, smallest for all-cause mortality.
 • Cancer: Taiwan Longitudinal Health Insurance Database — ED linked to higher adjusted cancer risk over 5
   years.
 • ED is a clinical barometer of overall male health; its management bears on prevention, health promotion
   and survival.

Health Policy
 • ED reduces quality of life (QOL) and occupational productivity and increases health care use; service use
   is rising with effective first-line therapy, awareness and acceptance.
 • United States outpatient ED management (excluding drugs) was the ninth most costly urological
   diagnosis in 2000, having roughly doubled since 1994; government drug expenditure projected in the
   billions over a decade.
 • Some urge weighing medical necessity and cost in insurance coverage; evidence supports rational
   coverage, and therapy is actually underused relative to prevalence. ED is not a frivolous indication.

 KEY POINTS: EPIDEMIOLOGY AND HEALTH POLICY
   • Approximately 20% of adult men worldwide experience ED.
   • Risk factors: increasing age and comorbidity — DM, obesity, CVD, hypertension, depression, prostate
     disease.
   • Outcomes research shows ED merits intervention for QOL and for health and life preservation.
   • ED ranks among the top 10 most costly urological diagnoses in the United States.

Concept — Because ED and vascular disease share endothelial dysfunction, the risk association runs in both
directions: comorbidity predicts ED, and ED predicts cardiovascular events and death — which is why ED is
treated as a sentinel, not a lifestyle complaint.

MANAGEMENT PRINCIPLES
 • ED management differs from most urological disease: diagnosis rests on the subjective complaint,
   extensive testing is generally unnecessary, and first-line oral therapy is easily prescribed and frequently
   successful.
 • Direct-to-consumer (DTC) services are rapidly shifting this paradigm.
 • Despite apparent simplicity, management is a structured process of direct evaluation and validation.

Early Detection
 • Many men with ED carry adverse conditions and lifestyle factors (DM, CVD, prostate disease, overweight,
   smoking, inactivity) → an increased global cardiometabolic risk profile.
 • Men with identifiable risk factors have or will develop ED → clinical screening of these men creates
   opportunities to diagnose and treat.

 • Pattern: drug exposures outrank every disease, and DM outranks every other disease.
 • Trap: CVD and hypercholesterolaemia, though central to the pathophysiology, carry almost no
   independent odds once confounders are adjusted — their effect is absorbed by DM, hypertension and
   drugs.
 • Genotype influences ED risk: molecular and genetic mechanisms underlie the phenotype; genetically
   determined biomarkers may define risk and responsiveness to specific therapy (precision medicine).
   Polymorphisms in pulmonary hypertension already predict benefit from phosphodiesterase type 5
   inhibitors (PDE5Is); genes linking ED and CV risk could target screening.
 • Medication-associated ED accounts for up to 25% of presentations; commonest culprits are thiazide
   diuretics, β-adrenoceptor antagonists and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs).
 • Drugs may also impair desire, arousal and orgasm, secondarily hampering erection.
 • Criteria for causal attribution to a drug: increased prevalence versus placebo after stratification for known
   risk factors (or versus a therapeutically equivalent drug) and an experimentally established physiological
   mechanism.

Shared Decision Making and Treatment Planning
 • The plan varies for each patient and couple; an informed decision-making process follows a balanced,
   clinician-led discussion of all options, medical and nonmedical, with advantages, disadvantages and
   individually weighted risks and benefits.
 • The patient may select a preferred option without following a prescribed succession of therapies, and
   may defer treatment altogether; the choice is pursued within the boundaries of safety.
 • Derived from goal-directed management and the patient-centred framework.

 • Meta-analysis of randomised trials: shared decision making yields more knowledgeable patients and
   better congruence between chosen therapy and values.
 • The AUA ED guideline panel (2018) endorses shared decision making as the state of the art.
Figure 72.1 — Algorithm for shared decision making and treatment planning (AUA)
 • Counsel male and partner on: the value of psychosocial/relationship support from a trained professional;
   the importance of lifestyle change (weight loss, exercise, smoking cessation) for erectile function and
   overall health; the benefits and risks/burdens of all non-contraindicated treatments → identify
   treatment by shared decision making on the values and priorities of male and partner: PDE5I, vacuum
   devices, intraurethral (IU) alprostadil, intracavernosal injections (ICIs), penile prosthesis surgery — offered
   in parallel, not ranked → assess outcomes, adverse events (AEs) and satisfaction of male and partner →
   if inadequate efficacy, unacceptable AEs or insufficient satisfaction: adjust dose (PDE5I, IU alprostadil, ICI),
   revisit instructions, revisit values and priorities, refer to a mental health professional for psychosocial or
   relationship barriers, or consider an alternate treatment.
 • Testosterone deficiency (symptoms and signs plus total testosterone <300 ng/dL): counsel that restoring
   testosterone is likely to increase the efficacy of ED treatments other than prosthesis surgery.

Step-Care Approach
 • Process of Care Model (1999): a stepwise methodology combining processes, actions and outcomes; its
   algorithm considers needs and preferences (goal-directed) but ranks therapies by ease of administration,
   reversibility, invasiveness and cost into first-, second- and third-line interventions, from lifestyle
   modification to surgery.
 • Endorsed by later panels, which placed education and counselling with medical therapy as initial
   management.
 • Contemporary management emphasises shared decision making over step-care.

Role of Partner Interview
 • The partner interview is critical: it alters diagnosis and treatment in a substantial proportion of cases (over
   half in some series).
 • The partner provides a new perspective on sexual issues, insight into relationship quality, and his or her
   own role in the dysfunction; partner attitude affects initiation of and adherence to therapy.
 • Partners are affected: female partners of men with ED more often have sexual dysfunction or cease
   sexual activity.
 • Recommended: additional office visits with the partner, during which the patient conveys educational
   information.

Cardiac Risk Assessment
 • Princeton Consensus Conferences (three, since the early 2000s): all men with ED, even asymptomatic,
   are regarded as having potential CVD risk.

Direct-to-Consumer Platforms
 • DTC platforms flourished through marketing, disease normalisation and easy access, especially among
   younger men.
 • Caution: no appropriate medical evaluation — questionnaire-only screening and nonadherence to AUA
   guidelines; DTC clinics offer unproven therapies, and few providers are urologists or even physicians.
 • Hence the importance of direct evaluation by a urologist, in person or by appropriately selected
   telemedicine.

Follow-Up Care
 • Essential: ensures continued success — discontinuation is high when patients are not reassessed.

 • Also reassesses medical and psychosocial conditions, need for dose titration or substitution, adverse
   effects and drug interactions, and offers education for patient and partner.
Concept — ED management is deliberately patient-driven in its choices but fixed in its safety gates: the
treatment is chosen by values, while cardiac stratification, referral triggers and follow-up are non-negotiable.

DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION
 • The cornerstone: detailed history (patient and partner), physical examination and proper laboratory
   tests.
 • Diagnosis rests on the man's report of consistent inability to attain and maintain an erection sufficient for
   satisfactory intercourse.
 • The original NIH definition set no duration; later statements require a minimum of 3 months, except in
   trauma or surgically induced ED.

Sexual, Medical and Psychosocial History
 • Brief checklists or questionnaires may open the evaluation, but a detailed interview is routine.
 • Sexual history taken with utmost sensitivity and cultural competence; private, confidential and
   nonjudgemental.
 • Do not assume a monogamous, heterosexual relationship.
 • With the patient's approval, the partner interview corroborates history and confirms mutual goals.
Sexual History
 • The central component: confirms the complaint and delineates onset, duration, conditions, severity and
   cause.
 • Conditions favouring erection: stimuli used, erections on awakening, self-stimulation. Conditions
   hindering: performance anxiety, inability with a designated partner, motivational factors.
 • Other issues: partner availability, interest and health; medical events at onset; previous treatment
   attempts.

 • What the table tests: the history alone can suggest aetiology. Preserved waking/nocturnal erections are
   the single strongest pointer to psychogenic ED, because they prove an intact neurovascular axis.
Medical History
 • Identifies predictors, risk factors and comorbidities; its payoff is reversible or treatable factors whose
   correction may improve erections, and guidance on choice of therapy.
 • Associated conditions: disease states (type 2 DM, CVD, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, neurological disease,
   hypogonadism, thyroid disorders); trauma (spinal cord injury, pelvic surgery or radiation, sexual injury);
   medications and recreational substances.
 • Record age; register depression, anxiety and anger — their relationship with ED is bidirectional.
Psychosocial History
 • Best sexual performance requires wellness of mind and body together; ascertain mental health
   problems, emotional stressors and relationship difficulties, past and present.
 • Also occupational status, financial security, family life and social support.

Physical Examination
 • Highly recommended; complements history and may reveal the cause. Comprises anthropometrics
   (height, weight, waist circumference), body habitus (secondary sexual characteristics), and CV,
   neurological and genital examination with focus on the external genitalia.
 • Bulbocavernosus reflex: squeezing the glans → contraction of bulbocavernosus felt by a finger in the
   anus.

Cardiovascular Risk Assessment Tools
 • CVD-risk prediction models — Framingham Risk Score or a global risk score (family history of coronary
   heart disease, BMI, metabolic biomarkers) — are a powerful first step in any man evaluated for ED.
 • Coronary artery calcium score correlates with ED severity; a possible future prognostic marker pending
   validation.

Laboratory Tests
 • Part of systematic evaluation: confirm associated conditions and uncover treatable or undetected
   disease. Standard panel, then tailoring.
 • Recommended panel: serum chemistries, fasting glucose or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), full blood
   count, lipid profile, morning serum total testosterone (T).
 • Low total T → measure free (or bioavailable) T and luteinising hormone (LH); prolactin may be added;
   thyroid tests at the clinician's discretion.
 • Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) as needed if prostate pathology is suspected (which exogenous T could
   promote).

Vascular Evaluation
 • Surveys the three vascular requirements of erection: arterial inflow, engorgement and retention → the
   two classic diagnoses, arterial impairment and veno-occlusive dysfunction.
 • Counsel on purpose, alternatives, risks and benefits before any haemodynamic test.
Combined Intracavernosal Injection and Stimulation
 • The combined intracavernosal injection and stimulation (CIS) test is the first-line evaluation of penile
   blood flow: ICI of a vasodilator plus genital or audiovisual stimulation, with the response rated by an
   independent assessor.
 • It bypasses neural and hormonal influences, testing penile vascular status directly.
 • Regimens: alprostadil alone 10–20 µg (Caverject or Edex); Bimix (papaverine + phentolamine) 0.3 mL;
   Trimix (all three) 0.3 mL.



                                                           page 10 of 29
Chapter 72 — Evaluation and Management of Erectile Dysfunction                                  SPECIALIZED EVALUATION AND TESTING
 • Technique: 27–29-gauge needle at the lateral penile base into the corpus cavernosum → manual
   compression 5 minutes (prevents haematoma) → periodic rating of rigidity and duration → redose if
   the response is poor → flaccidity required before discharge → if no spontaneous detumescence within
   about 1 hour, intracavernosal diluted phenylephrine (500 µg/mL) every 3–5 minutes until flaccid.
 • A sustained rigid erection = normal erectile haemodynamics → consider psychogenic, neurogenic or
   endocrine ED.
 • False positives (normal-looking response despite disease) occur in up to a fifth of men with borderline
   arterial inflow (peak cavernous systolic flow 25–35 cm/s on duplex). False negatives: anxiety, needle
   phobia, inadequate dose.
Duplex Ultrasonography (Gray-Scale or Color-Coded)
 • Duplex after pharmacostimulation or CIS is second-line evaluation but the most reliable and least
   invasive diagnostic modality for ED; it adds imaging and quantification to the assessor's judgement.
 • High-resolution 7.5–12 MHz real-time ultrasound with colour pulsed Doppler; dorsal and cavernous
   arteries, scanning from the crura in the perineum to the tip.
 • Colour convention: red towards the probe, blue away.
 • Protocol: baseline velocities → injection → measurements every 5 minutes up to 20 minutes; cavernous
   artery diameters; note communications between cavernous arteries or dorsal and cavernous arteries; rate
   erection quality simultaneously — a poor erection (often anxiety) prompts redosing, as for CIS.

   consistently exceeds 25 cm/s within 5 minutes; mean PSV after pharmacostimulation in normal men 35–
   47 cm/s; a 25 cm/s cut-off is highly sensitive and specific against abnormal pudendal arteriography.
 • In severe vascular ED, cavernous artery diameter increases by less than 75% and rarely exceeds 0.7 mm;
   but percentage dilation does not correlate with arteriography, whereas PSV does.
 • Anatomical variants confound interpretation: early cavernous branching, multiple branches, distal
   perforators from dorsal or spongiosal arteries, and site of measurement (crus, proximal, mid-cavernosal)
   → risk of a false diagnosis of arteriogenic ED.
 • Asymmetry is significant: a side-to-side PSV difference >10 cm/s, or reversal of flow across a collateral,
   suggests a significant atherosclerotic lesion.

 • Not first-line because of technical and cost limitations; counsel on use, risks and benefits.

Penile Tumescence and Rigidity Monitoring
 • Nocturnal penile tumescence (NPT) monitoring studies sleep-related erections as a measure of
   physiological erectile ability.
 • Sleep-laboratory NPTR (the standard): number, tumescence (strain-gauge circumference change),
   maximal rigidity and duration of episodes, with electroencephalography, electro-oculography and
   electromyography (EMG), nasal airflow and oxygen saturation → documents rapid eye movement
   (REM) sleep (the period of true erectile events) and hypoxia (sleep apnoea). Axial rigidity is measured on
   waking at maximal tumescence with a buckling device at the tip, plus photography.
 • RigiScan: automated, portable; two loops — penile base and coronal sulcus — record tumescence
   (circumference) and radial rigidity by timed constrictions; office initialisation → home calibration. Normal:
   four to five normal erectile episodes per night. May predict veno-occlusive dysfunction.
 • RigiScan limits: radial rigidity does not predict axial rigidity; REM sleep cannot be verified; wide
   variability even in normal men.
 • NPT electrobioimpedance (NEVA): three electrodes (hip, penile base, glans) and a thigh recorder; an
   imperceptible current glans → hip ground; impedance falls with tumescence, measuring volume and
   length change. No REM monitoring; wearable home sensors emerging but the volume–rigidity
   relationship is unproven.

 • PET and functional MRI, with video stimulation or an erectogenic drug (oral apomorphine), map the

Psychological Evaluation
 • Addresses psychological and interpersonal factors; population studies link ED with anxiety, depression,
   low self-esteem, negative outlook, emotional stress and sexual coercion history.
 • The urologist can initiate it simply; the Whooley two-question depression screen: during the past month,
   have you often been bothered by feeling down, depressed or hopeless? by little interest or pleasure in
   doing things?
 • The diagnostic interview is central, handled straightforwardly.

Hormonal Evaluation
 • Endocrinopathies relevant to erection: T deficiency (formerly hypogonadism), hyperthyroidism and DM

 • Screening questionnaires and the validated hypogonadism screener lack specificity (and sometimes
   sensitivity); the central feature is biochemical testing.

 • Low T is found in a variable minority of men presenting with ED, depending on the population.

 KEY POINTS: DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION
   • Basic evaluation: detailed history, physical examination, proper laboratory tests.
   • The sexual history defines onset, duration, conditions, severity and cause.
   • Cardiac risk assessment and risk reduction for all men undergoing ED evaluation.
   • Hormonal evaluation: T deficiency, hyperthyroidism and DM.
   • Questionnaires document presence, severity and treatment response.
   • Specialised testing for complicated or atypical presentations.

Concept — Hormonal work-up is a localisation exercise: total T screens, calculated bioavailable T corrects for
carrier-protein distortion, LH and prolactin place the lesion in testis or pituitary, and MRI is reserved for the
central lesion that could be a tumour.

TREATMENT CONSIDERATIONS
 • Treatment follows appropriate work-up; interventions are etiologically specific or nonspecific, and a
   cause-specific intervention ideally offers correction.
 • Shared decision making: all options presented with advantages and disadvantages, risks and benefits
   weighed for the individual (Figure 72.1).

Lifestyle Modifications
 • ED risk tracks preventable or treatable comorbidities (DM, CVD, metabolic syndrome); optimising them
   may prevent or ameliorate ED.
 • Smoking cessation is associated with recovery of erectile function.
 • Increased exercise benefits sedentary men with ED.
 • Randomised trial: obese men with moderate ED and no overt CVD improved IIEF scores with exercise and
   weight control versus education alone, with falls in BMI and C-reactive protein and rise in activity.
 • Mediterranean-style diet and caloric reduction improve erectile function in metabolic syndrome.
 • No-nose bicycle saddle restored erectile function in occupational cyclists (perineal trauma relieved).
 • Supported by systematic reviews and meta-analyses; mechanisms: reduced CV risk factors, raised serum
   T, improved mood and self-esteem.

Medication Change
 • Once a drug is implicated, change dose or class — this may reverse ED.
 • Antihypertensives: switch thiazides and β-blockers → calcium channel blockers or renin–angiotensin
   system inhibitors (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers).

Chapter 72 — Evaluation and Management of Erectile Dysfunction                                   TREATMENT CONSIDERATIONS
 • SSRI sexual dysfunction (ED, delayed ejaculation): substitution (bupropion, nefazodone, buspirone,
   mirtazapine), drug holidays, dose reduction, watchful waiting, or add a PDE5I.

Psychosexual Therapy
 • Justified by the interplay of psychological and interpersonal factors, but well-controlled outcome
   evidence is lacking; in practice an ill-defined mix of behavioural, relational, psychoanalytic and cognitive
   approaches.
 • Interventions: systematic desensitisation, sensate focus, interpersonal therapy, cognitive-behavioural
   therapy, sex education, couples' communication and sexual skills training, masturbation exercises.
 • Integrated treatment (psychosexual therapy + oral therapy, ICI or vacuum device) succeeds, especially
   with motivational obstacles.
 • For mild-to-moderate issues the urologist can use a biopsychosocial model (awareness plus counselling
   on normal function and acceptable behaviour); intensive therapy needs a mental health clinician.

Hormonal Therapy
 • Prescribed when a hormonal disturbance is identified. The urologist treats T deficiency and
   hyperprolactinaemia; endocrinologists lead for other endocrinopathies.
Testosterone Therapy
 • Goal: normal 24-hour levels resembling the diurnal pattern. Measure T before and during treatment, but
   judge efficacy by clinical response, not a precise level.
 • Short (≈3-month) therapeutic trial; stop if no response.
 • Adverse effects: erythrocytosis, sleep apnoea, urinary symptoms, prostate cancer progression risk,
   gynaecomastia, acne.
 • CV safety: a meta-analysis of all placebo-controlled randomised controlled trials (RCTs) did not support a
   causal relationship between T and CV events.
 • Monitoring sequence: baseline digital rectal examination (DRE) and PSA, haemoglobin/haematocrit,
   liver function tests, cholesterol and lipid profile → efficacy and AE review at 3–6 months → annually.
 • Prefer short-acting preparations initially, so therapy can be stopped promptly after an AE.

Hyperprolactinemia Treatments
 • T replacement is neither corrective nor sufficient; treat the cause.
 • Stop offending drugs (oestrogens, morphine, sedatives, neuroleptics).
 • Prolactinoma: medical first, surgical if needed. Bromocriptine (dopamine agonist) lowers prolactin,
   restores T and shrinks the tumour; neurosurgical ablation if medication fails or visual effects from optic
   nerve compression.
 • Erectile recovery is most evident when prolactin was markedly raised (>40 ng/mL).
Concept — Hormonal therapy works only when the hormone is the cause: testosterone restores erection in
true deficiency, is futile in hyperprolactinaemia where the prolactin must be corrected, and precursor or
alternative hormones carry risk without proven sexual benefit.

 • Pharmacotherapies mimic the natural biochemistry of erection: they promote proerectile or oppose

 • Regulatory approval standards now define which commercially developed therapies qualify.

Phosphodiesterase Type 5 Inhibitors
 • US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approvals: sildenafil 1998 (Viagra); vardenafil and tadalafil 2003
   (Levitra, Cialis); avanafil 2012 (Stendra).
 • Mechanism: sexual stimulation → nitric oxide (NO) from nerve endings and endothelium → cGMP →
   cavernosal smooth muscle relaxation; PDE5 degrades cGMP, and PDE5Is block this.
 • They augment but do not induce erection — sexual stimulation is obligatory. Selectivity arises from the
   high PDE5 concentration in cavernosal smooth muscle.
 • Structure: guanine-like base, ribose-like system and phosphodiester-like bond fit the catalytic site.
   Sildenafil and vardenafil are similar; tadalafil differs; avanafil differs most.
 • PDE6 (retina): sildenafil and vardenafil cross-react more → visual disturbance. PDE11: cross-reacted by
   tadalafil alone (significance unclear). Other side effects reflect PDE5 in vascular and gastrointestinal
   smooth muscle.
 • Tadalafil's long half-life gives the widest therapeutic window.

Intracavernosal Injection
  • The 1982 discovery that intracavernous vasoactive agents induce erection (Virag) launched medical
    therapy for ED.
 • Three agents in regular use — alprostadil, papaverine, phentolamine — alone or combined (Bimix,
   Trimix). Combinations act synergistically, rescue monotherapy failures and avoid single-agent side effects
   (e.g., alprostadil pain).
 • Start with a small dose, especially in nonvasculogenic ED. In-office training before home use, titrating to
   an erection rigid enough for intercourse lasting no more than 1 hour. Empirical and risk-based dosing give
   equal satisfaction, outcomes and complications.
 • Contraindications: psychological instability; history of or risk for priapism; severe coagulopathy; unstable
   CVD; reduced manual dexterity (a partner may be trained); monoamine oxidase inhibitors — an
   intracavernosal α-agonist used to reverse priapism could precipitate a hypertensive crisis.

Intraurethral Suppositories
  • Less invasive than injection: drug is absorbed through urethral mucosa into the corpus spongiosum, then
    via small vascular channels into the corpora cavernosa — transfer varies with anatomy.
 • Early prostaglandin E2 trial gave full or partial tumescence in a minority; synthetic PGE1 approved
   November 1996 as MUSE (Medicated Urethral System for Erection): a 1 × 3-mm pellet of alprostadil
   (125, 250, 500, 1000 µg) deposited 3 cm into the distal urethra.
 • Technique: insert applicator and deposit pellet → massage to distribute → remain upright for several
   minutes; in-office training and dose adjustment first.
 • Responder rate ≈50%; among responders ≈70% of administrations yield intercourse.
 • ACTIS adjustable constriction band (FDA-approved) improves retention; ALIBRA (alprostadil + α1-
   antagonist prazosin) raised home responder rates.
 • Niche role — inferior to PDE5Is and ICI. Indications: PDE5I nonresponse from autonomic nerve damage
   (radical prostatectomy, cystectomy, trauma); combination with PDE5I; soft (cold) glans syndrome (after
   prosthesis or as an entity).
 • Side effects: urogenital pain (about one-third), minor urethral bleeding, hypotension, dizziness; priapism
   rare.

 • Appeal: convenience, simplicity, limited systemic effect; obstacles limit use.

 • MD3000 (Eroxon): the first over-the-counter FDA-approved ED treatment — glans gel producing
   evaporative cooling then gradual warming, stimulating nerves and cavernosal flow; onset about 10
   minutes in the majority. Vehicle derived from a topical glyceryl trinitrate formulation trial.

Medical Device
 • Vacuum erection device (VED): for men who fail or decline oral or local pharmacotherapy.
 • Principle: negative pressure engorges the penis; a constriction ring retains the blood. Not a physiological
   erection — the trapped blood is predominantly venous — but sufficient for coitus. The glans is engorged
   as well as the corpora → advantage in soft glans syndrome.
 • Technique: clear cylinder over the flaccid penis → manual or battery pump (pressure-release valve
   prevents injury) → erection → elastic ring rolled to the base → vacuum released and cylinder removed
   → intercourse; ring off within 30 minutes.
 • Prescription devices advised; metal or inelastic rings contraindicated.
 • Efficacy up to 90% for satisfactory erection, but satisfaction 30–70%; long-term continuation up to 60%.
   Limited in severe vascular disease (proximal venous leak, arterial insufficiency) and fibrosis after priapism
   or prosthesis infection.
 • More acceptable to older men in steady relationships than young single men.
 • Counsel on: band discomfort; pivoting (rigid only distal to band); cyanosis and coolness; trapped
   ejaculate.
 • Complications: minor (pain, numbness, difficult ejaculation, ecchymosis, petechiae); major rare (skin
   necrosis, urethral varicosities, Fournier gangrene). Caution with anticoagulants (aspirin, warfarin) and
   bleeding disorders.
 • Special uses: combined with oral, ICI or intraurethral therapy; supplementing a malfunctioning
   prosthesis; preserving tissue elasticity after priapism, prosthesis explantation or Peyronie surgery;
   erectile recovery after prostate cancer treatment.

   unsuccessful or undesirable.

Combination Therapies
 • Monotherapy nonresponse approaches 40%; combinations may optimise response or avoid dose-limiting
   AEs.
 • Reported successful pairings: tadalafil 5 mg daily + sildenafil 50 mg on demand; PDE5I + psychosocial

 • Caution for compounded complications; consider in-office evaluation before home use.

Investigational Therapies
 • Potential future directions awaiting rigorous safety and efficacy trials.
Pharmacotherapies
 • Repurposed drugs, off-label and not FDA-approved for ED.
 • Botulinum neurotoxin (onabotulinumtoxinA) by ICI: blocks noradrenaline release from postganglionic

 • Proposed for poor PDE5I responders; expensive. Sexual Medicine Society of North America (SMSNA)

Alternative Therapies
 • Herbs, ointments, nutraceuticals and supplements; PDE5I success spawned counterfeit and imitation
   products and unapproved remedies.
 • Efficacy of ginkgo biloba, L-arginine, Korean red ginseng uncertain without rigorous RCTs; apparent

 • Mechanism and meaningful efficacy/safety must be shown before advocacy.

 KEY POINTS: TREATMENT CONSIDERATIONS
   • Risk modification — a healthy, fit lifestyle — preserves erectile health, though definitive evidence is
     needed.
   • Psychosexual therapy has a role in integrative management.
   • Oral, intracavernosal, intraurethral and transdermal routes are applied or under study.
   • VED is an alternative to oral or local pharmacotherapy.
   • Penile prosthesis when nonsurgical therapy is contraindicated, unsuccessful or undesirable.
   • Arterial revascularisation only for select men meeting stringent clinical and radiographic criteria.

Concept — Every therapy targets one final common pathway, cavernosal smooth muscle relaxation, and the
options are ordered by how much of the man's own physiology they still require: PDE5Is need intact nerves
and endothelium, alprostadil bypasses NO through cAMP, and the vacuum device and prosthesis bypass
smooth muscle altogether.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS
 • Science and technology remain the cornerstones: expect next-generation pharmacotherapy and gene,
   stem cell and regenerative therapies.
 • Surgery will evolve from reconstruction and prosthetics to tissue replacement (penile transplantation).
 • The paradigm will shift from symptom- and severity-based treatment to cause-specific treatment guided
   by disease states and genetics (precision medicine).
 • The ultimate goal: interventions that restore and maximise natural erectile function.
