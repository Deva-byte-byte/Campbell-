Chapter 57 — Etiology, Diagnosis, and Management of the
Undescended Testis
Kate H. Kraft — Chapter Notes

 • Cryptorchidism (undescended testis, UDT) is a common congenital anomaly that remains incompletely
   understood despite extensive study.
 • Descent is normally completed in the third trimester or shortly after birth; the anatomy is well
   described, but the mechanical function of the gubernaculum — the fetal structure orchestrating descent
   — is still unresolved.
 • Susceptibility is complex and multifactorial, plausibly combining genetic factors with exposure to
   endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), potentially in combination.
 • Diagnosis is frequently made well beyond the neonatal period, and any clinically significant long-term
   difference between congenital and acquired cryptorchidism remains obscure.
 • Retractile testes may represent a milder form of the disease, conferring higher risk of acquired
   cryptorchidism diagnosed later in childhood.
 • Ultrasonography and other imaging are rarely if ever indicated — they contribute little to diagnosis.
 • Hormonal therapy is no longer advocated as first-line treatment. Surgery is the preferred treatment,
   with approach determined by palpability, position, size, patient age and overall health status.
 • Primary scrotal and laparoscopic approaches are increasingly used.
 • Suboptimal semen quality is common in bilateral cases and can occur in unilateral disease, though
   paternity is more likely in unilateral cases.
 • Testicular germ cell tumour (TGCT) risk is raised in the affected testis and to a lesser degree in the
   contralateral testis; early surgery may reduce it.
 • Long-term outcome depends on laterality, age at treatment, and potentially the underlying aetiology.
 • The American Urological Association (AUA) cryptorchidism guideline (2014) provides the evidence-based
   framework for evaluation and management.

Concept — the whole taxonomy turns on two axes: where the testis is now, and what is known about where

AETIOLOGY

Embryology of Testicular Descent and Maldescent
 • Cryptorchidism is a failure of descent dependent on growth and hormonal function of the developing
   testis and its regulation of gubernacular growth and function.
 • Descent is regulated by two Leydig cell hormones: insulin-like 3 (INSL3) acting via relaxin/insulin-like
   family peptide receptor 2 (RXFP2), and androgens — dihydrotestosterone (DHT) and testosterone — via
   the androgen receptor (AR).
 • Descent also requires intact innervation of the gubernaculum by the genitofemoral nerve (GFN);
   downstream interactions between hormonal and neural pathways are poorly defined.
 • Genetic or environmental effects altering key molecules of testicular or gubernacular development are
   the presumed contributors to cryptorchidism.

Differentiation of the Testis
 • Gonadal differentiation is an active process in both sexes — specific multigene programmes are required
   for male and female determination, not a default pathway with a male override.
 • SRY (sex-determining region on the Y chromosome) expression in the indifferent gonad triggers
   testicular development, "tipping the balance" of antagonistic male–female signalling toward the male
   programme.
 • SRY is activated in part by steroidogenic factor 1 (SF1/NR5A1); SRY and SF1 cooperate to sustain SOX9
   (SRY box-containing 9) expression in pre-Sertoli supporting cells.
 • SRY specifies the Sertoli cell; SOX9, FGF9 (fibroblast growth factor 9), AMH (antimüllerian hormone),
   PTGDS (prostaglandin synthase) and others drive Sertoli differentiation and proliferation.

 • Establishment of the Sertoli lineage is the prerequisite for testicular cord formation.
 • Testis patterning requires a temporal sequence: germ cell migration into the undifferentiated gonad →
   migration of interstitial cell precursors from the coelomic epithelium and mesonephros.
Human germ cell timeline
1. 32 days post-ovulation — gonads with somatic and germ cells first identifiable on the medial aspect of
   the urogenital ridge; gonads and genitalia still sexually indifferent →
2. 40 days — SRY and SOX9 transcripts appear, followed by histological emergence of Sertoli cells →
3. 6 weeks — primordial germ cells (PGCs) migrate from the yolk sac and differentiate into gonocytes →
4. 7 weeks — gonocytes begin expressing c-KIT, rising through the first trimester →
5. 8 weeks — germ cells localised within testicular cords →
6. Second trimester — three distinguishable germ cell subpopulations: gonocytes, intermediate
   spermatogonia, prespermatogonia →
7. 20 weeks — POU5F1/OCT4 and c-KIT lost with the gonocytes; MAGE-A4 immunopositivity marks
   emergence of prespermatogonia.
Fetal Leydig cells
 • Arise from coelomic epithelium and perivascular cells at the gonad–mesonephric border, forming
   interstitial Leydig progenitors.
 • Progenitors express AR and are themselves androgen-regulated.
 • Development depends on multiple growth factors and on paracrine interaction with Sertoli and
   peritubular myoid cells; WT1 expression in Sertoli cells drives peritubular myoid and Leydig cell
   proliferation and differentiation.
 • Fetal Leydig cells (FLCs) become active at 6–7 weeks, proliferate until about 15 weeks, and involute after
   24 weeks.
 • By the end of the first trimester the external genitalia are fully masculinised and the testis cords are
   established, with both proliferating and degenerating germ cell populations present; Sertoli and germ cell
   proliferation continues well into the second trimester.
Concept — the testis is built in a fixed order, and each step licenses the next: SRY specifies Sertoli cells, Sertoli
cells make cords, and only a hormonally competent cord-bearing testis can drive its own descent. Every later
failure mode is a failure somewhere along that dependency chain.

Testicular Hormone Production
 • Fetal testicular hormone function is critical both for reproductive tract masculinisation and for descent.
 • Hormone production is activated at discrete intervals — fetal, postnatal and pubertal — rather than
   continuously.
 • Fetal Leydig cell phases (sequence): proliferation and differentiation, 7–14 weeks → maturation, to 18
   weeks → involution, continuing to term.

Sex steroid receptors in the fetal testis
 • AR and oestrogen receptor beta (ERβ) are expressed in the undifferentiated gonad at 7 weeks, role
   unclear.
 • AR is present in peritubular myoid cells and some Leydig/interstitial cells but is absent from Sertoli cells
   in early development.
 • This Sertoli AR absence is physiologically essential: AR expression there would permit inappropriate
   androgen-induced inhibition of AMH production and precocious stimulation of spermatogenesis.
 • ERβ is expressed in germ, peritubular myoid, Sertoli and some Leydig cells; ERα is limited or absent;
   concomitant aromatase expression suggests a role for locally produced oestrogen.
Mini-puberty — neonatal reactivation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis
 • Cross-sectional data place peak hormone levels at 1–3 months of age; LH and follicle-stimulating
   hormone (FSH) rise first, followed by testosterone, AMH and inhibin B (INHB); INSL3 is high in cord
   blood and at 3 months.
 • Individual longitudinal urinary data place the peak earlier — at or before 1 month — and more
   pronounced in preterm males.
 • Androgen insensitivity syndrome provides the mechanism: the surge is a response to maternal
   androgen withdrawal, producing secondary upregulation of hypothalamic and pituitary secretion.
 • FLCs regress after birth; a neonatal Leydig population emerges at 2–3 months and regresses by the end
   of the first year. As postnatal hormones wane, FLCs degenerate or regress to immature, partially
   differentiated cells less responsive to LH.
Postnatal germ cell events
 • Testicular volume increases postnatally, driven principally by Sertoli and germ cell proliferation; growth
   correlates with HPG activation in preterm males, implying a direct hormonal response.
 • Sertoli proliferation continues through the first year and is a major determinant of ultimate testicular
   size.
 • Prespermatogonia/gonocytes migrate to the basement membrane after birth and become
   undifferentiated type A spermatogonia; adult dark (Ad) and adult pale (Ap) spermatogonia contain the
   spermatogonial stem cell subpopulation.
 • Migration coincides with HPG activation, so a relationship is presumed but unproven; known regulators
   include platelet-derived growth factor, thyroid hormone and cell adhesion molecules.
 • Androgens are not established as regulators of neonatal gonocyte migration — fetal gonocytes express
   AR and androgens inhibit their proliferation, but no evidence supports an androgen effect on neonatal
   migration or differentiation.

 • Failed migration leaves prespermatogonia/gonocytes at increased risk of apoptosis or future malignant
   transformation.
Concept — mini-puberty is the therapeutic clock of this disease. It is the one postnatal window in which the
HPG axis is active, gonocytes are migrating and testicular size is being set; damage or delay here converts a
positional abnormality into an irreversible germ cell deficit.

Gubernacular Development and Testicular Descent
 • The physiology of normal descent and the aetiology of abnormal descent in humans remain
   incompletely understood; knowledge is inferred from human disorders and animal models.
 • The indifferent gonad develops adjacent to, and suspended from, the mesonephros, which has replaced
   the pronephros by 5 weeks.
 • A rudimentary cranial mesonephric ligament connects to the diaphragm and disappears by 13 weeks as
   the mesonephros regresses.
 • There is no "cranial gonadal ligament" in humans, unlike other species.
 • The gubernaculum attaches proximally to the epididymis, not directly to the testis.
 • "Transabdominal descent" in the human is relative, not true migration: fixed-section studies describe
   movement from C7–T8 to T9–L3 between 5 and 7 weeks and to sacral level by 10 weeks; dissection
   studies place the testis at L4 by 7 weeks; fetal studies confirm the testis stays close to the internal
   inguinal ring from 10 weeks onward.
 • If true transabdominal descent occurs, it is unrelated to gubernacular swelling or hormonal stimulation,
   because it precedes them.
 • Caudal movement of the ovary is prevented by the ovarian ligament and müllerian duct development.
Five phases of human testicular descent (Barteczko and Jacob)
1. Phase 1 (5 weeks) — caudal mesonephros contacts the future gubernaculum at the internal inguinal
   ring →
2. Phase 2 (7 weeks, extending 8–10 weeks) — GFN accompanies the newly formed gubernaculum
   (abdominal, interstitial and subcutaneous portions) and processus vaginalis; then gubernacular growth,
   deepening of the processus vaginalis and extension of cremaster fibres into the interstitial
   gubernaculum →
3. Phase 3 (10–14 weeks) — testicular growth with regression of müllerian ducts and mesonephros; male-
   specific gubernacular swelling begins at 12 weeks — after the testis is already at the internal ring →
4. Phase 4 (14–20 weeks) — gubernacular swelling, cremaster development and processus vaginalis
   migration widen the inguinal canal →
5. Phase 5 (20–28 weeks) — release of the distal subcutaneous gubernacular attachment and
   transinguinal passage; further caudal movement to birth accompanies gubernacular regression.
What the anatomy establishes
 • Apparent transabdominal movement precedes sexually dimorphic gubernacular change and is
   therefore not a male-hormone–specific event.
 • Male-specific gubernacular swelling parallels the Leydig secretory peaks — testosterone at 14–16 weeks
   and INSL3 at 15–17 weeks.
 • The GFN precedes and accompanies the gubernaculum and processus vaginalis in both sexes from 7
   weeks.

 • Both smooth muscle (abdominal portion) and skeletal muscle (interstitial and subcutaneous portions)
   are present in the gubernaculum, though not all observers agree; in situ studies from 20 weeks clearly
   show striated muscle bundles and GFN infiltration.
 • Whether cremaster fibres originate from abdominal wall musculature or within the gubernaculum is
   contested; unique innervation and transgenic mouse data favour intrinsic gubernacular origin.
Mechanics of passage
 • Gubernacular swelling — from cellular proliferation and extracellular matrix production — is required
   to enlarge the inguinal canal for testicular passage.
 • Once the canal is created, unknown mechanical factors trigger a typically rapid transinguinal passage.
 • The gubernaculum is distally unattached during and after transinguinal passage; settling into the
   preformed scrotum is gradual.
 • The scrotum develops from genital swellings visible at 7 weeks that fuse by 10–12 weeks in response to
   circulating DHT — the scrotum is ready long before the testis arrives.
 • Passage into the inguinal canal rarely occurs before 22 weeks; the majority of testes are scrotal after 27
   weeks.
 • Rapid transit, early gubernacular innervation, GFN significance in rodents and the complex cremaster
   distribution argue that neuromuscular forces are required for descent.
 • Microcomputed tomography in fetal rodents has further defined the role of the gubernacular bulb in
   formation of the processus vaginalis.

Concept — the gubernaculum does not pull the testis down; it makes room and then gets out of the way.
Outgrowth creates the canal and processus vaginalis while the testis stays still; regression permits the testis
to follow — which is why both an outgrowth defect and a regression defect produce the same clinical
phenotype.

Regulation of Testicular Descent
 • Direct evidence for hormonal control comes from animal models, primarily rodents, whose relevance is
   questioned on anatomical grounds.
 • Rodent vs human anatomy: rodents have a less prominent interstitial gubernaculum, a cranial gonadal
   ligament, a well-developed intrinsic cremaster at the gubernacular periphery, an absent inguinal canal,
   and a processus vaginalis that fails to close; the processus vaginalis and cremaster are nonetheless
   similar enough to justify translational study.
 • Transgenic or spontaneous rodent strains with inactivation of Insl3, Rxfp2 or Ar are cryptorchid, as are
   males with abnormalities of androgen synthesis or action.

 • The two-phase paradigm is no longer cleanly separable — apparent transabdominal descent, if it occurs,
   happens in the third month, before either hormone peaks.
 • Modern model: the testis remains stable as the gubernaculum swells (the "outgrowth" phase),
   facilitating creation of the processus vaginalis, and descends as the gubernaculum shrinks (the
   "regression" phase). INSL3 and androgens regulate both phases.
 • Both hormones directly regulate myogenic differentiation of the cremaster.
Downstream pathways
 • Myogenesis is the recurring theme in transgenic and human syndromic models.
 • Inactivation of Notch1 and Wnt pathway genes (Ctnnb1, Sfrp1, Sfrp2, Wnt5a, Ror2) markedly disrupts
   fetal muscle patterning.
 • Gubernacular gene expression after DHT or INSL3 stimulation activates Wnt pathways with strong
   transcriptional overlap.
 • Gubernaculum-specific Wnt4 knockout mice develop unilateral UDT: intra-abdominal testes show
   raised FSH and LH with absent germ cells; inguinal testes have normal hormones but reduced fertility.
Neural pathway
 • GFN innervation is essential to gubernacular function in rodents; the proposed mediator is calcitonin
   gene-related peptide (CGRP) released from GFN sensory endings.
 • A competing theory holds that androgens act indirectly via mesenchyme along the mammary line —
   this conflicts with data showing functional AR within the gubernaculum itself.
 • A CGRP role in human descent is undefined, but genome-wide association data link RBFOX genes —
   encoding RNA-binding proteins regulating CGRP production — to clinical cryptorchidism.
Concept — hormones do not move the testis, they build and remodel the gubernaculum. INSL3 and
androgen converge on one target tissue and one process — gubernacular myogenesis — which is why two
hormones with different receptors produce a single phenotype when either fails.

 KEY POINTS
   • Separate genetic pathways in gonadal determination lead to testis or ovary; SRY regulates
     downstream testis-determining genes in males.
   • Differentiation of gonocytes, Sertoli cells and Leydig cells occurs between 5 and 9 weeks' gestation;
     the gubernaculum appears at 7 weeks and guides descent.
   • Testosterone and INSL3, produced by Leydig cells, are critical for descent; levels peak between 14
     and 17 weeks.
   • From the second trimester, gubernacular swelling provides space for testicular passage into the
     scrotum between 20 and 28 weeks.


Epidemiology
 • Cryptorchidism is one of the commonest congenital anomalies, reported in 1–9% of full-term and 1–
   45% of preterm newborn males; the wide ranges reflect differing definitions and examination methods.

 • 80–85% of cases are isolated (nonsyndromic).
 • Cryptorchidism is a component of almost 500 syndromes, causally linked to 387 genes within 414 loci
   (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, OMIM).
 • A common subset of syndromic cases is associated with hormonal defects of the HPG axis.

Congenital Cryptorchidism
 • Prevalence studies are confounded by subjectivity of examination, inclusion or exclusion of high scrotal
   testes, differing populations and design.
 • Most studies support prevalence of 2–4% at birth and 1–2% at 3 months, with frequency as high as 9%
   in some geographies; no reproducible secular trend has been shown.
 • Cryptorchidism is more common in White than non-White populations.
 • Spontaneous descent by 3 months occurs in 45–87% of those cryptorchid at birth in population-based
   prospective series.
 • Position predicts spontaneous descent: extrascrotal testes descend by 1 year in about half, versus high
   scrotal testes in the large majority (≈88%).
 • Referral populations behave differently: spontaneous descent in a minority of those presenting before 6
   months and in none presenting after 6 months — reflecting exclusion of early neonatal descent and
   greater severity on referral.
 • Re-ascent (recurrent cryptorchidism) occurred in 10–22% in two large longitudinal series, usually
   between 1 and 5 years of age.

Acquired Cryptorchidism
 • Once held impossible — cryptorchidism was regarded as a congenital anomaly identifiable at birth —
   acquired cryptorchidism is now an established entity, first reported in 1975.
 • In practice: testes diagnosed as cryptorchid after apparent full descent at birth or in the neonatal
   period.
 • It most likely represents a milder presentation of congenital cryptorchidism that escaped detection in
   infancy.

Retractile testes and acquired cryptorchidism
 • Retractile testes are common in normal populations and resolve with age — suprascrotal in up to about
   a third of 4-year-olds and a tenth of those aged 4–12 years, and intrascrotal in all males older than 12
   years in the classic hospital series.
 • Population-based studies report retractile testes in roughly 11–15% up to 11 years and about 4% at 7–
   12 years.
 • A subset become undescended over time.
 • Selection bias is the central methodological problem: retrospective specialist series report
   cryptorchidism developing in up to 7–32%, whereas a large prospective birth cohort found only about
   2.6% developed ipsilateral cryptorchidism, with a further 13.5% developing contralateral or secondary
   cryptorchidism.
 • A prospective study of unilateral retractile testis found reduced testicular position and/or size after 5
   years in the majority, measured by pubic tubercle–to–mid-testis distance and orchidometer.
 • Pathology shared with cryptorchidism supports a continuum: reduced testicular volume, ipsilateral
   patent processus vaginalis, epididymal anomalies and altered cremaster innervation in retractile testes
   undergoing orchidopexy.
Prevalence of acquired disease
 • True prevalence is incompletely defined, since longitudinal documentation of prior scrotal position
   covers only the first few years of life.
 • In serial-examination cohorts, suprascrotal testes diagnosed at birth equalled those diagnosed
   subsequently, and congenital prevalence fell sharply by 18 months while a comparable number of
   acquired cases accrued — persistent congenital and acquired forms are of similar prevalence.
 • Retrospective series cannot separate true acquired cases from delayed referral of congenital cases.
 • Japanese screening (neonate, 1, 3, 10 and 18 months, and 3 years): only about 2% of ascending testes
   had prior retractile testes, and surgery beyond 4 years of age was far lower than the 30–50% reported in
   Western countries — comprehensive screening lowers acquired cryptorchidism and late surgery.
 • Serial physical examination is recommended to determine testicular position and identify acquired cases,
   particularly with retractile testes.
Concept — acquired cryptorchidism is almost certainly not a testis that fell out of a normal scrotum, but a
never-quite-descended testis revealed by growth. That reframes the question from "why did it ascend?" to
"why was it missed?", and justifies annual examination through childhood.

Pathogenesis
 • The specific cause is unknown in most cases; indirect evidence indicates a multifactorial disease of
   combined genetic and environmental risk.
 • The most consistent risk factors are prematurity and/or low birth weight for gestational age, familial
   factors relating to the maternal environment or rare genetic variants, maternal diabetes, and maternal
   smoking.
 • Maternal obesity was associated in large population studies but not in meta-analysis of smaller case-
   control studies — variability between populations.
 • Nonsyndromic disease shows no strong large-effect loci, but a multilocus, complex pattern of genetic
   susceptibility.
 • Few consistent human environmental exposures have been identified, though animal models implicate
   EDCs, potentially in combination.

Genetic Susceptibility
 • Family studies indicate heritability with polygenic, multifactorial susceptibility.
 • Familial clustering occurs with a variable phenotype; autosomal dominance with reduced penetrance
   was the mode most often cited.
 • Large registry data show moderate familial aggregation: recurrence risk ratio highest in twins,
   intermediate in brothers, lowest in offspring, and significantly higher in maternal than paternal half-
   brothers.
 • An independent Danish study reproduced the maternal-over-paternal pattern and highest concordance
   in twins; restricting to surgically treated persistent cryptorchidism raised concordance in all categories.
 • The maternal excess implies environmental and/or maternal factors, or X-linked risk alleles.
 • Earlier studies reported several-fold increased prevalence in fathers and brothers of affected subjects.
Candidate genes
 • INSL3 and RXFP2 are the most promising candidates, but coding variants are rare in persistently
   cryptorchid males.
 • Interpretation is confounded: variants associate with a range of phenotypes, and apparently deleterious
   mutations occur in normal family members; few INSL3 exonic variants are clearly functional.
 • The RXFP2 T222P polymorphism showed curtailed membrane localisation and receptor activation in
   vitro, but similar allele frequency in cases and controls elsewhere — not established.
 • Exome analysis of infertile men with a history of cryptorchidism found homozygous high-impact INSL3
   and RXFP2 variants correlating with bilateral disease, supporting autosomal recessive inheritance —
   superseding the prior assumption of dominant inheritance.
 • Other candidates have not replicated, plausibly because most series include fewer than 100 cases and
   because of heterogeneity.
 • Summary: specific causative alleles other than rare variants in INSL3, RXFP2 or NR5A1 are largely
   unknown, owing to confounding environmental risk, insufficient sample size, and geographic and
   phenotypic variability.
Concept — cryptorchidism behaves genetically like a threshold trait, not a Mendelian one. Strong familial
aggregation with vanishingly rare causal variants, and maternal-line excess with autosomal candidates, is the
signature of many small genetic contributions interacting with the intrauterine environment.

Environmental Risk Factors
 • The maternal environment appears to contribute, but it is unclear whether through exposures,
   characteristics or lifestyle.
 • Maternal smoking: mild-to-moderate increased risk, with possible increased germ cell abnormalities in
   cryptorchid offspring.
 • Maternal alcohol: inconsistent.
 • Analgesics (paracetamol, or more than one analgesic) in midpregnancy or earlier: several series suggest
   increased risk, supported by altered hormone production in cultured human fetal testis; recent meta-
   analyses judge the evidence weak.
 • Parental occupation: not consistently associated, though horticulture-related occupations and areas of
   higher pesticide use may carry risk.

Testicular dysgenesis syndrome and diethylstilbestrol
 • Testicular dysgenesis syndrome (TDS): cryptorchidism, hypospadias, infertility and TGCT attributed to
   impaired testicular function in utero, proposed to result from deficient fetal androgen production
   caused by EDC exposure.
 • Concern arose from increased cryptorchidism after maternal diethylstilbestrol (DES) exposure.
 • Large cohort: risk roughly doubled overall, higher with exposure before 11 weeks' gestation and
   cumulative dose ≥5 g — early and substantial exposure carries the clearest risk.
Endocrine-disrupting chemicals — the balance
 • Animal models support a link between prenatal antiandrogenic EDCs and cryptorchidism, but at
   exposures far exceeding clinical levels, with disputed applicability to humans.
 • Species-specific sensitivity is critical: the human testis appears insensitive to phthalate or DES inhibition
   of steroidogenesis, but sensitive to bisphenol A (BPA).
 • Measured EDC levels in placenta, breast milk or cord blood — pesticides, polychlorinated biphenyls
   (PCBs), dioxins, perfluorinated compounds, flame retardants (PBDEs), organotin — were not
   consistently higher in affected pregnancies; phthalate metabolites did not correlate with
   cryptorchidism.
 • Positive signals: flame retardants in maternal hair, BPA in placenta, organic pollutants in adipose tissue;
   the Norwegian Human Milk Study (PCB-74, PCB-114, PCB-194, β-hexachlorocyclohexane); Danish
   registry data on maternal airborne PCB exposure.

Neonatal studies
 • Results conflict: reduced testosterone and/or LH in some series, not in others.
 • Geography matters: INHB and FSH differed between Finnish and Danish cryptorchid infants while
   testosterone did not.
 • INSL3 was reduced in cord blood but not at 3 months in persistent cryptorchidism; cord testosterone
   did not differ.
 • The informative finding is ratio-based: raised LH/testosterone and LH/INSL3 ratios indicate
   "compensated Leydig cell function" — subtly reduced Leydig function driving compensatory LH.
 • In the largest series (Kollin et al., 2012), hormones did not differ among unilateral, bilateral and
   spontaneously resolving cases and did not correlate with spermatogenesis.
Older children
 • LH, FSH and testosterone fall to low or undetectable levels as the HPG axis becomes quiescent.
 • INHB and AMH remain measurable throughout childhood and are the sensitive Sertoli cell biomarkers
   of prepubertal testicular activity.
 • Sertoli dysfunction is increasingly evident, particularly in bilateral disease: AMH reduced roughly three
   times more often in bilateral than unilateral cases; gonadotrophins normal or rarely mildly raised.
 • Other series report more raised FSH and reduced INHB but used reference standards rather than
   controls; another found no differences before or after hCG stimulation.
 • Confounders include population and maternal smoking (associated with reduced INHB in offspring).
 • Low prepubertal gonadotrophins have been read as gonadotrophin deficiency causing cryptorchidism
   — but the axis is inactive in childhood and gonadotrophins may be normal even in anorchia.
Effect of surgery on hormones
 • INHB rose postoperatively in the large majority in one series; abnormal INHB was about twice as
   common in uncorrected as corrected cases.
 • In other series INHB normalised in fewer than half and did not correlate with histology; raised FSH in
   bilateral cases normalised in most.
 • Interpretation is limited by large intraindividual fluctuation of FSH and INHB in the first 2 years of life.

Syndromic Cryptorchidism
 • Undescended testes are frequent in androgen biosynthetic defects, androgen insensitivity, Leydig cell
   agenesis, and gonadotrophin deficiency; generalised failure of masculinisation constitutes a disorder of
   sex development (DSD).
 • Persistent müllerian duct syndrome (PMDS) — defective AMH signalling — is associated with
   cryptorchidism or transverse testicular ectopia, through hindrance by retained müllerian ducts and/or
   loss of AMH's proliferative effect on the gubernaculum.
 • Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) occurs in under 2% of nonsyndromic cryptorchidism series, more with
   hypospadias; conversely, more than half of prepubertal Klinefelter patients have cryptorchidism.
 • Other rearrangements and trisomies, including Down syndrome (trisomy 21), are associated.

 KEY POINTS
   • Cryptorchidism occurs in 1–4% of full-term males. Spontaneous descent may occur in the first few
     months, usually by 6 months. Testes may also ascend over time.
   • Causes are largely unknown; birth weight, gestational age, genetic and environmental factors likely
     contribute.
   • Acquired cryptorchidism is likely equivalent to cases diagnosed at birth. It may follow confirmed
     scrotal position at birth or spontaneous descent, and may be commoner with retractile testes. The
     risk warrants annual testicular examination throughout childhood.


DIAGNOSIS

Examination
 • Physical examination is the gold standard for diagnosing cryptorchidism.
 • Positions: supine, and where necessary upright — sitting cross-legged and/or squatting/standing.
 • With the patient supine, examine from the ipsilateral side of the testis in question.
 • Abduction of the thighs inhibits the cremasteric reflex, which is elicited by stroking the ipsilateral inner
   thigh.
 • Document palpability, position, mobility, size and lie, plus hernia, hydrocele, intratesticular lesions,
   penile size and urethral meatus position.
 • Adjuncts to limit cremasteric activity: distraction, warm room and hands, liquid soap or lubricant,
   repeated examinations.
 • Sustained gentle traction on the spermatic cord fatigues the cremaster and allows a retractile testis to
   remain stably scrotal — the manoeuvre that distinguishes retractile from undescended.
 • Scrotal asymmetry is a useful sign in unilateral cryptorchidism.
 • Contralateral testicular hypertrophy with a nonpalpable testis suggests a vanishing testis but is not
   diagnostic.
 • 75–80% of undescended testes are palpable; 60–70% are unilateral; right-sided involvement is
   commoner overall but less frequent among nonpalpable testes.

Palpable Testes
 • Undescended testes lie along the line of normal descent or in an ectopic position.
 • The commonest ectopic site is the superficial inguinal pouch; rarer sites: perirenal, prepubic, femoral,
   peripenile, perineal, transverse scrotal and contralateral scrotal.
 • Correct classification as palpable or nonpalpable is the critical step that determines further diagnosis
   and treatment; every effort must be made to find the lowest position the testis can attain.
Examination technique
1. Apply downward pressure with one hand along the ipsilateral inguinal canal, from the anterior iliac
   spine toward the scrotum →
2. Palpate with the opposite hand for the lowest attainable position →
3. While compressing the canal, invaginate the scrotum to palpate a testis distal to the external ring or
   within the SIP.
Limits of clinical classification
 • Difficulty lies in documenting position and distinguishing undescended from retractile testes — which
   may coexist.
 • The gold standard is careful examination in several positions, with confirmation of incomplete descent
   after induction of anaesthesia.
 • Interobserver and intraobserver variation is substantial: two examiners agreed completely on position
   and mobility in only a small minority.
 • Preoperative position correlates poorly with intraoperative findings.

The vanishing testis
 • Thought to represent cord torsion or a vascular accident after completion of genital masculinisation but
   before scrotal fixation.
 • Evidence: haemosiderin in excised "nubbins"; reported contralateral postnatal torsion.
 • Highly predictive signs: contralateral compensatory hypertrophy, and absence of palpable intrascrotal
   "appendage" tissue (processus vaginalis, wolffian structures, gubernaculum).
 • Contralateral volume >2 mL: sensitivity 72%, specificity 100%; contralateral length 19–20 mm accurately
   predicts a solitary testis.
 • Useful for counselling but must not preclude surgical work-up.
 • Diagnosis requires documentation of blind-ending spermatic vessels in the abdomen, inguinal canal or
   scrotum.
Concept — the contralateral testis is a hint, never a verdict. Hypertrophy and absent appendage tissue
support counselling, but because the diagnostic endpoint is anatomical — blind-ending vessels — no
measurement of the other side can end the operation early.

Hormonal Evaluation
 • Anorchia shows elevated basal gonadotrophins and no response to hCG stimulation — but
   gonadotrophins may be unexpectedly low in mid-childhood.
 • hCG stimulation testing is no longer the procedure of choice for documenting anorchia — poorly
   standardised, side effects, inaccuracy.
 • Most cases proceed to laparoscopic or surgical exploration; hormone testing may suffice for anorchia,
   particularly in the mini-puberty window.
 • If hormones unequivocally confirm testicular tissue, diagnostic laparoscopy may not be absolutely
   necessary — but proving absence remains difficult, and normal childhood gonadotrophins do not
   exclude anorchia; then laparoscopy is required.

GENERAL GUIDELINES FOR MANAGEMENT
 • Indications for surgical correction: optimise testicular function; reduce and/or facilitate diagnosis of
   malignancy; cosmesis; prevent hernia and torsion.
 • Except in complex medical illness or the postnatal period, treat once the diagnosis is confirmed.
 • The AUA algorithm governs the approach, with similar guidelines from the Canadian Urological
   Association and the European Association of Urology (EAU).
Timing
 • Observe for the first 6 postnatal months to allow spontaneous descent.
 • If descent does not occur, operate after 6 months, preferably by 12 months of corrected gestational age;
   18 months is the guideline outer limit.
 • Rationale:
    1. Spontaneous descent is unlikely in full-term males after 2–6 months
    2. Testicular growth is restored after orchidopexy at 9 months compared with 3 years
    3. Abdominal orchidopexy may be easier in young infants, soon after mini-puberty
 • Prematurity: observe for 6 months beyond the expected date of delivery, or — if position is marginal —
   until 1 year of age.
The implementation gap
 • Intervention is delayed in many children worldwide — Sweden, Australia, the United Kingdom, Germany,
   China, Canada and the United States.
 • Sweden: only about two-thirds of diagnosed boys underwent surgery, median age fell from 6.3 to 3.4
   years, under 5% were operated in the first year, and orchidopexy by 2 years rose from 10% to 30%.
 • The majority are still operated beyond 2 years; United States compliance has improved since the AUA
   guideline but remains a minority.
 • Reasons: provider education, local resources, socioeconomic factors, and recurrent cryptorchidism;
   studies cannot separate delayed diagnosis from delayed treatment.
 • Annual scrotal examination is needed after spontaneous descent in infancy, because of re-ascent risk.
Observation of acquired cryptorchidism
 • Dutch observational series reported spontaneous descent in 57% and 75% of acquired cryptorchid
   testes, most by midpuberty; in a larger cohort 90% of high scrotal and 64.5% of inguinal/nonpalpable
   testes descended at a mean age of 12.9 years.
 • Descended testes had volumes closer to normal than operated testes.
 • Confounded: high scrotal testes dominated those descending, retractile testes were included, and some
   required surgery for hernia, pain or torsion.
 • Oligospermia did not differ significantly between operated and observed cases, but studies were non-
   randomised with heavy loss to follow-up.

Chapter 57 — Etiology, Diagnosis, and Management of the Undescended Testis                                  MEDICAL MANAGEMENT



 • Without paternity and malignancy outcome data, observation cannot be recommended for acquired
   cryptorchidism, particularly bilateral.
Concept — the timing rule is a compromise between two clocks running in opposite directions.
Spontaneous descent is possible until about 6 months and argues for waiting; germ and Sertoli cell loss
accrues from infancy and argues for operating — the window between 6 and 12 months corrected age is
where the curves cross, and prematurity shifts the clock, not the logic.

MEDICAL MANAGEMENT
 • Historical indications: distinguish retractile from undescended testes; stimulate descent; stimulate germ
   cell maturation; facilitate abdominal orchidopexy.
 • hCG "success" in retractile testes varies from 58% to 100%, depending on age, retractility, diagnostic
   accuracy and dose — hCG does not reliably distinguish retractile from cryptorchid testes, nor remove
   the need for serial examination.

Effect on germ cells
 • Against: hCG associated with increased germ cell apoptosis and lower adult testicular volume; prior
   hCG or LHRH associated with reduced germ cell counts.
 • For: randomised LHRH before surgery raised germ cell counts, more so under 2 years of age.

 • Whether hormones help or harm germ cells is unclear, and research is unlikely as the approach is no
   longer recommended.

SURGICAL MANAGEMENT
 • Surgery once failure of spontaneous descent is confirmed is standard.
 • Retrospective early-versus-late studies are mixed; the prospective randomised Kollin study supports
   surgery in the first year for congenital cryptorchidism on growth and histology — long-term outcome
   data are lacking.
 • Median age at orchidopexy remains 1.5–4 years in many series; only 18% are operated by 1 year of age
   in a large national database.

PROGNOSIS
 • Major gaps remain in understanding long-term outcome, because surgery is expected to succeed and
   prospective follow-up into adulthood is difficult.
 • Adequate studies must account for confounders — severity (position, laterality, epididymal anomalies,
   hormones), aetiology, age at surgery, hormone exposure, procedure — and use standardised methods
   with complete follow-up.

Fertility
 • A history of cryptorchidism is strongly associated with subfertility, particularly bilateral but also
   unilateral.
 • Cryptorchidism accounts for 10–17% of men evaluated for infertility.
 • Individual prediction is difficult: diverse aetiology, age at treatment, approach and severity.

Retractile testes
 • Untreated childhood retractile testes: high paternity and normal adult volume in a small series.
 • Infertility clinic series report poor semen after retractility, but retractility was poorly documented.
 • Biopsy series found germ and Sertoli cell abnormalities in retractile testes qualitatively similar to
   cryptorchidism, some without controls.
 • Retractile cannot be separated from acquired undescended testes in these studies.
 • There is insufficient evidence of increased infertility risk in uncomplicated retractile testis.
Acquired cryptorchidism
 • Data are retrospective, non-randomised and mix spontaneous pubertal descent with orchidopexy.
 • Bilateral testes descending spontaneously after 10 years: volume <15 mL in most and count <20 million/
   mL in almost half.
 • Dutch cohorts: no semen or hormone differences between spontaneous pubertal descent and pubertal
   orchidopexy (groups dominated by high scrotal and likely retractile testes).

Sperm retrieval
 • Orchidopexy before 19 years gives significantly higher testicular sperm retrieval rates than after 19

 • Azoospermic men after orchidopexy have higher retrieval rates than those with idiopathic non-

 • A congenital cause affecting both testes may not be abrogated by surgery alone, though early surgery is
   preferred and may help.
Concept — in unilateral disease the normal testis carries the fertility, and in bilateral disease nothing does.
This explains abnormal semen with near-normal paternity in unilateral men, unchanged paternity after
orchidectomy of the affected side, and why every predictor — hormones, Ad spermatogonia, germ cell
absence — works best in bilateral cases.

 • Cryptorchidism carries a two- to five-fold increased TGCT risk (pooled OR about 4).

 • Shared risk factors: inguinal hernia, prematurity, low birth weight.

Concept — cancer risk is a function of how long the gonocyte defect sits in an abnormal environment,
layered on an intrinsic defect surgery cannot remove. The age dose–response and seminoma excess in
uncorrected testes are the modifiable component; the contralateral excess and residual risk after early

 KEY POINTS
   • Sperm counts are reduced in at least 25% of formerly unilateral and the majority of formerly
     bilateral cryptorchid men; paternity in the unilateral group is similar to controls.
   • Ad spermatogonia counts may predict fertility potential in some males with cryptorchidism.
   • TGCT risk is two to five times higher with cryptorchidism and rises with age at surgery.
