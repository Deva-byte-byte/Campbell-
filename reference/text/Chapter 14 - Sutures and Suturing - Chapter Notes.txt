Chapter 14 — Sutures and Suturing
 • Suture material, size, needle type and technique are chosen for the specific anastomosis, repair or
   closure required.
 • Three primary determinants: tissue type (skin, fascia, soft tissue, mucosa, viscera), anticipated tension on
   the suture, and required duration of tensile strength.
 • Most materials are decades old; barbed sutures are the main recent innovation. Knowing each suture's
   and needle's strengths and weaknesses reduces complications.

NEEDLE TYPES
 • The application dictates the needle; most sutures come with a range of needles, so the needle is chosen
   independently of the suture. Needles vary in point, diameter, arc and radius to suit tissue and operative
   space.

Needle Point
 • Broad division: non-cutting versus cutting — the examinable pair:
     ◦ Non-cutting (taper and blunt): tapers from a wider body to a narrow tip; stretches the tissue, which
       then retracts around the suture — less trauma.
     ◦ Cutting: cuts as it passes — passes more easily, at the cost of slightly more trauma.

Concept — the needle point is a trade-off between ease of passage and tissue trauma: taper stretches
(spares tissue), cutting pierces (for dense tissue), blunt protects the operator; the reverse-cutting needle
moves the edge outward so the wound does not tear toward the edge.

Needle Types, Sizes, and Uses
Free Versus Swaged Needles
 • Swaged: suture pre-attached — far more common, faster, and thinner (no eyelet) so less trauma. Free:
   suture threaded through an eyelet — thicker to accommodate the eyelet, so more trauma.
 • Use of a free needle: after a swaged needle has been passed one way, a free needle is attached to the
   needleless end to pass it back the opposite way. Alternative: double-armed swaged sutures, available
   only in limited needle and material combinations (mainly vascular Prolene).
 • Swaged needles are either permanently attached (the suture must be cut to free the needle) or "pop-off"
   (released by traction), which saves time between throws before a knot is tied.
Needle Thickness and Diameter
 • Needle thickness and diameter roughly match suture calibre: heavier suture needing more tensile
   strength rides on a thicker, larger-diameter needle, so that the needle hole approximates the suture
   diameter and the suture passes easily.
 • Smaller circular diameter → precision in fine suturing; larger diameter → force to penetrate dense tissue
   where precision matters less.
 • Small variations are described as fine, medium or heavy: medium for most applications; fine for delicate
   tissue such as bowel or urothelium; heavy ("hernia needles") for very dense or scarred tissue where a
   medium needle may bend or break.

SUTURE MATERIAL
 • Three axes of classification: absorbable vs non-absorbable, natural vs synthetic, and braided vs
   monofilament. Most sutures come in a range of sizes.
 • Natural sutures (chromic gut, silk) provoke a greater inflammatory reaction than synthetic ones.
 • Braided vs monofilament — the governing pair:
     ◦ Braided: easier to handle, resists memory, less knot unravelling; but harder to pass through tissue
       and more inflammatory.
     ◦ Monofilament: passes easily, less inflammation; more memory, knots less secure.

 • The trap: loss of strength precedes absorption by weeks to months (Vicryl strong at 2–3 weeks, gone by

Absorbable Sutures
Chromic Gut
 • Serosal lining of sheep or bovine intestine treated with chromium salt, which slows absorption.
   Monofilament in form, yet drags through tissue — may be coated with petroleum jelly.
 • For sites where rapid dissolution matters and strength does not: circumcision, scrotal skin. Provokes one
   of the greatest inflammatory reactions of all sutures.
Plain Gut
 • Same origin, not chromium-treated → even shorter strength; similar uses, but used less.
Monocryl (Poliglecaprone 25), Caprosyn and Biosyn
 • All three are synthetic monofilaments for subcuticular skin closure: pass easily, minimal inflammation.

Vicryl (Polyglactin 910)
 • A braided synthetic, among the most used sutures in urologic surgery: reapproximation of fat, muscle
   and fascia, ligation of small vessels, and anastomoses.
 • Adequate support (2–3 weeks) with early absorption (≤70 days) → less infection and inflammation than
   longer-lasting materials.
Polydioxanone (PDS) and Maxon (Polyglyconate)
 • Both synthetic monofilaments for wound and fascial closure, both available as a double-stranded loop
   for extra strength.
 • PDS: passes easily; security beyond 6 weeks, yet absorbed — less inflammation and infection than
   permanent material; the choice for prolonged fascial strength. Maxon: security up to 6 weeks, absorbed
   within 6 months.

SUTURING TECHNIQUES
 • Technique balances mechanical stress, operating time, infection risk and cosmesis, and is matched to
   tissue.

Skin Closure
 • Dermis and epidermis are reapproximated to restore the barrier unless drainage is required (e.g. pre-
   existing infection); some wounds carry tension, others need only edge approximation to limit scarring.
 • A correctly approximated, uninfected epidermis seals within 24–48 hours but lacks dermal strength — so
   the suture must hold, or remain before removal or absorption, until the dermis is strong (which varies
   with body area).
 • Running vs interrupted (applies to every technique): running saves knot-tying time, but a break
   anywhere can open the entire incision.

Fascial Closure
 • Fascial defects of 1 cm or larger are closed after abdominal surgery.
 • Fascia is poorly vascularised and heals slowly, so it needs longer tensile strength; some applications
   benefit from permanent sutures. Surgeon technique determines both avoidance of visceral injury and
   incisional hernia risk.
 • STITCH trial (landmark): midline laparotomy randomised to small bites (5 mm from the edge, every 5
   mm) vs large bites (1 cm from the edge, every 1 cm). Large bites were faster (10 vs 14 minutes) but gave
   more incisional hernia at 1 year (21% vs 13%; P = 0.022). Small bites are not yet universal standard, but
   spacing greater than 1 cm should be avoided.
