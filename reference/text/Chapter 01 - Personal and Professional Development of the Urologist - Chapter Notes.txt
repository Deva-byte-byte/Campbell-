Part I: Clinical Decision Making

 • Earlier editions addressed knowledge and skills; professional attitudes and attributes are now treated as
   core content.
 • Professional formation is ancient: Hippocrates taught his students ethics, duty, obligation and personal
   decorum, including dress and behaviour.
 • Breach of professionalism as a medical student is one of the most predictive factors for later
   disciplinary action by medical boards; state and medical society boards recognise professionalism as
   essential.
 • Development is lifelong — not complete at the end of medical school or residency; it needs ongoing
   reflection.
 • Mental and physical health are inseparable, and the physician's own health underpins professional
   conduct and patient care; burnout degrades performance and patient outcomes.
 • Ultimate goal: the health and longevity of the physician and of the physician's patients.

PERSONAL DEVELOPMENT
 • Stimulus → reflex reaction → chosen response: the initial reaction may be hard-wired, but the
   subsequent response is under personal control; this capacity allows one to direct personal development.
 • Growth towards goals aligned with life purpose gives reward, joy and energy.
 • Method: define core values and goals → prioritise activities that serve them → build routines and habits
   → review periodically.
 • Learning from adversity — drawing lessons from challenge, stress or suffering shifts perspective towards
   a "silver lining".
 • Reflection on professional ethics and career goals begins in medical school and is deliberately re-
   examined, refined and refreshed.
 • Disruptive events are inevitable ("Everyone has a plan until they get punched in the mouth" — Mike
   Tyson) and force reassessment of priorities, goals and even identity; a clear prior understanding of one's
   beliefs, values and core principles allows new plans congruent with personal and professional ethics.

Career Development and the Life Cycle of a Urologist
 • The career cycle is finite and nested within the longer human life cycle; accepting this early aids planning
   of career development and transitions.
 • Cellular model of life (Fig. 1.1): life as a cell, its cycles as organelles. Each cycle's products affect the
   others and the whole cell; activity varies with need and external factors.

Burnout
 • Definition: a symptom constellation of emotional and ethical exhaustion, depersonalisation and
   diminished personal accomplishment at work, in response to chronic emotional and interpersonal
   stressors.
 • A distinct entity from job dissatisfaction, fatigue, occupational stress and depression, although associated
   with each.
 • Prevalence: around 40% of United States physicians, including trainees, for several decades; recent figures
   approach 50% in urologists.
 • Rising despite attention: American Urological Association (AUA) census 2021 versus 2016 showed
   increased burnout among urologists, notably in women and in those under 45. Mid-career physicians,
   historically the highest group, fell slightly below early-career urologists.
 • Women have higher burnout, and female urologists are more often dissatisfied with work–life balance
   and more often report too little time for personal or family life.

 • Physician comorbidities: heart disease, diabetes, insomnia, anxiety, substance abuse, depression and
   suicide.
 • Physician suicide: about one United States physician per day; rate relative to the general population 1.4
   times in men and 2.7 times in women; 12–14% of burned-out physicians have suicidal ideation. Help-
   seeking is impeded by fear of losing the medical licence. Resources: physiciansupportline.com;
   988lifeline.org.
 • Effects on patients and workforce: lower patient satisfaction and safety, lower-quality care, more medical
   error and poor clinical judgement; lower productivity and job satisfaction; physicians leave practice →
   workforce shortage → delayed urological care.
 • Contributors: loss of autonomy; loss of purpose (time on tasks not requiring a physician's training); loss of
   community and isolation from colleagues; electronic health record (EHR) time; excessive workload;
   insufficient personal and family time; education debt; falling reimbursement; rising regulation and
   metrics; administrative burden.
 • Moral injury: when these factors prevent the physician from giving the quality of care they wish, the
   result is stress and moral injury that feeds burnout.
 • The 20% threshold: physicians spending less than 20% of work effort on the activity they find most
   meaningful are almost three times as likely to be burned out.

Maintaining Morale
 • Anchor on core goals and values: career goals serve overarching personal goals and purpose; routine
   review keeps sight of "the forest for the trees" and allows course correction. Effort towards core goals is
   energising.
 • Service to others is the value common to all physicians. Among peers, service seems commonplace;
   remember how selected, trained, needed and respected each physician is. A "routine" visit may be the
   most important part of a patient's day or week, and small positive changes can cascade into major impact.
 • The physician as role model to staff, students, colleagues, patients, families and society: care for patients
   demonstrates the inherent value and sanctity of every person; scientifically grounded counsel models
   thoughtful responsiveness. Recalling this impact restores self-image and self-esteem.

 • One's own role models: imitating inspiring colleagues and teachers sustains progress and perspective in
   difficult times; remember that one is also being watched.
 • Service itself is the reward — an honour and a privilege. Research, teaching, mentorship and
   administration are alternative routes to service, giving flexibility.
 • Expect adjustment: reflection may refine or discard goals, even to a change of career; career goals are
   subservient to personal growth goals.
Concept — Morale is sustained by reconnecting daily work to the purpose of serving others; when the work
no longer serves that purpose, the work is changed, not the purpose.

Sustainable Change and Self-Care
 • Change is inevitable, in both the internal and external (work) environment. Drivers: exponential growth of
   technology and scientific knowledge in the 20th and 21st centuries and corporatisation of United States
   health care from the 1960s.
 • Hence lifelong learning was built into the American Board of Urology maintenance of certification
   programme.
 • Change is beyond control; the response is not. After the initial reaction, respond by adjusting the cycles
   of Fig. 1.1 — temporarily or for longer: meditation, physical activity, work activity, social support, a new
   skill — directed at happiness, security, purpose or future opportunity.
 • Build the non-career cycles before the crisis — "broadening the portfolio": investments in the mental,
   physical and social cycles pay dividends to the other cycles and give more response options in hard times.
 • Habits are a powerful determinant of behaviour, often unrecognised without reflection, and can be
   deliberately constructed; an activity becomes an established habit after about 3 weeks.
 • Subtraction as well as addition: a response cannot always be a net addition; identify activities that no
   longer give energy or growth and modify or drop them.
Concept — Resilience is pre-built capacity: the options available in a crisis are only those already developed
in the physical, mental and social cycles.

Complications and Shortcomings
 • All physicians make mistakes and have complications — "the only way to avoid operative complications
   is to stop operating".
 • Systems view: many complications arise from a combination of potentially avoidable events within a
   system, not a single individual failure.
 • Reframe failure as an opportunity to learn and grow; recognising suffering as part of the shared human
   condition reduces feelings of being overwhelmed and isolated.
 • Self-compassion — how we relate to ourselves in perceived failure or inadequacy — is learnable; "To err
   is human, to forgive is divine" (Alexander Pope) applies to oneself.
 • Self-compassion is associated with more mindfulness, happiness, life satisfaction, resilience and health-
   promoting behaviour, and less shame, depression, anxiety, stress and suicidal ideation.
Concept — Complications are system events and learning opportunities; self-compassion rather than self-
blame converts them into growth.

Psychomotor Domain
 • Technology-driven field: urologists must maintain skills and periodically retool as devices and techniques
   arrive; infrastructure must ensure safe transition of innovation into patient care.
 • Proctorship is often required for credentialing at local hospitals in specialty skills such as robotic and
   laser surgery.
 • Routes: specialty- and industry-sponsored hands-on courses, proctorships, exchange programmes, formal
   fellowships, mini-fellowships, simulation.
 • Simulation serves to acquire and maintain skills and to warm up before operating. Models of varying
   validity: synthetic part-task trainers and complete surgical systems, virtual reality trainers, biological
   tissue, live animals.

Communication Skills Domain
 • Paramount with patients, colleagues and staff: routine interactions plus advanced skills such as breaking
   bad news and de-escalation.
 • Communication errors contribute to about 70% of medical errors and 27% of malpractice claims. Poor
   communication also lowers treatment adherence and patient satisfaction and wastes resources.
 • A formal communication curriculum is often lacking in urology residency.
 • TeamSTEPPS (Team Strategies and Tools to Enhance Performance and Patient Safety), supported by the
   American Hospital Association (AHA): a framework for formal training in practices and institutions that
   builds a culture of safety. Derived from high-reliability principles and more than 30 years of research;
   aims — teamwork, communication, every member able to speak up, employee satisfaction.
 • Evidence: improves attitudes, knowledge and behavioural skills; combined with simulation, in some
   cases improves patient outcomes. A meta-analysis of teamwork training showed a positive effect on team
   performance and patient outcomes; training should cover both routine and non-routine situations.
 • Train individuals to be good team members when whole-team training is impractical; the physician as
   leader sets the culture and tone. Local and national courses are available through the AHA.

ORGANISATIONAL SUPPORT OF PROFESSIONALISM
 • Regulators and certification boards (state licensure boards, The Joint Commission) take an interest in
   provider professionalism.
 • Domains of professionalism: availability, optimal communication, team-based contribution, respect,
   technical and cognitive competence, self-awareness — affecting colleagues, co-workers, patients and
   families.
 • Promotion requires contributions from the individual, group, institution and care system.
 • Elements of an optimal culture of safety: a supportive culture of service; oversight (identifying variance
   from optimal professional activity, data collection and aggregation, feedback to individuals and groups);
   support systems (wellness and employee assistance programmes); leadership support. The result is a
   high-reliability organisation with less unnecessary variation in care and human-interaction outcomes.
 • Infrastructure: committed leaders and dedicated teams; organisational cultural goals and values;
   enforced policies and procedures; resources for engaged teams; a tiered intervention model; data
   reporting, assessment and reproducible review; institutional support for education and training.
