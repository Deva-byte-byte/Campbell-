Chapter 127 — Male-to-Female Gender-Affirming Surgery:
Vaginoplasty

INTRODUCTION
 • Increased social acceptance has produced increased access to care for transgender patients. Urologists
   align physical anatomy with gender identity while maintaining physiologic function — the dual objective
   of the whole chapter.

PREPARATION FOR SURGERY

Mental Health Evaluation
 • Mental health services serve two distinct functions: management of dysphoria itself, and assessment of
   surgical readiness. The second has direct operative consequences.
 • Early involvement is justified by the long lead time — preparation for vaginoplasty takes months, giving
   time to identify and address psychosocial barriers of employment, housing and social support.
 • Interventions before surgery: smoking cessation, weight loss, and brief solution-focused supportive
   work to establish psychosocial stability.
 • Mental health professionals also equip patients with healthy coping skills for the psychological distress
   and pain of recovery, particularly when complications occur.
 • Because of discrimination and other stressors, psychiatric diagnoses are highly comorbid with gender
   dysphoria. Comorbid conditions should be treated before gender-affirming surgery wherever possible,
   as they adversely affect postoperative recovery.
 • Decisions to delay surgery until psychiatric or psychosocial stability improves are case-by-case and
   made in collaboration with the patient, never categorical.
Risk Framework
 • Risk related to psychosocial and psychiatric stability resolves into two categories: (1) non-adherence to
   postoperative treatment — in practice, failure of dilation; and (2) worsening psychosocial or psychiatric
   prognosis after surgery.
 • The nature and degree of risk are not static — a range of individual risk and protective factors in the
   patient's biopsychosocial context must be weighed.
 • The critical logical point: where depression and suicidal symptoms are largely driven by dysphoria,
   treatment of the dysphoria becomes more critical, and it is reasonable to expect surgery to reduce
   distress and improve function. Psychiatric symptoms are not in themselves a reason to defer.
 • Factors to assess: history of adherence concerns; safety in environment and relationships; help-seeking;
   health literacy; access to financial resources; social and instrumental support; adaptive or maladaptive
   coping including substance use; rapport and trust with the medical team and institution; impulsivity;
   distress tolerance; resource use; system navigation competencies; caregiving needs; self-advocacy; and
   involvement of support persons.

Updated World Professional Association for Transgender Health Criteria
 • Earlier World Professional Association for Transgender Health (WPATH) guidance was criticised for
   excessive gatekeeping creating unnecessary barriers to gender-affirming care including vaginoplasty.

   Standards of Care version 8 (SOC8) emphasises access to care and an informed consent approach. Six
   specific changes:
 • One assessment rather than two, justified by high rates of agreement among independent assessors.
 • Who may assess has widened. No longer confined to licensed mental health professionals: the assessor
   must hold a licence and at minimum a master's degree, be competent in transgender health, undertake
   continuing education, and have mental health evaluation within their scope of practice (Statement of
   Recommendation 5.1). Primary care providers and surgeons may be appropriate assessors depending on
   experience and expertise. Mental health professionals become consultants to an interdisciplinary team
   rather than the sole determinants of medical necessity.
 • Six months of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) before gonadectomy, consistent with the patient's
   goals, is sufficient — the rationale being experience of the effect of testosterone blockers before
   irreversible changes in hormone production.
 • Psychiatric and medical conditions need no longer be well managed before medical transition; instead
   risks and benefits are identified and discussed — the shift from prerequisite to disclosure.
 • The 12-month social transition or "experience in a role" requirement is removed, having been a serious
   barrier for people facing significant threats of violence for expressing their gender. Discussion of social
   transition is the patient-centred alternative; transition is nuanced and not dictated by providers.
 • Capacity to consent is procedure-specific, with emphasis where fertility is affected. Rarely, intellectual or
   developmental disability, severe psychiatric symptoms such as psychosis, or cognitive impairment may
   temporarily or permanently affect an adult's capacity. Where capacity is in doubt, psychologists help
   construct a plan to support or restore capacity, which may include formal or informal proxy decision
   makers in the consent process.
Concept — SOC8 substitutes information the patient must receive for thresholds the patient must clear: two
assessors become one, managed comorbidity becomes disclosed comorbidity, and mandated real-life
experience becomes discussed social transition. Capacity and fertility counselling remain hard obligations
because they alone are irreversible.

Care Coordination for Gender-Affirming Surgery
 • Postoperative depression is not uncommon and may severely affect recovery and vaginal dilation — the
   direct link from mood to surgical outcome.
 • Patients must identify sources of social support and a plan for postoperative care before surgery, and
   ready access to medical care in an emergency must be confirmed.
 • For the first 3 weeks after surgery the patient should be within one hour's driving distance of the
   operating hospital.
 • Misgendering may occur throughout the health care system and is a source of dissatisfaction. The
   electronic health record must reflect the patient's gender and preferred name.
 • Financial administrative staff require education on the surgery and WPATH guidelines to obtain prior
   authorisation and bill appropriately.
 • The surgeon should be deeply involved in the initial stages — confirming the WPATH criteria are being
   met and that insurance preauthorisation is correctly submitted.

Weight Limitations
 • Body mass index (BMI) is the major factor affecting wound healing and tension on the closure.
 • Superficial wound healing problems are cited across a wide range, from 3.3% to 33%.
 • There is no well-established BMI cut-off; in practice many providers require a BMI of 35 kg/m² or less.

 • A higher BMI may be appropriate based on provider comfort and if the patient does not desire a vaginal
   canal — that is, for vulvoplasty.
 • Very low BMI may signal an eating disorder, itself affecting wound healing.

Hair Removal
 • Scrotal skin is commonly used to line the neovaginal canal, so permanent hair removal by laser or
   electrolysis prevents hair growing within the canal after vaginoplasty. Hair removal is performed before
   surgery.
 • Intraoperative follicle removal alone may be insufficient, because hair growth occurs in several phases
   — the reason preoperative treatment cannot be substituted for at the table.
 • Intravaginal hair growth is a common long-term complaint and leads to infection, hairballs, and trapping
   of epidermal debris and lubricant.
 • Hair removal template (Fig. 127.1): the scrotal and perineal skin that will form the full-thickness skin
   graft lining the canal.

Perioperative Hormone Management
 • Venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk may be increased on hormone therapy, especially with malignancy,
   obesity and tobacco use.
 • Bottom line of the 2022 meta-analysis: continuing oestrogen therapy perioperatively incurs no
   additional VTE risk in vaginoplasty.
 • Discontinuation carries its own harm — mental distress.
 • Practice: continue hormone therapy through the perioperative period.

Orchiectomy
 • Gender-affirming orchidectomy may be the sole genital surgery or the first stage, for several reasons:
   some patients' dysphoria relates primarily to seeing and feeling the testicles, and removal satisfies them;
   it facilitates tucking of the phallus for concealment; and it minimises the hormone regimen.
 • Bottom line of the hormonal effect: after orchidectomy, essentially all patients were able to stop
   spironolactone, most discontinued progesterone, and a substantial minority reduced their oestradiol
   dose.
 • All patients undergo fertility counselling before orchidectomy, with referral for sperm banking before
   surgery if preservation is wanted. It must be made clear that orchidectomy and vaginoplasty lead to
   infertility, since misconceptions are common.
 • Donor-site controversy: prior orchidectomy may increase the chance of needing extragenital skin grafts
   at later vaginoplasty. Peritoneal flaps and allografts reduce that need.
 • Incision choice is the examinable point. Inguinal incisions may compromise the blood supply of the
   penile flap used to create the vulva at eventual vaginoplasty, whereas a scrotal incision is incorporated
   into the skin graft used for vaginoplasty.
 • Therefore: bilateral orchidectomy through a vertical midline scrotal incision.
Concept — Orchidectomy is never standalone in planning terms: it is either the endpoint or stage one of a
vaginoplasty, and is performed as though vaginoplasty will follow. That single assumption dictates the
midline scrotal incision and the preservation of peri-cord tissue for later labial bulk.

VAGINOPLASTY

Positioning and Set-Up
 • Dorsal lithotomy, with careful padding of the lateral aspects of the calf and the posterior heel to
   minimise neuropraxia; specific vigilance for common peroneal nerve injury in lithotomy.
 • For the robotic component, port setup as for prostatectomy, in steep Trendelenburg.
 • VTE prophylaxis: weight-dosed subcutaneous heparin plus a sequential compression device.
 • The procedure should not incur major blood loss, but bleeding is front-loaded — concentrated in penile
   disassembly, urethrectomy/urethroplasty and vaginal canal dissection. For this reason tranexamic acid 1
   g intravenously is given at the beginning of the procedure.

Perineal Approach
 • Penile inversion vaginoplasty (PIV) via the perineal approach is the commonest method of gender-
   affirming genital reconstruction for transgender women and supplies most of the published data, even
   where authors now favour robotic abdominal dissection with peritoneal flaps.
 • Goal: a functional and aesthetic organ that alleviates gender dysphoria.
 • Homologous tissues (Fig. 127.3) are the conceptual spine of the operation and are set out in the
   takeaway to this section.
Marking and Scrotal Skin Harvest
 1. Place a Foley catheter and drain the bladder, then plug the catheter so the penis can be moved during
    disassembly.
 2. Measure the genital anatomy before incising. A short penile shaft results in shorter labia minora but
    does not affect canal depth — an important counselling point.
 3. Mark the tendons of the adductor longus bilaterally as the landmark for clitoral position, and mark the
    midline on the dorsal penile skin to avoid torsion of the skin.
 4. Mark and harvest the scrotal skin for the full-thickness graft that lines the neovaginal canal. A small
    rhomboid posterior flap is raised at this incision, later advanced into the introitus to take tension off the
    posterior closure.
 5. Defat and thin the graft on the back table; perform hair follicle removal and fulguration; preserve the
    subcutaneous tissue beneath the scrotal skin during harvest.
 6. Tubularise the thinned full-thickness graft over a vaginal dilator, then use it to augment the distal
    portion of the penile skin that will be inverted into the canal.
Orchiectomy at the Time of Vaginoplasty
 • Performed now if not previously done. Retract the testicles with a towel clamp and dissect the spermatic
   cords proximally to the level of the inguinal canal.
 • Leave the tissue and fat surrounding the spermatic cord behind — it provides volume to the labia
   majora.
Penile Disassembly
 1. Circumcising incision, sparing an adequate mucosal collar that will serve as the clitoral hood.
 2. Elevate the proximal penile skin off Buck's fascia while keeping the dartos with the penile skin.
    Preserving the dartos maintains blood supply to the skin flap once it is rearranged to form the labia.
 3. Dissection between the dartos and Buck's fascia yields a cylindrical penile skin flap.
 4. Extend the dissection laterally and mobilise the surrounding tissue to produce the flap used for the
    labiaplasty.

Corporal Resection and Clitoroplasty
 • The clitoris is created from the glans penis attached to the neurovascular bundle.
 • Two techniques of separating corporal tissue from the neurovascular bundle — the examinable pair:
     • Separate the bundle from the tunica albuginea of the corpora cavernosa: incise the dartos layer and
       Buck's fascia longitudinally, with the initial incision along the lateral side of the urethra to minimise
       violation of the bundle, dissecting along the tunica until the entire bundle is elevated.
     • Maintain the bundle in continuity with the dorsal tunica albuginea — the authors' preference,
       because it minimises handling of the neurovascular bundle and decreases the risk of nerve damage.
 • Technique for the preferred method: open the corpus cavernosum longitudinally from glans to base
   ventrally → excavate the spongy tissue with a Molt periosteal elevator → coagulate the cavernosal
   arteries → close the proximal tunica from proximal to distal up to the level of the adductor longus
   tendon.
 • Complete excision of erectile tissue is mandatory: residual erectile tissue causes engorgement during
   arousal and obstruction of the vaginal canal.
 • Clitoroplasty: mark the proximal glans with a "W" → excise the ventromedial glans tissue, preserving
   the lateral wings → de-epithelialise the wings and suture them together to create the clitoris.
 • The size trade-off: preserve enough glans for an aesthetic, appropriately sized clitoris with the maximal
   number of nerve endings, but too much preserved glans yields a prominent clitoris resembling a glans
   penis.
 • The mucosal collar of distal penile skin becomes the clitoral hood.
 • After creation of the clitoris, the neurovascular bundle is accordioned onto itself and secured to the
   suprapubic abdominal fascia, laid flat, with the clitoris positioned at the level of the adductor longus
   tendons.
 • The mucosal collar of preputial skin is then secured inferiorly along the urethral plate to form the
   medial aspect of the labia minora.
Urethroplasty
 1. Mobilise the urethra from the distal pendulous portion proximally.
 2. Discard the entire pendulous urethra and the distal bulbar urethra above the level of the adductor
    longus tendon.
 3. Elevate the bulbospongiosus muscle — the dissection resembles urethroplasty, tracking the muscle
    ventrally then laterally and posteriorly, but here the muscle is excised rather than retracted. Debulking
    reduces obstructing tissue at the vaginal introitus.
 4. Keep the bulbospongiosus intact until canal dissection is complete — it provides an additional layer of
    coverage in the rare instance of rectal injury. This is the reason for the sequencing.
 5. Spatulate the urethra ventrally and downward to allow a straight urinary stream in the sitting position.
 6. A 3 cm dorsal segment of bulbar urethra creates the mucosal vestibule of the vagina, secured as a flat
    plate to the inferior segment of the neoclitoris and laterally to the medial labia minora. Oversew the
    lateral edges of the urethra to reduce significant bleeding.
 7. Mature the urethra to the inverted penile tissue after confirming the opening is patent and the stream
    trajectory correct, assessed by passing a Foley catheter or a bougie dilator.
Neovaginal Canal Creation and the Vulvoplasty Decision
 • Thorough preoperative discussion of patient preferences is critical, because of the commitment to
   lifelong dilation.

 • Vulvoplasty — also termed shallow, limited or zero-depth vaginoplasty — creates the vulva but not the
   vaginal canal, and suits patients certain they do not wish to engage in receptive intercourse.
 • The decision is not freely reversible: preferences may change after surgery; vulvoplasty discards the
   scrotal skin that would have lined the canal; and later full-depth vaginoplasty is complicated by scarring
   of the perineal dissection and the need for additional graft tissue.
 • Vulvoplasty reduces risk of rectal or bladder injury and avoids the need for abdominal access for robotic
   or laparoscopic peritoneal flap harvest — relevant with multiple comorbidities.
 • Prior radical prostatectomy is an indication for vulvoplasty, since canal dissection may injure the
   external urinary sphincter and cause incontinence.
Canal Dissection
 1. Initiate along the urethra by division of the central tendon, then carry the dissection proximally toward
    the prostate. Canal dissection requires in-depth familiarity with perineal and pelvic anatomy.
 2. Avoid injury to the rectum and urethra. Drape so that digital rectal examination can be performed
    routinely to evaluate the thickness of tissue between the neovaginal canal and the rectum; a latex rectal
    probe cover inserted into the rectum and secured to the skin or drape allows easy repeated assessment.
 3. A Lowsley retractor may be used to manipulate the prostate and aid the dissection.
 4. Sharply dissect the bulbospongiosus muscle off the urethra.
 5. For combined abdominoperineal dissection, carry the dissection proximally to the base of the prostate
    for adequate depth; the proximal canal is then developed robotically for further depth.
 6. For a purely perineal approach, carry the dissection more proximally along the posterior aspect of the
    prostate; lighted retractors help.
 7. Preserve Denonvilliers fascia posteriorly. Violation exposes the perirectal fat, and the fascia should be
    closed where possible, especially if rectal examination shows thin tissue overlying the rectum.
 8. Control bleeding with judicious bipolar cautery or suture ligation; LigaSure may also help.
 9. Calibrate the space to confirm adequate depth and width.
 • If the prostatic capsule is violated — a recognised hazard as exposure becomes difficult — check for
   urethral injury by palpating for the catheter or by intraurethral irrigation, and anticipate significant
   bleeding from the prostatic stroma.
 • In vulvoplasty, the dissection is carried only to the membranous urethra to give the appearance of a
   vaginal canal.
 • Calibration reference: Soul Source GRS vaginal dilator #4 to the last dot — 22.9 cm long, 3.8 cm in
   diameter. Previous literature places vaginal depth at 10 to 15 cm, a figure now being revised upward with
   the abdominoperineal and robotic approaches.
Canal Lining
 1. Defat the scrotal skin and excise or fulgurate any remaining hair follicles to reduce regrowth.
 2. Tubularise the scrotal skin and suture it to the distal aspect of the penile skin, most easily over a dilator
    with a running absorbable suture.
 3. Make a midline incision on the proximal inverted penile skin to allow delivery of the clitoris and an
    uninterrupted path for urine flow from the urethra.
 4. Secure the penile skin to the inferior aspect of the urethra to prevent it recoiling upward.
 5. Pass the remaining inverted penile skin and tubularised scrotal skin into the canal. Graft contact with
    the surrounding canal tissue is crucial for graft take; in perineal vaginoplasty, vaginal packing is relied on
    to secure that contact.

 6. Secure the medial aspect of the labia majora to the lateral aspect of the labia minora, and carefully
    approximate the lateral labia majora to the defect left by the scrotal excision. Excise rugated scrotal
    tissue for better aesthetics.
 7. Secure the fat residual after the orchidectomy to give adequate bulk to the labia majora before skin
    closure.
 8. Close in multiple layers, with a layer of Scarpa's fascial closure before the skin; the labial edges are
    approximated with running sutures or interrupted mattress sutures.
 9. Keep the incision at least 1 cm away from the groin crease to reduce the chance of wound dehiscence.
10. Pack the canal with antibiotic-soaked vaginal packing and apply a negative pressure dressing.
Labiaplasty
 • Shape and volume depend on natal anatomy — the length of shaft skin and the prepubic adipose tissue
   affect labial appearance.
 • Labia minora, medial aspect: the distal penile skin mucosal collar, secured to the lateral aspect of the
   urethral plate that forms the vestibule. A running suture achieves haemostasis on the lateral side of the
   urethral plate, which otherwise bleeds significantly postoperatively.
 • The labia minora is accentuated with horizontal mattress quilting sutures to create a more pronounced
   ridge.
 • The labia majora is created from the lateral scrotal skin.
Concept — Penile inversion vaginoplasty is best held as a homology map rather than a list of steps: glans →
clitoris; preputial mucosal collar → clitoral hood and medial labia minora; dorsal bulbar urethral plate →
vestibule; penile shaft skin → canal lining; scrotal skin → full-thickness graft lining the canal; lateral scrotal
skin and peri-cord fat → labia majora. Every technical rule — preserve the dartos, spare the collar, leave the
fat, excise all erectile tissue — protects the blood supply, sensation or bulk of one transferred structure.

Abdominoperineal Approach
Peritoneal Vaginoplasty
 • Increasing availability of robotic surgery has made the abdominoperineal approach feasible. Often called
   the Davydov peritoneal vaginoplasty, the technique was originally described for vaginal agenesis in
   cisgender women and has since been adapted.
 • The external part of the procedure is identical to perineal vaginoplasty; only the proximal canal is
   created by abdominal dissection.
 • Rationale: historical rectal injury rates of approximately 7% during canal dissection, many attributable to
   poor exposure and visualisation. Creating the proximal canal robotically, in a dissection similar to
   radical prostatectomy, reduces the risk of rectal injury.
 • Critical differences from robotic prostatectomy:
     • Start by identifying the bilateral vas deferens and tracking them medially to identify the most
       proximal aspect of the prostate.
     • Be vigilant for organs that mimic the landmarks — the rectum can appear to mimic the seminal
       vesicles.
     • After elevating the seminal vesicles, carry the dissection distally along the posterior aspect of the
       prostate.
     • Minimise electrocautery to decrease the risk of rectal injury.
     • Sharply incise Denonvilliers fascia and enter the plane between its two leaflets. If the correct plane is
       established it is bloodless and develops distally with blunt dissection.

     • Connect the robotically dissected canal with the perineally dissected canal. Good communication is
       critical, as the two surgeons are often in slightly different planes, which is easily addressed if
       identified. Calibrate for adequate depth and width.
 • Peritoneal flap techniques — two named alternatives:
     • Two-flap technique: flaps raised from the posterior bladder wall (10 cm × 10 cm) and lateral to the
       sigmoid (10 cm × 10 cm) to augment the most proximal aspect of the canal. The tubularised penile
       and scrotal skin is passed up by the perineal surgeon and secured circumferentially to the peritoneum
       before the proximal-most canal is closed.
     • Peritoneal pull-through: a single large flap (11 cm across × 20 cm longitudinally) along the anterior
       abdominal wall, based on the bilateral deep inferior epigastric arteries, elevated and passed through
       the canal to the perineal surgeon, then secured to the introitus.
 • The proximal peritoneal defects must be closed by the robotic surgeon to minimise the risk of hernia
   formation.
Intestinal Vaginoplasty
 • Less commonly performed than penile inversion vaginoplasty. Principal indications: severe penoscrotal
   hypoplasia with insufficient scrotal tissue to line the canal, and canal stenosis in revision vaginoplasty.
 • Contraindications: inflammatory bowel disease, extensive abdominal adhesions, irradiated bowel, short
   gut, or a history of gastrointestinal cancers.
 • Cited advantages — vaginal lubrication, achievement of depth, potentially decreased risk of stenosis —
   have not been extensively studied, so providers must be cautious in offering the procedure as a routine
   option for primary vaginoplasty.
 • Disadvantages: injury to surrounding abdominal organs during bowel harvest; excessive mucus
   production causing difficulty with hygiene; diversion colitis; and neovaginal prolapse.
 • Technique: an isolated segment of colon or small intestine, through a combined abdominoperineal
   approach, the perineal portion as already described; the abdominal portion may be laparoscopic, robotic
   or open. Adequate mesenteric mobility is essential to pass the bowel into the pelvis without significant
   tension.
 • Evolving data with abdominoperineal approaches: improved visualisation of the proximal canal
   dissection has reduced rectal injury to 2.3%, against approximately 7% historically; urethral injury is
   1.1%.
 • Bottom line of the complication review: roughly one in eight intestinal vaginoplasties is removed
   because of postoperative complications, and the large majority require at least one reoperation such as
   introitoplasty.
 • Late hazards: bothersome mucocele, and later onset of inflammatory bowel disease.
 • Dilation remains essential after intestinal vaginoplasty — the vaginal introitus may still close.
Concept — The three lining options form an escalating ladder, each entered when the one before runs out of
tissue or depth: penile and scrotal skin first, peritoneum when more proximal depth is needed or exposure
demands a robot, and bowel only when there is not enough genital skin to line anything. Moving up the
ladder buys depth and lubrication but imports the donor organ's own pathology, and never removes the
obligation to dilate.

Common Complications and Incidence
Wound Dehiscence
 • Superficial wound dehiscence is the commonest minor complication after vaginoplasty, with wound
   complication rates of 12% to 33% in systematic review and possibly higher in clinical experience.
 • Most often at the vaginal introitus, possibly because of added tension from passing the vaginal dilators.
 • Managed with local wound care in most cases — dressings, general hygiene, and healing by secondary
   intention.
 • Granulation tissue within the wound causes bleeding and discharge, treated with curettage or
   cauterisation and topical steroids.
Rectovaginal Fistula
 • Potentially the most devastating complication, typically related to rectal injury during canal dissection.
 • Incidence 0.8% in primary surgery and 6.3% in revision cases. Revision must be approached with
   caution, as the altered anatomy raises the rate of rectal injury.
 • If rectal injury is recognised intraoperatively: close the rectum in a watertight fashion; cover the site
   with a bulbospongiosus muscle flap if available; and consider faecal diversion according to the degree
   of injury. This is why the bulbospongiosus is preserved until canal dissection is complete.
 • No consensus exists on the technique of repair.
 • The central dilemma: closing a fistula requires rest, but preventing stenosis requires dilation, and
   dilation traumatises the repair. Two strategies:
     • Stop dilation and allow the canal to stenose in anticipation that the fistula will close, then perform
       revision surgery for the stenosis. Drawback: later revision to widen and deepen the canal risks
       reopening the fistula.
     • Defer closure until the canal is sufficiently stable to maintain width and depth, so the repair can heal
       without dilation.
Urethrovaginal Fistula
 • Incidence 0.8–3.9%. Causes: unrecognised urethral injury during canal dissection, or distal urethral
   stenosis. The same balance between closure and dilation applies.
 • Location determines the symptom — the examinable pair: fistulas distal to the bladder neck cause
   vaginal voiding; fistulas at the bladder neck cause incontinence.
 • Distal fistulas often need no repair, as vaginal voiding is the only symptom.
 • A proximal fistula is repaired transvaginally, through mobilisation of the vaginal wall and prostatic
   urethra.
 • Tissue interposition may be difficult: the labia majora does not have the blood supply to allow a
   Martius flap, and the gracilis muscle may obstruct the vaginal introitus.
Urethral Stricture
 • Cited between 1% and 6%, with the highest study reporting 40%.
 • Presentation: dysuria, weak stream, haematuria, recurrent urinary tract infections, urinary spraying,
   split stream, or retention.
 • Workup should include functional aetiologies, for which pelvic floor physical therapy can be offered.
 • Meatal stenosis may be diagnosed on physical examination alone; more proximal pathology warrants
   full workup such as cystoscopy.

 • Dilation may be an appropriate first step for short obstruction, but reconstruction usually involves
   spatulation and maturation of the urethral meatus.
Urethral Bulge or Malposition
 • Inadequate debulking of the corpus spongiosum leads to a urethral bulge, which becomes more
   significant with genital arousal and may obstruct the vaginal canal and cause dyspareunia, pain and
   dysphoria.
 • Inadequate mobilisation or dissection of the spongiosum down to the crus of the cavernosa leads to an
   upward-pointing meatus, causing spraying over the edge of the toilet seat.
 • Treatment: debulking of the excess tissue and downward fixation of the malpositioned meatus, with
   closure in multiple layers, to give the stream an appropriate trajectory.
Vaginal Stenosis
 • Consequence: inability to engage in intercourse.
 • Causes: poor adherence to dilation, granulation or scar tissue formation, poor graft take, and pelvic
   floor dysfunction leading to inadequate depth of dilation. Stenosis may occur at the introitus or within
   the canal.
 • First step is pelvic floor physical therapy, to maximise canal depth and prevent future pelvic pain from
   dilation. Steroid cream or injection may be used but has not been well studied.
 • Surgery only after physical therapy has been completed: excision of the scar with graft use may restore
   calibre and depth; peritoneal flaps or intestinal augmentation are alternatives.
 • Emerging experience with allograft in revision surgery: the canal is dissected robotically, lined with
   allograft, and peritoneal flaps placed at the apex.
 • A newly dissected revision canal is treated exactly as a primary canal dissection — postoperative
   packing then dilation.
Vaginal Prolapse
 • Mechanistically different from prolapse in cisgender women. Not weakness of the pelvic floor, but poor
   graft take or poor fixation of skin flaps.
 • Reported incidence 4–10%, although the definition is not uniform and the data derive primarily from
   perineal approaches.
 • Incidence may be lower with peritoneal interposition and robotic surgery, because securing the
   proximal penile and scrotal skin to the peritoneum acts as a colpopexy. Fixation of the canal to
   surrounding pelvic muscles has been described, for example using a Capio device.
 • No standard treatment. Early postoperative prolapse may be repacked, but prolapse signifies poor graft
   take, and vaginal stenosis will often follow even if the canal is repacked.
Concept — Nearly every complication traces to one of three failures: a plane violated during canal dissection
(rectovaginal and urethrovaginal fistula), tissue inadequately excised or debulked (urethral bulge, erectile
engorgement, obstructing bulbospongiosus), or a graft that did not take or was not maintained (dehiscence,
stenosis, prolapse). Only the third is shared with the patient, and it is why dilation is counselled as surgical
rather than optional.

Long-Term Outcomes
 • Evidence on long-term function and satisfaction is scant, and until recently there was no transgender-
   specific patient-reported outcome measure — the principal methodological limitation.
 • Bottom line of the available series: the large majority report successful sexual arousal, normal genital
   function, and partner satisfaction with their genitalia.

 • Orgasmic function: the large majority are able to orgasm, with a median time to orgasm of 180 days —
   worth knowing because patients expect it sooner.
 • Neovaginal depth is reported at 12 to 16 cm (5 to 6.5 inches), against a typical cisgender vaginal depth
   of 9 to 12 cm (3.5 to 5 inches) — the neovagina is deeper, not shallower, than the cisgender norm.
 • Some patients report self-lubrication from their peritoneal segments, but surgeons should counsel that
   this is not the norm.
 • Setting appropriate expectations before surgery is crucial to postoperative satisfaction.
Concept — The outcome literature is weakest exactly where counselling matters most — function, lubrication

POSTOPERATIVE CARE

Immediate Perioperative Course
 • Counsel before surgery to expect approximately 5 days in hospital.
 • Maintain subcutaneous heparin, sequential compression devices, broad-spectrum antibiotics, an
   aggressive bowel regimen and intravenous fluids.
 • Sitting is permitted, but not prolonged periods with direct pressure over the perineum.
 • On postoperative day 5, the vaginal packing and the urinary catheter are removed.
 • Dilation teaching is given by a member of the team — appropriate technique, duration and signs of
   complications — together with a douching protocol after dilations to maintain hygiene.
 • Urinary spraying is common after the procedure and generally improves as the localised tissue oedema
   resolves.
 • If urinary retention occurs, replace the catheter. Dilation can be performed with the catheter in place,
   and the catheter is removed a week later in the outpatient setting.

Role of Physical Therapy
 • Establish care with a pelvic floor physical therapist before surgery.
 • Postoperatively, routine visits confirm that dilation is being performed appropriately.
 • Four to five postoperative visits are adequate for most patients.

Clinical Follow-up
 • Frequent follow-up addresses both physical recovery from major surgery and a host of new sentiments
   related to the genitalia, which generate logistical questions and a need for emotional support.
 • At each visit: physical examination of the external genitalia for healing, and speculum examination of
   the canal for depth and excessive granulation tissue.
 • Significant granulation tissue is removed with a curette or sharply excised; if persistent, low-dose steroid
   ointment placed on the tip of the dilator delivers treatment to the proximal canal.
 • For foul odour or concern for bacterial infection, an antibiotic gel is used in the same way.
 • Inadequate preoperative hair removal manifests here — regrowth of hair in the canal requiring
   intermittent removal to maintain hygiene.
 • Minor wound dehiscence, especially at the six o'clock position of the canal, is common and is
   successfully managed with local wound care.
 • Example protocol: weekly for the first 3 weeks, then 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 12 months, and
   annually thereafter.

Importance of Dilation
 • The neovaginal canal may take months to years to heal fully, and even then may be physiologically
   perceived by the body as a pelvic defect — hence a lifelong risk of canal stenosis if adequate dilation is
   not maintained.
 • An aggressive early dilation protocol is crucial to maintaining depth and calibre. Example: four times
   daily for 15 to 20 minutes for the first 6 weeks; three times daily for 15 to 20 minutes until 3 months;
   twice daily for 15 to 20 minutes from 3 months to 1 year; then once daily.
 • After 1 year the patient may dilate less frequently if depth and calibre are not being lost.
 • At any sign of canal stenosis, increase the frequency of dilation and contact the surgeon, as additional
   pelvic floor physical therapy may be warranted to regain depth and calibre.
Concept — The operation creates a space the body treats as a wound to be closed, so the canal is maintained
rather than healed — a lifelong mechanical obligation that tapers in frequency but never ends. This is why
non-adherence risk is assessed by mental health services before surgery, and why postoperative depression
counts as a surgical complication here.
