 Chapter 135 — Minimally Invasive Urinary Diversion
   • The share of radical cystectomies performed by a minimally invasive approach rose steadily in the
     early 2010s (National Cancer Database, NCDB).
   • Robot-assisted radical cystectomy (RARC) with lymph node dissection is now an established
     technique; oncological outcomes are safe and effective in both early and long-term follow-up.
   • The Quality Cystectomy Score (Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center) uses star-based criteria
     spanning neoadjuvant chemotherapy consultation → adequate lymph node yield → 30-day mortality.
   • Patients undergoing minimally invasive cystectomy were more likely to receive neoadjuvant
     chemotherapy (NCDB).
   • Oncological equivalence of RARC to open cystectomy has been validated in several randomised
     controlled trials (RCTs), including the landmark RAZOR trial — but in these trials the diversion was
     performed extracorporeally; the role of intracorporeal urinary diversion (ICUD) was not tested by
     them.
   • Laparoscopic urinary diversion dates from 1992; adoption was hindered by prolonged operative time,
     limited instrument manoeuvrability and a steep learning curve → hybrid procedures: laparoscopic
     extirpation and lymph node dissection, then diversion through a modified open approach.
   • Perceived advantages of the robot-assisted approach: less pain, minimal blood loss, earlier return of
     bowel function → quicker return to previous quality of life (QoL).
 Concept — Extirpation went robotic first and was proven equivalent; diversion lagged because it is the
 technically hardest, longest part, which is why the chapter's question is whether the reconstruction can
 also be done inside.

 DECLINE IN CONTINENT RESERVOIRS
   • Continent reservoirs have declined steadily since the early 2000s, most visibly in Europe and North
     America — a trend well under way before the robotic era.
   • Robot-assisted radical prostatectomy was adopted rapidly; RARC was adopted cautiously — cystectomy
     patients are complex, the operation is long, and it needs an advanced facility.
   • Early robotic adopters concentrated on extirpation and lymph node removal; diversion was neglected
     because of its technical complexity, learning curve and operative time.
   • International Robotic Cystectomy Consortium (IRCC): intracorporeal neobladders increased
     significantly over time and were performed more often with higher annual institutional RARC volume
     and in the more recent era (2016–2020 vs 2011–2015) — comfort evolves from extirpation to ICUD.
   • Landmark RCT of open versus complete intracorporeal cystectomy (iROC): only about one in nine (11%)
     diversions were continent, in both arms.
   • Core problem: no high-quality evidence that continent diversion gives better QoL than incontinent
     diversion; body image has not proved critical, and stoma appliances and education have been
     streamlined.
 Concept — The fall in continent diversion is not a robotic artefact: it predates robotics and is sustained by
 the absence of QoL evidence in favour of continent reservoirs.

 INCORPORATION OF ROBOT-ASSISTED APPROACH
   • NCDB 2010–2013: slower adoption by academic institutions, attributed to robot availability
     (cystectomy occupies the robot far longer than kidney or prostate surgery) and many surgeons with
     limited minimally invasive training.
   • Cystectomy volumes are low: modest median hospital volume, with individual surgeons averaging only
     a couple of cases per year.
   • Despite slow adoption, conversion to open surgery was rare.
   • Two readings: experience with robotic prostatectomy paved the way by showing how to reduce
     cystectomy morbidity; the downside is suboptimal oncological surgery during the learning curve and
     diversions below open-surgery standards.
   • Simplified operative steps are thought to produce a smaller-radius pouch → geometrically low-
     capacity, high-pressure reservoir.
   • IRCC: a multinational quality-assurance consortium. Early data: only a minority of RARCs were
     completely intracorporeal. Later: among robotic cystectomy-focused centres, ICUD rose from a small
     minority (2005) to nearly all cases (2016), an annual increase of about 11% — driven mainly by ileal
     conduit, and starting earlier in Europe than North America.
   • Unresolved: whether the robotic approach inclines surgeons toward ileal conduit at the expense of
     continent diversion. A high-volume single centre found surgeon preference explained incontinent
     diversion in only a small minority of patients eligible for continent diversion.

   KEY POINTS
    • Advantages of the robot-assisted approach: less pain, minimal blood loss, earlier return of bowel
      function.
    • Incorporation of ICUD has increased recently — annual increase of about 11%.
    • Ileal conduit is the commonest diversion performed robotically.

 Concept — Adoption tracks volume and experience: ICUD grew first where robotic cystectomy was
 concentrated, and first for the simplest diversion, the ileal conduit.

 PATIENT SELECTION
   • Selection has broadened as the learning curve flattened: high body mass index (BMI), locally advanced
     disease, older age and prior abdominal surgery are no longer exclusions.
   • Contraindication to the minimally invasive approach: reduced cardiac and pulmonary compliance →
     inability to tolerate steep Trendelenburg, especially with the prolonged operative time of ICUD.
     Mitigated by the da Vinci Xi system and AirSeal, which permit a less steep position.
   • Neobladder contraindications are the same as for open surgery.

 SURGEON CHOICE
   • Pasadena Consensus Panel best practice: focus on oncological outcomes first, then add reconstruction
     stepwise.
   • Sequence: RARC with extracorporeal diversion → intracorporeal ileal conduit (experience of about
     30–100 cases) → continent diversion (about 25–50 cases).
   • Once proficient, acceptable overall operative time: ≤4 hours with ileal conduit; ≤5 hours with
     neobladder.
 Concept — The learning pathway is oncology before reconstruction, conduit before reservoir.

 PREOPERATIVE CARE

 Enhanced Recovery After Surgery
   • Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways now have more than 20 components, tailored to
     cystectomy.
   • Recent modifications: early alvimopan, intraoperative goal-directed fluid therapy, immediate oral
     diet, reduced opioid use.
   • ERAS versus traditional pathway: less daily opioid, less ileus, shorter stay — but more pain.
   • Breakthrough analgesia shifted from opioid patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) and epidurals to high-
     dose paracetamol and/or ketorolac.
   • RARC with ICUD on ERAS: shorter stay, more likely to receive a neobladder, fewer gastrointestinal
     complications and fewer 90-day readmissions. ERAS participation and younger age independently
     predicted a stay of <10 days.
   • Meta-analysis: ERAS reduces complications and length of stay without changing overall readmission;
     integrated multidisciplinary pathways improve outcomes and satisfaction.
   • Caution: a stay of <5 days carried about a twofold risk of 90-day major complications — shortening
     stay is not an end in itself.
   • Post-discharge emphasis: trained nurse visits, and home attention to hydration, exercise and
     ambulation.

   KEY POINTS
    • Mechanical bowel preparation and oral antibiotics should be avoided.

 PATIENT POSITION AND PORT PLACEMENT
   • Set-up: general endotracheal anaesthesia → nasogastric tube and Foley catheter → lithotomy, arms
     adducted and padded, legs abducted and slightly lowered on spreader bars or stirrups → traditionally
     steep Trendelenburg.
   • da Vinci Xi integrates table motion, allowing repositioning without undocking → a less steep or even
     flat position.
   • Insufflation by Veress needle or Hasson technique (Hasson when there has been previous abdominal
     surgery).
   • Six- to seven-port transperitoneal approach; all ports after the camera placed under vision, in a more
     cephalad orientation — aids small-bowel manoeuvring during diversion and extended lymph node
     dissection to the aortic bifurcation.

 ANATOMIC CONSIDERATIONS IN MALES
   • Nerve sparing is more feasible because of anatomical studies and the robot's three-dimensional (3D)
     view, magnification and manoeuvrability in a narrow space.
   • Reserved for anatomically safe tumour location, lower tumour burden and adequate baseline sexual
     function; benefits both sexual function and neobladder continence.
   • Four danger points, in operative sequence (Fig. 135.2): (1) origin of nerves at the medial common iliac
     vessels and presacral area (extended lymph node dissection) → (2) high ligation of the bladder
     vascular pedicle (preserves the proximal neurovascular complex) → incision of peritoneum over the
     rectovesical pouch → (3) athermal seminal vesicle dissection after the vasal ampullae, sparing
     Denonvilliers fascia, where the pelvic plexus is concentrated; middle and lower seminal vesicle may be
     left → (4) proximal prostatic pedicles controlled close to the prostate: intrafascial, traction-free,
     athermal.
   • Complete lateral preservation (veil of Aphrodite) and its distal extent require apical dissection after the
     bladder is dropped.
   • Computer-assisted 3D reconstruction of the inferior hypogastric plexus has mapped the pelvic nerves
     serving continence and erection.
   • About one-third of the external urethral sphincter cross-section is laterally overlapped by the dorsal
     venous complex at the apex.
   • Dorso-cavernous anastomoses matter for cavernous haemodynamics (Doppler); anastomoses link
     dorsal, cavernous and bulbourethral arteries — all except the bulbourethral are contractile (muscular
     wall plus nerves).
   • Cavernous nerves converge close to the urethra at the sphincter before penetrating the pelvic floor.
   • Cadaveric study: the sphincter receives branches of the dorsal nerve of the penis (after it leaves the
     pudendal nerve), as close as 3 mm from the apex and unilateral in half → meticulous periurethral
     dissection.

 ANATOMIC CONSIDERATIONS IN FEMALES
   • Pelvic splanchnic nerves carry adrenergic and cholinergic fibres, mediating vaginal lubrication and
     genital swelling; injury reduces vaginal and vulval blood flow.

   • Inferior hypogastric plexus: from hypogastric and pelvic splanchnic nerves, retroperitoneal →
     anterolateral to rectum → lateral to cervix and vaginal fornix → lateral vaginal wall and bladder base,
     where it is most vulnerable.
   • Cavernous nerves arise along the lateral edge of the vagina, run in the vesicovaginal septum and pass
     through the urogenital diaphragm.
   • Continence can be preserved despite removal of the bladder neck and cranial third of the urethra;
     traction on the Foley balloon defines the bladder neck and landmarks.
   • The striated intrinsic sphincter lies mainly in the mid- to distal third of the urethra and can be
     preserved oncologically safely; preserve the periurethral complex and endopelvic fascia.
   • Urethral nerve branches run by the lateral vaginal wall → preserve tissue around the proximal urethra
     and lateral to the bladder neck; despite controversy, preserve innervation for continence.
   • With nerve-sparing goals: preserve the anterior vaginal wall; avoid transvaginal extraction → small
     Pfannenstiel incision.

 ILEAL CONDUIT

 Transfer of Left Ureter and Selection of Bowel
   • Left ureter crosses below the sigmoid mesentery and over the great vessels to the right.
   • Identify duplex ureters → implant separately or together according to calibre.
   • Short left ureter → retract sigmoid with the fourth arm; a 30-degree down lens helps free the proximal
     left ureter.
   • Segment: 12 cm of ileum, 15–20 cm proximal to the ileocaecal valve (the figure legend gives 12–15
     cm, about 15 cm from the valve).
   • Leave adequate length at the ileocaecal end → avoids metabolic disturbance and kinking once
     exteriorised.
   • Avoid a redundant segment → residual urine → stasis, infection, absorptive and metabolic
     complications, loss of renal function.

 The Marionette Stitch
   • Sequence: 60-inch 1-0 silk on a Keith needle through the hypogastrium → through the distal end of
     the segment (future stoma) → back out through the same site → held by an instrument, not tied.
   • Untied → free movement of the segment during conduit construction.

   • Compensates for fixed ports, brings each part of the conduit into the field, and lowers the segment into
     the pelvis to keep work within the optimal range of the robotic joints — valuable when working space
     is limited.

 Isolation of the Bowel Segment and Creation of the Ileal Conduit
   • Sequence: fourth arm holds proximal segment at stretch against the marionette → hook cautery
     incises mesenteric peritoneum (a vascular stapler is quicker) → two mesenteric windows → 45-mm
     Endo GIA divides bowel proximally and distally → cut ends held with 0-silk to prevent malrotation →
     bowel continuity not yet restored.
   • Keep orientation and avoid narrowing the conduit base, which limits mesenteric blood supply.
   • Conduit washout has been abandoned: no reduction in infection, and it causes spillage during stenting
     and anastomosis.

   • Sequence: two openings on either side of the proximal conduit → athermal, cold-scissors wide
     buttonhole enterotomy with serosal removal (adopted to reduce strictures) → marionette lowered →
     ureter implanted with minimal traction → after clear urine efflux, wide spatulation, fourth arm
     retracting the cut distal ureter → left side first.
   • First suture: conduit outside-in, then ureteric angle inside-out; conduit bite perpendicular to the
     proximal staple line → alignment, no malrotation or back-walling.
   • The operating position of the anastomosis is not the final lie of the conduit.
   • Interrupted sutures along both sides to halfway → stent; alternatively Van Velthoven double-armed
     4-0 Vicryl (about 5 cm) from the spatulation angle along both sides.
   • Stenting after the posterior wall: metal laparoscopic suction tube via 15-mm right assistant port into
     distal conduit → advanced to the anastomosis → held by needle driver → 90-cm 8.5-French (Fr) single-
     J stent over guidewire to renal pelvis → secured with 3-0 chromic → guidewire removed only after
     anchoring (no tactile feedback; stent otherwise hard to find).
   • Distal ureter excised completely for final histopathology; no routine frozen section.
   • Marionette turns the conduit → right side done identically. Stent ends drain via the 15-mm port and are
     not clamped to the drapes (dislodgement).
   • Peritoneal flap preserved during right periureteric dissection → covers and retroperitonealises the
     proximal anastomosis → less local inflammation.

 Restoration of the Bowel
   • Reanastomose bowel after both ureters → easier conduit handling; reanastomosis tailored to avoid
     kinking or tension.
   • Robot-sewn ileoileal anastomosis is feasible — short sewing time, no leaks, much cheaper than
     staplers.

    • The marionette stitch is not tied but held by an instrument, allowing free movement.

 Technique
 Configuration and Setup
   • 65 cm of ileum: 44 cm pouch, 16 cm chimney, 5 cm discarded (the discard allows ileoileal anastomosis

 Configuration and Setup

 Postoperative Care
   • Optimise fluid balance and avoid overhydration — fluid shifts in an elderly population.
   • No benefit from leaving a nasogastric tube (Cochrane meta-analysis).
   • Pelvic drainage preferred. Adequate drainage and ureteric stenting aid bowel recovery and reduce
     metabolic acidosis; stentless ureteroileal anastomosis for conduits is feasible.
   • Paediatric feeding tubes tied to the urethral catheter avoid cystoscopic removal; drawback — stents
     lost if the Foley falls out.
   • Neobladder saline irrigation every 8 hours → prevents mucus plugging and catheter blockage.

 Outcomes
   • ICUD aims to minimise bowel manipulation and reduce pain through minimal wound retraction and
     smaller incisions.
   • Enablers: standardised RARC and extended pelvic lymph node dissection with open-equivalent
     oncology, improved console skills, and EndoWrist articulation for suturing, far easier than conventional
     laparoscopy.
 Operative Time
   • Historically the major limitation of ICUD; the techniques in Table 135.1 take 305–420 minutes.
   • Prolonged cystectomy time → more complications and perioperative mortality, independent of stage
     or comorbidity → operative time is a quality measure for RARC.
   • Determinants: demographics and comorbidity, stage, complexity, technical modification, surgeon
     experience, hospital volume.
   • IRCC: operative time fell over the decade; the strongest determinant was diversion type — ileal
     conduit about 70 minutes shorter than neobladder.
   • Older, comorbid patients had shorter times (surgeons mindful of anaesthetic risk); women longer
     (hysterectomy, vaginal reconstruction); high BMI and prior surgery longer.
   • No significant difference between intracorporeal and extracorporeal diversion — undocking,
     repositioning and redocking for extracorporeal work absorbs the difference.
   • High-volume institutions faster. IRCC proficiency threshold exceeded the earlier estimate of 22 RARCs:
     surgeons with ≥66 RARCs saved about 55 minutes per conduit case → case for fellowship training and
     dedicated cystectomy programmes.
 Learning Curve
   • Intracorporeal neobladder: operative time and conversion risk fell substantially after only 10
     consecutive cases; early and late complications both fell with experience.
   • USC: a consistent console surgeon roughly halved operative time.
   • Mentoring reduced time and avoided conversion; conversion to open prolonged length of stay.
 Complications
   • Most complications are attributable to the diversion. Minimally invasive advantages: less bowel
     manipulation, fewer insensible losses, less analgesia.
   • Graded by modified Clavien-Dindo or modified Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC)
     systems, usually to 3 months.
   • Two-thirds of significant complications needing reoperation occur after 3 months (Roswell Park) →
     90-day reporting underestimates the burden.
   • IRCC over two decades: overall and high-grade complication rates unchanged; complicated patients
     more often had neobladders and reoperation, and more aggressive disease (higher node yield, variant
     histology, adjuvant therapy); wound complications fell.
   • Meta-analysis of RCTs: no difference in 90-day major complications, open versus robotic.

   • Most reoperations are diversion-related and late; in the authors' series about a quarter needed

   • The authors' intracorporeal conduit had higher stricture rates, rising over 5 years → abandoned the

   • Fistula is uncommon, about half repaired robotically; intervention for bowel obstruction rises with

   • Causes of intervention: parastomal and incisional hernia, adhesions, wound dehiscence.

 Functional Outcomes
 Continence after creation of neobladder.
   • Cystectomy abolishes the vesicourethral reflex → reservoir disconnected from sphincter, spinal reflex
     inactive → no desire to void.
   • Voiding cue: urine reaching posterior urethral sensory mechanisms (intact pudendal nerves). The
     urethrosphincteric guarding reflex contracts the sphincter → daytime continence.
   • At night, in deep sleep, the guarding reflex is inactive → continence depends on reservoir functional
     capacity.
   • Goal: adequate detubularisation and capacity → compliance, plus preservation of the sphincter
     complex.
   • Incontinence factors: age, reservoir volume and shape, functional urethral length, nerve supply.
   • Assess continence at 6–12 months, when capacity is maximal. Keep nocturnal urine output below
     functional capacity; night alarm until fullness sensation is recognised.

 Quality of Life
   • Evidence limited by absent preoperative baselines, short follow-up and non-validated tools.
   • Single-centre, patient-centred metrics to 24 months: physical domains decline to 6 months, then
     recover by 24 months in all domains except sexual function.
   • Earlier RCTs of open versus robotic cystectomy used open diversion. iROC (landmark multicentre RCT,
     intracorporeal versus open): worse QoL with open surgery at 5 weeks, greater disability at 5 and 12
     weeks; no difference after 12 weeks.
 Concept — ICUD's measurable gains are early and bowel-related (recovery, gastrointestinal
 complications, short-term QoL); long-term complications, function and QoL converge with open surgery,
 and the late burden is dominated by the diversion itself.

 Tissue Engineering
   • Aim: eliminate bowel from urinary reconstruction; regenerative attempts since 2007 — small intestinal
     submucosa seeded with 3T3 fibroblasts (rat); tubular polypropylene mesh scaffolds (patency and
     survival to about 16 weeks).
   • Neo-Urinary Conduit Trial in Humans (NCT01087687): small enrolment; engineered conduit survived
     about 250 days on average; half had stenosis and stricture.
   • Lessons: material stiff and non-collapsible, failed to generate cells around the connective-tissue sleeve,
     no blood supply.

 Robotic Bladder Auto Transplantation
   • USC stepwise preclinical models: porcine → pulsatile-perfused cadavers → heart-beating brain-dead
     human donors.
   • Technique: robotic retrieval of a vascularised composite bladder allograft → back-table vascular
     reconstruction → robotic transplantation; console time fell with experience; ICG fluorescence assessed
     graft perfusion; vascularity and mucosal integrity held for over 12 hours.
   • Could avoid bowel and its complications; immunosuppression is the major limitation.
 Concept — Both frontiers target the same root cause of diversion morbidity — bowel in the urinary tract;
 engineered conduits failed on vascularity, and transplantation trades bowel complications for
 immunosuppression.

 FUTURE DIRECTION
   • With RARC and lymph node dissection safely established, ICUD is performed worldwide.
   • Incorporate ICUD stepwise: ileal conduit → neobladder.
   • Understanding the anatomical, pathophysiological and technical nuances prevents long-term
     complications and upper-tract failure.
   • ICUD's permanent place depends on operative time and on optimised neobladder function → less
     morbidity, quicker return of QoL.
