HYPOTHALAMIC-PITUITARY-GONADAL AXIS
 • The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis governs four processes, in development and adulthood:
   phenotypic sex development in embryogenesis → sexual maturation at puberty → testis endocrine
   function (testosterone) → testis exocrine function (sperm).

 • Signalling is hierarchically governed by a free-running hypothalamic pulse generator; the amplitude and
   frequency of secretion determine downstream organ responsiveness.
 • Feedback control is the principal regulatory mechanism — a hormone may regulate its own synthesis and
   action or another's; negative feedback minimises perturbation and maintains homeostasis.
Concept — the axis is read by its rhythm as much as its level: pulse frequency and negative feedback, not
absolute hormone quantity, set downstream responsiveness.

Components of the Reproductive Axis
Hypothalamus
 • Integrative centre of the axis: input from amygdala, thalamus, pons, retina, olfactory cortex and other
   areas; it is the pulse generator for cyclic pituitary secretion.
 • Linked to the pituitary by neuronal pathways and a portal vascular system; the portal system bypasses
   the systemic circulation, delivering hypothalamic hormones directly to the anterior pituitary.
 • Gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH): 10-amino-acid peptide from neurones of the preoptic and
   arcuate nuclei; its only known function is to stimulate LH and FSH secretion.
 • Plasma half-life about 5–7 minutes; almost entirely removed on first pass through the pituitary (receptor
   internalisation or enzymatic degradation).
 • GnRH output integrates stress, exercise and diet from higher centres, pituitary gonadotrophins and
   circulating gonadal hormones (Table 67.1).
 • Kallmann syndrome: congenital hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism — GnRH precursor neurones fail to
   migrate → absent hypothalamic GnRH → delayed puberty or infertility from absent testosterone
   production.

 • Three rhythms: seasonal (months; peaks in spring), circadian (higher testosterone in the early morning),
   pulsatile (peaks every 90–120 minutes).
 • Pulsatility is essential: exogenous GnRH agonists (e.g. leuprolide acetate) stop testicular testosterone
   production by converting pituitary GnRH exposure from cyclic to constant.

 • Pituitary lies within the bony sella turcica.
 • GnRH stimulates FSH and LH production and release by a calcium flux-dependent mechanism;
   gonadotroph sensitivity to GnRH varies with age and hormonal status.
 • LH and FSH are glycoproteins of two subunits, α and β, each coded by a separate gene. The α subunit is
   identical between them and similar to other pituitary glycoprotein hormones; the unique β subunit
   confers biological and immunological specificity. Both subunits are required for activity.
 • Their oligosaccharide side-chains (sialic acid residues) differ in content and likely account for different
   plasma clearance rates.
 • Both act only in the gonads, via adenylate cyclase → intracellular cAMP.

 • Prolactin: globular protein of 199 amino acids (23 kDa); milk synthesis in pregnancy and lactation; no
   human mutations known in the prolactin gene or its receptor.
 • Male role less clear: may increase Leydig cell LH receptors and sustain high intratesticular testosterone;
   may potentiate androgen effects on accessory sex glands; normal levels may be needed for libido.
 • Low prolactin is not necessarily pathological; hyperprolactinaemia abolishes gonadotrophin pulsatility
   by interfering with episodic GnRH release.
 • ACTH, GH and TSH from the anterior pituitary can also significantly affect male reproduction.
Testis
 • Virility and fertility need the endocrine testis (interstitial Leydig cells — steroidogenesis) and the exocrine
   testis (seminiferous tubules — spermatozoa) working together.
 • Testosterone production is about 5 mg/day, secreted in a damped, irregular, pulsatile (nyctohemeral)
   pattern.

Development of the Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Gonadal Axis
 • Sex determination is genetic. SRY (sex-determining region Y) on the short arm of the Y chromosome
   encodes a protein with a high mobility group (HMG) box — a conserved DNA-binding motif that kinks
   DNA → altered gene expression → testis formation → male phenotype.
 • SRY does not act alone. DAX1 (dosage-sensitive sex reversal, adrenal hypoplasia critical region, on
   chromosome X, gene 1), a nuclear hormone receptor gene, is needed for proper testis development, yet
   depending on timing can suppress SRY by inhibiting its downstream testis genes.
 • WNT4 (wingless-type integration site family member 4), largely confined to the adult ovary, also behaves
   as an "antitestis" gene.
 • The female pathway is no longer regarded as the passive SRY-negative "default": WNT4 and DAX1 can
   proactively induce female gonadal development even in the presence of SRY.

 • Deficiencies in these pathways produce birth defects or intersex disorders.
 • Feedback relationships are established during gestation; kisspeptin helps activate GnRH neurones and
   trigger GnRH release; steroidogenic factor 1 (SF-1), an orphan nuclear receptor from developing Sertoli
   cells, contributes to axis development.
Postnatal sequence: placental steroid withdrawal at birth → neonatal high gonadotrophin secretion → axis
sensitivity rises → FSH and LH fall to low childhood levels → puberty begins with GnRH pulsing
(hypothalamic capacity usually from about the 12th year) → gonadotrophins reach adult levels → sex
hormones rise.
 • Puberty begins at critical growth, weight and nutritional rates; likely initiated by kisspeptin, melatonin
   and leptin. Leptin (adipocyte hormone) signals fat-store size and modulates hypothalamic and pituitary
   activity.
Concept — male development is built hormone by hormone — testosterone the ducts, DHT the external
genitalia, MIS the removal of Müllerian structures — and sex determination itself is a contest between pro-
testis SRY and active anti-testis genes, not a default.

Aging and the Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Gonadal Axis
 • Progressive decline in testosterone and sperm production: mean plasma testosterone in the seventh
   decade is about one-third lower than in young men.
 • Terms: male menopause, male climacteric, andropause; more appropriately partial androgen deficiency
   in the aging male (PADAM).
 • Seminiferous epithelium: decreased tubule volume and length; lower sperm output from decreased
   germ cell proliferation, not increased degeneration; FSH rises (about threefold).

 • Causes are multifactorial: fewer Leydig cells and more testosterone-binding proteins; loss of diurnal
   variation; blunted feedback to low testosterone (despite generally high gonadotrophins) and to GnRH;
   pulsatile GnRH replaced by irregular, less effective pulses.
Concept — ageing is a double failure — peripheral (fewer Leydig cells, reduced proliferation, rising FSH) and
central (blunted feedback, disordered pulsatility) — so neither compartment alone explains the decline.

 KEY POINTS: HYPOTHALAMIC-PITUITARY-GONADAL AXIS
   • Normal testosterone and sperm production depend on pulsatile secretion of hypothalamic GnRH and
     pituitary LH and FSH.
   • Regulation of HPG hormones occurs primarily through negative feedback.
   • Maleness derives from SRY on the Y chromosome; WNT4 and DAX1 are antitestis genes that can
     proactively induce female gonadal development.
   • With paternal age: lower testosterone, blunted feedback, irregular pulsatility.


TESTIS
Gross Architecture
 • White, ovoid; 15–25 mL volume; 4.5–5.1 cm long.
 • Tunica albuginea contains smooth muscle within collagen: may give the capsule contractility, affect
   testicular blood flow and promote outflow of seminiferous tubule fluid.
 • Septa divide the parenchyma into lobes, each with a centrifugal artery. Tubules harbour developing germ
   cells.
 • Interstitial tissue: Leydig cells, mast cells, macrophages, nerves, blood and lymph vessels — 20–30% of
   testicular volume.
 • Seminiferous tubules are long, coiled and looped, both ends terminating in the rete testis; 600–1200
   tubules, combined length about 250 m.
 • The rete testis (the "hub") coalesces into 6–12 ductuli efferentes → caput epididymis.

 • Countercurrent exchange in the pampiniform plexus (counterflowing artery and veins) exchanges heat
   and small molecules — e.g. testosterone diffuses passively from vein to artery (concentration-limited).
 • Arterial blood reaching the testis is 2–4 °C below rectal temperature; loss of this differential is associated
   with testicular dysfunction in varicocele and cryptorchidism.
 • Varicocele surgery relevance: arteries in the spermatic cord — single in about half of cords, two in about
   30%, three in about 20%.
 • Below the pampiniform plexus near the mediastinum the artery is highly coiled and branches before
   entry; at the testis a single artery most often enters, otherwise two or more branches (angiography).
 • Collaterals, especially internal spermatic–deferential, maintain viability after division of the internal
   spermatic artery; but where there is a single testicular artery, its interruption can cause atrophy.

Intratesticular course: arteries pierce the tunica albuginea → run inferiorly along the posterior surface
within the parenchyma → branches pass anteriorly over the parenchyma; major branches also cross the
inferior pole and fan over the anterior surface. These vessels are at risk at orchidopexy, testis biopsy and
sperm retrieval; the midsection has relatively fewer vessels than superior or inferior areas.
 • Centrifugal arteries run in the septa → arterioles → two capillary beds, stated as a pair: intertubular (in
   the columns of interstitial tissue) versus peritubular (ladder-like, alongside the tubules).
 • Testicular blood flow about 9 mL/100 g/min.
 • Intratesticular veins do not run with the arteries: parenchymal veins drain to surface veins or to a
   mediastinal group along the rete; these join deferential veins to form the pampiniform plexus, whose thin
   walls favour diffusion of testosterone and heat to the gonadal artery.
 • No known somatic innervation; autonomic supply from the intermesenteric nerves and renal plexus
   along the testicular artery. Adrenergic fibres are restricted mainly to small vessels supplying Leydig cell
   clusters and may regulate steroidogenesis.
 • Vascular tone is regulated at several levels — capsular artery autoregulation, regional metabolic control by
   peptides such as atrial natriuretic peptide, and assisted transendothelial transport of LH — a highly
   specialised microvasculature.
 • Prominent lymphatics in the cord; their obstruction dilates the interstitium but not the tubules (the
   interstitium is lymph-drained) and causes hydrocele — a known complication of varicocelectomy and
   herniorrhaphy.
 • Intratubular fluid bathes Sertoli cells, flows tubule → rete testis → caput; isosmotic with plasma, mainly
   of tubular origin; its reabsorption in the rete and efferent ductules is regulated by oestrogens. Its
   composition differs markedly from plasma and lymph → basis of the blood–testis barrier.
Concept — the testis is engineered to run cool and sealed: countercurrent pampiniform exchange keeps it 2–4
°C below core and the tubules keep their own fluid; varicocele, cryptorchidism, post-varicocelectomy
hydrocele and atrophy after single-artery ligation are each failures of that engineering.

Testis Cytoarchitecture
Interstitium
Leydig Cells
 • Interstitium contains blood vessels, lymphatics, fibroblasts, macrophages, mast cells and Leydig cells,
   which produce the bulk of testicular steroid.
 • Leydig cells differentiate from mesenchymal precursors by the seventh week of gestation; activation of
   steroidogenesis coincides with androgen-dependent differentiation of the reproductive tract.
 • Differentiation is driven by LH and placental human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG) and local paracrine
   factors such as insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1).
 • By 3 months after birth, a second wave of Leydig differentiation in response to pituitary LH briefly
   elevates testosterone; neonatal androgen imprints the hypothalamus, liver and prostate for appropriate
   later androgen response.
 • After pubertal reactivation, a young adult testis contains about 700 million Leydig cells.
Testosterone
 • Testosterone, synthesised from cholesterol, is the principal testicular steroid; numerous C18, C19 and C21
   steroids are also produced.

Seminiferous Tubules
 • A unique environment for gamete production: support cells (Sertoli cells; fibrocytes and myoid cells of the
   basement membrane) and germ cells (slowly dividing stem cells → rapidly proliferating spermatogonia
   and spermatocytes → metamorphosing spermatids).
Sertoli Cells
 • Line the tubules, resting on the basement membrane with cytoplasmic ramifications to the lumen;
   irregular nuclei, prominent nucleoli, low mitotic index.
 • Tight junctions between adjacent Sertoli cells — the strongest intercellular barriers in the body — divide
   the tubule into basal and adluminal compartments, the basis of the blood–testis barrier and of an
   immunologically privileged site.
 • Sertoli cells are nurse cells: germ cells are nourished within and between their cytoplasmic projections; a
   polarised epithelium whose base faces the plasma environment and whose apex holds the unique tubular
   environment.

 • Nurturing functions: specialised adluminal microenvironment; gap junctions with germ cells; migration

Testis Stem Cell Migration, Renewal, and Proliferation
Testis Stem Cell Migration
Primordial germ cells migrate to the gonadal ridge → associate with Sertoli cells in primitive cords → called
gonocytes once the gonad forms seminiferous cords → called spermatogonia after migrating to the tubule
periphery.
 • Early migrating germ cells resemble embryonic stem cells and are likely the source of adult germ cell
   tumours.
 • Failure to migrate into the primitive testis is thought to cause extragonadal germ cell tumours and
   azoospermia with Sertoli cell–only histology.
Testis Stem Cell Renewal
 • Spermatogonia in the stem cell niche are replenished (stem cell renewal) via the kit ligand/c-kit system
   and the niche factor glial cell line–derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF).
 • Human spermatogonial stem cells from adult biopsies can be reprogrammed in vitro to embryonic-like
   stem cells: express pluripotency markers (octamer-binding transcription factor 4 [OCT-4], sex-determining
   region Y-box 2 [SOX-2], STELLAR, growth differentiation factor 3 [GDF-3]), form all three germ layers, keep
   a normal karyotype, form teratomas, and express epigenetic markers and telomerase → possible patient-
   specific cell therapy.
 • Figure 67.15 legend: colonies grown under human embryonic stem cell conditions show the cobblestone
   appearance of embryonic stem cells and are functionally multipotent.
Testis Stem Cell Proliferation
 • Ap spermatogonia in the basal niche divide every 16 days to form B spermatogonia, which are
   committed to become spermatocytes.
 • Daughter cells remain joined by cytoplasmic bridges, seen between germ cells of all classes — probably
   for synchronised proliferation, differentiation and gene regulation.
Testis Stem Cell Aging or Senescence
 • Renewal continues from puberty to the eighth decade — reproductive longevity at a cost:
 • (1) Paternal age effect (PAE) disorders — gamete quality control declines, more disease in offspring.
 • (2) "Selfish spermatogonia" — non-random mutations in the fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 and 3
   (FGFR2, FGFR3) RAS-receptor pathways give clonal advantage; one consequence is oncogenesis — the
   basis of spermatocytic seminoma in men of advanced reproductive age.
Meiosis
 • Somatic cells replicate by mitosis (DNA duplication → nuclear envelope breakdown → equal division;
   identical daughters); germ cells by meiosis, which generates genetic diversity for natural selection.
 • Essential difference: after a single DNA duplication, meiosis divides twice, so a diploid (2n) parent yields
   haploid (n) gametes.

Epididymal Function
 • Variation in anatomy and histology suggests several functional tissues; epididymal passage produces

Sperm Transport
 • Transit takes 2–12 days; caput–corpus transit ≈ cauda transit; related to daily testicular sperm production,
   not age or ejaculation frequency.
 • Sexual frequency does not alter caput and corpus transit, but recent emissions markedly shorten cauda
   transit.
Mechanism (sperm are immotile on entry and remain so in the caput): rete testis fluid carries sperm into the
ductuli → flow drawn on by fluid resorption by ductal epithelium, mediated by the oestrogen receptor →
motile cilia and myoid contraction in the ductuli → within the epididymis proper, spontaneous rhythmic
contraction of the duct's contractile cells — the principal mechanism.
Sperm Storage
 • Sperm are held in the cauda for variable periods depending on sexual activity; in men under 55, each
   epididymis holds 155–209 million sperm, about half in the cauda.
 • Cauda sperm, unlike testicular sperm, show progressive motility, chemotaxis to follicular fluid and
   fertilising ability.
 • Animal sperm stay viable several weeks after vasal ligation, but fertility declines with prolonged storage;
   in humans, extended transit and storage may raise DNA fragmentation and reduce fertility.

Sperm Fertility
 • Testicular sperm cannot fertilise unless injected by micromanipulation.

   cyclase, altered phospholipid fatty acids.

DUCTUS (VAS) DEFERENS
Gross Architecture
 • Derived from the mesonephric (Wolffian) duct; 30–35 cm long, from the cauda epididymis to the
   ejaculatory duct, medial to the seminal vesicle and posterior to the prostate.
 • Five regions: sheathless epididymal segment (within tunica vaginalis) → scrotal → inguinal →
   retroperitoneal (pelvic) → ampulla.
 • Layers: outer adventitia (vessels, small nerves) → muscular coat: inner longitudinal, middle circular,
   outer longitudinal → mucosa with epithelium.
 • Outer diameter 1.5–3 mm; unobstructed lumen 200–700 µm.
 • Blood supply: deferential artery, a branch of the superior vesical artery; venous drainage corresponds.
 • Innervation: sympathetic and parasympathetic; cholinergic supply unimportant for motor activity. Rich
   adrenergic supply from the hypogastric nerve via the presacral nerve, in all three muscle layers, densest
   in the outer longitudinal layer; short adrenergic neurones; abundant purinergic receptors →
   sympathetic–purinergic cotransmission in transport and ejaculation.
 • Neurones with neuropeptide Y, enkephalin, galanin, somatostatin, vasoactive intestinal polypeptide and
   nitric oxide are present; roles unclear.
 • At vasovasostomy, testicular-side segments show reduced noradrenergic and secretomotor nerve
   density compared with abdominal segments — may affect transport and reversal success.

 • Structural defects occur in every compartment. Head shape abnormalities are categorised by the strict
   (Kruger) classification.
 • Most head abnormalities do not reflect sperm DNA quality — exceptions: headless (pinhead) and
   multiple-tailed (polyploid) sperm.
 • Globozoospermia (acrosomeless, round-headed): severe head phenotype; usually no DNA payload
   problem, but causes infertility and failed fertilisation with IVF and even ICSI; a recently identified cause is
   dysfunction of phospholipase C zeta (PLCζ), the calcium-signalling oocyte-activation factor essential for
   fertilisation.
 • Midpiece defects are uncommon in fertile men: bent neck (neck and tail at an angle >90°), asymmetrical
   midpiece insertion, thick, thin or irregular midpiece.

Concept — motility lives in the axoneme, so tail defects are mostly genetic and bypassed by ICSI; head defects
matter chiefly where they signal a DNA problem (pinhead, polyploid) or a failure of oocyte activation
(globozoospermia, PLCζ).

 KEY POINTS: SPERM
   • Sperm are ciliated cells with a "9 + 2" axoneme that enables motility.
   • Motility depends on several hundred proteins — 200–300 in the axoneme, about 1000 across the
     whole tail.
   • Motility defects — ciliary dyskinesias or MMAF — typically cause motility <10% and are
     predominantly genetic.
   • Sperm mtDNA is a circular, histone- and intron-free ring encoding respiratory complex proteins and is
     highly mutation-prone.


SUMMARY
 • GnRH, LH and FSH drive spermatogenesis; perturbation is a common cause of male infertility.
 • Production is optimal 2–4 °C below body temperature, takes about 64 days, is constant through cycles
   and waves, and needs very high intratesticular testosterone.
 • Sperm leave the testis immotile and largely unable to fertilise; epididymal transit makes them motile and
   fertile; the ejaculate feeds, protects (antioxidant) and bars later gametes.
 • Advanced paternal age brings genetic, epigenetic and fragmentation changes linked to infertility and
   offspring disease.
