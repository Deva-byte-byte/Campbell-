HISTORY OF MINIMALLY INVASIVE SURGERY IN CHILDREN
 • First paediatric urological laparoscopy was diagnostic, for the non-palpable testis (1976): it confirmed
   presence or absence of the testis, avoided open exploration and changed the management of
   cryptorchidism. Initially resisted, it irreversibly changed paediatric urology.
 • 1990s, extirpative surgery: laparoscopic nephrectomy was established in adults, then reported in children
   (1994). Paediatric uptake was slower because of port size and ergonomics.
 • Laparoscopic pyeloplasty was the first reconstructive minimally invasive surgery (MIS) in children
   (1995). Early cases were long because laparoscopic suturing is difficult; as exposure grew, adoption
   widened and indications expanded.
 • The da Vinci platform was approved for adult urology in 2000; the first paediatric robotic procedure
   followed in 2002. It corrected the ergonomic and technical difficulties of pure laparoscopy, and robotic
   pyeloplasty and ureteric reimplantation are now routine, with increasingly complex reconstruction.
 • Sequence: diagnostic (1976) → extirpative (1994) → reconstructive (1995) → robotic (2002).
Concept — Each stage was gated by the same bottleneck, intracorporeal suturing and ergonomics; the robot
removed that bottleneck, which is why reconstruction, not extirpation, is where paediatric robotics has
grown.

Veress technique
 • Site: the umbilicus in children. If entry fails or prior scarring raises concern, use Palmer's point: left upper
   quadrant, midclavicular line, about 3 fingerbreadths below the costal margin.
 • Confirmation: aspirate first, to exclude a vessel or hollow viscus → inject saline, which should drop
   immediately; if it does not, the needle is malpositioned or the patient insufficiently relaxed → connect
   insufflation and monitor opening pressure.

 • Right common iliac vein injury is reported with Veress entry because it lies near the umbilicus.

 KEY POINTS: ACCESS
   • Open (Hasson) access gives direct visualisation and port placement into the peritoneum before
     insufflation.
   • Closed (Veress) access uses a spring-loaded needle to enter and insufflate before port placement.
   • The right common iliac vein is the vessel most commonly injured during Veress placement.
   • In children there are no data showing superiority or lower risk of either modality.

Concept — The two techniques trade direct vision against speed, yet outcomes do not differ; what protects
the patient is confirmation of intraperitoneal position (aspiration, saline drop, low opening pressure), not
the choice of method.

PHYSIOLOGY OF PNEUMOPERITONEUM
 • Working space is made by filling potential space with inert gas; raised intra-abdominal pressure (IAP)
   causes changes absent in open surgery.
 • Routine MIS raises IAP to 12–15 mm Hg, with haemodynamic, intracranial, pulmonary and renal effects. In
   children these are minimised by keeping IAP below 12 mm Hg, and lower pressures (about 7 mm Hg) are
   feasible and safe.

Cardiovascular Changes
 • Insufflation above 10 mm Hg decreases venous return and so cardiac index; lower pressures increase
   venous return and cardiac index.
 • Catecholamine release with renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) activation causes
   vasoconstriction, raising mean arterial pressure and systemic vascular resistance.

 • Management: tell the anaesthetic team when insufflation starts → if unstable, desufflate, then

Pulmonary Changes
 • Pressure on the diaphragm reduces pulmonary excursion; cephalad lung displacement raises peak airway
   pressure.
 • CO₂ is the gas of choice — non-combustible, soluble and excreted by respiration. Greater absorption
   needs higher minute ventilation; with lung disease it can cause hypercarbia, arrhythmia and acidosis.
 • Infants and small children are affected most: IAP up to 15 mm Hg reduces tidal volume and compliance.
   Trendelenburg for pelvic surgery worsens this, especially with cardiopulmonary comorbidity.

Intracranial Pressure
 • Hypercarbia raises cerebral blood flow, and intracranial blood flow can rise even when CO₂ is controlled
   (mechanism unclear) — relevant to prolonged Trendelenburg and ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunts.

Renal Effects
 • Raised IAP reduces renal perfusion and oxygenation. Rat: IAP of 15 mm Hg or more significantly reduces
   renal perfusion, with an inverse IAP–oxygenation relationship. Swine renal-insufficiency model (20 mm
   Hg for 6 hours): reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR), urine output and renal blood flow immediately,
   but GFR at baseline by 1 week — the effect is temporary.
 • Children, flank near-infrared spectroscopy: age-appropriate pressures do not significantly reduce renal
   perfusion or urine output.

 • Intraoperative and early postoperative oliguria is most pronounced under 1 year, from reduced renal

   resuscitation.

   • Insufflation pressures even below 10 mm Hg may affect cardiac preload and afterload, with
     implications in complex cardiac anatomy.

Robotic Technology
 • First human robotic trial: 1997, "Mona" (after the Mona Lisa; Intuitive Surgical). The bedside assistant
   held the camera, but wristed, exchangeable instruments preserved the sterile field — the first
   generation.
 • da Vinci is the most widely adopted, with up to four independent arms.

PATIENT SELECTION
 • Individual risk–benefit assessment, including the surgeon's ability and experience; anaesthetic risk and
   overall health are usually the primary drivers.
 • Significant cardiac or pulmonary pathology: evaluation by the primary physician and discussion with the
   anaesthetist about tolerance of pneumoperitoneum.
 • Learning-curve progression: healthy children → unoperated abdomens → larger children → only then
   complex procedures in medically complex patients.

 KEY POINTS: PATIENT SELECTION
   • The only strict contraindication to MIS is inability to tolerate pneumoperitoneum.
   • Significant cardiopulmonary comorbidity requires preoperative evaluation and optimisation with other
     specialists and anaesthetists.

Concept — Selection is physiological, not anatomical: tolerance of pneumoperitoneum decides candidacy,
not age, size or diagnosis.

INFANT ROBOTIC SURGERY
 • Age and size are not contraindications; the infant problem is working space — about 1 L at 1 year versus
   6 L in an adult.
 • Robotic adoption is greatest in adolescents; most infant pyeloplasties remain open, though robotic use is
   rising.
 • Visualisation aids: limit bowel distension by minimising inhalational anaesthetic; preoperative enema;
   intraoperative rectal tube to decompress flatus.
 • Infant robotic pyeloplasty is safe, feasible and equivalent; against matched open cohorts it gives shorter
   operative time and stay and less narcotic. Deterrents: small workspace, arm collisions, reduced range of
   motion, learning curve.

 KEY POINTS: INFANT ROBOTIC SURGERY
   • Minimising inhalational anaesthetic decreases bowel distension and aids visualisation in the small
     abdomen.

Concept — Every infant difficulty follows from one number — a working volume about a sixth of the adult's
— so every adjunct is an attempt to recover space.

 • In adults, transmesenteric pyeloplasty shortened operative time and stay with equivalent function; it is
   considered advantageous in children when feasible.

Retroperitoneal Approach
 • Traditional for open urology. Versus open renal surgery: shorter stay, equivalent outcomes — useful in
   peritoneal dialysis, avoiding dialysis delay from peritoneal violation.
 • Systematic review of transperitoneal versus retroperitoneal laparoscopic nephrectomy: no significant
   advantage to either; major complications rare. Used selectively; specific mentorship advised.
 • Retroperitoneal lymph node dissection is reported for paratesticular rhabdomyosarcoma and non-
   seminomatous germ cell tumours.

Vesicoscopic Approach
 • First described 2005 for cross-trigonal reimplantation: 5-mm camera port through the bladder dome
   under cystoscopic vision → two small working ports through the bladder wall → urethra occluded →
   bladder insufflated. Outcomes equivalent to other approaches.
 • Robotic vesicoscopy is described and may grow, but the 8-mm ports of the Si/Xi limit it.
Concept — The approaches trade working space against the peritoneum: transperitoneal buys space and
familiarity by entering it; retroperitoneal and vesicoscopic preserve it at the cost of space — a trade that
matters most where the peritoneum is needed, as in peritoneal dialysis.

BENEFITS OF MINIMALLY INVASIVE SURGERY
 • Paediatric MIS benefits remain debated despite widespread robotic use.

Pain
 • Smaller ports are believed to reduce pain. Robotic versus open pyeloplasty: one study found less narcotic
   use, another no difference.
 • No randomised trials; most supportive data come from adult robotic versus open prostatectomy. Opioid-
   free analgesia is feasible after robotic prostatectomy, but no paediatric MIS data exist.

Admission / Length of Stay
 • Length of stay (LOS) is a surrogate for recovery and pain; enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) is a
   separate field, but approach also shapes convalescence.
 • Pyeloplasty meta-analyses: MIS shorter than open, and robotic shorter than both laparoscopic and
   open. Ureteric reimplantation meta-analysis: shorter LOS with MIS.
 • Gains are in hours for pyeloplasty and reimplantation, but days for robotic Mitrofanoff and augmentation
   cystoplasty, despite longer operative times. MIS is a recommended consideration in paediatric urology
   ERAS.

Cosmesis
 • Secondary, but scars last for life. Incisions under 14.8 mm contract; larger ones enlarge — favouring ports
   over open incisions, which usually exceed 20 mm.
 • Pursuit of fewer scars: smallest robotic ports; "scarless" laparoscopy through stab incisions without
   ports.
 • Parents are more satisfied after robotic than open pyeloplasty.

THREE- VERSUS FOUR-PORT COSMESIS

 Prefer three ports                                         No difference in scarring

 • Single-site laparoscopy improves cosmesis but is adopted slowly for technical difficulty; relocating ports,
   as in HIdES, improves satisfaction.

Pelvic Access
 • As robotic prostatectomy showed, the robot has revolutionised surgery in the confined pelvis; it is
   feasible and potentially advantageous for prostatic utricle, urogenital sinus, bladder neck reconstruction
   and distal ureteric pathology.
Concept — The benefits measured objectively — length of stay and scar — are consistent; the benefit most
often assumed, pain, is the least proven and largely borrowed from adults.

DRAWBACKS OF MINIMALLY INVASIVE SURGERY
 • Cost: a da Vinci Xi with dual consoles exceeds US$2 million, plus annual service — potentially prohibitive
   for a paediatric hospital; competition may lower prices.

 KEY POINTS: ADVANTAGES AND DRAWBACKS OF MINIMALLY INVASIVE SURGERY
   • Smaller ports may help perioperative and postoperative pain control.
   • MIS is associated with decreased LOS.
   • Cosmesis is improved compared with open incisions.
   • MIS may help in hard-to-access areas such as the deep pelvis.
   • Robotic surgery costs more than laparoscopic and open techniques.

Concept — The robot's drawbacks are institutional — capital cost and training — rather than clinical, and
both diminish with volume.

COMPLICATIONS
Access Complications
 • Entry risks vascular injury, bowel injury, port-site hernia and subcutaneous emphysema; Hasson and
   Veress complication rates do not differ.
 • Preperitoneal placement: tip preperitoneal → high opening pressure → CO₂ forced preperitoneally →
   subcutaneous emphysema. Both are uncommon; surgeon experience is the greatest predictor of access
   complications.

Vascular and Visceral Complications
 • Major complications are rare: vascular injury, small bowel injury, bleeding needing conversion, bladder
   perforation and vas deferens injury are all uncommon. Surgeons doing more than 12 cases a year have
   fewer complications; rates mirror adult urology.

TRAINING AND EDUCATION
 • Just over half of practising paediatric urologists operate robotically; the main barrier is lack of formal
   training. Use is associated with years in practice, formal robotic training and robot access.
 • Residency training is variable: most recent graduates felt unable to operate robotically unsupervised and
   lacked confidence in robotic pyeloplasty. Resident robotic cases rose, mostly oncological; paediatric cases
   stayed overwhelmingly open. Fellows' MIS volumes vary widely, and despite minimum case numbers
   there are no formal MIS training guidelines.

ERGONOMICS
Laparoscopic Ergonomics
 • Laparoscopy causes work-related musculoskeletal disorders: the fulcrum effect (instruments pivot at the
   abdominal wall) and hands in supination and ulnar deviation. Most urologists report musculoskeletal
   pain, mostly ergonomic; thumb pain tracks laparoscopic time.
 • Poor screen position, bed height and arm position cause fatigue and injury; teach ergonomics and a
   neutral stance. Port placement must balance triangulation with arm position; height, arm span and hand
   size matter.
 • Women are disproportionately affected; smaller hands increase hand injury and instrument difficulty.
   Instruments for varied hand sizes and core and back strengthening help.

Robotic Ergonomics
 • Seated at an adjustable console (office-chair standards apply), with adjustable foot pedals and a saved
   ergonomic profile.
 • Surgeons comfortable with the clutch report less discomfort; poor clutch use leaves shoulders abducted
   and elbows lifted off the armrest.
 • Motion tracking and electromyography: the robot causes less upper-limb strain than laparoscopy, but
   not for the neck, which is held more statically.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS
 • Pyeloplasty and reimplantation are increasingly robotic — robotic pyeloplasty use rose steeply each year
   — while complex procedures lag.
Same-day discharge and drainage
 • Most MIS reconstructions are observed overnight, but uncomplicated robotic renal and ureteric cases,
   with minimal stents and no catheters or drains, were discharged the same day with low emergency
   return and readmission — routine overnight stay is not always necessary.
 • Stentless robotic pyeloplasty is feasible in a small literature, but stenting and urethral catheter drainage
   remain the gold standard.
Expanding indications
 • Augmentation cystoplasty for neurogenic bladder raises capacity and compliance, protecting the upper
   tracts; it needs a bowel segment, often with a catheterisable channel. Usually open, sometimes a
   laparoscopic start with a small incision for extracorporeal suturing.
 • First paediatric robotic augmentation: 2008, da Vinci S, fully intracorporeal. Barriers: VP shunts with
   fear of bowel spillage, intracorporeal bowel work, case volume, prior surgery, habitus, learning curve.
   Later reports: feasible, equivalent, longer operative times; still performed by few.
 • Deep pelvis: ureteric reimplantation is commonest; robotic bladder neck reconstruction for neurogenic
   bladder (first 2011), utricle excision and urogenital sinus repair also described.
 • Fetal surgery is open (fetus exteriorised) or fetoscopic; fetoscopy shares laparoscopy's limits for fine
   repair such as myelomeningocele closure. Robotic fetal surgery is animal-only; port size is a main barrier.

Single-port surgery
 • da Vinci SP approved for urology in 2018, the first formal robotic single-site platform: 2.5-cm cannula, up
   to three multijointed wristed instruments plus a wristed 3D high-definition camera, manoeuvring to
   restore triangulation.
 • First paediatric SP series (2023): pyeloplasties for pelviureteric junction obstruction and one Mitrofanoff.
 • Limitation: at least 10 cm from port to target is needed to deploy the instruments — prohibitive in small
   patients. Workarounds: HIdES Pfannenstiel port to maximise distance; a gel-port to "float" the cannula
   outside the body with sterile continuity. SP versus multiport pyeloplasty: equivalent outcomes, longer
   operative time.
Competing platforms
 • da Vinci predominates; competitors could accelerate innovation. Smaller lenses and instruments would
   help infants, and the 2.5-cm SP cannula is too large for many; paediatrics' small market share makes
   economically driven change less likely.

 • Purpose: maps the competitive field that should drive miniaturisation and lower cost.
 • Pattern: rivals differ mainly by haptic feedback (absent in da Vinci; present in Senhance, Versius,
   Mirosurge, MASTER), modular or separate-cart arms, and 9 DOF in NOTES platforms.
 • Table versus text: the table lists Hugo as "development"; the text, being more recent, describes it as
   available outside the U.S. under FDA investigational exemption. Senhance's developer TransEnterix in the
   table is Asensus in the text — the same company, renamed.
 • Trap: market-leading da Vinci has 7 DOF but no haptic feedback; SurgiBot is the one denied by the FDA.

 KEY POINTS: FUTURE CONSIDERATIONS
   • Comfort with robotic platforms will expand indications and complex robotic procedures.
   • Smaller robotic ports and instruments may aid fetoscopic fetal surgery.
   • New single-port platforms have been used in paediatric patients.
   • New platforms will increase innovation and market competition.

Concept — The limit on paediatric robotics is the size of the hardware, not the capability of the operation;
progress depends on miniaturisation, which a small paediatric market cannot drive alone — hence the hope
placed in competition.
