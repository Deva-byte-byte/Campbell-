Chapter 145 — Surgical and Radiographic Anatomy of the
Adrenals
 • The adrenal glands are paired retroperitoneal organs, unique in their embryology, anatomy and vital role
   in homeostasis.
 • They produce mineralocorticoids, glucocorticoids, sex steroids and catecholamines, which regulate
   crucial physiological processes throughout the body.
 • Knowledge of adrenal anatomy underpins medical and surgical management of adrenal disease and is
   essential for the retroperitoneal surgeon.

ANATOMIC RELATIONSHIPS
 • The glands lie cephalad to the kidneys in the retroperitoneum; their position differs between right and
   left.
 • Both lie at the level of the 11th or 12th rib: the right gland is higher, and the left extends inferiorly to the
   first lumbar space.
 • Both are enclosed within the perirenal (Gerota) fascia and entirely surrounded by perirenal fat; a thin
   layer of connective tissue separates each gland from the upper pole of its kidney.
 • Grossly yellow-orange, noticeably more orange than the surrounding fat.
 • Dimensions 2–3 cm wide and 4–6 cm long; weight approximately 5 g each (range 2–6 g), with no
   difference between the sexes.

SURGICAL LANDMARKS
 • Dorsolaterally, both glands lie against the crus of the diaphragm.
 • The relationships to surrounding intra-abdominal and retroperitoneal organs determine the surgical
   approach; cross-sectional anatomy (Fig. 145.3) shows the right gland with the liver and inferior vena cava
   (IVC) and the left with the splenic vasculature and pancreas, each lying anterior to its diaphragmatic crus.

 • Microvascular sequence (Fig. 145.6): capsular artery → subcapsular plexus → sinusoidal vessels → deep

 • The medulla therefore has a dual supply: arterial blood from medullary arterioles and venous blood from

 • Cortical venous blood reaching the medulla carries a high glucocorticoid concentration, which plays a role
   in epinephrine (adrenaline) synthesis; the dual supply is thus important for medullary catecholamine
   production.

 • The source groups the system into a cortical supply (cortical sinusoids draining to medullary capillaries
   and the medullary vein) and a medullary supply (medullary arterioles and cortical sinusoids).

Venous Drainage
 • Each gland is drained by a single central vein exiting anteromedially; emissary veins connect it to the
   pericapsular plexus, forming an intricate intraglandular network.
 • Right versus left (Fig. 145.7): right vein short, into the posterior IVC | left vein longer, into the cranial
   aspect of the left renal vein, sometimes first joining the inferior phrenic vein.
Concept — Many arteries in, one vein out: supply is a diffuse cascade from three sources, drainage
converges on a single central vein, and the medulla is perfused in series downstream of the cortex, which
couples cortical glucocorticoid output to medullary epinephrine synthesis.

ADRENAL NERVES
 • Innervation governs catecholamine release from medullary chromaffin cells (Fig. 145.8).
 • Pathway: preganglionic sympathetic fibres from the lower thoracic and lumbar cord (Fig. 145.8 depicts
   the intermediolateral cell column at T5–T8) → sympathetic chain → greater, lesser and least thoracic and
   first lumbar splanchnic nerves → through the coeliac ganglion without synapse → plexus at the adrenal
   capsule → traverse the cortex → ramify around medullary cells.
 • Postganglionic fibres from the coeliac ganglion supply the adrenal blood vessels (Fig. 145.8).
 • Medullary secretory products enter the circulation via fenestrated capillaries.
 • Cholinergic innervation of the cortex has been described, but is less well characterised than medullary
   innervation.
Concept — The medulla behaves as a modified sympathetic ganglion: it receives preganglionic fibres
directly, and its chromaffin cells take the place of postganglionic neurones.

EMBRYOLOGY
 • Adrenal development was hypothesised until 1911, when anatomy across stages of human development
   was first described; the original illustration showed an early fetal zone of immature cortical cells and
   groups of sympathogonia.
 • The fetal zone is important during development and shrinks dramatically after birth.

Developmental Sequence
 • Weeks 3–4: columns of coelomic epithelium condense → over the next 2 weeks they proliferate and
   migrate to the cranial end of the mesonephros → adrenogonadal ridge, common precursor of adrenal and
   gonad → cephalic portion forms the adrenal cortex, caudal portion the gonads (Fig. 145.9).
 • Weeks 8–10: differentiation into definitive and fetal zones → surrounding mesenchyme forms the capsule
   → neural crest cells migrate medially to form the medulla → vessels and nerves form.
 • About week 8: the kidneys ascend from the pelvis to meet the adrenals.
 • Birth: glands relatively large, twice adult weight.
 • First year: fetal zone involutes, replaced by the definitive zone, which forms the zona glomerulosa (ZG)
   and zona fasciculata (ZF) under adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) → chromaffin cells scattered in the
   fetal zone aggregate into the medulla, homologous to a sympathetic ganglion without postganglionic
   processes.
 • Ages 6–8 years: the zona reticularis (ZR), minor until early childhood, expands to adult volume.

Developmental Anomalies
 • Renal agenesis: the adrenal is still found in its orthotopic position, but may be discoid rather than
   triangular or crescentic.
 • Adrenal rests — ectopic cortical or medullary tissue:
    ◦ Found in about 1% of adults, near the adrenals and close to the coeliac axis.
     ◦ Along the path of gonadal descent: gonad-associated rests in 7.5–15% of neonates, usually regressing.
     ◦ In congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) they may present as testicular masses.
     ◦ Clinical relevance: compensatory hypertrophy after adrenalectomy, inadvertent excision of
       heterotopic tissue at unrelated surgery, and neoplastic transformation.

Concept — Two origins, one gland: the cortex is coelomic mesoderm sharing an adrenogonadal ridge with

HISTOLOGY
 • An outer cortex (about 90% of mass) and an inner medulla, within a capsule of hypocellular fibrous

 • The cortex has three concentric zones, each identifiable by cell appearance and architecture (Figs.

RADIOLOGY
 • Modalities: CT, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ultrasound (US), angiography,
   metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scan and positron emission tomography (PET); imaging evaluates
   abnormal morphology or function (covered in later chapters). This section reviews normal anatomy on CT,
   MRI, US and angiography (Fig. 145.12).

Computed Tomography
 • Most widely used modality; perinephric fat allows the gland to be seen with outstanding resolution.
   Excellent morphology, but no functional data.
 • Axial appearance: inverted V or Y; limbs 3–6 mm wide, usually thinner than the adjacent diaphragmatic
   crura. Position of each gland: see the right-versus-left table.
 • Unenhanced attenuation ≤10 Hounsfield units (HU) reflects intracellular lipid — the threshold that
   identifies lipid-rich adrenal tissue on noncontrast CT.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging
 • Spatial resolution slightly inferior to CT; a useful adjunct in selected pathology, and preferred where
   radiation exposure is a concern.
 • Coronal or sagittal images confirm the adrenal origin of a mass when axial images are equivocal.
 • Contrast resolution (T1- and T2-weighted) superior to CT, enabling differentiation of adrenal masses;
   flow-related enhancement evaluates arteries and veins.
 • Usually the transverse plane at slice thickness ≤3 mm.
 • T1: uniform intermediate signal, slightly lower than liver and renal cortex (Fig. 145.12C). T2: gland hard
   to distinguish from retroperitoneal fat, because intracellular lipid gives similar signal.

Ultrasonography
 • Performed mainly to identify abnormalities; normal glands are hypoechoic (Fig. 145.12D).
 • Limited by retroperitoneal fat, body habitus, the glands' small size and respiratory motion.
 • Principal use: differentiating solid from cystic adrenal masses.

Angiography
 • Adrenal venous sampling: blood for metabolic assay — functional information.
 • Inferior vena cavography: evaluates the intraluminal component of an adrenal malignancy when CT, MRI
   and US are equivocal.
 • Adrenal arteriography is rarely required: CT and MRI nearly always supply the morphology needed for
   surgical planning.

CONCLUSION
 • The adrenals combine unique anatomic, embryological and histological features; their transformation
   from fetal life to adulthood is unlike any other organ.
 • Understanding their vascular and anatomic relationships is critical for surgery on the adrenals and nearby
   organs; advances in imaging improve characterisation of normal and pathological anatomy.

 KEY POINTS: ADRENAL GLANDS
   • Embryologically the adrenals are distinct from the kidney: in renal agenesis the adrenal remains
     orthotopic, although its shape may be altered.
   • The gland comprises cortex and medulla; the cortex has three zones — ZG, ZF and ZR.
   • The medulla receives preganglionic sympathetic input that releases catecholamines from chromaffin
     cells.
   • Arterial supply: branches of the inferior phrenic artery, aorta and renal artery.
   • Venous drainage differs by side: right into the IVC, left into the left renal vein.
