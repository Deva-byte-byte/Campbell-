Chapter 110 — Overactive Bladder
TERMINOLOGY AND DEFINITIONS
 • Overactive bladder (OAB) — International Continence Society (ICS): "urinary urgency, usually
   accompanied by frequency and nocturia, with or without urgency urinary incontinence (UUI), in the
   absence of urinary tract infection (UTI) or other obvious pathology."
 • Component storage symptoms: increased daytime frequency, nocturia, urgency, urgency (urinary)
   incontinence. "Urge incontinence" was renamed "urgency incontinence" to match the abnormal
   sensation.

TABLE 110.1 STANDARDIZED TERMINOLOGY AND DEFINITIONS TO DESCRIBE LOWER URINARY TRACT DYSFUNCTION
RELATED TO OVERACTIVE BLADDER
 Term                                Definition

 Increased daytime frequency         Patient considers he/she voids too often by day (7 voids = upper limit of normal)
 Nocturia                            Waking one or more times to void; each void preceded and followed by sleep
 Urgency                             Sudden, compelling desire to void that is difficult to defer
 UUI                                 Involuntary loss of urine associated with urgency

 • Bother: all components reduce health-related quality of life (QoL); urgency and UUI most strongly; UUI
   the most bothersome LUTS in both sexes. Frequency bothers at ≥8 daytime voids (every ≤2 h), though
   fewer can bother; nocturia bothers at ≥2 episodes, markedly at ≥3. Isolated nocturia suggests nocturnal
   polyuria or poor sleep, not LUT dysfunction.
 • OAB ≠ detrusor overactivity (DO): OAB is a symptom syndrome; DO is a urodynamic observation of
   involuntary detrusor contractions during filling (spontaneous or provoked). OAB patients may lack DO; DO
   may be asensate or an artefact of instrumentation.

Mixed Symptoms Incorporating Urinary Urgency
 • Mixed urinary incontinence (MUI) — involuntary leakage associated with urgency and also with effort/
   exertion/sneezing/coughing = SUI + UUI ("OAB wet") in one person.
 • SUI + "OAB dry" is not MUI — describe descriptively ("SUI and urgency without UUI").
 • Urinary + faecal incontinence = double (dual) incontinence, not MUI.
 • MUI generally means more severe leakage; relative contributions hard to quantify, especially with
   insensible incontinence (unaware how leakage occurred).
 • Fig. 110.1: incontinence spans SUI → MUI → "OAB wet"; "OAB dry" can coexist with SUI.

Distinguishing Overactive Bladder From Bladder Pain Syndrome
 • Bladder pain syndrome (BPS): persistent/recurrent chronic pelvic pain, pressure or discomfort perceived
   as bladder-related + ≥1 other urinary symptom (urgency, frequency). Pain is required in BPS, not typical
   of OAB.

 KEY POINTS: OVERACTIVE BLADDER TERMINOLOGY
   • OAB = urgency ± frequency/nocturia ± UUI, without UTI or other obvious pathology.
   • OAB is symptomatic; DO is urodynamic.
   • Urgency = sudden, compelling desire to void that is difficult to defer.
   • MUI = UUI + SUI.
   • "Fear of leakage" vs "fear of pain" separates OAB from BPS, though both may express central
     sensitisation.

Concept — OAB is defined by a sensation, urgency, so every boundary in the terminology — DO, MUI, BPS,
nocturia — is drawn by asking what the patient feels and fears, not what the bladder does on a trace.

 • Pattern: looser definitions ("urgency at least sometimes", internet) give the highest rates; strict ICS
   definitions (EPIC) the lowest. Trap: comparing raw prevalence across studies with different case

 • Sex: OAB 1.5–2× commoner in women, UUI 2–3×. NOBLE: OAB similar by sex (~16–17%) but UUI 7.6%

 • Race/ethnicity: consistently highest in Black men and women (EpiLUTS, OAB-POLL); NHANES — highest
   UUI in Black women.

Incidence and Progression of Overactive Bladder
 • Dynamic, waxing–waning condition. Incident OAB in women ~3–8%/year; progression from OAB dry to
   UUI ~1%/year; remission ~6%/year.

Health Burden of Overactive Bladder
 • Impairs daily activity, sleep, self-esteem, relationships, sexual life; UUI adds burden beyond dry OAB; less
   leisure physical activity.
 • Work: higher unemployment, more absenteeism (medical and disability) and impaired productivity.
 • Care-seeking is poor: under half ever discuss symptoms despite seeing clinicians; reasons — not thought
   important, never asked, "something to cope with"; many unaware treatment exists.
 • Drug persistence is poor: <6% take medication; antimuscarinics only modestly superior to placebo in
   aggregate; ~92% fail initial antimuscarinic within 2 years, most stop within 6 months, half never refill.
   Reasons: unmet expectations > side effects > cost.
 • Costs: patient costs mostly containment (pads, laundry), heavier with severity and in Black and Hispanic
   patients and those with unmet social needs; oral drug costs high, no agent universally cheap. Third-line
   cost-effectiveness: sacral nerve stimulation (SNS) costliest over 10 years; onabotulinumtoxinA 100 U
   most cost-effective vs conservative care. US societal cost ~$66 billion (2007), projected ~$83 billion
   (2020).
 • Social determinants: lower socioeconomic status associated with UUI (dose–response); food insecurity,
   financial strain, housing and toilet access, poor social support, interpersonal violence linked to OAB.
Concept — OAB is common, age-related and female-predominant for UUI, yet under-reported and under-
persisted — the burden lies less in lack of effective treatment than in failure to ask, expectations unmet, and
cost.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND ETIOLOGY
 • Multifactorial, across all levels of neural control. DO was once the sine qua non of OAB but is no longer:
   only about half with DO have urgency; 44–69% with urgency show DO.
 • No animal model of OAB (urgency is subjective) → research uses surrogates: sensory activity, motor
   control, LUT reflexes.
      ◦ Sensory: transduction → afferent traffic → gating → sensitisation → conscious perception;
        urothelium/suburothelium release mediators, cytokines and growth factors; gut inflammation
        sensitises bladder nerves; brain imaging identifies candidate centres.

      ◦ Motor: detrusor motility and drivers (efferents, interstitial cells, mediators); localised micromotions
        beside quiescent areas summate into whole-bladder pressure.
      ◦ Integration (Fig. 110.4): sacral cord — immature reflex voiding (infants, neurologic disease);
        midbrain/brainstem (PAG, PMC) — main regulatory level, homeostasis, voiding reflex, switching
        storage ↔ voiding; forebrain — conscious sensation, volition, emotion, decision to store or void;
        periphery — urothelium, interstitial cells, myovesical plexus.
 • Functional brain imaging: insula and prefrontal cortex involved; OAB alters regional activity; SNS, tibial
   nerve stimulation and antimuscarinics change brain activity.

 • Other increased afferent signalling — pelvic organ cross-talk: signals from other organs converge at
   dorsal root ganglia, spinal cord or brain; bowel–bladder best understood (shared thoracic and sacral
   innervation). Rectal distension or inflammation alters detrusor activity via convergent C-fibres; colonic
   stimulation worsens bladder pain in cystitis models. Clinically, rectal distension changes capacity,
   sensation and DO; constipation and straining impair emptying and worsen storage and voiding
   symptoms.
 • Abnormal handling of afferent signals: continuous "afferent noise" reaches the central nervous system
   (CNS); normally most is filtered below consciousness. If CNS modulation fails, normally unconscious
   sensations are perceived as urgency.

Other Pathophysiologic Mechanisms
 • Ischaemia–reperfusion (atherosclerosis, arterial obstruction) → hypoxia, oxidative stress → damage to
   urothelium, muscle, microvasculature, nerves.
 • Chronic inflammation — raised cytokines, chemokines, growth factors in tissue, urine and blood.
 • Urinary microbiome differs in UUI/OAB — relevance to bladder–brain signalling unknown.

Etiology and Attributable Risk Factors
 • Cause usually unknown and multifactorial.
 • Aging — clearly associated; multifactorial cellular and CNS change.
 • Sex — higher prevalence (especially younger women) and more UUI in women.
 • BOO — classic cause of DO in animal models, but in humans not a direct cause: OAB prevalence similar
   in women (BOO rare) and men (BOO common); DO often persists after prostate surgery. BOO may act
   indirectly, accelerating ageing changes.
 • Race/ethnicity — differences exist; mechanisms unknown.
 • Psychological factors: depression and anxiety most studied; embarrassment, shame, low self-esteem;
   OAB wet carries more psychological burden than OAB dry. Possible serotonergic or hypothalamic–
   pituitary–adrenal axis mechanisms; stress acts via this route; anxious women show greater temporal
   summation of heat pain (central sensitisation marker).
 • Voiding behaviours: convenience ("just in case") voiding, delayed voiding, straining, positional
   preference may cause OAB; coping strategies (mapping toilets, altering plans, fluid restriction)
   associated with stress and anxiety independent of symptoms and perpetuate a negative bladder
   perception even after objective improvement.
 • Wider associations: bowel function, metabolic syndrome, hormonal status, psychosocial burden.

 KEY POINTS: PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND ETIOLOGY
   • Afferent information is integrated at multiple CNS levels; main regulation at midbrain/brainstem.
   • Failed high-level integration may let normally unconscious sensations be felt as urgency.
   • The two bladder-centric theories are urothelium-based and myogenic.
   • BOO and OAB are probably separate consequences of ageing rather than cause and effect.

Concept — urgency is the perceived output of an afferent system — too much signal from urothelium,
muscle or neighbouring organs, or too little central filtering — which is why DO, obstruction and bladder
findings correlate only loosely with OAB and why psychological state and behaviour modulate it.

CLINICAL ASSESSMENT

Initial Evaluation
 • OAB is a symptom-based diagnosis; empiric treatment may follow history and non-invasive testing.
 • Minimum (AUA/SUFU non-neurogenic OAB guideline): careful history, physical examination, urinalysis;
   European Association of Urology (EAU) male and female LUTS guidelines agree and encourage validated
   questionnaires and bladder diaries.

History
 • Each OAB symptom and its bother; other storage (SUI) and voiding symptoms suggesting BOO; post-
   micturition symptoms, dysuria, haematuria. Questionnaires help but are limited by health literacy and
   language.
 • Fluid intake — caffeine, polydipsia; patients may already restrict.
 • Occult neurologic disease — new OAB with erectile dysfunction or tremor, spinal surgery/disc disease.
 • Obstetric/gynaecologic, surgical, radiotherapy, bowel (faecal incontinence 7–15% in general population,
   ~50% in women with OAB) and medication history.
 • Comorbidities that cause LUTS (polyuric diabetes), relatively contraindicate antimuscarinics
   (uncontrolled closed-angle glaucoma, cognitive impairment, retention, impaired gastric emptying) or β3
   agonists (uncontrolled hypertension, arrhythmia), or ↑ urine output (obstructive sleep apnoea,
   psychogenic polydipsia, lithium-induced diabetes insipidus).

Physical Examination
 • Abdomen, pelvis, extremities, basic neurology, cognition and frailty — even when the diagnosis seems
   straightforward.
 • General appearance: gait, mobility, stature, frailty (performance status, neurology).
 • Abdomen: scars, masses, hernias, tenderness, suprapubic distension.
 • Lower-limb oedema — fluid shifts driving nocturia.
 • Pelvic/genital examination (± digital rectal examination in men) — the most important component:
   women — pelvic floor tone, pain, prolapse, urethral prolapse/caruncle, labial adhesions, vaginal atrophy
   (oestrogen status), supine stress test; men — prostate size and abnormality; all — perineal sensation
   and rectal tone.
 • Cognition — observe during interview; Mini-Mental State Examination if concerned.
 • Frailty predicts poor surgical outcome; Timed Up and Go Test (rise, walk 10 feet) is quick.

Urinalysis
 • Mandatory — exclude UTI, haematuria, pyuria; culture if infection suspected; microhaematuria → full
   evaluation.

Additional Assessment
Other Laboratory Testing
 • Renal function not routine (review if available); prostate-specific antigen (PSA) in men if it alters
   management; culture for acute irritative symptoms with a negative dipstick (dipstick misses low counts).

Postvoid Residual Volume
 • Not required before lifestyle, behavioural or oral therapy in uncomplicated patients.
 • Measure PVR in complicated patients: retention risk, obstructive/voiding symptoms (especially men),
   prior anti-incontinence or prostate surgery, neurologic disease.
 • Portable bladder ultrasound preferred; catheterisation if unavailable.

Bladder Diary
 • Records intake and voiding: useful when history is poor, recall unreliable, or polyuria suspected; guides
   behavioural change; objective response measure; self-monitoring itself therapeutic.
 • 3–7 days ideal; at least 24–48 h (burden); time, volume, leakage, circumstances, urgency grade.

Advanced Evaluation
 • Urodynamics, cystoscopy and renal/bladder ultrasound are not for the initial work-up of the
   uncomplicated patient.
 • Urodynamics indicated when compliance, DO or other findings will decide for or against an invasive,
   irreversible or morbid treatment, or after failure of multiple treatments; also suspected BOO (e.g. OAB
   after an outlet procedure). May improve satisfaction but not proven to improve outcome for the index
   patient (woman, low PVR, no significant prolapse, no neurologic disease, minimal SUI).
 • Aim: reproduce the symptoms; two OAB diagnoses — DO and early/exaggerated filling sensation ("early
   and persistent desire to void" when DO absent).
 • Stopping antimuscarinics before testing: stop → best chance of seeing DO; continue → explains
   refractory symptoms.
 • DO vs symptoms (Fig. 110.5): DO correlates best with UUI, worst with nocturia. OAB dry: DO in ~60–
   70% of men and 40–50% of women; OAB wet: DO in nearly all men, but absent in 30–40% of women
   with reported UUI (SUI and pre-emptive voiding misread as urgency).
 • Technique: use provocations (running water, standing); fast filling provokes DO and may obscure other
   parameters; test seated/standing (symptoms occur upright); if severe low-volume DO hides SUI → second
   fill supine (reduces DO).
 • Always assess BOO and emptying: (1) OAB treatment may precipitate retention — each 10 mL of PVR ↑
   risk of catheterisation after onabotulinumtoxinA by ~27%; (2) severe BOO associated with DO — OAB
   may not improve unless BOO treated, yet relief of BOO does not guarantee improvement (DO in up to
   ~60% after TURP).

 KEY POINTS: OVERACTIVE BLADDER CLINICAL ASSESSMENT
   • Minimum: history, examination, urinalysis.
   • Consider malignancy, neurologic disease, systemic disease, elevated PVR.
   • Urine culture and PVR at the provider's discretion when indicated.
   • Diaries and validated PROMs clarify symptoms and track response.
   • No urodynamics, cystoscopy or ultrasound in uncomplicated initial work-up.
   • Urodynamics when findings will change an invasive/irreversible decision, or after multiple failed
     treatments.

Concept — because OAB is a symptomatic diagnosis of exclusion, assessment is built to exclude what
mimics it (infection, malignancy, neurology, retention, polyuria) with minimal testing; invasive testing is
justified only when its answer will change an irreversible decision.

MANAGEMENT
 • OAB is chronic, affects QoL, and is not life-threatening — counsel in that context. Choosing no treatment
   is acceptable after counselling (benefits may not outweigh costs; severe cognitive impairment).
 • Address mobility limits (correcting leakage will not correct immobility); incontinence-associated
   dermatitis (pain, infection, pressure ulcers) may justify more aggressive treatment.
 • Set goals and realistic expectations — improvement, not cure; gradual and irregular; goals may change;
   critical before invasive therapy.
 • Education empowers and itself improves perception; substantial placebo response in OAB trials shows
   the power of non-pharmacologic intervention.

 • AUA/SUFU lines (first–fourth) balance benefit against invasiveness, adverse events and reversibility —

Conservative Management
 • Behavioural therapy and lifestyle: bladder training, urge-control strategies, pelvic floor muscle therapy
   (PFMT), weight loss, fluid management — mostly studied for UI in women; improvement, rarely
   resolution.
 • Fluid: ~25% reduction meaningfully improves symptoms (cost: thirst, underhydration); many already
   restrict.
 • Caffeine reduction improves urgency and frequency but not consistently UI — let the patient judge the
   trade-off.
 • Weight loss: reduces SUI and UUI (intensive programme in obese women); bariatric surgery ~7–8% lower
   UI risk per 5% weight lost.
 • PFMT and bladder training: incontinence episodes fall 50–80%, negligible risk; combine with drugs
   according to preference.

Augmentation Cystoplasty or Urinary Diversion
 • Rarely for intractable OAB; evidence mainly neurogenic → little known of QoL in non-neurogenic OAB;
   weigh against containment (pads) or indwelling catheter.

Approach
 • ICI (sixth consultation) grades evidence and gives care pathways for men, women and the frail elderly
   (Fig. 110.6); AUA/SUFU algorithm and SUFU OAB care pathway concur.
 • Common sequence: assess to confirm OAB and exclude alternatives → educate → conservative
   (lifestyle, behavioural, PFMT) → pharmacologic → invasive (tibial stimulation, SNM,
   chemodenervation); reassess symptoms, side effects and preferences at every step.
 • Minimum trial before judging efficacy: 4–12 weeks for behavioural and for drug therapy.

 KEY POINTS: CLINICAL MANAGEMENT
   • Conservative: caffeine and fluid reduction, weight loss, bladder training, PFMT.
   • Pharmacologic: antimuscarinics (oral or transdermal) or β3 agonists — caution with chronic
     antimuscarinics (dementia risk).
   • Procedural: tibial neuromodulation, botulinum toxin, sacral neuromodulation — shared decision-
     making.
   • Augmentation or diversion for the refractory, weighed against containment or indwelling catheter.

Concept — OAB treatment escalates by invasiveness and reversibility, not by severity alone, because the
condition is benign and chronic; every step is gated by the patient's goals and tolerance, and no treatment
remains a legitimate endpoint.
