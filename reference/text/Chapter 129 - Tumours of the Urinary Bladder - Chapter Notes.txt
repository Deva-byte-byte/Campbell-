Chapter 129 — Tumours of the Urinary Bladder
EPIDEMIOLOGY
Incidence, Prevalence, and Mortality
 • A global disease: about 550,000 new cases and 200,000 deaths worldwide in 2018; in the United States
   (US) about 82,000 diagnoses and 17,000 deaths a year.
 • Age-adjusted incidence and mortality fell through the 1990s and early 2000s and have since
   plateaued.
 • Crude incidence is rising because of population ageing and growth; adjusted for demographic change,
   incidence is stable, and the US and Europe show a slow decline.
 • Risk is highest in high-income countries and lowest in low-income countries; the highest regional rates
   are in North America and Western and Central Europe, the lowest in Oceania and Andean and Central
   Latin America.
 • The burden is shifting to the developing world: more than 60% of incident cases and nearly half of
   deaths occur there, attributed to a narrowing gap in life expectancy between low- and high-income
   countries.
 • Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) are the World Health Organization (WHO) measure of disease
   burden: years of life lost (deaths × remaining life expectancy) plus years lived with disability. Bladder
   cancer caused about 3.4 million DALYs in 2015; age-standardised DALY rates fell in both sexes.
 • Prevalent out of proportion to incidence: deaths are few relative to cases, so bladder cancer is the
   fourth most prevalent cancer affecting both sexes globally and in the top five in countries of every
   income level. Five-year prevalence understates the number of survivors still managing disease
   sequelae.
 • Regional variation reflects demography and environmental exposure: bladder cancer was the
   commonest cancer in Egyptian males (2015) because of Schistosoma haematobium, which causes
   squamous cell carcinoma (SCC); in Europe the highest rates are in Belgium (males) and Hungary
   (females).

Gender, Racial, and Age Differences
 • A disease of ageing and cumulative exposure: average age at diagnosis 73 (US); about 9 in 10 patients
   are diagnosed after 55. Incidence rises from age 50–54 in both sexes, more sharply in males from 60–
   64, and is highest at 90 and over.

  • Male predominance about 3:1: nearly three-quarters of cases are in males. US lifetime risk is 1 in 27 for
    males and 1 in 89 for females.
  • Why males — smoking: smoking is far more prevalent in males worldwide.
  • Why males — carcinogen metabolism: the disparity persists after controlling for smoking. Uridine 5′-
    diphospho-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT; aromatic amine metabolism) and glutathione S-transferase
    mu 1 (GSTM1; detoxification) are expressed differently in the sexes, so equal pack-years may yield
    unequal carcinogen exposure.
  • Why males — sex steroids: in females, later menarche (≥15 years), parity and oestrogen or progestin
    therapy are associated with lower risk. The androgen receptor (AR) is downregulated in bladder
    tumours, more so at higher stage and grade, and castration or AR knockout suppresses tumour growth
    in transgenic and carcinogen-induced mouse models.
  • Females present later and do worse stage for stage: they lose a larger share of remaining life
    expectancy, and stage at presentation explains only about 30% of the disparity.
  • Causes of the female disadvantage: tumour biology, environmental exposure and implicit bias (implicit
    social cognition — bladder cancer framed as a "male" disease), with a longer delay from first
    haematuria to diagnosis because females are more often managed as urinary tract infection (UTI),
    which delays referral to a urologist.
  • Race: incidence is highest in White Americans (1.5 times that of Black, twice that of Hispanic and six
    times that of Native Americans), but Black patients more often present with muscle-invasive disease,
    more often have nonurothelial histology (highest in Black females), and have worse stage-for-stage 5-
    year relative survival.

 Economic Impact
  • The most expensive cancer to treat per patient over a lifetime: lifetime cost roughly US$129,000–
    251,000 per patient; annual direct costs exceed US$4 billion in the US and €4.9 billion in the European
    Union.
  • Costs vary several-fold between countries and between institutions, driven mainly by differences in
    the management of non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC); per capita, the US spends about
    twice as much as Europe.

  • Two targets for cost reduction: intensive NMIBC surveillance (annual procedures and laboratory tests)
    and metastatic disease (costly late-stage therapeutics and end-of-life care).
 Concept — Prevalence and cost follow from the same fact: bladder cancer is common, rarely fatal early
 and repeatedly recurrent, so patients accumulate years of surveillance; the rising burden reflects an ageing
 population, not a rising age-specific risk.

 RISK FACTORS
  • An exposure surface: like skin and lung, the bladder is in constant contact with environmental
    carcinogens and inflammation.
  • Aromatic amines (2-naphthylamine, 4-aminobiphenyl, benzidine) are the central causative agents;
    once hydroxylated they form DNA adducts. Tobacco and occupation are the two commonest routes of
    exposure.

 Smoking
  • The main known cause: tobacco accounts for 30–40% of urothelial carcinoma; smokers carry a
    twofold to threefold risk. There are more than 1 billion smokers worldwide.
  • Cigarettes are commonest, but pipe and cigar smoking also raise risk; bladder carcinogens have been
    found in electronic cigarette users, suggesting possible risk. US adult smoking prevalence fell
    substantially between 2005 and 2015.
  • N-acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2) slow acetylators are at particularly high risk when they smoke, because
    NAT2 acetylation detoxifies aromatic amines.
  • Supplements do not protect: dietary beta-carotene may be protective, but supplemental beta-carotene
    and vitamins C, E or folate do not reduce risk in exposed people.
  • Cessation lowers risk progressively: risk remains raised 1–3 years after stopping and approaches that
    of never-smokers after more than 15 years.
  • About 20% are still smoking at diagnosis; active smoking raises local recurrence in NMIBC and cancer
    death in muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), and many continue after diagnosis.
  • Quitting after diagnosis: its survival benefit is unproven, but continued smoking raises the risk of a
    second primary cancer, peripheral vascular disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).

 Occupational Risk
  • Occupation accounts for 5–10% of bladder cancer; lifetime risk is greatest in work with chemicals and
    dyes.
  • Exposure among females is rising as many of these occupations become increasingly female.

 Environment
  • Arsenic in drinking water (a toxic metalloid released from weathering rock) is associated with bladder
    cancer. The worst-affected regions are West Bengal, Bangladesh and Taiwan (and parts of Chile), with
    concentrations hundreds of times the recommended limit of 10 µg/L.
  • Mechanism is multifactorial: oxidative stress, epigenetic effects and impaired DNA repair.

 Radiation
  • First recognised in atomic bomb survivors, in whom attributable risk rose with dose.
  • Latency 15–30 years; radiation-associated bladder cancer is not age related.
  • Studies are conflicting and confounded, but risk scales with modality:

 Genetics
  • NAT2 and GSTM1 deletion are the most-studied genes; both govern aromatic amine metabolism and
    so matter chiefly in those with carcinogen exposure. NAT2 regulates acetylation of the aromatic amines
    in cigarette smoke.
  • Several low-penetrance variants probably combine with occupational, environmental and behavioural
    exposures to potentiate risk.

 Hereditary
  • First-degree relatives have a twofold risk, but high-risk families are exceedingly rare; there is no
    mendelian pattern, so linkage studies are unhelpful — the aetiology is gene–environment interaction.
  • Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer, HNPCC) raises urothelial cancer risk,
    mainly in MSH2 carriers.
  • Pathogenic germline variants in 13–24% of patients with urothelial carcinoma (mostly advanced
    disease), commonest MSH2 and BRCA1/2. In NMIBC they occur in high-grade but not low-grade
    disease, so germline testing may help in selected high-grade patients to identify risk in family
    members.

 Body Mass Index
  • Increasing body mass index (BMI) raises risk in multiple meta-analyses, with a dose–response
    relationship, and independent of smoking.
  • Obesity worsens prognosis after diagnosis: more recurrence and a shorter time to recurrence.
  • Proposed mechanisms: insulin resistance, chronic hyperinsulinaemia, increased bioavailability of
    steroid hormones and local inflammation with proinflammatory cytokines; the exact mechanism is
    unknown.

 Medical Conditions
  • Chronic inflammation → keratinising squamous metaplasia → SCC is the usual direct pathway.

  • Neurogenic bladder and spinal cord injury with chronic indwelling catheters carry a modestly
    increased SCC risk; bladder calculi, outflow obstruction, recurrent UTI and catheter trauma are thought
    additive.
  • Bladder exstrophy: malignancy in 3.3–7.5%, several times age-matched controls; adenocarcinoma
    accounts for more than 90%, SCC and urothelial carcinoma the remainder.
  • As patients with spinal cord injury and congenital bladder anomalies live longer, bladder cancer rates in
    these groups may rise.

 Schistosomiasis
  • Endemic in up to 76 developing countries, affecting up to 218 million people, and a major cause of
    bladder cancer in tropical countries despite WHO eradication campaigns.
  • Life cycle: blood flukes with mammalian hosts and freshwater snails as intermediate hosts. Of the four
    human schistosomes, only S. haematobium is linked to bladder SCC.
  • Mechanism: worms live in the vesical venules and lay eggs → irritation and fibrosis via a T-helper 2
    (TH2) proinflammatory response; infection also potentiates other risk factors, notably smoking.

 Recurrent Infection
  • Chronic UTI: a modest, confounded association — confounded by catheterisation, stones, smoking and
    occupation; there are no prospective studies of UTI, and early data have not been validated in
    contemporary cohorts.
  • Gonorrhoea: a prospective study found nearly twofold risk in males, with more invasive tumours; the
    mechanism may be nitrosamine production.
  • Urinary microbiome: some bacterial and viral colonisation of the urothelium may be protective;
    mechanisms are under investigation.

 Concept — Almost every risk factor is either a chemical load (aromatic amines, modified by acetylator and
 glutathione S-transferase genotype) or chronic inflammation, and the pathway predicts the histology:
 chemical injury gives urothelial carcinoma, chronic inflammation gives squamous or glandular tumours.

 DRUGS
 Pioglitazone
  • Thiazolidinedione for non-insulin-dependent diabetes. The PROactive randomised trial (2005)
    unexpectedly found more bladder cancers with pioglitazone than placebo; later studies have both
    confirmed and refuted this.

 Chemotherapy
  • Cyclophosphamide is the only chemotherapeutic agent shown to cause bladder cancer; the
    mutagenic metabolite is phosphoramide mustard.
  • Risk about 4.5-fold, dose dependent: most pronounced above 20 g cumulative; above 50 g, about 7
    excess cases per 100 patients treated.

   KEY POINTS
    • Bladder cancer is related to age and environmental carcinogen exposure.
    • Smoking accounts for 30–40% of urothelial carcinoma.
    • Bladder cancer is about three times as common in males, but females present with more advanced
      tumours and a worse prognosis.
    • It is the most expensive cancer to treat per patient over a lifetime.
    • Pathogenic germline variants are present in a select group of patients.
    • No dietary intervention has reduced bladder cancer risk.


 DETECTION OF UROTHELIAL CARCINOMA
  • Rarely an incidental autopsy finding, unlike many cancers.
  • Painless gross haematuria in 85% of newly diagnosed patients; microscopic haematuria in nearly all.
    Haematuria is typically intermittent and may follow Valsalva manoeuvres.
  • Any episode of gross haematuria must be evaluated, even if subsequent urinalysis is negative;
    antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy does not preclude full evaluation.
  • Irritative voiding symptoms (frequency, urgency) may signal bladder cancer, particularly carcinoma in
    situ (CIS).
  • After a complete negative evaluation, the risk of malignancy with recurrent gross or microscopic
    haematuria is nearly zero for 6 years; for persistent haematuria, repeat evaluation at 3–5 years is
    reasonable.

 Cystoscopy
  • Gold standard for diagnosis: cystoscopy and biopsy. Flexible office cystoscopy is as reliable as rigid.
  • White light cystoscopy (WLC) is the standard of care, allowing mapping and resection of all visible
    tumours; excellent for large papillary tumours, less reliable for small papillary tumours and CIS.
  • Hexaminolevulinate blue light cystoscopy (HAL–BLC): a meta-analysis found BLC detected more Ta
    tumours and substantially more CIS than WLC; in about a quarter of patients CIS was seen only on BLC,
    with benefit in both primary and recurrent disease. Flexible BLC in the office finds recurrences missed
    on WLC in about a fifth of patients.
  • Narrow-band imaging (NBI) improves CIS detection in mostly single-centre or underpowered studies. In
    a multicentre randomised trial of NBI-assisted versus white light TURBT, overall 12-month recurrence
    was similar, but NBI reduced recurrence in low-risk patients.

 Urine Cytology
  • Papanicolaou (1945) first proposed that exfoliated urinary cells could detect urinary tract malignancy.
  • Sensitivity 31–62%, specificity 94–100%; reporting varies between institutions, compounding a
    subjective test.
  • The Paris System for Reporting Urinary Cytology (introduced 2013 at the International Congress of
    Cytology) standardises categories and cytomorphological criteria for high-grade urothelial carcinoma
    (HGUC); it appears to reduce inconclusive "atypia" calls and improve risk stratification.
  • Positive or suspicious high-grade cytology must be investigated because the test is highly specific.
  • Negative cystoscopy with persistently positive high-grade cytology → random biopsies of bladder and
    prostatic urethra, plus ureteral washings for cytology, with or without ureteroscopic inspection of the
    upper tracts.

 Urine-based biomarkers
  • Adjuncts, not replacements: developed because cystoscopy is costly and uncomfortable; most are
    more sensitive than cytology but less specific.
  • Nuclear matrix protein 22 (NMP22). A nuclear structural protein about 20 times more abundant in
    malignant urothelial cells. Two FDA-approved tests for surveillance: a laboratory quantitative assay
    and a point-of-care qualitative assay; accuracy is similar at diagnosis and in surveillance.

 Fluorescence in Situ Hybridization
  • UroVysion fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) uses labelled DNA probes to detect aneuploidy of
    chromosomes 3, 7 and 17 and homozygous loss of 9p21 in exfoliated cells.

  • Positive test: ≥5 cells with gains of ≥2 chromosomes, or ≥10 cells with gain of a single chromosome,
    or homozygous 9p21 deletion in >20% of cells.
  • Reflex test after atypical cytology: a negative FISH correlates with benign cytological change; with
    atypical cytology and negative cystoscopy, a positive FISH predicts lower recurrence-free survival.
  • Limitations: low sensitivity for low-grade tumours; no consensus on criteria for abnormal cells.

 Bladder Tumor Antigen
  • Bladder tumour antigen (BTA) assays detect complement factor H–related protein and complement
    factor H with monoclonal antibodies; both forms are FDA approved for diagnosis and follow-up.
  • BTA stat: qualitative, dipstick, point of care. BTA TRAK: quantitative, enzyme-linked immunosorbent
    assay (ELISA).
  • High false-positive rate with haematuria: complement factor H is abundant in serum, so blood cross-
    reacts.

 ImmunoCyt
  • A cell-based adjunct to cytology (late 1990s): fluorescent antibodies against a glycosylated
    carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and two mucins.
  • Combined with cytology it raises sensitivity but lowers specificity.
  • Operator dependent, with high interobserver variability; adequate training may overcome this.

 CxBladder
  • A cell-based assay of five messenger RNA fragments: HOXA13, CDC2, MDK, CXCR2, IGFBP5.
  • Sensitivity and specificity about 85% in the body text versus 0.82 and 0.85 in Table 129.1 — the table
    figure is a single study at a threshold fixed for specificity 0.85, the text figure a summary of the few

  • Pattern: ImmunoCyt has the highest sensitivity; FISH and qualitative NMP22 the highest specificity;
    performance is generally better when evaluating symptoms than in surveillance, where tumours are
    smaller and lower grade. FISH in symptomatic patients gives the highest positive likelihood ratio.
  • The trap: a negative marker does not exclude cancer. Head-to-head comparisons are too limited to rank
    the assays.
  • Authors' practice: in microhaematuria, adding a biomarker does not remove the need for cystoscopy;
    in gross haematuria a negative result still leaves a ≥10% probability of cancer, so cystoscopy is still
    required. Biomarkers are used with cystoscopy in NMIBC surveillance; multiplex gene assays await
    validation.

 On the Horizon: Urinary Tumor DNA
  • Urinary tumour DNA (utDNA) enables urinary genomic sequencing. High levels track tumour burden
    and stage and predict minimal residual disease and local recurrence.
  • Its appeal: it reflects the molecular profile of the primary tumour, so it could tailor therapy and detect
    early recurrence.

   KEY POINTS
    • Painless gross haematuria is the key presenting symptom, in 85% of new diagnoses.
    • A full haematuria evaluation comprises focused history and examination, cystoscopy, upper tract
      imaging and urine culture.
    • The gold standard is cystoscopy and biopsy; HAL–BLC and NBI are increasingly used as adjuncts for
      occult malignancy.
    • No urine marker yet has high enough sensitivity or specificity to replace office cystoscopy.

 Concept — Cystoscopy remains indispensable because every adjunct trades one property for another:
 cytology is specific for high-grade disease but blind to low grade, and urine markers are more sensitive but
 not specific enough — so a positive adjunct triggers investigation and a negative one never excludes.

 PATHOLOGY
  • Numerous benign bladder tumours exist, each with its own presentation and disease burden.

 Epithelial Metaplasia
  • A focal area of transformed urothelium with normal nuclear and cellular morphology, typically on the
    trigone; squamous or glandular.
  • Glandular metaplasia: raised, red, inflammatory clumps that can be mistaken for cancer.
  • Squamous metaplasia: common — up to 40% of females and 5% of males; associated with UTI, trauma
    and prior surgery.
  • No biopsy needed; no treatment required or effective.

 Papilloma and Inverted Papilloma
  • Inverted papilloma: benign proliferative lesion associated with chronic inflammation or bladder outlet
    obstruction; anywhere in the bladder but commonest on the trigone; <1% of bladder tumours.
    Presents with painless gross haematuria (commonest), microscopic haematuria or irritative symptoms.
  • Endophytic growth into the lamina propria forming nests and anastomosing trabeculae. When strictly
    defined (no cytological atypia) it behaves benignly, with about 1% recurrence.

  • Urothelial papilloma: delicate stalks lined by normal-appearing urothelium; formerly classed as low-
    grade Ta cancer, now benign with no invasive risk. It carries fibroblast growth factor receptor 3
    (FGFR3) mutations but lacks TP53 and RB mutations.

 Nephrogenic Adenoma
  • Caused by chronic urothelial irritation: trauma, prior surgery, renal transplantation, intravesical
    chemotherapy, stones, catheters, infection.
  • Glandular tubules resembling renal tubules in the mucosa and submucosa; cystoscopically may look
    papillary and mimic an aggressive neoplasm.
  • Causes dysuria and haematuria; recurrence is uncommon and managed by repeat resection.

 Leukoplakia
  • Keratinising squamous metaplasia with white, flaky plaques floating in the bladder; if not incidental,
    presents with recurrent infection, urgency and frequency. The mucosa is chronically inflamed.
  • Premalignant on other squamous surfaces, but not in the bladder: historical reports called it
    premalignant, but DNA analysis for TP53 found no evidence of premalignancy, and it is no longer an
    independent risk factor → further cystoscopy, biopsy and treatment are unnecessary.

 Cystitis Cystica and Glandularis
  • Common in normal bladders, associated with inflammation or chronic obstruction; cystic nests lined by
    columnar or cuboidal cells, arising from proliferating von Brunn nests.
  • Cystitis glandularis can transform to adenocarcinoma, though the risk is low → regular endoscopic
    evaluation.
  • Treatment: transurethral resection and relief of the obstruction or inflammation.

 PRECURSOR MALIGNANT LESIONS
  • Normal urothelium: four to seven cells thick, transitional between nonkeratinising squamous and
    pseudostratified columnar epithelium, maturing in order from small cuboidal basal cells →
    intermediate cells → superficial umbrella cells. Umbrella cells may show nuclear atypia and form the
    urine–blood barrier that protects deeper cells from toxins.
  • A continuum: hyperplasia → atypia → dysplasia → cancer. The 2004 WHO classification lists
    premalignant lesions as urothelial hyperplasia (flat and papillary), reactive atypia, urothelial atypia of
    unknown significance, urothelial dysplasia and low-grade intraurothelial neoplasia.
  • Urothelial hyperplasia: flat or papillary; mucosa ≥10 cell layers; nuclei may be slightly enlarged but no
    atypia and no mitoses. Not malignant in isolation but often accompanies neoplasia. Flat hyperplasia
    may precede low-grade papillary tumours (FGFR3 mutation; loss of heterozygosity at 3q, 5q, 10q, 13q
    and 17p). Papillary hyperplasia is cystoscopically indistinguishable from, and often adjacent to, low-
    grade papillary tumours.
  • Reactive atypia: nuclear abnormality on an inflamed background (chronic inflammation, regeneration,
    noxious stimuli), presenting with irritative symptoms; oedematous and inflamed endoscopically. Nuclei
    may be enlarged, but maturation and nuclear polarity are preserved. Not independently malignant,
    but coexists with dysplasia and CIS → investigate rigorously.
  • Atypia of unknown significance (AUS): used when reactive versus premalignant is unclear; lamina
    propria inflammation with atypia disproportionate to the inflammation. CK20 and CD44
    immunostaining help separate it from dysplasia. Not independently progressive; limited clinical utility.
  • Urothelial dysplasia: normal thickness with loss of cytoplasmic clearing (basal and intermediate cells),
    loss of polarity, nuclear enlargement, hyperchromasia and membrane irregularity; lamina propria
    usually normal. CK20 and p53 staining highlight dysplastic cells. Umbrella cells are retained. A marker
    of urothelial instability: 14–19% progress de novo, but with a prior bladder cancer dysplasia leads to
    CIS in 60%.

 MALIGNANT LESIONS
  • Urothelial carcinoma is the commonest malignancy of the urinary tract and the second commonest
    cause of death among genitourinary tumours.
  • NMIBC comprises about 70% of bladder urothelial carcinoma: low- and high-grade noninvasive
    papillary tumours, CIS, and lamina propria invasion (T1).
  • CIS: flat, noninvasive, high grade; severe atypia, loss of polarity, noncohesive large pleomorphic cells
    with clumped chromatin and abnormal mitoses. Loss of umbrella cells separates it from dysplasia. Its
    genetics (RB, TP53, PTEN) resemble high-grade invasive tumours — hence a precursor of invasive
    carcinoma — and it has no FGFR3 mutations, a lineage distinct from low-grade papillary tumours. CIS
    with an invasive tumour carries a 45–65% 5-year mortality.
  • Papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential (PUNLMP): very good prognosis, and
    whether it is "cancer" is debated. Papillary, >7 cells thick, minimal atypia, generally solitary, typically on
    the trigone; polarity maintained, nuclei mildly enlarged. Recurrences are nearly always PUNLMP or low
    grade; rare high-grade recurrences may be new tumours rather than progression.
  • Low-grade urothelial carcinoma: papillary with a fibrovascular stalk and frequent branching, enlarged
    cells, some atypia, occasional mitoses; chromosome 9 alterations almost universal, with FGFR3, PI3K
    or RAS mutations.
  • High-grade papillary carcinoma: fused papillae, disordered growth, numerous mitoses, pleomorphic
    cells with exaggerated nuclei; more than 80% invade the stroma if untreated — a precursor of invasive
    high-grade cancer.

  • Prostatic urethral urothelial cancer accompanies bladder cancer in 90% of cases, but among primary

 MOLECULAR BIOLOGY
  • Recurrent alterations: FGFR3, the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway, mitogen-activated protein
    kinase (MAPK) signalling, cell cycle regulation, and hedgehog and WNT signalling.
  • Cell cycle genes, mutated especially in MIBC: RB1 and CDKN2A inactivation is common; p53
    inactivation in up to 76% of MIBC (The Cancer Genome Atlas, TCGA); RB1 loss drives amplification and
    overexpression of the oncogene E2F3.
  • FGFR3 — the NMIBC hallmark, with deletions of 9p and 9q: activating point mutations in up to 80% of
    low-grade Ta versus 10–20% of MIBC. Normal urothelium expresses the IIIb isoform (binds FGF1); a
    switch to IIIc may drive oncogenesis by autocrine and paracrine signalling. FGFR3 fusions with TACC3
    or BAI1 are highly activated oncoproteins.
  • MAPK — RAS (HRAS or KRAS): first identified in the T24 bladder cancer cell line; mutually exclusive
    with FGFR3 because both lie on the RAS–MEK–ERK pathway; unlike FGFR3, equally frequent in NMIBC
    and MIBC.
  • PI3K — PIK3CA point mutations in 25% of NMIBC, fewer in MIBC; they co-occur with FGFR3. PTEN
    negatively regulates PI3K; PTEN loss in MIBC accompanies TP53 alteration and poor prognosis. Dual
    deletion of p53 and PTEN produces metastatic bladder cancer in transgenic mice; PTEN's protein (not
    lipid) phosphatase activity restrains invasion and metastasis.
  • WNT/β-catenin cooperates with PI3K/PTEN: organoid cultures show its role in proliferation, and
    activated β-catenin with PTEN deletion promotes bladder cancer.

 HISTOLOGIC VARIANTS OF UROTHELIAL CARCINOMA
  • Formerly "transitional cell carcinoma" because of its propensity to differentiate into other tumour
    types — SCC, adenocarcinoma, clear cell carcinoma.
  • The variant spectrum now includes distinct growth patterns, altered differentiation, stromal reactions
    and unique responses to treatment and external stimuli.

 Micropapillary Variant
  • 0.7–2.2% of urothelial tumours (first described 1994); strong male predominance.
  • Advanced at diagnosis: more than half are T3 or T4, and survival is lower than with other histologies.
    Even at lower stage it carries a high progression rate from NMIBC to MIBC and high subsequent
    metastasis despite treatment.
  • Histology: small tight clusters or thin papillae of atypical cells with eosinophilic to clear cytoplasm,
    surrounded by prominent retraction artefact.
  • Any amount of micropapillary component defines the tumour as micropapillary and dictates
    management.
  • Surgery is the most effective therapy at all stages: transurethral resection plus bacillus Calmette–
    Guérin (BCG) is ineffective unless the tumour is completely resected, and early cystectomy is
    encouraged for NMIBC (better cancer-specific survival than TUR plus BCG). In MIBC, NAC appears
    ineffective → immediate cystectomy.
  • Molecular: ERBB2 amplification, activation of miR-296 and RUVBL1 targets; evolves through a luminal
    pathway (PPAR-γ enrichment, p63 suppression); a subset with wild-type p53 activation is the most
    aggressive.

 Sarcomatoid Variant
  • 0.3–0.6% of bladder cancers; carcinosarcoma and sarcomatoid carcinoma, once separate, share a
    common clonal origin.
  • Large infiltrative masses at higher stage; poor cancer-specific survival, and worse than pure urothelial
    carcinoma stage for stage.
  • Poor chemotherapy response → immediate radical cystectomy where feasible, sometimes with
    intraoperative radiation therapy (IORT).

 Plasmacytoid Variant
  • <1% of urothelial tumours; the 2016 WHO classification includes the signet ring variant (cytoplasmic
    vacuoles with or without mucin).
  • Histology: eosinophilic cytoplasm with eccentric, enlarged, hyperchromatic nuclei.
  • Advanced at presentation, with deep muscle and perivesical invasion and characteristic intraperitoneal
    spread; driven by truncating CDH1 alterations (84% in one study).
  • Chemosensitive → NAC whenever possible, but relapse is common; median survival in nonmetastatic
    disease about 1.5 years (17.7 months).

 Nested Variant
  • Rare but aggressive; male-to-female ratio 6:1.
  • Mimics benign lesions: von Brunn nests, cystitis cystica, inverted papilloma.
  • Typically upstaged at cystectomy and locally advanced at presentation, yet stage-matched outcomes
    resemble pure urothelial carcinoma.

 Urothelial Carcinoma With Divergent Differentiation
  • Squamous differentiation is reported in 16–22% and probably underestimated — up to 60% have
    squamous elements; underreporting obscures its prognostic effect. It is associated with the basal
    subtype, more advanced stage and focal aggressiveness, and with recurrence and progression.
  • Glandular differentiation (small tubular or gland-like spaces) in about 10%: associated with high stage
    and clinical-to-pathological upstaging, but recurrence-free and overall survival match conventional
    urothelial carcinoma.

 NONUROTHELIAL MALIGNANCY
 Small Cell Carcinoma
  • A rare, poorly differentiated neuroendocrine neoplasm, primarily of lung but also prostate and
    bladder.
  • Presentation: usually haematuria; paraneoplastic hypercalcaemia, Cushing syndrome and sensory
    neuropathy reported.
  • Histology (Fig. 129.19): sheets and nests of basophilic cells with high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio,
    resembling lung small cell; chromogranin and synaptophysin positive.
  • Genetics: high somatic mutational burden (APOBEC-mediated); frequent TP53, RB1 and TERT promoter
    mutations. Evolutionary studies suggest dedifferentiation from urothelial carcinoma, not a shared
    lineage with lung small cell.
  • Aggressive and chemosensitive → platinum-based chemotherapy first, regardless of stage; cisplatin
    plus etoposide (the lung regimen) is the treatment of choice.
  • Sequence matters: primary cystectomy gives poor disease-free survival not rescued by adjuvant
    chemotherapy, whereas neoadjuvant chemotherapy yields markedly better cancer-specific survival.
    One series found stage-matched outcomes similar to urothelial carcinoma, with a possible adjuvant
    role.
  • Chemoradiation, historically preferred and still advocated by some as trimodal therapy, is less
    supported: chemotherapy plus cystectomy gives the highest response rates, and chemoradiation
    needs further study.

 Squamous Cell Cancer
  • Chronic infection with S. haematobium or other bacteria causes SCC; in endemic countries SCC is the
    majority of bladder cancer, with its own growth pattern and algorithm. In the US and Europe it is 2–5%.
  • Chronic inflammation is central: SCC in catheterised spinal cord injury patients has fallen substantially
    with the move to intermittent self-catheterisation, supporting the role of infection and foreign bodies.
  • Presentation: fewer nodal and distant metastases but more advanced local stage than urothelial
    carcinoma.
  • Radical cystectomy is the mainstay; NAC has no consensus. Preoperative radiation worsened survival
    (1970s–1980s) → use only if cystectomy is not planned. Adjuvant therapy and IORT lack consensus.
  • Molecular data are limited, mainly schistosomal; FGF2 is associated with aggressive features and worse
    outcome after cystectomy.

 Adenocarcinoma
  • Primary (pure glandular phenotype from bladder urothelium) or secondary — metastasis or, more
    commonly, direct extension from colon, prostate, endometrium or cervix. Urachal adenocarcinoma
    shares pathological and clinical features.

  • About one-third of bladder adenocarcinomas; arises from the allantoic remnant connecting bladder to
    umbilical cord. Although urachal remnants are lined by urothelium, the cancer is almost always
    adenocarcinoma.

    wall; no widespread cystitis cystica/glandularis; secondary source excluded.
  • TNM is unsuitable (nearly all are muscle invasive by location) → Sheldon staging.
  • Treatment: en bloc resection of the bladder dome, urachal ligament and umbilicus.
