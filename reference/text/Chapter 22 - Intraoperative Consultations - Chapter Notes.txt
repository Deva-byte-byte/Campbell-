 Chapter 22 — Intraoperative Consultations
   • Operative difficulty rises with disease, prior surgery, comorbidity and habitus; altered anatomy and
     unexpected findings raise error and complication rates however prepared the team.
   • Definition — a call from the primary surgeon to a colleague (same or other discipline) for help,
     prompted by unanticipated additional surgery, iatrogenic injury or unexpected findings; a daily event.
   • Scope: disease processes, iatrogenic genitourinary injury and trauma; non-iatrogenic trauma excluded
     (trauma chapters).
   • No formal didactics exist; the precepts are those of everyday surgery, and the challenge is applying
     judgement and ethics when the situation is unforeseen or inconvenient.
   • Consent gap — unexpected findings may have been discussed generally, but never the specifics of an
     unforeseen change of plan.
   • Consultant's role: advice only → operative help → complete takeover when the consultant's skillset
     best suits the remainder.
   • Constraints: no prior relationship, history or knowledge of the patient's values; information only from
     the primary surgeon ± family; the patient is unconscious and at their most vulnerable.
   • Injuries recognised postoperatively carry severe morbidity and far more complex management than
     those recognised at surgery.
   • Culture: finding an (especially occult) injury reflects vigilance and integrity; calling for help is
     courageous and patient-centred; the consultation is never the time for critique or blame. Injuries may
     be careless, unavoidable or intentional (oncological cure). Teamwork calms the theatre; being asked is
     an honour.
 Concept — The consultant inherits a patient who cannot speak, from a colleague under strain; every later
 rule — stakeholder involvement, deferral of irreversible choices, fact-only documentation — follows from
 protecting that absent patient without blaming the colleague.

 ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

 The Patient–Physician (and Family–Physician) Relationship
   • Accepting the consultation enters the consultant into a patient–physician relationship.
   • Often the critical part is how a life-affecting decision is made, not the procedure. If stable, pause to
     gather information — primary surgeon, record, family.
   • Decisions during surgery require full disclosure of the reason for consultation to family or surrogate, so
     that their choice is informed.
   • Such discussions build trust (anecdotal evidence), though the patient learns of them afterwards;
     involving many stakeholders minimises surprise.
   • Straightforward decisions may proceed without family discussion, followed by a joint postoperative
     discussion (primary surgeon + consultant) with patient and family → transparency and expectations for
     recovery and quality of life. The consultant may become the follow-up physician → establish trust
     early.

 DECISION MAKING
   • Mostly straightforward; a minority have no option free of significant risk. The consultant makes the
     assessment and plan: alter or keep the plan, abort, or add procedures.
   • Stakeholders (Fig. 22.1, intraoperative decision-making stakeholders): primary surgeon,
     anaesthesiologist, family or surrogate, urology colleagues — around the consulting urologist. The most
     important stakeholder, the patient, cannot participate, which the consultant must recognise and
     respect.
   • Discussing alternatives often makes the choice clear; calling colleagues confirms all options were
     considered.

 Surrogates.
   • May be absent or unreachable; the companion may not be the right decision maker. Most hospitals ask
     for a pre-designated surrogate → less guesswork, better knowledge of preferences.
   • Surrogates may decide contrary to the patient's wishes → strain on the patient–surrogate and patient–
     physician relationships, especially if recovery is complicated.
   • No surrogate, or an emergency → the physician acts in the patient's best interest.
 Disagreement between surgeons.
   • Uncommon. Default: defer to the primary surgeon (pre-existing relationship) — unless deference is
     potentially harmful; then the consultant, whose expertise differs fundamentally, acts in the patient's
     best interest.
   • Discussion usually yields consensus; persistent disagreement → escalate to department chairs or
     hospital medical director.

   • Ultimately the consultant is the decision maker: others inform, but only the urologist has the expertise
     for the urological problem; act in the patient's perceived best interests, balancing risk and benefit.
 Concept — Every intraoperative choice trades autonomy against the harm of delay: reversible, low-
 morbidity acts proceed now, while irreversible, life-altering reconstruction waits for the patient unless
 waiting is itself the greater harm.

 DOCUMENTATION
   • Resist editorialising; record observable facts only.
   • Appropriate: a urethral stricture as the cause of difficult catheterisation (directly observed); the
     primary surgeon's fact-based account of mechanism when it changes management — cautery versus
     cold-shears ureteric injury alters the plan and, if directly observed, is documented.
   • Inappropriate: guessing the cause or assigning blame. Calling an injury "careless" or "avoidable" in an
     operative note is itself careless and avoidable.
   • The consultant must scrub in, assess personally and write a separate summary of findings; relying on
     the primary surgeon to document is irresponsible and incomplete.
   • Even if no injury is found and no intervention made, the consultant's primary observations must be
     recorded — lack of documentation leaves the consultant vulnerable to legal action.
 Concept — The note records what the consultant saw, never why it happened: fact protects both patient
 and consultant, conjecture protects neither.

   KEY POINTS — ETHICS, DECISION MAKING, DOCUMENTATION
    • Consultation creates a new patient–physician relationship; full disclosure of its reason is critical.
    • Include all relevant stakeholders in difficult intraoperative decisions where possible.
    • Temporise or act: weigh patient condition, procedure morbidity, long-term consequences,
      reversibility.
    • Complex reconstruction, with selected exceptions, is deferred with temporising measures until SDM
      is possible.
    • Documentation is fact-based, free of conjecture, and records the consultant's own observations and
      assessment.


 SURGICAL TIME OUT
   • The surgical time out (preoperative safety checklist) has no defined role in consultation: a traditional
     time out is not feasible beforehand, because procedure and supplies are unknown until the field is
     explored → once information is gathered and family consulted, review the plan with the whole
     theatre.
   • Patient condition, absent from the standard time out, is added: it varies widely at the time of
     consultation, and its inclusion confirms the team agrees the patient can tolerate the extra procedure
     and reaffirms the plan.

 URETHRAL INJURY
   • Catheter-related male urethral injury in theatre is common — catheters are passed across disciplines
     by operators of variable experience.
   • Contributors: urethral stricture, prior surgery or pelvic radiotherapy, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH),
     operator inexperience.
   • Mostly inconsequential; occasionally: deferral of the planned surgery, voiding dysfunction, prolonged
     gross haematuria, acute renal failure, urethral stricture, prolonged catheterisation.
   • Adds cost and length of stay; catheter duration drives bacteriuria and catheter-associated urinary tract
     infection (CAUTI), affecting reimbursement. Severe injuries rarely need surgery.
   • History is central — prior cystoscopic or abdominal surgery, prostate cancer, prostatectomy,
     radiotherapy, anticoagulation, planned operation → dictates catheter choice, cystoscopic placement,
     and suprapubic risk.
   • Female urethral injury occurs in pelvic reconstructive surgery, sometimes with concomitant bladder or
     ureteric injury → markedly more complex repair.

 Incidence
   • Theatre incidence unknown; in hospitalised patients 3.2–6.7 injuries per 1000 men catheterised.
   • Most men with an iatrogenic urethral injury suffer a complication of Clavien–Dindo grade 2 or higher.

 Mechanisms
   • Commonest: benign prostatic enlargement plus an inexperienced operator.
   • Two mechanisms: balloon inflation within the urethra, and false passage created by forcing the
     catheter tip. A tortuous course or stricture increases false passage.

   • Male: failed catheterisation ± blood per urethra or in catheter; cystoscopy confirms and gauges extent.

 Management
   • Male catheter injury: specific catheter choice → one blind attempt by the urologist (as in trauma) → if
     it fails, cystoscopy, guidewire into bladder, catheter over the wire → if stricture or injury precludes safe
     placement, percutaneous or open suprapubic catheter.
   • Duration of catheterisation depends on extent of injury, need for concomitant urethral dilatation, and
     postoperative condition.
   • Non-catheter urethral injury is rare — the urethra lies outside most operative fields → mainly
     transvaginal or transperineal reconstruction.
   • Female repair: vaginal approach carries fistula risk → interpose peritoneum, omentum or labial fat
     between anterior vaginal wall and bladder or urethra when fistula risk is anticipated.
 Concept — In men the urethra is injured by the catheter, in women by the trocar or dissection; anatomy
 predicts who is injured, and one blind attempt followed by vision is the universal rule.

 BLADDER INJURY
   • Evaluation and management overlap heavily with bladder trauma (Chapter 125); this section addresses
     iatrogenic injury.

 Incidence
   • The bladder is the most commonly injured organ in pelvic surgery.
   • The pelvis is a fixed space → hard exposure and retraction, little room. Distortion (tumour, prior
     surgery, radiotherapy, prolapse) and vaginal-approach reconstruction raise risk.

   • Pattern: grade rises with size and with the peritoneal compartment. Trap: an intraperitoneal tear under
     2 cm is grade III, not II; trigonal extension is grade V whatever the size.

 Diagnosis and Recognition
   • High index of suspicion. Indicators: visible laceration, visible Foley catheter, extravasation of fluid
     (urine) into the field, blood (and gas at laparoscopy) in the catheter bag.
   • Suspicion without a visible defect: instil fluid ± methylene blue via the catheter; contrast cystography if
     fluoroscopy available; cystoscopy — now standard of care after urethral sling surgery and
     hysterectomy.
   • Once bladder injury is found, exclude concomitant ureteric or orifice injury, especially with trigonal
     injury.

 Management
   • Repair sequence: debride devascularised urothelium or wall → close in one or more layers, absorbable
     suture → test by filling (allows extra sutures and exposes missed injuries) → urethral catheter plus
     intra-abdominal drain → drainage 7–14 days.
   • No routine suprapubic tube alongside a urethral catheter — no supporting data.
   • Postoperative cystography selective (mostly negative): doubtful integrity, complex repair or fistula
     concern — confirms no leak before catheter removal.
   • Injury suspected but not seen → cystotomy to find it.
   • Trigonal injury: establish ureteric integrity; anterior vaginal wall may be involved → fistula risk. When in
     doubt, reimplant the ureter — an unrepaired ureteric injury has serious consequences.
 Concept — Every bladder repair answers three questions — is it closed (fill test), is it drained (catheter and
 drain), are the ureters intact — and the third is the one missed at the trigone.

 URETERAL INJURY

 Incidence
   • Iatrogenic injury is the commonest cause of ureteric injury (75–80%), with every approach; rarely after
     spinal surgery (seldom recognised intraoperatively). Fixed pelvis, complex anatomy, prior surgery and
     radiotherapy displace and obscure the ureter.
   • Over 90% involve the distal third.
   • Gynaecological surgery causes the majority (>50%); colorectal, vascular and urological also. Some
     series now find endoscopy leading, but endoscopic avulsion is now rare with modern equipment.
     Minimally invasive surgery is thought to increase injury.

   • Most iatrogenic ureteric injuries go unnoticed: only about a third are recognised at open surgery,
     fewer still at laparoscopy.
   • Missed injury → pain, infection, azotaemia, fistula, reoperation; immediate recognition and repair
     gives better outcomes and fewer complications.
   • Signs: extravasation of fluid (urine), hydroureteronephrosis, a visible pre-placed ureteric catheter;
     subtle — wall discolouration, absent capillary refill.

 Management
   • Set by location, mechanism, patient and consultant's expertise; goals: preserve renal function,
     minimise morbidity.

     patients (grade C); extrapolated to acute iatrogenic injury.

   • Unstable patient → temporary diversion and delayed definitive repair: ligate ureter + percutaneous
     nephrostomy, or ureterostomy. Also if the consultant lacks the skill — though seek help to spare repeat
     procedures; first do no harm.

 Principles of Repair
 Same at every level: debride devascularised tissue → spatulated, tension-free, mucosa-to-mucosa
 anastomosis with absorbable suture → watertight closure → indwelling stent (keeps lumen patent,
 directs urine across rather than out of the anastomosis) → external drain → interpose omentum or
 peritonealise where possible to aid healing and prevent fistula.

 Ureteral Catheters
   • Requests (before colorectal or gynaecological surgery) vary by institution; for anticipated distortion —
     diverticulitis, large pelvic mass, prior radiotherapy or surgery.
   • Prophylactic catheters do not reduce the risk of ureteric injury, but aid recognition of injury when it
     occurs → less morbidity.
   • Risks: iatrogenic ureteric injury; added cost; more postoperative acute kidney injury (AKI), greater with
     bilateral than unilateral catheters — catheter-related ureteric oedema the likely cause.
   • Almost always anticipated → counsel preoperatively by a urologist; the primary surgeon's ethical duty
     is to reserve intraoperative consultation for unanticipated findings.
 Concept — Recognise, then let the level choose the repair: distal injuries go to the bladder, mid and
 proximal injuries are joined end to end, and anything needing irreversible reconstruction waits for the
 patient.

 RENAL INJURY

 Incidence
   • Mostly traumatic → renal consultation rare; iatrogenic injury rare but morbid.

 Mechanisms
   • Trauma: blunt and penetrating. Iatrogenic: haemorrhage from vascular or parenchymal injury —
     percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), renal biopsy, endopyelotomy, open abdominal and
     endovascular surgery.
   • PCNL is now the commonest cause of renovascular injury (0.9–3.0%).

 Diagnosis and Recognition
   • Mostly recognised postoperatively: gross haematuria, acute blood-loss anaemia, flank pain or
     haematoma, haemodynamic instability.
   • Most → conservative or selective embolisation; acute renal bleeding rarely reaches urological
     consultation (renal trauma: Chapter 125).

 Management
   • Renal trauma trend: conservative even for high grades. AUA (2017, grade B): immediate intervention
     (surgery or angioembolisation) only if haemodynamically unstable or only transiently responsive to
     resuscitation.
   • Iatrogenic injuries are mostly minor → supportive care.

 PLACENTAL CASES
   • Abnormal placental attachment classified by depth: accreta → increta → percreta, with increasing
     invasion and complexity; "placenta accreta" often denotes the whole spectrum. High risk of
     haemorrhage and morbidity.
   • Incidence rising, likely from increasing caesarean delivery (a known risk factor). Most accreta occurs
     with placenta praevia (low-lying placenta partly or wholly covering the cervix) → suspect and evaluate
     accreta in every praevia.
   • Antenatal diagnosis enables planning. Ultrasound is commonest; sensitivity data conflict — high in
     high-volume centres, yet most cases still go undetected before delivery → high clinical suspicion
     needed.
   • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI): unclear added accuracy, no clear change in management or
     outcome; may define depth of invasion in percreta. Imaging studies are biased (expert centres, high-
     risk patients).
   • Management: planned early caesarean at 34–38 weeks (avoids emergency surgery in labour);
     hysterectomy at the same time avoids dissecting the placenta; controlled caesarean hysterectomy =
     gold standard — less morbidity than attempted placental removal with uterine preservation. Other
     options: internal iliac artery embolisation, postoperative methotrexate.
   • Multidisciplinary team including urology, because percreta can invade bladder and ureter; commonest
     invasion site: posterior bladder.
 Urologist's roles.
   • Antenatal: evaluate suspected bladder percreta. Commonest presentation is bleeding — vaginal
     bleeding far more often than haematuria.
   • Cystoscopy is insensitive (mucosa may be normal despite muscularis invasion) but still warranted for
     evaluation and planning. Never biopsy suspected percreta with cystoscopic abnormality — risk of
     exsanguinating haematuria.
   • Intraoperative: consultation when accreta was unknown or antenatal care absent. Cystotomy may be
     intentional or incidental → complete resection of placental tissue, two-layer closure, consider omental
     interposition against fistula → urethral catheter on dependent drainage ± pelvic drain → cystography
     before catheter removal per repair quality and surgeon preference.
   • Subtotal hysterectomy with invasive tissue left may be chosen when removal would cost anatomy or
     function (e.g., defunctionalised bladder capacity, need for ileal ureter); methotrexate has been used to
     shrink residual tissue — but haemorrhagic shock has occurred with retained placenta → standard of
     care remains removal of all placental products.
   • Ureteric injury or involvement in up to 7%. Ureteric catheters may reduce ureteric injury and early
     morbidity in placental cases, elective and emergency.
   • Suspected ureteric injury → manage as above: do what is best for the patient and within the
     consultant's skillset.
