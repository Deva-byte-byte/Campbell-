PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF PAEDIATRIC URINARY TRACT OBSTRUCTION
 • Definition: any anatomic or functional abnormality that impedes urine flow, injuring the kidney and
   lower urinary tract; may occur anywhere from calyces to urethral meatus, upper and/or lower tract.
 • Four classifying axes: acute vs chronic · partial vs complete · intrinsic vs extrinsic · unilateral vs bilateral.
 • The governing risk is permanent damage to the developing kidney.

Epidemiology
 • Overall incidence ≈ 1 in 500 live births worldwide.
 • Fetal lower urinary tract obstruction (LUTO): ≈ 2–3 per 10,000 fetuses (per-pregnancy figure under
   Urinary Tract Sites).
 • Prevalence varies by population and region; severity forms a spectrum.

Association With End-Stage Renal Disease and Kidney Transplantation
 • Untreated severe obstruction → chronic kidney disease (CKD) → end-stage renal disease (ESRD).
 • High-grade obstruction: lifetime ESRD risk up to 30%.
 • ESRD means lifelong dialysis or transplantation, each with significant morbidity — hence the urgency of
   early detection.

 KEY POINTS
   • Urinary tract obstruction is relatively common in children.
   • Severe uncorrected obstruction → CKD, ESRD or renal failure.

Concept — paediatric obstruction strikes a kidney that is still developing: the same pressure that a mature
kidney partly tolerates can permanently cap nephron number, so the subject is organised around detecting
early and decompressing before development is lost.

 • Purpose: obstruction is not only congenital — intraluminal, neoplastic, functional and extrinsic causes
   apply at every level.
 • Pattern: calculi appear at both levels; the crossing vessel is filed as extrinsic although it produces UPJO.
 • Traps: detrusor hypertrophy is a lower tract cause of an upper tract (UVJ) obstruction — the thickened
   wall narrows the intramural ureter; neurogenic bladder obstructs without an anatomical lesion.

 KEY POINTS
   • Manifestations depend on location, severity, age at onset and duration; location dictates the
     symptoms.
   • Prenatal detection is by routine US; postnatal presentation is more variable, from asymptomatic to
     life-threatening.
   • LUTO carries significant long-term morbidity and mortality; UPJO accounts for the vast majority of
     upper tract obstruction.

Concept — LUTO is a bilateral, whole-fetus disease (kidneys and lungs fail together through loss of amniotic
fluid), whereas upper tract obstruction is usually a unilateral, organ-level problem with a normal
contralateral kidney — which is why LUTO dominates mortality despite being far rarer than UPJO.

DEFINITION OF OBSTRUCTION
 • Obstruction is an impedance of urinary flow at any level, involving one or several components.

Partial                                                               High-grade / complete
Fewer signs and symptoms; may resolve spontaneously                   More signs and symptoms; requires intervention

 • Causes: congenital malformation, structural abnormality, inflammation, tumour, extrinsic compression
   (Table 51.1).
 • Spectrum: incidental imaging finding → recurrent UTI, retention, hydronephrosis, impaired renal
   function.
 • Consequences: untreated severe obstruction → CKD; when prenatal, also pulmonary hypoplasia and
   limb deformity.
 • Management aims: relieve severe obstruction → preserve renal function → prevent long-term
   complications, by conservative, pharmacological, endoscopic or surgical means chosen by cause, severity
   and clinical status.

 KEY POINTS
   • Obstruction may be partial or high grade, at any level, intrinsic or extrinsic, congenital or acquired.

DIAGNOSIS OF URINARY TRACT OBSTRUCTION
 • The central task: separate children who need surgery to preserve renal function from those who will
   resolve spontaneously.
 • Imaging and laboratory studies grade severity and time treatment.

Timing of Obstruction: Prenatal Diagnostic Testing
 • Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) are among the commonest prenatal US
   anomalies: 1 in 250 – 1 in 1000 pregnancies.
 • Severity is judged by degree of hydronephrosis, associated urinary tract changes and amniotic fluid
   volume; fetal blood and urine biomarkers and proteomics may add prognostic information.
Imaging
Ultrasound. The primary prenatal modality — cost-effective, widely available, part of standard anomaly
screening; grades obstruction by APD, calyceal dilation, parenchymal thickness and appearance, and bladder
and ureteral abnormalities.
 • Landmarks: kidneys visible from 9 weeks; bladder filling by 12 weeks — not seen by 15 weeks is
   abnormal; normal fetal ureters are not visualised, so a visible ureter is dilated.
 • UTD classification: unified prenatal and postnatal grading from eight societies (Society for Pediatric
   Urology, Society for Fetal Urology, American Society of Pediatric Nephrology, Society for Maternal-Fetal
   Medicine, American College of Radiology, American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine, Society for
   Pediatric Radiology, Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound), stratified before and after 28 weeks.
 • European Rare Kidney Disease Reference Network (ERKNet) consensus, May 2022: APD is the most
   reliable parameter; obstructive uropathy is considered at APD ≥ 7 mm at 16–27 weeks and ≥ 10 mm at ≥
   28 weeks.
 • Below these thresholds, obstruction is not considered unless there is megacystis and/or reduced
   amniotic fluid.
Magnetic resonance imaging. Fetal MRI is a selective adjunct to US (Fig. 51.2).
 • Indications: US technically limited (oligo- or anhydramnios, maternal obesity); complex anatomy in
   severe obstruction; planning fetal intervention.
 • Advantages: not limited by fetal position; multiplanar. Three-dimensional reconstruction and functional
   renal imaging are developing.
 • Fig. 51.2 — T2-weighted fetal MRI of cloacal malformation: dilated ventral bladder, with uterus didelphys
   and massive hydrometrocolpos behind it.
Laboratory Studies
Bladder taps. Fetal vesicocentesis — US-guided aspiration of fetal urine to distinguish obstructive from non-
obstructive hydronephrosis and predict postnatal renal function; tests sodium, chloride and calcium (most
important), osmolarity and low-molecular-weight proteins (β2-microglobulin, proteomics). Supporting
evidence is still accruing.

Pressure-Flow Studies
The Whitaker test. Invasive assessment of upper tract obstruction.
 • Indications: (1) equivocal less invasive tests; (2) suspected obstruction with poor renal function; (3)
   suspected intermittent obstruction; (4) gross dilation with positive diuresis renogram.
 • Technique: percutaneous access → high-rate perfusion of the renal pelvis with saline or dilute contrast
   → serial renal pelvic and bladder pressures.
 • Interpretation: unobstructed → drains freely, no appreciable pressure rise; obstructed → pressure > 12
   cm H2O or a continuous rise.
Uroflowmetry. Measures voided volume, maximum flow rate (Qmax) and flow curve to diagnose lower tract
dysfunction or obstruction.

 KEY POINTS
   • Prenatal: imaging (US, MRI) and laboratory studies (bladder taps, urinary markers, biomarkers).
   • Postnatal: laboratory studies plus primary, secondary and tertiary imaging, as tabulated above.

Concept — each postnatal study answers one question — US for anatomy, MAG3 for drainage, DMSA for
scar, VCUG for the bladder outlet and reflux — invasive or irradiating tests are escalations used only when
these fail to separate obstruction from dilation.

PATHOLOGICAL CHANGES OF THE URINARY TRACT WITH OBSTRUCTION
 • Driven by mechanical stress, inflammatory responses and molecular signalling.

Lower Urinary Tract
Pathological Changes in the Bladder
 • BOO → detrusor hypertrophy (larger fibres, more mass) and hyperplasia (more smooth muscle cells) →
   wall thickening and reduced compliance.
 • Changes span urothelium, suburothelium, detrusor cells, extracellular matrix (ECM) and nerves.
 • Remodelling stimuli: cyclic stretch → raised hydrostatic and cyclic hydrodynamic pressure and hypoxia.
 • Three-stage remodelling: hypertrophy → compensation → decompensation.
 • Compliance is mediated by the ECM: chronic inflammation releases interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumour necrosis
   factor-α (TNF-α) and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) → ↑ collagen and fibronectin →
   fibrosis → poorly compliant, hostile bladder.
 • High-pressure bladder (> 40 cm H2O) → risk of renal damage and failure.
Concept — bladder failure in obstruction is a matrix disease, not only a muscle disease: hypertrophy
compensates, but inflammatory collagen removes compliance, and lost compliance — pressure above 40 cm
H2O — transmits injury to the kidneys.

Haemodynamic Changes With Renal Obstruction
 • Sequence: urine production continues → proximal pressure rises → net filtration pressure falls →
   reduced perfusion and ischaemic injury.
 • Bilateral obstruction or a solitary kidney → more profound disease.
 • GFR and renal blood flow fall in direct relation to the high-pressure system, which compensatory
   hypertrophy and smooth muscle stretch perpetuate as hydronephrosis.
 • Ischaemic injury — inflammation, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis — begins within 2 hours of
   complete obstruction.

Effects of Obstruction on Renal Tubular Function
 • Sustained obstruction → hyposthenuria (concentrating defect) and impaired acidification → polyuria
   and electrolyte derangement.

Egress of Urine From the Obstructed Kidney
 • Partial vs complete obstruction sets how far egress is compromised; retained urine → progressive

 KEY POINTS
   • Changes in both tracts are caused by mechanical stress, inflammation and molecular signalling.
   • Lower tract: detrusor hypertrophy and hyperplasia with deleterious ECM change. Upper tract:
     impaired nephrogenesis and abnormal development.
   • Chronic obstruction → scarring, impaired tubular and concentrating function, altered ion transport;
     haemodynamic change; reduced perfusion → inflammation, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis.

Concept — the age of the kidney decides the injury: the immature kidney dies and fails to develop, the
mature kidney proliferates then scars — and in both, falling perfusion and transporter loss explain the
polyuria, acidosis and salt wasting seen clinically.

CLINICAL IMPACT OF RENAL OBSTRUCTION
Hypertension
 • Obstruction causes secondary hypertension; renal parenchymal disease accounts for 60–80% of
   secondary hypertension in children.

Bilateral ureteral obstruction        Unilateral ureteral obstruction
≈ 77% hypertensive                    ≈ 20% hypertensive; renin secretion lateralises to the affected kidney in most

 • Mechanism: ↑ intrarenal pressure → ↓ perfusion and GFR → renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system
   (RAAS) activation → ↑ angiotensin II and aldosterone → vasoconstriction and sodium retention.

Compensatory Renal Growth
 • Significant functional loss → compensatory hypertrophy driven by insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1),
   TGF-β1 and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF): glomerular enlargement and tubular hypertrophy —
   glomerulomegaly, not nephrogenesis.

Renal Recovery
Chronic renal insufficiency risk. Injury begins within 2 hours; prolonged obstruction → scarring and atony →
end-stage renal failure over days to months. Treatment can reverse damage; incomplete recovery is
common.
Acute-on-chronic renal injury. Acute obstruction on pre-existing CKD → rapid deterioration into acute kidney
injury (AKI).
Renal reserve. The capacity to raise function under demand — compromised by obstruction, limiting the
response to further insults.

Management of Oliguria
 • In severe obstruction or AKI: treat the cause, optimise fluid and electrolytes, support renal perfusion.

Management of Postobstructive Diuresis
 • Monitor intake and output, correct electrolytes, replace fluid as needed.
 • Usually resolves within 24 hours; seldom lasts beyond 48 hours.
 • Do not overcorrect — over-replacement perpetuates POD.
 • Risk factors after valve ablation: oligohydramnios (prolonged, severe obstruction); preterm birth (low
   aquaporin-2 expression); raised serum creatinine (renal impairment).

Management of Chronic Kidney Disease
 • Blood pressure control, dietary modification and medication to slow progression; severe disease or ESRD
   → peritoneal dialysis, haemodialysis or transplantation.

Emerging Therapeutic Options With Precision Medicine
 • Individualised treatment based on patient characteristics and genetics may allow more targeted
   interventions.

 KEY POINTS
   • Treatment depends on location, aetiology and duration of obstruction.
