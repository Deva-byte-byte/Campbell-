Chapter 95 — Urinary Lithiasis: Etiology, Epidemiology,
and Pathogenesis
 • Stone disease is ancient but has changed its site and its sex: with Westernisation, stone formation has
   migrated from the lower to the upper urinary tract, and a disease that historically favoured males is
   becoming increasingly sex-neutral.
 • Minimally invasive and non-invasive techniques have transformed stone removal, but surgery does not
   alter the course of the disease — the stone is removed, the tendency to form stones is not.
 • Cost: annual United States (US) expenditure on insured patients with nephrolithiasis exceeded $10 billion
   in 2012, a nearly fivefold rise since 2000.
 • Because recurrence is frequent, a medical prophylactic programme is desirable, and that requires
   understanding of cause, epidemiology and pathogenesis.
Concept — Stone treatment is two separate problems: removal is surgical, recurrence is metabolic, and the
rest of this chapter exists to explain the metabolic half.

EPIDEMIOLOGY OF RENAL CALCULI

Prevalence and Incidence
 • Lifetime prevalence 1–15%, varying with age, sex, race and geography.
 • Regional prevalence: North America 7–13%, Europe 5–9%, Asia 1–5%.
 • National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES): linear rise in US adult prevalence 1976–
   2010; a transient dip (2007–2008 to 2011–2012) was followed by renewed rise to about 10% in 2015–
   2016; age-standardised prevalence 2007–2016 ≈ 9.3%.
 • 12-month incidence (2015–2018) ≈ 2.1%, about 2000 stones per 100,000 adults.
 • The rise is global (Europe, Japan, US) and partly a detection artefact — more computed tomography (CT)
   finds more asymptomatic calculi.

Sex
 • Historically male-predominant: by inpatient, outpatient and emergency-department indicators, males
   were affected two to three times more often than females.
 • The gap is narrowing:
      ◦ US inpatient discharges 1997–2002: female discharges rose, male discharges fell; the male-to-female
        ratio fell from 1.7 to 1.3.
      ◦ NHANES 2007–2018: prevalence rose in females, unchanged in males — persisting after adjustment
        for sociodemographic and dietary factors.
      ◦ Population data 1997–2012: female incidence rose about 15% per 5 years; male incidence stable; the
        steepest female rise was in 10- to 19-year-olds.
 • Females carry more stone-related morbidity (retrospective data): more positive urine cultures,
   nephrostomy placement, intensive care admission and longer stays.

Race and Ethnicity
 • NHANES 2007–2016 prevalence by group (highest to lowest): non-Hispanic White > Hispanic > non-
   Hispanic Black > non-Hispanic Asian.
 • Trend: prevalence rose in White and (slightly) Hispanic groups; stable in Black and Asian groups.

Age
 • Paediatric stones are rising at 5–10% per year; historically uncommon under age 20.
 • Paediatric incidence peaks in female adolescents — the reverse of the adult sex pattern; young females
   are also more often hospitalised.
 • Population data (Olmsted County, 1984–2008): overall paediatric rise about 4% per year, about 6% per
   year in 12- to 17-year-olds.
 • Proposed drivers of the paediatric rise: obesity and metabolic syndrome reaching younger generations,
   or changes in the exposome rather than genetics or comorbidity alone.
 • Adult incidence peaks in the fourth to sixth decades; prevalence rises with age in both sexes — in males
   highest over 80 years.
 • Females have lower prevalence than males at every age except 20–39 years, where female prevalence is
   higher.
 • Recent NHANES: female prevalence rose only in those under 60; no rise in females over 60.
 • Bimodal female distribution: a second peak in the sixth decade at menopause, attributed to loss of
   oestrogen.
      ◦ Oestrogen is protective — it enhances renal calcium reabsorption and reduces bone resorption.
      ◦ Females have lower urinary calcium and calcium oxalate and brushite saturation than males until
        beyond age 50; oestrogen-treated postmenopausal females have lower urinary calcium and
        saturation than untreated ones.
Concept — Age and sex patterns are largely hormonal and metabolic: oestrogen holds urinary calcium down
until menopause, while obesity and metabolic syndrome are pulling stone disease into younger, and
increasingly female, populations.

Geography and Climate
 • Prevalence follows environmental risk: highest in hot, arid or dry climates (mountains, desert, tropics),
   although genetic and dietary factors may outweigh geography.
 • US veteran data: calcium oxalate stones highest in the Southeast, uric acid stones highest in the East.
 • Prevalence rises from north to south and from west to east, highest in the Southeast (the "stone belt");
   ambient temperature and sunlight are independent predictors.
 • Seasonal variation: peak in summer (July–September), within 1–2 months of maximal mean
   temperature — via perspiration fluid losses and possibly sunlight-induced vitamin D. Confirmed
   worldwide by systematic review.
 • Short latency: once mean daily temperature exceeds 10 °C, stone presentations rise within the ensuing
   10 days.
 • Military translocation studies (a natural experiment):
      ◦ Soldiers in desert postings — more renal colic in summer.
      ◦ Personnel deployed to Kuwait and Iraq — mean time to stone formation ≈ 93 days.

 • Heat response is mainly male: prevalence rises more steeply per degree in males, and heat-related
   emergency presentation rises more in males and most at 40–69 years (confounders: sunlight, occupation,
   hydration).
 • Global warming projections: modelled temperature–prevalence relationships (peaked/non-linear in one
   data set, linear in another) predict 1–1.5 million additional lifetime cases by 2050 and a northward
   expansion of the stone belt; a further projection anticipates about 10,000 additional emergency
   presentations between 2025 and 2089.
 • Urban heat islands: urbanisation, architecture and reduced vegetation make cities warmer — a proposed
   contributor, not yet tested against fluid intake, diet and socioeconomic status.
Concept — Climate acts indirectly — heat drives low urine volume and sunlight drives vitamin D-mediated
hypercalciuria — so geography is really a proxy for these two urinary effects, predominantly in males.

Occupation
 • Heat exposure and dehydration are occupational risk factors:
      ◦ Royal Navy — cooks and engine-room personnel had the highest stone rates.
      ◦ Steelworkers in hot areas — markedly higher stone incidence than those at normal temperatures,
        with low urine volume and hypocitraturia.
      ◦ Glass-plant workers exposed to heat — lower urine volume and pH, higher uric acid and specific
        gravity, hence high uric acid saturation; stone formers among them had a strikingly high proportion
        of uric acid stones.
 • Sedentary occupations (managerial, professional) carry increased risk, reason unclear:
      ◦ consistent with higher risk in affluent individuals and societies (richer diet and lifestyle);
      ◦ alternatively, inactivity increases bone turnover and urinary calcium — the same mechanism seen in
        astronauts under weightlessness, where exercise to limit bone demineralisation is the best
        prevention.
 • Restricted bathroom access (taxi drivers, operating-room staff) increases risk.

Obesity, Diabetes, and Metabolic Syndrome
 • Stone prevalence and incident risk rise with weight and body mass index (BMI) in both sexes — the
   association is stronger in females (two large prospective cohorts).
 • Obesity and weight gain are independent risk factors, not explained by diet alone; confirmed in further
   cohorts and claims data.
 • Women's Health Initiative Observational Study (postmenopausal females without prior stones): higher
   BMI, less physical activity and energy intake > 2200 kcal/day each independently raised incident risk —
   supporting diet and exercise components of weight-loss counselling in prevention.

 • Metabolic syndrome (visceral obesity with hyperlipidaemia, hypertriglyceridaemia, hyperglycaemia and/
   or hypertension) increases stone risk, with risk rising with the number of metabolic traits; it is also a
   precursor of type 2 diabetes mellitus.
 • Diabetes and stones — a bidirectional, sex-specific link:
      ◦ Diabetes predicts incident stones in females but not males (after adjusting for BMI, diet and
        thiazides).
      ◦ A stone history predicts incident diabetes in both sexes; stone formers are about 1.3 times more
        likely to be diagnosed with diabetes within 5 years.
      ◦ In asymptomatic stones followed by CT, fasting glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL predicted stone growth —
        suggesting glycaemic control may reduce recurrence.
 • Unifying mechanism: insulin resistance:
      ◦ Obesity and insulin resistance → low urine pH → uric acid stones.
      ◦ Hyperinsulinaemia → hypercalciuria → calcium stones.
      ◦ Overweight (BMI 25–30 kg/m²) is already associated with lower urine pH and higher urinary sodium,
        uric acid and calcium.
      ◦ Abnormalities are harder to correct with rising BMI: response to potassium citrate for low pH and
        hypocitraturia falls as BMI rises, requiring more dose adjustment.
 • Hepatic link to oxalate: in a mouse model, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) down-regulated
   alanine-glyoxylate aminotransferase (AGXT) — the enzyme deficient in primary hyperoxaluria type 1
   (PH1) — increasing oxalate production; a proposed explanation for calcium oxalate stones in diabetes,
   obesity and metabolic syndrome.
 • Higher BMI → more urinary oxalate, uric acid, sodium and phosphorus.

Concept — Obesity, diabetes and metabolic syndrome converge on insulin resistance, which lowers urine pH
(uric acid stones) and raises promoter excretion (calcium oxalate stones) — so the stone is a urinary marker of
a systemic metabolic state.

Cardiovascular Disease
 • Hypertension: meta-analysis shows stone formers have a significantly increased risk of hypertension,
   stronger in females.
      ◦ Proposed shared dietary exposures: calcium, sodium, potassium.
      ◦ Hypertensive individuals have higher urinary calcium, uric acid, oxalate and calcium oxalate
        supersaturation; hypertensive stone formers excrete about 25 mg/day more calcium than
        normotensive stone formers.
 • Heart disease — association, causation unproven:
      ◦ Stone history associated with higher incidence of myocardial infarction (MI), independent of chronic
        kidney disease (CKD).
      ◦ Stone history associated with subclinical carotid atherosclerosis in young people.
      ◦ In three large prospective cohorts, stones predicted heart disease in both female cohorts but not the
        male cohort.

      ◦ Large population cohort (> 3 million): stone formers had higher risk of acute MI and stroke, greater in
        younger individuals (< 50 years) and in females.
 • Candidate mechanisms:
      ◦ Dyslipidaemia (high total cholesterol, high triglycerides, low high-density lipoprotein (HDL)) alters
        urine chemistry towards stone formation.
      ◦ Calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) dysfunction — a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed in kidney and
        vasculature — a molecular bridge.
      ◦ Oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction and inflammation.

Chronic Kidney Disease
 • Stone history is associated with CKD after controlling for confounders (case–control study).
 • Stone formers, especially with BMI ≥ 27 kg/m², have a lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)
   and are nearly twice as likely to have stage III CKD.
 • Prospective cohorts (insured, predominantly White) confirm higher incident CKD.
 • Stones associated with CKD and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) requiring dialysis — accounted for
   largely by females; no association in males.
 • Symptomatic stone formers have increased ESRD risk, possibly from repeated obstruction.
 • Causal versus shared-risk-factor relationship remains unresolved.
Concept — Across hypertension, cardiovascular disease and CKD the pattern repeats: the association is real,
stronger in females, and not proven causal — stones and these diseases probably share a metabolic soil.

Water
 • Fluid intake is inversely related to incident stones (two large observational studies).
 • Landmark trial — Borghi (1996), randomised trial in first-time idiopathic calcium stone formers: high
   fluid intake raised urine volume and cut recurrence to 12% versus 27% in controls given no advice.
 • Water hardness (calcium carbonate content):
      ◦ Some studies found fewer stones in "hard"-water regions; others found no difference.
      ◦ Hardness correlates with urinary calcium and citrate, but not with stone episodes.
      ◦ Systematic review (1989–2018): hard water and mineral water raise calcium intake (and potentially
        urinary calcium), yet the net effect is reduced calcium stone formation, putatively from high
        magnesium and bicarbonate — but studies were heterogeneous, no meta-analysis, so interpret with
        caution.

Concept — Volume is the one universally effective modifier — proven by randomised trial — whereas water
composition is a weak, conflicting and poorly evidenced influence.

 KEY POINTS: EPIDEMIOLOGY
   • Upper-tract stones are commoner in males, but the gap is narrowing.
   • White individuals have the highest incidence compared with Asian, Hispanic and Black individuals.
   • Geographic variability, with highest prevalence in the Southeast (US).
   • Risk correlates with weight and BMI.

PHYSICOCHEMISTRY AND PATHOGENESIS
 • Stone formation is a cascade along the nephron: urine becomes supersaturated → ions precipitate as
   crystals or nuclei → crystals either pass in the urine or are retained at anchoring sites → growth and
   aggregation → stone.

State of Saturation
 • Concentration product (CP) — the product of the concentrations of a salt's pure chemical components
   (e.g. CP = [Na⁺][Cl⁻]).
 • Thermodynamic solubility product (Ksp) — the CP at saturation, where dissolved and crystalline phases
   are in equilibrium; adding crystals beyond this causes precipitation unless pH or temperature change.
 • Formation product (Kf) — the CP at which the salt can no longer be held in solution and crystals form
   spontaneously.
 • Urine holds salts above Ksp without precipitating because of inhibitors and other molecules — this
   supersaturated-but-stable state is metastable.

 • How crystals still form in metastable urine — three routes:
    1. Local CP exceeds Kf in parts of the nephron long enough for nucleation.
    2. Obstruction or stasis prolongs transit time.
    3. Heterogeneous nucleation — impurities adsorb crystal components in a lattice-like geometry, needing
       much less energy than homogeneous nucleation.
 • Estimating saturation:
      ◦ Activity product ratio — accounts for urine pH and ionic activities of all major ion species and overall
        ionic strength.
      ◦ EQUIL 2 — computer program, commonly used; gives the relative saturation ratio (RSR), also called
        concentration product ratio (CPR) = urine CP ÷ Ksp of the salt.
      ◦ RSR < 1 → crystals dissolve; RSR > 1 → crystals form and grow.
 • Lowering RSR:
      ◦ Reduce urinary concentration of stone components (calcium, oxalate), reduce filtered load, or
        increase tubular reabsorption.
      ◦ Complexation — citrate binds calcium, cutting free ionic calcium.
      ◦ pH manipulation works for phosphate (strongly pH-dependent) but not for oxalate — oxalic acid is a
        strong acid (pK ≈ 4), so physiological pH changes barely alter oxalate.

 • Calcium versus oxalate — which matters more?
      ◦ Historical view: oxalate dominates — a rise in oxalate raised calcium oxalate saturation more than a
        rise in calcium, and calcium-driven saturation plateaued below Kf whereas oxalate-driven saturation
        did not.
      ◦ Revised view: equal contributors — the relative effect depends on the stability constant chosen; with
        the accepted constant (2.746 × 10³, used in EQUIL 2) calcium and oxalate have equivalent effects (Fig.
        95.4). Re-analysis with JESS confirmed equivalence, though dependence was less marked.
      ◦ Therapeutic corollary: lowering either calcium or oxalate reduces calcium oxalate saturation.
Concept — Every prevention strategy is an attempt to push urine down out of the metastable zone — by
dilution, by lowering calcium or oxalate (equally effective), by complexation with citrate, or (for phosphate
and uric acid, never oxalate) by changing pH.

Nucleation and Crystal Growth, Aggregation, and Retention
 • Normal urine holds calcium oxalate at about four times its aqueous solubility.
 • Promoters of calcium oxalate supersaturation: low volume, low citrate; high calcium, oxalate,
   phosphate, uric acid.
 • With inhibitors present, calcium oxalate precipitates only at 7–11 times its solubility.
 • Homogeneous nucleation — nuclei form in pure solution; nuclei = earliest crystal structures that will not
   dissolve.
      ◦ Small nuclei are unstable — below a critical size, dissolution is favoured.
      ◦ Nuclei persist if driving force (supersaturation) and stability are favourable and lag time to nucleation
        is short relative to nephron transit.
      ◦ Inhibitors (e.g. citrate) destabilise nuclei; promoters stabilise them by offering a binding surface.
 • In vivo, nucleation is usually heterogeneous — on epithelial cells, cell debris or other crystals.
 • Nephron transit ≈ 5–7 minutes — too short for a single crystal to grow large enough to occlude a tubule,
   but aggregation of many nuclei can produce occluding particles within minutes.
 • Inhibitors can block growth or aggregation at each step (see the inhibitor table).

Randall Plaque
 • Randall (1937) found subepithelial papillary plaque in 17% of autopsy kidneys; proposed an origin in the
   basement membrane, with erosion through the papillary surface creating a nidus.
 • Endoscopy: papillary plaques in 74% of stone formers versus 43% of controls.
 • Papillary biopsy at percutaneous nephrolithotomy in idiopathic calcium oxalate stone formers localised
   the sequence: basement membrane of the thin limbs of the loop of Henle → medullary interstitium →
   subepithelial papilla → erosion through urothelium → anchored surface → calcium oxalate stone grows
   attached.
 • Plaque mineral = calcium apatite (carbapatite) — by infrared microspectroscopy, electron diffraction and
   analysis of > 5000 plaque-associated stones.
 • Microstructure: laminated particles of alternating mineral and organic layers; osteopontin sits at the
   crystal–organic interface; apatite forms spherulites that grow, aggregate and fuse; in the basement
   membrane they lie in amorphous matrix and membrane-bound vesicles; migrating through the
   interstitium they propagate a mineralisation front on collagen and cell debris until they reach the
   papillary subepithelium.
 • Calcifying nanoparticles ("nanobacteria") — implicated in atherosclerotic calcification; associated (not
   proven causal) with Randall plaque.
 • Vascular hypothesis: injury to the vasa recta near the papilla → atherosclerosis-like repair and
   calcification → erosion into interstitium and collecting ducts → nidus.
      ◦ Supported by osteogenic transformation: vascular smooth muscle becomes osteoblast-like with high
        calcium and phosphate; rat tubular cells show osteogenic activity with high oxalate or crystals,
        mediated by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase and reactive oxygen
        species.

 • Brushite pathogenesis (proposed): apatite crystallises in collecting ducts → duct injury, cell death, duct
   dilatation → interstitial inflammation → progressive involvement of adjacent tissue.
 • Calcium oxalate-to-brushite conversion hypothesis: an insult such as infection or shock-wave lithotripsy
   causes tubular dysfunction, alkaline environment, inflammation and intraductal hyaluronic acid,
   converting a calcium oxalate former into a brushite former.
 • Evidence that idiopathic stones start on plaque: plaque area correlates with stone number and 24-hour
   urine parameters; papillary mapping and micro-CT show stones originating on interstitial plaque.
 • The distinction has blurred (consecutive endoscopic series):
      ◦ Randall plaque in almost all stone formers (99%) — including uric acid formers.
      ◦ Ductal plugs in half of uric acid formers and in up to a third of idiopathic calcium oxalate formers
        (severe in about a tenth), correlating with urine risk parameters.
      ◦ Plaque covered less papillary surface than earlier reports and did not correlate with urine parameters
        — possibly because this cohort was more metabolically diverse (not restricted to hypercalciuria).
      ◦ Practical rule: ductal plugs should raise suspicion of a secondary cause.
 • "Unified" theory of plaques and plugs:
      ◦ Plaque: oxidative stress from excess urinary calcium, oxalate and phosphate and/or deficient
        inhibitors → tubular cells de-differentiate into osteoblast-like cells → calcium phosphate crystals in
        membrane-bound vesicles → breach basement membrane → contact collagen and membrane debris
        → plaque spreads through interstitium with inflammation and fibrosis → matrix metalloproteinases
        breach papillary epithelium → plaque exposed to pelvic urine → stone growth.
      ◦ Plugs: supersaturated tubular fluid and pelvic urine form plugs that slow outflow from ducts,
        promoting crystal formation and aggregation by retention.
 • Knowledge will remain incomplete without a reliable animal model.
Concept — Two routes to a stone: plaque (an interstitial apatite anchor on which calcium oxalate grows —
the idiopathic pathway) and plug (intratubular crystal obstruction with tubular injury — the pathway of
secondary, systemic disease); finding plugs should prompt a search for the secondary cause.

Inhibitors and Promoters of Crystal Formation
 • Urine is normally supersaturated for calcium, oxalate and phosphate; stones do not form routinely
   because inhibitors raise the supersaturation needed for nucleation or slow growth and aggregation.
 • Inhibitors exist for calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate — none is known for uric acid.
 • Blocking crystal adhesion to epithelium can also prevent retention and growth.
 • Whole urine raises the formation product of calcium phosphate; inorganic pyrophosphate accounts for
   a substantial share (25–50%) of this inhibitory activity by one method; by another, citrate, magnesium
   and pyrophosphate together account for a smaller share, citrate being the most important.

Concept — Stone formation is a balance between supersaturation and inhibition; most inhibitors act on
calcium salts by complexation (citrate–calcium, magnesium–oxalate) or surface binding (glycoproteins), and

Matrix
 • Matrix = the non-crystalline component of stones — ≈ 2.5% of stone weight.
 • Matrix-dominant stones (a majority of the stone, up to about 65%) occur with chronic urinary tract
   infection.
 • Composition hard to define (only about a quarter is soluble): roughly two-thirds protein, with non-
   amino sugars, glucosamine, bound water and organic ash.
 • Proteins in matrix: Tamm-Horsfall protein, nephrocalcin, a γ-carboxyglutamic acid-rich protein, renal
   lithostathine, albumin, glycosaminoglycans, free carbohydrates and matrix substance A.
 • Matrix substance A — immunologically unique mucoprotein present in the matrix of all stone formers;

 • 1,25(OH)2D3 (calcitriol) is the most potent stimulator of intestinal calcium absorption.
 • Synthesis sequence: 7-dehydrocholesterol (skin) → sunlight → previtamin D3 → liver 25-hydroxylation

   brush border → gut calcium absorption ↑; calcitriol with PTH also recruits osteoclasts; rising serum
   calcium then suppresses PTH and calcitriol, and calcitriol inhibits PTH synthesis by up-regulating

        reabsorption, ↑ calcitriol. Gut: no direct effect — only via calcitriol.

Classification of Nephrolithiasis
 • Drug-related stones (triamterene, silica, indinavir, ephedrine) are uncommon and usually preventable.

Primary Hyperoxaluria
 • Rare autosomal recessive disorders of glyoxylate metabolism: glyoxylate cannot be converted to glycine,
   so it is oxidised to oxalate, a metabolic end product.
 • Urinary oxalate markedly high (≥ 100 mg/day) → calcium oxalate crystals in tubular lumens → some
   attach to epithelium and aggregate into stones, others are internalised and extruded into the
   interstitium → marked nephrocalcinosis.
 • Renal injury: direct toxicity of oxalate or crystals (reactive oxygen species), recurrent obstructing stones,
   and inflammation and interstitial fibrosis from nephrocalcinosis.
 • Vicious circle: falling renal function → oxalate elimination fails → systemic oxalosis (systemic calcium

Enteric Hyperoxaluria
 • The commonest cause of acquired hyperoxaluria.
 • Mechanism (Fig. 95.9) — a sequence: fat malabsorption → luminal fatty acids saponify calcium and
   magnesium → less calcium available to complex oxalate → more free luminal oxalate → colonic
   hyperabsorption; malabsorbed fatty acids and bile salts also ↑ colonic permeability to oxalate.
 • Fecal fat correlates strongly with urinary oxalate in steatorrhoea (Fig. 95.10).
 • Co-existing risks in chronic diarrhoea: dehydration, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesiuria, hypocitraturia, low
   urine pH.
 • Any malabsorption — small-bowel resection, intrinsic small-bowel disease, jejunoileal bypass — can
   cause it.
 • Requires an intact colon — the colon is the principal site of oxalate hyperabsorption; in terminal-ileal
   Crohn disease stone incidence rises with length of bowel resected, provided the colon is intact.
 • Cystic fibrosis: increased stone risk (overall prevalence about 4.5%); proposed causes — enteric
   hyperoxaluria from fat malabsorption, loss of O. formigenes, hyperuricosuria, hypocitraturia.
 • Bariatric surgery:
      ◦ Jejunoileal bypass was abandoned partly for renal failure and stones from severe hyperoxaluria.
      ◦ Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) — initially considered safer, but oxalate nephropathy with dialysis or
        transplantation was reported; retrospective, cross-sectional and prospective studies confirm ↑
        urinary oxalate after RYGB and other malabsorptive procedures.
      ◦ Hyperoxaluria develops ≥ 6 months after surgery.
      ◦ Falling urinary calcium and uric acid partially offset the oxalate rise — net effect on calcium oxalate
        saturation varies.
      ◦ Nonetheless stone incidence is higher after RYGB than in obese controls (claims data).
      ◦ Cause: loss of O. formigenes is unlikely (colonisation is already low before surgery and does not differ
        from obese controls); an exaggerated urinary response to oral oxalate load points to malabsorption /
        enteric hyperoxaluria.

 • Humans lack oxalate-degrading enzymes; some gut bacteria degrade oxalate.

      ◦ Cystic fibrosis (prolonged antibiotics) — absent O. formigenes, raised urinary oxalate.

Idiopathic Hyperoxaluria
  • Mild hyperoxaluria may matter as much as hypercalciuria in idiopathic calcium oxalate stones.
 • Arabian Peninsula: calcium stone prevalence far higher than in the West despite near-absent
   hypercalciuria — hyperoxaluria is the predominant risk factor.
 • Abnormal oxalate metabolism and transport may contribute.
Concept — Outside the rare primary forms, hyperoxaluria is a problem of free luminal oxalate reaching the
colon — raised by fat malabsorption, low calcium intake, high oxalate intake or loss of degrading bacteria —
so colonic presence and luminal calcium are the levers.

Hypocitraturia
 • An important and correctable abnormality: isolated in up to 10% of calcium stone formers; combined
   with other abnormalities in 20–60%.
 • Actions of citrate (four):
    1. Complexes calcium → ↓ saturation of calcium salts.
    2. Prevents spontaneous nucleation of calcium oxalate.
    3. Inhibits agglomeration and sedimentation of calcium oxalate crystals, and growth of calcium oxalate
       and calcium phosphate crystals.
    4. Enhances the inhibitory effect of Tamm-Horsfall glycoprotein at normal levels.
 • Definition: urinary citrate < 320 mg/day.
 • Acid–base status is the primary determinant of citrate excretion: metabolic acidosis → ↑ tubular citrate
   reabsorption and ↓ peritubular citrate synthesis → hypocitraturia.
 • The defect is renal, not intestinal:
      ◦ Stone formers and controls have similar serum citrate and filtered load, but formers show lower
        urinary citrate and higher tubular reabsorption.
      ◦ Oral citrate is rapidly and almost completely absorbed (96–98% within 3 hours) in both hypocitraturic
        formers and controls — so absent overt bowel disease, gut malabsorption is not the cause.

Low Urine pH
 • Below pH 5.5, undissociated uric acid predominates → uric acid and/or calcium stones; calcium oxalate

 • Chronic metabolic acidosis → low urine pH, hypercalciuria (↑ bone resorption and renal calcium leak)
   and hypocitraturia.
 • Idiopathic low urine pH (formerly "gouty diathesis") — stone propensity with low urine pH of unknown
   cause, with or without gouty arthritis.

Hypomagnesiuria
 • Rare: isolated in < 1% of stone formers; combined with other abnormalities in 6–11%.
 • Mechanism: magnesium complexes oxalate and calcium salts, so low magnesium means less inhibition;
   low urinary magnesium also tracks low urinary citrate (cause or effect unclear).
 • Causes: poor dietary intake, intestinal malabsorption from chronic diarrhoeal syndrome.
 • Evidence is contradictory:
      ◦ Rat studies both implicate and question magnesium.
      ◦ Hypomagnesiuric patients had higher recurrence in one study; others found no difference in
        magnesium excretion between formers and controls — possibly because few formers are
        hypomagnesiuric.
 • Magnesium raises urinary pH, citrate and magnesium and lowers calcium oxalate saturation in vitro and
   in vivo, but two randomised trials of magnesium oxide versus placebo or no treatment showed no
   clinical benefit.
Concept — Magnesium is a physicochemically plausible but clinically unproven inhibitor: favourable urine
chemistry has not translated into fewer stones in randomised trials.

Uric Acid Stones
 • Humans and apes lack hepatic uricase (which converts uric acid to allantoin) — the gene was silenced
   (pseudogenised) in evolution, leaving higher blood and urine uric acid.
 • Allantoin is 10–100 times more soluble than uric acid — hence human susceptibility to uric acid stones.
 • Frequency: 8–10% of US stones, up to 25% in parts of Germany; rising — a US registry saw the
   proportion double (≈ 7% → 14%) over 30 years; a Norwegian series rose from 2% to 9% over 40 years.
 • Uric acid is a weak acid, pKa 5.35 at 37 °C — at that pH, half is urate salt, half free acid.

      ◦ Uric acid stones are far commoner in type 2 diabetes (about a third of stones in diabetics versus

      ◦ Controlled for urinary sulphate, net acid excretion is higher in uric acid formers and in non-forming

      ◦ Matched for age, sex and BMI on a metabolic diet, uric acid formers show lower pH, higher titratable

   the main urinary buffer; acid–base balance is kept, but at a lower urine pH than ammonium (higher pKa)
   would allow.
 • Why ammoniagenesis fails — renal lipotoxicity:
      ◦ Metabolic switch: proximal tubule uses free fatty acids instead of glutamine → less ammoniagenesis.

      ◦ Uric acid stone matrix proteomics: proteins mainly of inflammation and complement; pathways
        chiefly phospholipid and fatty acid metabolism.
      ◦ Visceral fat on CT is associated with low urine pH (< 6) and independently with uric acid composition.

   opposing factors — not calcium oxalate or calcium phosphate risk.

Infection Stones
 • Composition: magnesium ammonium phosphate hexahydrate (struvite, MgNH₄PO₄·6H₂O), often with
   carbonate apatite (Ca₁₀[PO₄]₆·CO₃).
 • History (sequence of discovery): a Swedish geologist found magnesium ammonium phosphate in guano
   and named it struvite after his mentor H.C.G. von Struve → bacteria proposed to split urea (early 20th
   century), with Proteus vulgaris isolated from a stone → a bacterial enzyme proposed to hydrolyse urea →
   urease isolated (from Canavalia ensiformis, the jack bean) → now established that struvite forms only
   with urea-splitting infection.

Pathophysiology
 • Urease is absent from sterile human urine — infection with a urease-producing organism is a
   prerequisite.
 • Chemical cascade (step → step):
    1. Urea + H₂O → 2NH₃ + CO₂ (bacterial urease).
    2. Alkaline urine (pH 7.2–8.0) → NH₃ + H₂O → NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ (pKa 9.0). Normally alkalinity would halt
       ammonium generation, but urease keeps producing ammonia, pushing pH higher.
    3. CO₂ hydration → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (pKa 6.3) → H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ (pKa 10.2).
    4. Phosphate dissociation: H₂PO₄⁻ → H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻ (pKa 7.2) → H⁺ + PO₄³⁻ (pKa 12.4).
    5. With physiological magnesium → struvite; with calcium, phosphate and carbonate → carbonate
       apatite and hydroxyapatite (Fig. 95.17).
 • Obstruction or stasis may associate with or exacerbate infection stones → growth can be rapid.

Bacteriology
 • Enterobacteriaceae make up most urease producers, but many gram-positive and gram-negative
   bacteria, some yeasts and Mycoplasma species make urease (Table 95.3).
 • Commonest urease producers: Proteus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas and Staphylococcus; Proteus mirabilis
   is the organism most associated with infection stones.
 • Escherichia coli — a common uropathogen, but only rare strains produce urease.
 • Yet contemporary series culture E. coli (and sterile urine) from struvite patients surprisingly often; E. coli
   matched Proteus as the commonest isolate in one international series; higher struvite content predicts
   urease-producing organisms.
 • Explanation: bladder urine and stone cultures are frequently discordant → voided urine culture is
   unreliable for identifying the stone organism.

Pathogenesis
 • Beyond chemistry, urease-producing bacteria damage the urothelial mucosal layer → ↑ bacterial
   colonisation and crystal adherence.
 • Ammonium from ureolysis alters the protective glycosaminoglycan layer → ↑ bacterial adherence →
   biofilm.
 • Bacteria live within an exopolysaccharide matrix; struvite crystallises inside and around bacterial cells
   → stone growth, bacteria encased within the stone.

Epidemiology
 • 5–15% of all stones.
 • Staghorn calculi are not mostly infection stones: in a modern analysis less than half were infection
   stones; the majority were metabolic, most often calcium phosphate.
 • Incidence is falling worldwide (Australia, Japan; in the US in females, little change in males) — attributed
   to better diagnosis and treatment of urinary tract infection (UTI) in females and more effective stone
   surgery.
 • Geography: highest in India and Pakistan (poor sanitation → female UTI); lowest in Canada, China,
   Argentina, Poland, Iraq and Italy.
 • Female-to-male ratio ≈ 2:1.
 • At-risk groups: elderly; premature infants and congenital urinary tract malformation; diabetes; urinary
   stasis from obstruction, urinary diversion or neurological disorders.

Ammonium Acid Urate Stones
 • ≈ 1% of stones; endemic bladder stones in children in developing countries.
 • Radiolucent.
 • Associations: chronic diarrhoea, inflammatory bowel disease, ileostomy, laxative abuse, recurrent UTI,
   recurrent uric acid stones, morbid obesity; a contemporary series added prostate surgery with bladder
   neck contracture and found no laxative association.

   ammonium acid urate and uric acid stones, not calcium oxalate; inflammatory bowel disease with
   ileostomy gave the highest ammonium urate content.
 • Laxative abuse mechanism (sequence): gastrointestinal fluid loss → dehydration → intracellular acidosis
   → ↑ urinary ammonia; low urinary sodium leaves urate to complex with ammonium → ammonium acid
   urate supersaturation.

Matrix Stones
 • Protein suspensions can promote calcium stones; osteopontin and calprotectin help build the matrix of
   calcium stones.
 • Predominantly matrix stones are rare, radiolucent, and may be mistaken for tumour or uric acid stones;
   literature is mostly case reports.
 • Matrix is ≈ 2.5% of calcium stone dry weight; pure matrix stones contain > 65% protein.
 • Composition ≈ two-thirds mucoprotein, one-third mucopolysaccharide; matrix of crystalline stones
   closely resembles that of matrix stones.
 • Why they fail to calcify is unclear — low urinary calcium was proposed, but patients had normal urinary
   calcium.
 • Dialysis patients: proteinuria may contribute; matrix contains microfibrillar protein and β₂-microglobulin.
 • Proteus infection: electron microscopy shows fibrous net-like laminations with bacterial, cellular and
   crystalline material; proteomics found proteins mostly previously unreported, the majority inflammatory
   or defensive.

Medication-Related Stones
 • Two routes: direct (the drug or a metabolite precipitates) or indirect (the drug alters urine chemistry to
   favour metabolic stones).
 • Stones of the drug itself (with excessive intake): ephedrine, triamterene, guaifenesin, silicate, indinavir,
   ciprofloxacin.
 • Calcium-stone promoters: loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide) and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors
   (acetazolamide, topiramate, zonisamide).

Anatomic Predisposition to Stones
 • Anomalies causing obstruction or stasis carry high stone rates. The long-running question: stasis
   (delayed transit → crystal formation and retention) or the same metabolic abnormalities as other stone
   formers?

Ureteropelvic Junction Obstruction
 • Stones in nearly 20% of patients with ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO).
 • Evidence that metabolic risk persists after the obstruction is corrected (adult series):
      ◦ Most recurred after UPJO repair, and a large share of recurrences were in the contralateral kidney.
      ◦ Most patients with non-infectious stones had a metabolic abnormality — a rate comparable to other
        stone formers.
      ◦ The spectrum mirrored the general stone population — hypercalciuria commonest, then
        hypocitraturia, hyperuricosuria, primary hyperparathyroidism, RTA.
      ◦ Treating the metabolic abnormality substantially cut recurrence compared with conservative
        management.
 • Pre-repair metabolic evaluation: UPJO patients with stones had more metabolic abnormalities (higher
   urinary calcium, hypercalciuria and hyperuricosuria) than UPJO patients without stones.
 • Children:
      ◦ Stone-forming children — with or without UPJO — differ in urinary citrate and oxalate from non-
        stone-forming children with UPJO (prospective comparison), supporting a metabolic contribution.
      ◦ UPJO confers a markedly increased (about seventyfold) stone risk versus normal children.

Horseshoe Kidneys
 • Pooled stone incidence (meta-analysis): ≈ 36% in adults, ≈ 3% in children.
 • High ureteral insertion → impaired drainage → UPJO tendency — risk traditionally attributed to stasis.
 • But metabolic evaluation found at least one abnormality in every patient tested; the distribution
   resembled normal-anatomy formers except hypocitraturia, which was over-represented.
 • Composition (meta-analysis): calcium-based ≈ 89%, struvite ≈ 4%, uric acid ≈ 4%, other ≈ 3%.

Calyceal Diverticula
 • Stones in up to 40%.
 • Conflicting evidence:
      ◦ One series: metabolic abnormalities in half, and most had stones elsewhere (synchronous or
        metachronous) — supports metabolic risk.
      ◦ Another: low metabolic abnormality rates in patients with and without diverticula — but pH and
        citrate were not measured, so abnormalities were under-counted (low pH and hypocitraturia affect
        about 10% and 28% of recurrent formers).
      ◦ 24-hour urine of diverticular stone patients resembled calcium oxalate formers (hypercalciuria, ↑
        calcium oxalate supersaturation) — yet urine aspirated from the diverticulum had lower calcium
        oxalate supersaturation than either renal pelvis.
 • Composition: mainly calcium oxalate monohydrate; struvite–carbonate apatite when infected;
   concomitant UTI in up to 40% (E. coli, Proteus, Pseudomonas).

Medullary Sponge Kidney
 • Medullary sponge kidney (MSK) = ectasia of the renal collecting ducts; nephrocalcinosis and calculi are
   frequent.
 • Candidate mechanisms: infection and stasis in ectatic ducts, plus tubular defects — hypercalciuria,
   impaired concentrating ability, defective acidification after ammonium chloride load.
 • Acidification defects: common on acid loading (mostly distal RTA, occasionally proximal); impaired
   concentrating ability in most; H⁺-ATPase gene mutations (ATP6V1B1, ATP6V0A4) found in MSK patients
   — supporting an MSK–distal RTA link.
 • Yet three studies of MSK patients with stones found no RTA.
 • Hypercalciuria is the commonest abnormality in some series (mostly absorptive), less frequent in others;
   sometimes renal leak from damaged tubules.
 • Hypocitraturia the commonest abnormality in other series.
 • Urine proteomics (Italian network) proposed biomarkers distinguishing MSK from idiopathic calcium
   formers — laminin α2, ficolin 1, mannan-binding lectin serine protease 2, complement component 4-
   binding protein, sphingomyelin, ephrins — role not yet established.
