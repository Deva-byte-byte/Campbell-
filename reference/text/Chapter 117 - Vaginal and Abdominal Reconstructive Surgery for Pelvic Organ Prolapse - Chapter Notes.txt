PREPARING THE PATIENT FOR PROLAPSE SURGERY
  ▪ Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is largely a quality of life (QoL) condition, so the decision to operate rests on

   the interaction of prolapse grade, symptoms and the degree of QoL impairment — not on anatomy alone.
  ▪ Procedure selection is individualised to the clinical presentation: physiological age, comorbidities,

   previous surgery, level of physical and sexual activity, and overall bother.
  ▪ The first operation offers the best chance of restoring normal support and function. After it the normal

   anatomical planes no longer exist, adding difficulty to any subsequent repair.
  ▪ Recurrence rates rise with each successive attempt at surgical correction.

  ▪ The surgeon must understand the anatomical alterations each technique produces, and how the

   operation will affect the continuity and function of the endopelvic fascia and its related viscera.
  ▪ Preoperative discussion of risks and benefits allows the patient to choose the operation — or to decline

   surgery altogether, which is a legitimate informed decision where bother does not justify the risk.

Classification of Prolapse Repairs
Three categories, defined by what they do to the native tissue:
  ▪ Obliterative — for patients whose comorbidities preclude prolonged surgery; symptom relief with

   minimal morbidity. Contraindicated if the patient wishes to remain sexually active.
  ▪ Restorative — for discrete defects in the fibromuscular layer of the endopelvic fascia; corrects the defect

   using endogenous support structures.
  ▪ Compensatory — for weak native tissue or previous failed surgery; reinforces the repair with a

   synthetic, biological or autologous graft.

Route of Reconstruction
  ▪ Reconstruction may be vaginal, abdominal (open, laparoscopic or robotic), or a combination.

  ▪ Because POP frequently affects multiple compartments, surgery often combines repairs, and an anti-

   incontinence procedure may be performed concurrently.
  ▪ Vaginal: fewer complications, shorter recovery, shorter operative time, shorter hospital stay.

  ▪ Abdominal: greater durability of repair.

  ▪ Open versus laparoscopic: complication rates equivalent, but recovery better with the minimally invasive

   approaches, making them progressively more attractive.

Preoperative Counselling Before Vaginal Prolapse Surgery
  ▪ The definition of surgical success is evolving, because success and objective resolution of the anatomical

   defect are not the same thing.
  ▪ Absence of symptoms — resolution of vaginal bulge and minimisation of de novo postoperative

   symptoms — correlates most strongly with the patient's own assessment of cure. Success must
   therefore incorporate patient satisfaction and symptom improvement alongside objective measures.
  ▪ The absence of symptoms influences satisfaction more than the elimination of anatomical prolapse.

  ▪ The process begins preoperatively: expectations and readiness for surgery demonstrably affect later

   satisfaction and perception of improvement.
  ▪ Satisfaction correlates highly with achievement of the patient's own self-described goals — hence the

   value of asking directly what she expects and what changes she wants.
  ▪ Patients misinterpret routine postoperative events as complications — pain, discharge home with a

   catheter, constipation, urgency incontinence, minor anaesthetic effects. These perceived complications
   drive dissatisfaction even where objective cure rates are high.
  ▪ Feeling unprepared for surgery was strongly associated with a negative perception of outcome.

  ▪ After a three-page handout on what to expect, women who felt fully prepared were more likely to be

   improved on the Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I) and had lower Pelvic Organ Prolapse
   Distress Inventory (POPDI) and Urinary Distress Inventory (UDI) scores. Objective measures of cure did
   not differ by preparedness — preparation changes perception, not anatomy.
The consent discussion, in sequence
  ▪ Alternatives to surgery → the purpose of the planned operation, what it can and cannot achieve → the

   benefits, which symptoms can improve → risks and complications → what to expect in hospital → what to
   expect at home → strategies for coping with a catheter in both settings.


 KEY POINTS: PREOPERATIVE COUNSELLING FOR PELVIC ORGAN PROLAPSE SURGERY
    ▪ Absence of recurrent POP symptoms influences patient satisfaction more than elimination of

     anatomical prolapse.
    ▪ Understand the patient's goals and expectations, and review the risks and benefits of each surgical

     approach carefully.
    ▪ Postoperative "events" — catheterisation, urinary tract infection (UTI) — are significant to the

     patient and negatively influence perception of outcome.
    ▪ The informed consent discussion must cover goals and expectations alongside risks and benefits of

     each surgical option.
    ▪ Educate the patient on the whole perioperative course, because perioperative events are otherwise

     perceived as complications and degrade overall perception of the procedure.

Anterior Compartment
Anterior Colporrhaphy
  ▪ Kelly (1913) first described cystocele repair as a treatment for urinary incontinence, emphasising

   plication of the pubocervical fascia to correct the central defect.
  ▪ The operation became the anterior colporrhaphy, or native-tissue cystocele repair, and is now used for

   anterior prolapse rather than for incontinence.
  ▪ Its use for incontinence collapsed after the American Urological Association (AUA) Female Stress Urinary

   Incontinence (SUI) Clinical Guidelines Panel meta-analysis showed failure approaching 40%.
Central versus lateral defect
  ▪ Central defect = compromise of the pubocervical fascia in the midline, producing an anterior bulge.

  ▪ Lateral (paravaginal) defect = separation of the pubocervical fascia from its attachment to the arcus

   tendineus fasciae pelvis (ATFP).
  ▪ Anterior compartment defects are commonly combined central and lateral, so anterior colporrhaphy

   usually must be combined with a paravaginal repair or lateral anchoring.
  ▪ Isolated central defects are rare; only there does colporrhaphy alone suffice.

Why the reported failure rates conflict
  ▪ Early series reported high objective success; a later randomised trial of three techniques reported a high

   failure rate for native tissue repair, and re-analysis of that same dataset with a contemporary definition
   gave a substantially better anatomical result.
  ▪ The spread in the literature is explained by two things: variability in the definition of failure, and the

   fact that early series operated on patients with multiple uncorrected defects.
  ▪ Recurrence rates today may be no better than those of 150 years ago, owing to variation in evaluation,

   technique and follow-up — even between urogynaecologists within one practice.
Anterior Colporrhaphy: Technique
  ▪ Dorsal lithotomy, all pressure points padded, hip and knee flexed to approximately 90 degrees →

   preparation and cleansing of vaginal and perivaginal tissue → retraction, fixed or hand-held, by body
   habitus and available assistance →
  ▪ Indwelling urethral catheter; bladder drained continuously or intermittently → hydrodissection, with or

   without a vasoconstricting agent → midline incision in the anterior vaginal wall, from the vaginal apex to
   the bladder neck →
  ▪ Dissect the vaginal wall off the pubocervical fascia, midline first and then laterally to the ATFP — the

   dissection must expose enough to delineate both central and lateral defects → exposure with Allis
   clamps or a self-retaining ring retractor →
  ▪ Reduce the bladder with finger or instrument, permitting lateral exposure and reapproximation of the

   lateral pubocervical tissues in the midline → interrupted 2-0 or 3-0 delayed absorbable plication sutures
   placed sequentially from the bladder neck to the vaginal apex → tie while the assistant reduces the
   prolapsed tissue →
  ▪ Trim excess anterior vaginal wall judiciously → close with running absorbable suture → intravenous dye

   and cystoscopy to exclude iatrogenic bladder injury and confirm ureteric patency and efflux → vaginal
   packing to cover the field and assist haemostasis.
Incision planning where a sling is performed concurrently
  ▪ Midurethral sling: do NOT extend the colporrhaphy incision to the urethra. Place the sling through a

   separate midurethral incision.
  ▪ Pubovaginal sling: DO create a single incision from the vaginal apex to the midurethra, allowing precise

   positioning over the bladder neck.
Safety point
  ▪ Avoid excessively deep suture placement, which may penetrate the bladder or ureter, or kink the distal

   and intramural ureter — the rationale for mandatory completion cystoscopy.

Results of Anterior Colporrhaphy
  ▪ Earlier studies reported cure rates approaching 100%; large retrospective and randomised series from

   that era gave very low recurrence.
  ▪ A patient-satisfaction study found objective recurrence considerably exceeded subjective failure,

   demonstrating that objective anatomical recurrence does not correlate negatively with overall patient
   satisfaction.
  ▪ Anterior prolapse recurrence is roughly halved by a concomitant sling.

  ▪ In series comparing colporrhaphy against augmented repairs, recurrence approaches 40% — markedly

   higher than the older series, largely because of alternative and composite outcome measures, not a
   change in the operation.
Augmented anterior repair — the randomised evidence
  ▪ Sand trial — anterior colporrhaphy with polyglactin 910 (Vicryl) suture alone versus colporrhaphy plus a

   free Vicryl mesh inlay placed beneath the trigone. At one year, failure 25% with the inlay versus 43%
   with suture plication alone (P = 0.02).
  ▪ Weber trial — three arms in symptomatic cystocele (predominantly POP-Q stages 2 and 3): colporrhaphy

   reinforced with Vicryl mesh, traditional plication without tension using polydioxanone suture, and
   "ultralateral" plication with polydioxanone and tension. With failure defined as POP-Q stage 2 or worse,
   failure was 30%, 46% and 42% — no significant difference, and the trial was underpowered. Its inference
   is that no suturing technique is superior to another.
  ▪ The same investigators had earlier reported recurrence after standard colporrhaphy of only 20% — a

   discrepancy generated purely by the outcome definition.
  ▪ The Sand and Weber trials are not sufficiently similar to permit meta-analysis.

  ▪ Chmielewski re-analysis of the Weber dataset, failure redefined as recurrent prolapse beyond the

   hymen: 11%, 23% and 9%; combined objective recurrence 12%, no significant difference between
   groups.
  ▪ It was the pre-re-analysis figures — apparent high recurrence and poor durability in moderate- and high-

   grade prolapse — that drove investigators towards augmented repair.
Graft and mesh evidence
  ▪ Long-term randomised follow-up of colporrhaphy versus ultralateral colporrhaphy with dermal graft found

   no difference in success at 7 to 10 years.
  ▪ Cochrane review (2016), 33 trials: biological graft repair or absorbable mesh confers minimal advantage

   over native tissue repair. However, against polypropylene mesh, native tissue repair was associated
   with greater awareness of prolapse, higher rates of repeat prolapse surgery, and more anterior
   compartment recurrence.
  ▪ A large retrospective cohort found biological grafts had higher recurrent POP, but graft complications

   were far higher with synthetic grafts.
  ▪ This asymmetry — synthetics work better but complicate more — together with the FDA 522 post-

   market surveillance studies, justifies the withdrawal of transvaginal synthetic mesh for POP repair from
   the global market.

Complications of Anterior Colporrhaphy
  ▪ Reported complications: de novo or worsened SUI; de novo overactive bladder symptoms; postoperative

   urinary retention; incomplete bladder emptying; significant bleeding, greater than 350 mL; wound
   infection; bladder or ureteric injury; vesicovaginal fistula; vaginal shortening; epithelial inclusion cyst;
   dyspareunia.
Detrusor overactivity
  ▪ De novo detrusor overactivity occurs in 5–7% after standard colporrhaphy.

  ▪ Pre-existing detrusor overactivity resolves in up to 63% after surgical repair of POP.

De novo stress urinary incontinence — CARE trial secondary analysis
  ▪ The incidence of de novo SUI correlates directly with the degree of cystocele on preoperative

   examination.
  ▪ By POP-Q stage of anterior prolapse: stage 2 → 41.3%; early stage 3 → 52.5%; advanced stage 3 or stage

   4 → 66.1%.
  ▪ Point Ba was significantly associated with de novo SUI on univariate (odds ratio 1.17, P = 0.015) and

   multivariate analysis (odds ratio 1.16, P = 0.04).

Urinary retention — mechanism and management
  ▪ Usually follows a concomitant anti-incontinence procedure, but may occur without one, from

   neurological trauma and impaired bladder contractility caused by the plication itself.
  ▪ Most retention resolves after a brief period of clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC) → persistent

   retention after a concomitant anti-incontinence procedure → sling incision or urethrolysis → impaired
   emptying from pre-existing hypocontractility → chronic CIC, then neuromodulation, then suprapubic
   tube drainage as a last resort.
Bleeding
  ▪ Two sources: the high vascularity of the vagina, and the venous complex beneath the inferior pubic

   bone.
  ▪ Problematic bleeding indicates dissection in the wrong plane. The vaginal wall must be dissected off the

   pubocervical fascia directly on its white, shiny surface.
  ▪ Repeat anterior colporrhaphy carries higher bleeding risk because scarring obscures the plane.

Bladder and ureteric injury
  ▪ Rare in standard anterior colporrhaphy. Bladder injury occurs on perforating into the retropubic space

   or during dissection of the vaginal flaps, especially with atrophic tissue. Ensure proper bladder drainage
   before perforating the endopelvic fascia.
  ▪ Inadvertent bladder injury → two-layer closure with absorbable suture. Prior pelvic irradiation or a

   tenuous repair → consider labial fat pad interposition (Kreder) to prevent vesicovaginal fistula.
Confirming ureteric efflux
  ▪ Indigo carmine — blue (intravenous); fluorescein 10% — green (intravenous); phenazopyridine — orange

   (preoperative, oral). National shortages of indigo carmine drove adoption of the alternatives.
  ▪ If stained urine does not efflux from both orifices → ureteric catheterisation, retrograde pyelography, or

   removal of the plication sutures.
  ▪ A patent ureter may still fail to efflux — consider inadequate fluid resuscitation or ureteric kinking. If the

   ureter cannot be catheterised, take whatever steps are needed to ensure patency.

Concept — The anterior compartment's entire literature is a dispute about definitions, not about
operations. One randomised dataset yields 30–46% failure or 9–23% depending on whether the threshold is
POP-Q stage 2 or descent beyond the hymen — so every quoted recurrence rate is uninterpretable without its
definition, and the apparent superiority of augmentation shrinks as the definition is made clinically
meaningful.

PROCEDURES FOR LATERAL AND COMBINED DEFECTS

Vaginal Paravaginal Repair
  ▪ George White first described the vaginal paravaginal repair: a cystocele repair suturing the lateral

   vaginal sulci to the "white line", the ATFP.
  ▪ White's premise: a cystocele results from weakness of the lateral vaginal attachments to the ATFP —

   the lateral, not the central, hypothesis.
  ▪ White's concept fell into disfavour for roughly 70 years while Kelly's anterior colporrhaphy dominated.

  ▪ Richardson (1976) reintroduced the paravaginal defect repair, but by an abdominal approach.

  ▪ Whatever the route, the goal is to repair the lateral defect by reattaching the pubocervical fascia to the

   ATFP, and the repair may be combined with anterior colporrhaphy where lateral and central defects
   coexist.

Vaginal Paravaginal Repair: Technique
  ▪ Midline vertical incision through the anterior vaginal wall, bladder neck to vaginal apex → sharply

   dissect the vaginal wall off the attenuated pubocervical fascia and bladder → carry the dissection
   laterally to the pelvic sidewall, blunt dissection permissible → perforate the endopelvic fascia to access
   the sidewall →
  ▪ On entering the retropubic space, palpate to identify the ATFP as it courses from the ischial spine to the

   inferior aspect of the pubic ramus → deep lighted retractors → retract the bladder medially and drain it
   adequately →
  ▪ Place five to seven interrupted non-absorbable sutures at 1 cm intervals through the ATFP, using the

   Capio suture-capturing device in the deep retropubic space → pass them through the lateral edge of the
   detached pubocervical (vesicopelvic) fascia → tie no suture until all are placed, on one side or both →
  ▪ If indicated, place central defect plication sutures now to complete a combined repair → cystoscopy to

   confirm ureteric patency and absence of intravesical sutures → trim the epithelium judiciously and
   close with 2-0 absorbable suture → vaginal packing.
Results of Vaginal Paravaginal Repair
  ▪ Most patients are free of any evidence of prolapse at short follow-up, and of the recurrences, only a

   minority reach or pass the hymen, all of lesser degree than preoperatively.
  ▪ In grade 3 anterior wall prolapse, lateral recurrence was 8% and central recurrence 22%. Midline central

   defects proved common, which is what drove surgeons to combine the repair with anterior
   colporrhaphy.
  ▪ In grade 2–4 combined cystocele treated by paravaginal repair plus concomitant anterior colporrhaphy,

   lateral recurrence was 2% and central recurrence again 22%.
  ▪ In grade 2–4 prolapse, central recurrence was 3%, but of those with concomitant SUI, 57% had persistent

   SUI after the repair.
  ▪ The paravaginal repair, like standard anterior colporrhaphy, is ineffective as a treatment for stress

   urinary incontinence.
  ▪ Anterior colporrhaphy alone versus colporrhaphy plus paravaginal repair showed no significant difference

   in subjective or objective outcome.
Complications of Vaginal Paravaginal Repair
  ▪ Infrequent, but frequently significant when they occur: major intraoperative haemorrhage; lower limb

   neuropathy after prolonged lithotomy positioning; transfusion rate 12%; vaginal abscess requiring
   drainage; retropubic haematoma requiring re-exploration; bilateral ureteric obstruction that resolved
   only after takedown of the repair and replacement of the sutures.
  ▪ Technically more demanding than standard anterior colporrhaphy, with higher and more serious

   complication rates.
  ▪ The conventional repair relies on suture placement through weakened pubocervical fascia — it anchors

   to the tissue that failed. To compensate, most surgeons now correct lateral defects transvaginally using
   grafts attached bilaterally to the pelvic sidewalls.

Concept — The paravaginal repair reliably controls the lateral defect (2–8% recurrence); the central defect
recurs at around 22% regardless of whether colporrhaphy is added. The compartment is therefore treated
as two independent failures, not one.

OTHER PROCEDURES TO CORRECT ANTERIOR COMPARTMENT DEFECTS

Abdominal Paravaginal Defect Repair: Technique
  ▪ Position and prepare for both abdominal and vaginal access; drain the bladder → enter the retropubic

   space through a low midline or transverse incision, laparoscopic or robotic equally acceptable → identify
   bladder neck, symphysis pubis, endopelvic fascia, ATFP and obturator fascia →
  ▪ Identify the site of normal vaginal attachment on the pelvic sidewall, from the internal aspect of the

   superior pubic ramus to the ischial spine → place the non-dominant hand in the vagina and elevate the
   lateral superior vaginal sulcus to its normal attachment along the ATFP →
  ▪ With the bladder retracted medially, place four to six interrupted non-absorbable sutures at 1 cm

   intervals through the ATFP, from ischial spine to pubic bone → pass these into the corresponding
   position in the lateral vaginal wall → avoid the paravaginal veins →
  ▪ Tie, then perform cystoscopy for ureteric patency and absence of intravesical sutures → close; leave the

   catheter indwelling.
Suture counts
  ▪ Vaginal paravaginal repair: five to seven sutures. Abdominal paravaginal repair: four to six sutures. Both

   non-absorbable, at 1 cm intervals along the ATFP between ischial spine and pubic bone; both mandate
   completion cystoscopy.

Anterior Compartment Repair With Sling
The Mechanism
  ▪ In high-stage anterior compartment prolapse, descent creates urethral kinking and compression. This

   compression prevents leakage despite poor urethral function, so stress incontinence is unmasked once
   the prolapse is corrected.
  ▪ SUI occurring in a presumably continent woman after prolapse correction is termed stress incontinence

   on prolapse reduction — formerly "occult SUI".
  ▪ Every patient with high-stage prolapse must be evaluated with the prolapse reduced.

  ▪ Across four prospective studies, prolapse repair in clinically continent women produced postoperative

   SUI in 8.3–66.1% — the width of that range is itself the point.
Methods of Reducing the Prolapse for Testing
  ▪ Options: vaginal packing, rectal swabs, speculum blade, or pessary.

  ▪ Vaginal pack of two rolled 4 × 4-inch gauzes → occult SUI in 69%, with superior visualisation of the

   vesicourethral angle during fluoroscopic urodynamics.
  ▪ Gauze packing plus speculum blade in high-stage prolapse → occult SUI in at least 50%.

  ▪ Fitted vaginal pessary → occult SUI in 58%, with no urethral obstruction caused by the pessary.

  ▪ Comparing rectal swabs, Gellhorn pessary and Graves speculum blade, rectal swabs were judged

   superior because reduction with swabs revealed a significantly lower midurethral closure pressure.
  ▪ Swab reduction gave the highest positive predictive value (79%); the speculum identified the most SUI

   (30%) versus only 6% with pessary; overall reduction tests had low sensitivity and high specificity.
  ▪ The techniques are not standardised, which is why the reported incidence of occult SUI varies so widely.

   No method has been proven superior, and preoperative reduction during urodynamics cannot reliably
   predict postoperative SUI.

Screening and Selective Use
  ▪ There is no standardised test for occult SUI.

  ▪ All patients with advanced-stage anterior compartment prolapse should be screened with the prolapse

   reduced, and a concomitant anti-incontinence procedure considered.
  ▪ Those with preoperative SUI symptoms, occult SUI or urodynamically proven SUI are at elevated risk

   and should consider a simultaneous anti-incontinence procedure.
  ▪ In continent women the risks and benefits must be reviewed individually. The literature supports

   selective, not routine, use.


 KEY POINTS: ANTERIOR COMPARTMENT REPAIR
    ▪ Anterior compartment defects are central, lateral, or combined. Central = midline separation or

        attenuation of the pubocervical (perivesical) fascia. Lateral = detachment of the pubocervical fascia
        from the ATFP.
    ▪ Most anterior compartment defects have concomitant lateral and/or apical defects. Isolated anterior

        colporrhaphy is not recommended when additional defects are present.
    ▪ Anterior compartment repair does not suspend the vaginal apex. Coexisting anterior and apical

        prolapse requires concomitant repairs addressing each defect separately.
    ▪ Grafts improve anatomical results, but graft complications occur. Subjective results are unchanged by

        biological grafts and improved by synthetic grafts. QoL measures, reoperation for prolapse and
        dyspareunia rates are unchanged by synthetic mesh. Despite the anatomical evidence, most mesh
        products and many biologics are no longer available.
    ▪ Anterior colporrhaphy and paravaginal repair are both ineffective alone in the treatment of SUI.

    ▪ All women with advanced-stage anterior compartment prolapse should be screened for occult SUI

        with prolapse reduction, and the risks and benefits of a concomitant anti-incontinence procedure
        discussed.

Apical Vault Prolapse Repair via Uterosacral Ligament Suspension
  ▪ Uterosacral ligament suspension preserves the natural orientation of the vaginal axis, potentially

   preventing recurrence in other segments.
  ▪ The uterosacral ligaments break at specific points rather than attenuating diffusely across the

   uterosacral–cardinal complex — this is what permits a site-specific repair.
  ▪ Counterpoint: histological assessment questions the integrity of the structure for long-term apical

   support.
Surgical Anatomy of the Uterosacral Ligaments
  ▪ Attachment: broadly to S1–S3, variably to S4. Course: fanlike, anterolaterally, to the cervical os and onto

   the proximal posterior vagina. Three portions: sacral, intermediate, cervical.
  ▪ The superior gluteal vein runs beneath the sacral portion.

  ▪ Position relative to the ischial spine is highly variable; the ureter lies near the anterior margin of the

   ligament close to the cervix, and the ligament-to-ureter distance narrows from 4 cm near the sacrum to
   0.9 cm near the cervix.
  ▪ The fibrous ureteric sheath makes the ureter liable to kinking from traction by sutures in adjacent tissue

   — kinking, not ligation, is the mechanism.
  ▪ Sutures close to the sacrum risk entrapping fibres of the sacral plexus trunk (S1–S4), producing buttock

   pain radiating to the posterior thigh and popliteal fossa.
  ▪ Cadaveric work: the ischial spine is the most useful pelvic landmark, and S3 nerve injury increases with

   suture placement beyond a mean of 4.6 cm from it, cephalad along the ligament.
The safe zone — three rules
  ▪ The intermediate portion is the optimal suture site: fewest structures at risk, stable fixation, and furthest

   from the ureter and its sheath, so least traction transmitted to it.
  ▪ Locate the intermediate segment 1 cm posterior along the palpable ligament at the level of the ischial

   spine, with the ligament on tension.
  ▪ Place sutures lateral to medial to avoid catching the ureter or its attachments.

  ▪ Advantage over sacrospinous fixation: less injury to the pudendal and gluteal vessels.

Results of Uterosacral Ligament Suspension
  ▪ The transvaginal evidence base is predominantly uncontrolled retrospective case series: mean objective

   success 85%, range 48–96%; mean reoperation rate 5.8%.
  ▪ Meta-analysis by compartment: apical 98.3%, posterior 87.4%, anterior 81.2%. Where postoperative

   symptoms were reported, 82–100% obtained relief. Ureteric kinking or injury 1–11%.
  ▪ The largest series: 87% had no recurrent support defect at any site on any postoperative examination;

   only 5% had grade 2 or worse prolapse, and the commonest site of recurrence was the anterior
   compartment. One patient required ureteroneocystostomy; two required removal of suspensory sutures.
  ▪ Another large series: 89% satisfaction; 5.5% underwent subsequent surgery for recurrent prolapse, with

   major intraoperative complications predominantly ureteric injury, plus single cases of small bowel injury
   and pelvic abscess requiring diverting colostomy.

  ▪ At a mean of 5.1 years: failure in one or more compartments 15.3%, but apical recurrence in very few.

The OPTIMAL Trial
  ▪ OPTIMAL, a randomised controlled trial of the Pelvic Floor Disorders Network, compared uterosacral

   suspension with sacrospinous ligament fixation.
  ▪ At 2 years, surgical success for uterosacral suspension was 64.5%, and results were similar for

   sacrospinous fixation.
  ▪ Perioperative pelvic floor muscle training was not associated with improved outcome.

  ▪ Counterpoint: a single-institution study of stage 3 prolapse found failure 25.7% after uterosacral

   suspension versus 7.8% after abdominal sacrocolpopexy.

Concept — The apex is the compartment this operation actually secures; the anterior compartment is its
weakest result. Apical success approaches 98% while anterior success sits near 81%, so recurrence after a
technically sound suspension is anterior by default — and the safe-suture rules exist because the ureter
converges on the ligament from 4 cm to under 1 cm as it runs towards the cervix.

Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation
  ▪ First described by Richter in 1942, but not widely adopted until Nichols reported the technique in 1982.

  ▪ Its appeal rests on an extraperitoneal approach and a consistently strong structure on which to anchor

   the apex. Access was originally posterior; anterior access via the paravaginal space is also possible.
  ▪ Stated advantages: success comparable to abdominal procedures; ability to repair concomitant defects;

   no laparotomy; shorter hospital stay; preservation of vaginal length and function; cost effectiveness.
   These have not been fully re-evaluated in the era of laparoscopic and robotic repair.
  ▪ Meta-analysis: both sacrospinous fixation and laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy restore apical support

   efficiently, but sacrocolpopexy may be favoured for durability and sexual function; in subanalysis there
   was no difference in recurrence between sacrospinous fixation and the laparoscopic route.
  ▪ Structures at risk: pudendal or inferior gluteal vessels; sciatic or pudendal nerves. Pudendal entrapment

   → posterior buttock pain radiating down the back of the thigh.
  ▪ The signature disadvantage is alteration of the vaginal axis: unilateral fixation displaces the apex

   posteriorly and to the right, and this can produce anterior compartment recurrence even when an
   anterior repair has been performed.
  ▪ Unilateral right-sided placement is generally preferred, being technically easier for a right-handed

   surgeon. Bilateral anterior support gives a more midline apex, but there is little outcome evidence
   favouring bilateral over unilateral fixation.

Surgical Anatomy of the Sacrospinous Ligament
  ▪ The sacrospinous ligament is approximately 7 to 8 cm long, running from the ischial spine laterally,

   medially beneath the coccygeus muscle, to insert into the sacrum.
  ▪ The pudendal nerve and vessels lie just proximal to their course around the ischial spine. Hypogastric

   venous plexus superiorly; haemorrhoidal vessels medially — retract carefully in both areas.
  ▪ To avoid the gluteal vessels, place the suture INTO the ligament, not behind it.

  ▪ Pudendal entrapment causes pain localising to the buttock or perineum; avoid it by placing sutures

   approximately 1.5 cm medial to the ischial spine.
  ▪ Branches from the S3 and S4 nerve roots are the likelier cause of postoperative gluteal pain.

  ▪ Three rules to avoid nerve entrapment: precise placement on the ligament; limited depth of needle

   penetration; no needle entry or exit above the upper extent of the ligament.
  ▪ The middle segment has the lowest density of associated nerves and arteries, and the nerves supplying

   the pelvic floor muscles are at higher risk of entrapment than the pudendal nerve.
  ▪ The optimal suture position is 1.5 to 3.0 cm medial to the ischial spine, placed directly into the

   ligament.
  ▪ Placing it too far medially risks failure of the repair, because the ligament fans and thins as it inserts

   into the sacrum, and there is a higher concentration of sacral nerves in that medial location.
  ▪ Cadaveric histology: nerve concentration is highest in the sacral portion and lowest near the ischial

   spine; the postulated link between embedded nerves and neuropathic pain could not be correlated
   clinically.
  ▪ Both the localisation and the depth of the suture influence sciatic neuralgia.

Reconciling the apparent contradiction
  ▪ Nerve density is lowest near the ischial spine, yet the clinical rule forbids suturing there. These are not in

   conflict: the danger at the spine is the pudendal neurovascular bundle passing around it, not nerves
   embedded within the ligament, while the danger medially is intraligamentous sacral nerve fibres plus a
   thin, fanned-out ligament. The middle segment avoids both.
  ▪ Uterosacral ligament: the intermediate portion. Sacrospinous ligament: the middle segment, 1.5 to 3.0

   cm medial to the ischial spine. Both are safest in their middle third and dangerous at their bony ends.

Results of Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation
  ▪ The foundational series, including massive vaginal eversion and procidentia, reported no recurrences of

   vault prolapse but a small number describing the vagina as too narrow postoperatively — and the same
   signal appeared in the 1981 long-term series, where a few patients were reluctant to resume sexual
   relations because of perceived narrowing.
  ▪ Global success 90.1%, apical success 95.7% in a mixed series, the few failures being anterior

   compartment prolapse, with acceptable long-term results and minimal adverse effects.
  ▪ Benson trial, prospective randomised bilateral sacrospinous fixation versus abdominal sacrocolpopexy:

   time to failure shorter with sacrospinous fixation (11 versus 22 months), and cystocele reoperation
   three times more frequent in the vaginal group. This is the trial underpinning the chapter's opening
   statement that the abdominal approach is more durable.
  ▪ A small series reported minimal blood loss, no transfusions, and mild postoperative buttock pain

   resolving by 3 months.
  ▪ Excellent posterior and apical results but disappointing anterior results, with 16% having grade 2 or

   worse prolapse.
  ▪ Anatomical cure 96% in one series, with intraoperative rectal and bladder laceration and haemorrhage

   requiring transfusion, and postoperative vaginal cuff abscess, fever and transient femoral neuropathy.
  ▪ Cure of only 53% with 19% buttock pain in the least favourable series quoted.

  ▪ At a median of over five years, overall cure 84.1%, with preoperative stage 4 prolapse the only

   independent predictor of recurrence.
  ▪ Systematic review of 24 studies (1995–2011): overall cure 84.6%; apical recurrence 5.3%; anterior

   recurrence 18.3%.
  ▪ Medium and long-term single-institution results: recurrence 35% at 1 to 5 years and 20% beyond 5 years,

   and only 12% of those with recurrence reported any adverse effect on quality of life.
The Anterior Compartment After Sacrospinous Fixation
  ▪ The anterior compartment is a particularly vulnerable site for a new defect. Reported recurrent

   cystocele rates span 7.6–92%.

  ▪ The mechanism is vaginal retroversion — posterior displacement of the axis — and it may be evident

   intraoperatively as well as postoperatively.
  ▪ Many anterior defects remain asymptomatic and are never reoperated.

  ▪ Pelvic Floor Disorders Network data on whether concomitant anterior repair helps after vaginal apical

   likelihood of rectal injury.
  ▪ Early healing predicts late success: absence of any compartment defect at 6 weeks conferred only a 3%

   likelihood of requiring further reconstructive surgery within 2 to 5 years.
Complications of Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation
  ▪ Ipsilateral gluteal pain in 15%, generally resolving spontaneously within 2 to 3 months when delayed

   absorbable sutures are used.
  ▪ Management of neuropathic pain: observation, with counselling that symptoms may last up to 3 months;

   injection of the nerve with local anaesthetic where treatment is needed.
  ▪ Rectal perforation is reported; recognised intraoperatively and repaired primarily, no sequelae

   followed.
  ▪ Injury to the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels occurs with sutures placed too near the

   ischial spine.

Concept — The three-to-one ratio of anterior to apical recurrence is the defining outcome signature of this
operation. Deflecting the apex posteriorly and to the right is what buys the strong extraperitoneal anchor,
and the anterior compartment pays for it — which is why an anterior repair is added only where the anterior
defect is already high-stage.

Abdominal Sacrocolpopexy
  ▪ Indications: failed previous vaginal repair; isolated uterine prolapse and/or enterocele; younger women;

   highly active lifestyle; sexually active women; those wanting one of the consistently most durable repairs
   and accepting a more invasive approach.
  ▪ It maximises functional vaginal length without significant distortion of the anatomical vaginal axis, and

   other pelvic floor defects and SUI can be addressed at the same operation.
  ▪ Robotic-assisted and pure laparoscopic approaches are now more common than open; the principles

   are identical regardless of approach.

  ▪ Exposure: adhesiolysis → sweep the bowel out of the pelvis and above the pelvic brim → identify the

   aortic bifurcation and iliac vessels; delineate the promontory at the midline → identify the ureters,
   particularly the RIGHT ureter, which lies close to the dissection → incise the peritoneum over the
   promontory, extending along the right lateral aspect of the rectum, creating peritoneal flaps that will
   later retroperitonealise the graft →
  ▪ Divide the fatty tissue with electrocautery; avoid shearing the presacral veins; avoid the LEFT common

   iliac vein, which is not infrequently located more medial than the artery; avoid the middle sacral vessel
   → visualise the anterior longitudinal ligament → extend the peritoneal incision along the right pelvic
   sidewall into the pouch of Douglas.
Vaginal dissection
  ▪ Elevate and distend the upper vagina and apex with a vaginal obturator — large EEA bowel sizer, Lucite

   stent, or ColpAssist Vaginal Positioning Device → incise the peritoneum covering the vagina
   transversely; separate peritoneum and bladder from the anterior vaginal wall sharply and bluntly →
   extend this dissection approximately 6 to 8 cm caudally →
  ▪ Dissect just above the fibromuscular layer, in the loose areolar tissue of the vaginal wall — the correct

   plane is what prevents accidental entry into the vagina → if the wall is opened: irrigate copiously, then
   close in two layers with 2-0 or 3-0 delayed absorbable suture → redirect the apex anteriorly; open the
   peritoneum over the posterior wall → open the rectovaginal space bluntly to the rectal reflection,
   approximately 9 cm → for a sacrocolpoperineopexy, continue beyond the reflection to the perineal
   body.
  ▪ Why the plane matters: wrong plane → unintentional entry into the vaginal lumen → increased risk of

   future graft exposure, particularly with synthetic mesh.
Graft fixation, tensioning and completion
  ▪ Synthetic mesh should be lightweight, flexible and porous; most often a prefabricated Y-shaped mesh,

   tapered to preference.
  ▪ Switch to robotic needle drivers with a bowel-safe grasper; pass the mesh in via the assistant's trocar →

   secure the graft to anterior and posterior vaginal walls with delayed absorbable or permanent
   monofilament suture, or a combination → place sutures FULL THICKNESS without entering the vaginal
   lumen. Absorbable suture gives equivalent long-term outcomes to permanent for mesh fixation.
  ▪ Tensioning: direct the obturator posteriorly towards the promontory and extend it maximally cephalad →

   release tension, allowing the apex to lie in a normal position with the prolapse reduced → avoid
   excessive tension — hypersuspension causes pain or de novo SUI. The suspension should reduce
   prolapse of the apex AND of the anterior and posterior vaginal wall segments.
  ▪ Sacral fixation: suture the graft tail to the anterior longitudinal ligament at the S1 vertebral body or at

   the level of the promontory. Too low — S3 or S4 — increases the risk of presacral venous plexus injury.
   Too high — the upper promontory — risks laceration of the middle sacral vessel or the LEFT common
   iliac vein. At S1 the middle sacral vessels are readily visible and can be isolated and avoided. Two to
   three permanent monofilament sutures.
  ▪ Completion: cystoscopy to exclude bladder injury, intravesical suture or mesh perforation and to confirm

   ureteric patency → close the peritoneum over the vaginal apex with delayed absorbable suture,

Laparoscopic Sacrocolpopexy: Technique
  ▪ Anaesthetic and positioning considerations, abdominal access, camera trocar placement and

   adhesiolysis are similar to the robotic approach. A 0° laparoscope is typically used.
  ▪ Port placement: two additional trocars under direct vision in the right and left lower quadrants, lateral to

   the inferior epigastric vessels; one or two further 5 mm ports at the level of the umbilicus, lateral to the
   rectus muscle; additional 5 mm ports on the principal surgeon's side to permit two-handed operating.
  ▪ Instruments: monopolar scissors, bipolar cautery graspers, laparoscopic blunt-tipped dissector.

   Trendelenburg; bowel swept out of the pelvis. Further steps are nearly identical to the robotic
   procedure.
  ▪ Surgeon preference determines the needle holder and whether an open- or closed-ended knot pusher

   is used for extracorporeal tying. Some surgeons secure the mesh with barbed suture (V-Loc or Quill).
  ▪ The apical tail is sutured to the anterior longitudinal ligament at S1 with two or three permanent

   monofilament sutures.
  ▪ Titanium tacks are an alternative. Tack use may be associated with lumbosacral osteomyelitis and

   spondylodiscitis — rare but devastating complications — but most reported cases have in fact been
   related to SUTURES placed in the presacral space, not tacks.
  ▪ Cystoscopy, peritoneal closure, retroperitonealisation and closure are as for the robotic approach.


RESULTS OF SACROCOLPOPEXY
  ▪ Abdominal sacrocolpopexy maintains a functional vagina and restores maximal vaginal length and

   support; vaginal length was longer after sacrocolpopexy than after sacrospinous fixation.
  ▪ A non-absorbable monofilament synthetic mesh should be used. A randomised comparison of

   absorbable cadaveric fascia lata against monofilament polypropylene found objective recurrence
   substantially more frequent with the fascial graft — approximately one third versus under one tenth.
  ▪ Numerous authors report success rates above 90%.

  ▪ Against vaginal apical repair: abdominal repair gave higher success with equivalent hospital stay; at two

   years against sacrospinous fixation, symptoms and anatomical success were equivalent but
   asymptomatic failures to the introitus and recurrent cystoceles were less frequent after the abdominal
   operation. Vaginal procedures carry a higher rate of recurrent anterior defects even when a
   concomitant anterior colporrhaphy is performed.
  ▪ The distal defect problem: after an isolated abdominal repair that does not address concomitant pelvic

   floor defects, recurrent cystocele and rectocele occur in up to one third. Most remain asymptomatic, but
   secondary vaginal repair is required in up to 20%. Counsel preoperatively.
  ▪ E-CARE: at 7 years, POP recurrence increases progressively. Anatomical failure defined as POP-Q point C

   greater than −2, or Ba/Bp greater than +1. Across the two groups, symptomatic failure 24–29%;
   anatomical failure 22–27%; composite failure 34–48%. Half of the failures were asymptomatic, and half
   of these patients were never retreated.
  ▪ Laparoscopic series: one large series at a mean operative time of 97 minutes reported 96% satisfaction,

   no sexual dysfunction, POP recurrence 4%, with isolated vaginal mesh exposure, bowel incarceration,
   spondylitis and mesh infection. A prospective series at a mean operative time of 180.5 minutes reported
   objective cure 98% with an 18% failure rate concentrated in the posterior compartment and no
   reoperation, plus de novo SUI 7.4%, mesh erosion 4.5% all within one year, and mesh-related pain 2.7%.

COMPLICATIONS OF SACROCOLPOPEXY
  ▪ Intraoperative: haemorrhage or transfusion 4.4%; cystotomy 3.1%; enterotomy 1.6%; ureteric injury

   1.0%.
  ▪ Postoperative: urinary tract infection 10.9%; wound infection 4.6%; ileus 3.6%; deep venous thrombosis

   or pulmonary embolism 3.3%; small bowel obstruction 1.1%.
  ▪ Presacral haemorrhage arises from disruption of the presacral vessels, is reduced by fixing the graft

   higher on the promontory, and may be prevented by pre-emptive bipolar cautery, particularly in
   minimally invasive surgery.
  ▪ CARE: reoperation for gastrointestinal complications 1.2%.

Mesh exposure
  ▪ Heralded by persistent pain, discharge or infection — vigilance in follow-up is required.

  ▪ Meta-analyses report vaginal mesh exposure in 3.4–5.4%, varying with mesh type; the extended CARE

   long-term follow-up gives 9.9% — the figure surgeons should quote.
  ▪ Symptoms develop on average 14 months after surgery, classically vaginal bleeding and discharge.

  ▪ Exposure is commoner with MICROPOROUS materials (Teflon, Gore-Tex) and less common with

   MACROPOROUS monofilament meshes.
  ▪ Management: transvaginal mesh excision with or without partial colpocleisis, or abdominal mesh

   excision.
Hysterectomy at the time of sacrocolpopexy
  ▪ Concomitant hysterectomy is suggested to increase mesh extrusion, but findings are not uniform and

   no randomised trial addresses the question.
  ▪ With meticulous two-layer closure of the vaginal cuff, exposure after concomitant hysterectomy was

   0.8%, against 0% with a prior hysterectomy.
  ▪ Perform a meticulous two-layer cuff closure in a combined operation; strongly consider

   SUPRACERVICAL hysterectomy, which is preferred in conjunction with sacrocolpopexy; and inform the
   patient that vaginal hysterectomy with vault suspension is also effective for uterine prolapse.

Learning Curve
  ▪ Laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy: mean operative time fell from 196 to 162 minutes comparing the first 15

   with the last 30 cases, with blood loss and complication rates unchanged. Approximately 30 to 40 cases
   are needed to master the operation, with an inflection point in operative duration after 18 to 24 cases.
  ▪ Robotic sacrocolpopexy: mean operative time fell from 222 minutes over the first 10 cases to 183 over

   the next 10, and the learning curve comprises about 50 cases.

Cost of Sacrocolpopexy
  ▪ Cost may be calculated in several ways, so comparison across studies is difficult.

  ▪ One cost-minimisation analysis found an overall 10% saving favouring robotic over abdominal ($10,178

   versus $11,307), with the largest cost drivers the number of institutional robotic cases performed
   annually, length of stay, and cost per postoperative hospital day.
  ▪ Another, across all three routes, found robotic the MOST expensive ($8508), then laparoscopic ($7353),

   then abdominal ($5792), with cost equivalence only when robotic operative time fell to 149 minutes.
  ▪ A randomised comparison found robotic cost $1936 more than laparoscopic (95% CI $417–$3454, P =

   0.008), driven mainly by operating room costs ($1667, 95% CI $448–$2885, P = 0.008), excluding the
   initial robot purchase and yearly maintenance.
  ▪ The two cost-minimisation analyses reach opposite conclusions; the reconciling variables are annual

   robotic case volume and operative time — the 149-minute threshold is the number to hold.

Comparison of Apical Procedures
  ▪ Cochrane review (2016), vaginal apical procedures versus sacrocolpopexy — vaginal surgery carried a

   higher risk of awareness of prolapse (relative risk 2.11), repeat surgery (RR 2.28), recurrent prolapse (RR
   1.89), SUI (RR 1.86) and dyspareunia (RR 2.53).
  ▪ Important qualifier: vaginal procedures nonetheless have high success rates with low reoperation rates,

   and may be the procedure of choice for many older patients.
  ▪ No evidence of any difference in any primary outcome between the different types of apical vaginal

   native tissue repair.
  ▪ Comparative effectiveness work found sacrospinous fixation and uterosacral suspension both effective,

   with similar anatomical and functional outcomes and few adverse events, and sacrospinous
   hysteropexy NOT INFERIOR to vaginal hysterectomy with uterosacral suspension for apical uterovaginal
   prolapse.
  ▪ More recent work goes further: sacrospinous hysteropexy is SUPERIOR to uterosacral suspension with

   vaginal hysterectomy, and superior to uterosacral hysteropexy.
  ▪ But abdominal sacrocolpopexy ultimately achieves higher success than sacrospinous fixation (91.4%

   versus 88.3%) with lower dyspareunia (4.67% versus 14.36%).

Concept — Whichever route is used, the apex holds and the posterior compartment fails. Every
comparative dataset locates failure and retreatment posteriorly, with essentially no apical retreatment after
sacrocolpopexy — so route selection is a choice about morbidity, cost and operating time, not about whether
the apex will be supported.

COLPOCLEISIS
  ▪ An obliterative procedure for post-hysterectomy vault prolapse or significant uterovaginal prolapse.

   Indication: properly counselled older patients who do not desire a functional vagina for sexual activity.
  ▪ TOTAL colpocleisis: for the post-hysterectomy patient. Epithelium removed to approximately 2 to 3 cm

   from the urethral meatus, with COMPLETE closure of the vaginal canal.
  ▪ PARTIAL colpocleisis: for uterovaginal prolapse with the uterus in situ — the Le Fort operation. Lateral

   channels are created to allow potential uterine drainage.
  ▪ Advantages: shorter operative time; regional anaesthesia or local anaesthesia with sedation feasible;

   minimal complications; minimal recurrence; decreased recuperative time.
  ▪ Where the uterus is left in situ: Papanicolaou smear, pelvic ultrasonography, and endometrial biopsy if

   indicated.
  ▪ Hysterectomy at colpocleisis eliminates the risk of cervical and endometrial cancer, and eliminates the

   risk of PYOMETRA — a serious complication of partial colpocleisis when the channels become obstructed.
   However, most patients selected for colpocleisis are older with significant comorbidity making
   concomitant hysterectomy undesirable — the reason the channels exist at all.

Partial Colpocleisis: Technique
  ▪ Standard lithotomy, indwelling urethral catheter → perform any anti-incontinence procedure first →

   grasp the cervix with a tenaculum → mark a rectangular segment of epithelium on the anterior and
   posterior walls, from 3 cm from the urethral meatus to 3 cm from the cervix →
  ▪ Leave sufficient epithelium LATERALLY to allow creation of the drainage channels → excise the

   epithelium → optionally place a 14 Fr red Robinson catheter or vessel loop along the sidewalls to assist
   in forming the channels →
  ▪ Starting at the leading edge of the prolapse, place successive layers of 2-0 delayed absorbable sutures

   in transverse rows — linear sutures through the pubocervical fascia anteriorly and the rectovaginal fascia
   posteriorly → as each row is tied the prolapse is reduced proximally; continue until it is above the
   levator plate and completely reduced →
  ▪ High perineorrhaphy: excise a triangular segment of epithelium and approximate the fibromuscular

   tissues of the perineal body in the midline with absorbable suture, narrowing the introitus → indigo
   carmine and cystoscopy to confirm ureteric patency.

Total Colpocleisis: Technique
The differences from partial colpocleisis: no lateral channels, and the entire prolapsed vagina is denuded.
  ▪ Denude the entire prolapsed vagina from the apex to 4 cm proximal to the urethral meatus → carry the

   dissection laterally to the posterior lateral sulcus → dissect the prolapse sac away from the epithelium
   — the enterocele usually reduces easily and needs no further treatment →
  ▪ Beginning at the leading edge, place circumferential PURSE-STRING sutures of 2-0 absorbable suture,

   incorporating pubocervical and prerectal (rectovaginal) fascia → tie each progressive suture, reducing
   the prolapse proximally, until adequately reduced → alternatively use transverse rows of interrupted
   sutures as for partial colpocleisis →
  ▪ High perineorrhaphy (levator myorrhaphy) to narrow the introitus and prevent recurrence →

   cystoscopy to confirm ureteric patency.
  ▪ The principle common to both: success depends on the amount of vaginal tissue sutured together. This

   creates a septum of support, enhanced by bringing the levator muscles together with the
   perineorrhaphy.

   into which bowel might protrude. If the sac cannot be reduced, use purse-string absorbable sutures
   incorporating the sac and the uterosacral remnants.

Results of Colpocleisis
  ▪ A 20-year series in which the enterocele sac was deliberately left unopened reported a single vaginal

   eversion recurrence, treated successfully by repeat colpocleisis, and all but one patient expressly denied
   regret over an operation precluding intercourse.
  ▪ At a mean of 2 years: no recurrent prolapse, no complications, all satisfied; mild SUI occurred only in

   those who had undergone Kelly plication for incontinence, and was treated successfully with collagen
   bulking.
  ▪ Regret over loss of coital ability 12.9% in the most quoted series.

  ▪ A multicentre prospective study at one year: 84% very satisfied or satisfied with the decision for vaginal

   closure, only two dissatisfied, one repeat colpocleisis for recurrence, and most did not experience
   worsening of body image.
  ▪ Where three quarters had total colpocleisis: success on the PGI-I in 91%, corroborated on examination,

   with urinary frequency and urgency the most bothersome residual symptoms, in a third of patients.
  ▪ Systematic search 2000 to 2020, follow-up 30 days to 5 years: over 90% of patients aged between 69 and

   84; success 62.5–100% by POP-Q stage 1 or better, and 87.5–100% by no prolapse beyond the hymen;
   coital regret 0–12.9%; general decision regret 0–13.8%. Colpocleisis is a heterogeneous procedure with
   high subjective and objective success, low coital regret and low complication risk.
  ▪ Review using validated questionnaires: colpocleisis remains a viable option without compromising

   quality of life, body image or sexuality — provided patient selection is diligent.
  ▪ Large population-based study: overall complications during admission 9.6%, higher with concomitant

   hysterectomy (11.4% versus 9.5%, P = 0.023), and concomitant hysterectomy independently increased
   complication risk (odds ratio 1.93, 95% CI 1.45–2.57, P < 0.001). Shared decision-making is essential, as
   these patients are older and more comorbid than those having non-obliterative surgery.
Results of Partial (Le Fort) Colpocleisis
  ▪ The original Le Fort series: good anatomical results and symptom relief in the large majority; three

   recurrences, none reoperated; immediate complications were one pulmonary embolism, fever and
   urinary tract infection.
  ▪ Another series: no recurrences and no de novo SUI, transient postoperative retention in a minority, and

   no reported regret about sexual intercourse.
  ▪ Over 300 patients: anatomical success 98.1%, patient satisfaction 92.9% — an effective, low-risk

   procedure for the appropriate elderly patient with advanced prolapse.
  ▪ Multi-institutional database: 43% of colpocleisis procedures performed on patients over 80; mean length

   of stay 2.3 days; mortality 1.5%; overall complications 6.8%; readmission 4.2%.
  ▪ Multicentre and retrospective series: improved body image, improved pelvic floor symptoms, low regret

   and high satisfaction after both partial and total colpocleisis.

Urinary Considerations After Colpocleisis
  ▪ Two mechanisms of postoperative incontinence: unmasking of occult SUI on reduction of the

   urethrovesical angle, and traction on the urethra when it is approximated to the posterior vaginal
   muscularis.

  ▪ All patients should be screened for occult SUI before colpocleisis, and an anti-incontinence procedure

   these patients are less able to perform intermittent catheterisation. Discuss in advance; offer a

POSTERIOR COMPARTMENT REPAIR
Symptoms — three conceptual groups
  ▪ Herniation symptoms: vaginal bulging, or bleeding from excoriation of the epithelium.

  ▪ Defecatory dysfunction: stool trapping requiring vaginal splinting or manual digitation, defecatory

   urgency, constipation. Defecatory dysfunction does NOT include faecal incontinence.
  ▪ Sexual dysfunction: principally dyspareunia, with decreased desire and anorgasmia as contributors.

Distinguishing the Causes of Defecatory Dysfunction
  ▪ Outlet obstruction — from defects in the support of the posterior compartment, perineum and rectum,

   and from anismus. Obstruction due to pelvic floor defects may be relieved by surgical restoration of
   anatomy.
  ▪ Motility disorders — impaired transit of stool through rectum and anus; treated with dietary

   modification and medication, not surgery.
  ▪ Anismus is failure of the puborectalis to relax during defecation, and often responds to biofeedback and

   pelvic floor physical therapy.
  ▪ In combined disorders, treat anismus or slow transit constipation — the commonest motility disorder

   — non-surgically BEFORE embarking on surgery.
Who Benefits from Rectocele Repair
  ▪ Predictors of benefit: sensation of vaginal mass or bulge; need for digitation (splinting) to complete

   evacuation; non-emptying or partial emptying on defecography; a large rectocele.
  ▪ Not predictive: sensation of incomplete emptying and constipation — neither is specific to rectocele, and

   both may reflect irritable rectum or slow transit constipation.
  ▪ What to tell the patient: surgery will likely reduce vaginal protrusion symptoms and decrease or eliminate

   the need for splinting; but constipation may persist, because motility disorders and anismus coexist
   independently with prolapse and survive an otherwise successful repair.
Route — Transvaginal Versus Transanal
  ▪ A randomised comparison of rectovaginal fascia plication versus transanal repair: symptom resolution

   was high with both (93% vaginal versus 73% transanal), but the vaginal approach gave better objective
   findings and a far lower recurrence rate (7% versus 66%).
Historical Development
  ▪ The traditional posterior colporrhaphy was devised in the 19th century to treat perineal tears sustained

   during vaginal delivery.

  ▪ The original description — plication of the pubococcygeus muscles and the posterior vaginal wall with

   reconstruction of the perineal body — was termed posterior colpoperineorrhaphy. It produced a rigid
   inferior shelf, reduced posterior wall herniation, and helped prevent descensus of the vault or uterus.
  ▪ Francis and Jeffcoate (1961): 50% incidence of dyspareunia after colporrhaphy with levator plication.

  ▪ Later work reported increased postoperative faecal incontinence, constipation, incomplete evacuation

   and dyspareunia after traditional posterior colporrhaphy with levator plication.
  ▪ Because of the postoperative dyspareunia, plication of the levator ani has largely been abandoned.

   Site-specific repair and midline fascial plication WITHOUT levator ani plication are now the
   predominant surgical treatments for rectocele.

Posterior Colporrhaphy: Technique
  ▪ Dorsal lithotomy. If anterior or middle compartment repair is required, perform it FIRST → retract the

   anterior wall with a Heaney or Lone Star retractor with hooks → hydrodissection with saline or local
   anaesthetic, with or without adrenaline →
  ▪ Midline incision through the posterior vaginal epithelium — extending to the vaginal apex for a high

   rectocele, or starting at the most caudal position for smaller ones → separate the rectovaginal fascia
   (muscularis) from the epithelium with Metzenbaum scissors, tips pointed TOWARDS the epithelium to
   avoid rectal injury → dissect laterally until the pararectal attachments to the pelvic sidewall are
   visualised →
  ▪ For large posterior defects, place a purse-string suture of 2-0 or 3-0 absorbable suture at the base of the

   rectal herniation — this also draws the attenuated fascia together to aid reapproximation — taking care
   not to foreshorten the posterior wall cephalad to caudad →
  ▪ Plicate the rectovaginal fascia in the midline with interrupted or continuous 2-0 absorbable sutures —

   avoid excessive lateral placement or wide spacing, which cause overtightening or painful ridges →
   continue distally, incorporating the perineal body reapproximation → trim excess epithelium judiciously
   and close with running 2-0 absorbable suture → perineorrhaphy before closing the epithelium if
   introital laxity dictates, avoiding overtightening.

Site-Specific Repair: Technique
  ▪ Devised to minimise the complications of posterior colporrhaphy. Richardson described discrete defects

   in the rectovaginal fascia, identified both at operation and in cadaveric dissection.
  ▪ The commonest defect is the TRANSVERSE configuration, separating the vaginal septum from the

   perineal body — effectively a separation of the rectovaginal fascia from the perineal body. The goal is to
   restore anatomy by closing these discrete defects, rather than plicating the midline.
  ▪ Midline incision on the posterior vaginal epithelium, as for posterior colporrhaphy → separate the

   rectovaginal fascia from the epithelium through a virtually bloodless plane → insert the index finger of
   the non-dominant hand into the rectum to identify the fascial defects → close with interrupted
   absorbable sutures → trim excess epithelium judiciously and close with running absorbable suture.
  ▪ The contrast: posterior colporrhaphy plicates the midline; site-specific repair closes discrete defects,

   identified by rectal examination during the operation.

Perineorrhaphy: Technique
  ▪ Excise a triangular incision at the mucocutaneous junction → sharply remove the triangular island of

   posterior vaginal wall from the prerectal levator fascia → approximate the attenuated fibromuscular
   tissue of the perineal body with HORIZONTAL MATTRESS sutures → bringing these together
   reconstitutes the muscles of the urogenital diaphragm and restores support to the central tendon →

Results of Posterior Colporrhaphy
  ▪ Posterior repair with levator plication achieved anatomical success, but many patients reported

   postoperative bowel symptoms and dyspareunia — the adverse effects that led to abandonment of
   levator plication.
  ▪ A randomised trial of posterior colporrhaphy versus site-specific repair versus site-specific repair with

   porcine small intestinal submucosa graft found similar functional and anatomical outcomes for
   colporrhaphy and site-specific repair, no anatomical benefit from the porcine graft, and no difference in
   dyspareunia between the three groups.
  ▪ Follow-up of that cohort for bowel symptoms: anatomical cure was associated with reduced

   postoperative straining and reduced sensation of incomplete evacuation, but not with other bowel
   symptoms; overall, incomplete emptying, straining, splinting and faecal incontinence improved
   significantly after rectocele repair.
  ▪ Retrospective comparison in advanced posterior prolapse: recurrence was HIGHER after site-specific

   repair than after midline plication — 33% versus 14% for second degree, and 11% versus 4% for third
   degree; symptomatic rectocele recurrence likewise greater after site-specific repair (11% versus 4%),
   with de novo dyspareunia and bowel symptoms the same in both groups.
  ▪ Prospective study of rectovaginal fascial plication: improved anatomical outcome correlated with

   improved function; 87% no longer had obstructive defecation; significant improvements in awareness of
   prolapse, obstructive defecation, straining, hard stools, dyspareunia and digitation; dyspareunia fell from
   37% to 5%.
  ▪ Another prospective fascial plication series: 87% improvement in bowel symptoms at 6 weeks; vaginal

   symptom relief 73–92% at 18 months; 65% improvement in defecatory symptoms; 38% improvement in
   sexual discomfort; no de novo dyspareunia or bowel symptoms.
Site-specific repair series
  ▪ Prospective series: the great majority had a genuinely discrete defect, a small minority only tissue

   attenuation. 85% of those with preoperative bowel symptoms reported resolution at 3 months; de novo
   dyspareunia 3%.
  ▪ Retrospective series: improvement or cure for pain or pressure, vaginal mass, splinting and difficulty with

   defecation — but CONSTIPATION DID NOT CHANGE SIGNIFICANTLY. Preoperative dyspareunia improved
   in 73%, worsened in 19%, and arose de novo in a small number. Emotional health improved, specifically
   thoughts of embarrassment and frustration.
  ▪ Site-specific repair with reattachment of the rectovaginal septum to the perineal body in all: significant

   relief of protrusion, manual evacuation, difficult defecation and dyspareunia; constipation unchanged.
   At one year, roughly two thirds of those who used manual evacuation preoperatively had returned to
   doing so — attributed to functional decompensation of the rectum, which surgery does not correct.
  ▪ Discrete fascial defect repair: 86% had prolapse stage 1 or better at 12 months; splinting eliminated in

   63% of those reporting it; mean satisfaction 8.6 out of 10. Mean improvement did NOT correlate with
   anatomical correction but DID correlate with relief of defecatory symptoms.

Biological Interposition Graft Repairs of the Posterior Compartment
  ▪ Grafts have been used, but outcomes are not significantly better than traditional repair. Several authors

   caution against SYNTHETIC materials in the posterior compartment because of dyspareunia and visceral
   erosion, favouring biological grafts.
  ▪ Site-specific repair with cadaveric dermis, graft fixed proximally to the vaginal apex, laterally to the

   levator ani, distally to the perineal body: 93% success by POP-Q at an average of 13 months.
  ▪ Cadaveric fascia lata: 83–89% symptomatic improvement; 90% of those examined had a grade 0

   rectocele by the Baden–Walker classification.
  ▪ PelviSoft, a fenestrated porcine dermal acellular matrix: no wound complications.

  ▪ Against these encouraging results: at 3 years, 41% had recurrence of stage 2 or worse after collagen

   mesh for symptomatic rectocele, and more than half reported incomplete rectal evacuation; the authors
   advocated further study before routine use.
Cochrane review (2018) — traditional versus biological graft repair
  ▪ Awareness of prolapse: insufficient evidence of any difference (relative risk 1.09, 95% CI 0.45–2.62).

   Repeat surgery for any prolapse: insufficient evidence (RR 0.60, 0.18–1.97). Recurrent posterior wall
   prolapse: no conclusive difference (RR 0.55, 0.30–1.01).
  ▪ Postoperative complications were MORE common with biological repair (RR 1.82, 1.22–2.72) — the only

   statistically significant finding of the four, and it is against grafts.
  ▪ Counselling figures: graft extrusion 1.3–12%; dyspareunia may reach 60%; biological graft options are at

   present quite limited.


 KEY POINTS: POSTERIOR COMPARTMENT REPAIR
    ▪ Because motility disorders often coexist with posterior compartment prolapse, counsel patients that

     constipation may persist despite successful anatomical correction.
    ▪ Levator ani plication should NOT be performed during rectocele repair, because of the high risk of

     postoperative dyspareunia.
    ▪ Repair of the perineal body is often performed at the time of rectocele repair, to recreate

     approximation of the rectovaginal connective tissues to the central tendon of the perineum.
    ▪ Use of reconstructive materials, synthetic or biological, to augment posterior vaginal wall repair is

     NOT supported by the body of evidence, although some smaller trials report conflicting outcomes.


Concept — Constipation is the symptom that does not improve. Across every series, anatomical repair
relieves bulge, splinting and straining while leaving constipation unchanged, because motility disorders and
anismus are independent of prolapse — which is why the operation is selected on bulge and splinting, and
why satisfaction tracks defecatory symptom relief rather than POP-Q.
