BLADDER DIVERTICULA
 • Definition: a subjectively large herniation of bladder urothelium through the muscularis propria,
   producing a thin-walled, urine-filled sac that communicates with the bladder lumen through a neck
   (ostium) of variable size.
 • Wall: mucosa, subepithelial connective tissue (lamina propria), scattered thin muscle fibres and
   adventitia. An outer fibrous pseudocapsule is often present and gives a useful surgical plane at excision.
 • The residual smooth muscle in the wall is disorganised and non-functional, so the sac cannot contract.
   Diverticula therefore empty poorly at micturition, leaving a large postvoid residual (PVR) urine volume —
   the origin of the characteristic presentation and imaging findings.
 • Neck anatomy (Fig. 122.1): the diverticular neck is ringed by circular fibres of the middle detrusor layer
   (fine, circularly oriented fibres), with the longitudinal bundles of the outer layer running past it. This ring is
   the basis of the narrow, sphincter-like neck that transurethral neck resection later aims to disrupt.

Classification, Pathophysiology, and Etiology
 • About 90% of bladder diverticula occur in adults. They are far commoner in males, with a male-to-
   female ratio of about 9:1 in both adult and paediatric groups.
 • Diverticula are classified as congenital or acquired (secondary). Pathophysiology, presentation, clinical
   implications and imaging differ between the two.

Congenital diverticula
 • May occur with normal voiding dynamics and no BOO, yet up to 60% are associated with an underlying
   syndrome, neuropathic voiding dysfunction or BOO. Incidence in children is about 1.7% in a large
   paediatric genitourinary database.
 • Less common presentations: enuresis, pyelonephritis, acute retention and stones. Secondary BOO can
   arise when the diverticulum extends distally towards the bladder neck.
 • In prune-belly syndrome or posterior urethral valves (PUV), diverticula may lie at the dome with
   aberrant voiding dynamics or anatomy; these must be distinguished from the urachal diverticula seen in
   some paediatric conditions.
 • Syndromic associations: Menkes syndrome (kinky hair, copper deficiency), Williams syndrome, Ehlers-
   Danlos syndrome and fetal alcohol syndrome. Genetic predisposition without a syndrome is unclear
   (reports in twins and a possible autosomal dominant family).
 • Chromosomal testing has been suggested because of the syndromic links, but it is not recommended in a
   child with a single simple lesion.
Acquired diverticula
 • Associated factors in adults: BOO from benign or malignant prostatic disease, bladder neck obstruction
   (hypertrophy, contracture) and urethral stricture — although obstruction is not demonstrable in every
   case.
 • About 70% are associated with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). Historically, moderate to large
   diverticula were found in about 1–6% of men with "prostatism".
 • In females, careful evaluation of the outlet usually reveals an obstructive cause: dysfunctional voiding,
   vaginal prolapse, primary bladder neck obstruction (hypertrophy), urethral stricture, or iatrogenic
   obstruction after anti-incontinence surgery (Fig. 122.3).
 • In children and young adults, acquired diverticula follow bladder neck obstruction (dysfunction), PUV or
   neurogenic vesicourethral dysfunction.
 • Hutch diverticulum: a diverticulum superolateral to the ureteric orifice, not involving the trigone, in a
   neuropathic bladder with VUR — described in paraplegic patients; also seen in dysfunctional voiding.
 • Iatrogenic diverticula: inadequate closure of the muscular layers after cystotomy leaves a weak point in
   the suture line; they also arise at the ureteroneocystostomy site after ureteric reimplantation.
Concept — A diverticulum is a mucosal hernia without functioning muscle, so it cannot empty; stasis,
infection, stones, malignancy and the urodynamic artefacts all follow from that. Congenital diverticula are a
weak wall under normal pressure; acquired diverticula are a normal wall under high pressure — which is why
the acquired type always sends the search back to the outlet.

Diagnosis
Presentation and Evaluation
 • Acquired diverticula produce no specific symptoms; most are found during investigation of nonspecific
   LUTS, haematuria or infection, or incidentally on imaging or endoscopy for these conditions. An
   enormous diverticulum may be found on imaging for unrelated complaints in a patient with no LUTS or
   UTI (Fig. 122.4).
 • Symptoms, when present, come from stasis or mass effect. On direct questioning, incomplete emptying,
   lower abdominal fullness and double voiding may be attributable to a large diverticulum, but these
   overlap with prostatic enlargement and other outlet conditions.
 • Congenital diverticula most often present with UTI; haematuria, abdominal pain or an abdominal mass
   may also occur.

 • Rare presentations: lower-limb oedema from iliac vein compression; diverticula contained within
   inguinal hernias.
 • UTI in a male is often what triggers the investigation that uncovers a diverticulum. Stasis both predisposes
   to UTI and makes an established infection harder to eradicate.
Initial evaluation (sequence): history and examination including digital rectal examination → prostate-
specific antigen (PSA) in males where appropriate → quantify LUTS, query occult neurogenic causes (for
example spinal surgery) and characterise prior lower urinary tract surgery → urinalysis, urine culture and
urine cytology, especially if nonoperative management is contemplated.
 • Patients should be counselled that bladder cancer risk may be somewhat higher with diverticula.
 • Haematuria and pyuria are common. Relapsing or persistent pyuria unresponsive to antibiotics may by
   itself justify diverticulectomy in an otherwise asymptomatic patient.
 • An incidental diverticulum in an adult should prompt evaluation for BOO or occult neuropathic
   dysfunction, plus endoscopy and imaging of both the lower and upper urinary tract.
Imaging
 • Diagnosis rests on radiographic and endoscopic findings. Cellules → saccules → diverticula form a
   continuum of increasingly large, and therefore more severe, manifestations of raised intravesical voiding
   pressure. Cellules and saccules are small outpouchings between hypertrophied muscle bands (saccules
   larger). There are no agreed size cut-offs; the distinction is subjective.
 • Differential diagnosis of a fluid-filled paravesical structure: müllerian cysts; uterine, ovarian and tubal
   abnormalities; urachal cysts; ectopic ureter or ureterocele; postsurgical change including lymphocele.
Voiding cystourethrography
 • Fluoroscopic voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) is an excellent detection study, although false
   negatives occur. Anteroposterior, oblique and lateral views show anatomy, location and size; the study
   also detects associated VUR and, importantly, whether the diverticulum empties with voiding. PVR in the
   diverticulum and in the bladder should both be recorded.
 • VUR occurs in up to 13% of congenital diverticula. Historically every such child underwent
   ureteroneocystostomy; the current approach is selective reimplantation, reserved for complicating
   factors such as renal scarring or progressive deterioration of renal function.
 • Paradoxic enlargement: during a detrusor contraction, contrast flows from the high-pressure bladder
   through the ostium into the low-pressure diverticulum, which enlarges as the bladder empties. The
   bladder may discharge partly into the sac and partly through the urethra; after voiding stops, the empty
   bladder refills from the diverticulum (Fig. 122.2 — a fluid–fluid level forms as denser contrast settles
   below urine).
Cross-sectional imaging
 • Characterises the number, anatomy and location of diverticula and looks for masses within them. Filling
   defects or other bladder abnormalities demand further investigation (Fig. 122.5: multiple filling defects
   proved urothelial carcinoma; a diffusely thickened, irregular wall with two diverticula from chronic BOO
   showed trabeculation only; asymmetrical wall thickening with the ostium visible).
 • If the neck is obstructed by tumour or otherwise non-patent, contrast will not enter, and cross-sectional
   imaging may be required for diagnosis.
 • Provides surgically relevant anatomy — ureters and adjacent structures such as the rectum, which large
   diverticula can distort.

Upper tract imaging
 • Options: intravenous pyelography, ultrasonography, computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance
   imaging (MRI). In adults without haematuria or suspected malignancy, the goal is silent
   hydroureteronephrosis related to the diverticulum.
 • Adults versus children: hydroureteronephrosis in up to about 7% of adults; upper tract abnormalities
   (scarring, dysplasia, hydronephrosis) in up to 30% of children.
 • Causes of hydronephrosis: ureteric obstruction by the diverticulum; the underlying urodynamic
   abnormality that created it; associated VUR; inflammation; or an unrelated cause. A multichannel
   urodynamic study with VCUG, or a videourodynamic study (VUDS), separates upper from lower tract
   causes (Fig. 122.6: hydronephrosis with an ipsilateral diverticulum and retention resolved after
   transurethral resection of the prostate; the diverticulum persisted but was smaller).
 • Ureteric deviation: the pelvic ureter is most commonly deviated medially, occasionally laterally (Fig.
   122.7). This guides the surgical approach and whether to place preoperative ureteric stents.
 • A diverticulum that encompasses the ureteric orifice creates a functionally shortened intramural ureter
   and causes VUR.
Urodynamics
 • In adults, BOO or neurogenic dysfunction causes the diverticulum, and VUDS is very helpful. The role of
   urodynamics in congenital diverticula is less well defined.
 • Failure to identify and treat the underlying urodynamic abnormality before or during definitive surgery
   may lead to recurrent diverticula or other postoperative problems, especially in adults — so the
   evaluation is critical. Treating the abnormality may improve emptying enough to avoid diverticulectomy.
 • Associated findings: BOO, impaired contractility, raised PVR and detrusor overactivity.
 • Pressure-sink effect: as the detrusor contracts, bladder contents decompress into the diverticulum (the
   path of least resistance) rather than through the urethra, so contractility appears falsely diminished.
   When carefully measured, contractility does not differ from men with BPH without diverticula.
 • Raised PVR may simply be urine retained in the diverticulum, regardless of contractility or obstruction.
Endoscopic Examination
Inspect the entire interior of every diverticulum for stones or abnormal epithelium (a flexible cystoscope is
often needed) → record location relative to the ureters and outlet, and size (Fig. 122.8) → obtain cytology
directly from the diverticulum → biopsy any abnormal epithelium with extreme care (Fig. 122.9).
 • Every lesion under consideration for biopsy is malignant until proven otherwise.
 • The wall is very thin because it lacks muscularis propria; perforation during biopsy risks tumour seeding
   into the perivesical fat, with adverse prognostic implications.
 • A papillary urothelial tumour can be seen endoscopically inside a diverticulum whose CT shows no mass
   (Fig. 122.9) — imaging alone does not exclude tumour.
 • Patients electing nonoperative management need close follow-up with periodic endoscopy and cytology.
   The natural history is unknown, and malignant transformation from stasis over time should be considered.
Concept — The diagnostic workup has two jobs: find the cause (outlet or neurogenic) and exclude cancer
inside a sac that imaging, endoscopy and even urodynamics each see imperfectly — so the tests are
combined rather than any one relied upon.

Associated Conditions
Bladder Outlet Obstruction
 • Acquired diverticula commonly coexist with BOO. Common male causes: benign and malignant prostatic
   disease, urethral stricture. Less common: primary bladder neck obstruction (hypertrophy), vesicourethral
   anastomotic stricture after prostatectomy, and functional obstruction from neurogenic dysfunction
   (bladder neck or striated sphincter dyssynergia).
 • Treat the BOO before, or together with, the diverticulum. Whether to do both at once or stage them is
   controversial.
 • Especially with small diverticula, treating the BOO alone may permit complete, low-pressure emptying of
   the diverticulum and avoid future diverticulectomy.
Malignancy
 • Risk of malignancy is elevated in adults, attributed to urinary stasis (prolonged carcinogen exposure) and
   chronic inflammation.
 • Inflammatory urothelial change is found in 45–80% of diverticulectomy specimens; overall tumour
   prevalence within diverticula is 0.8–10%. Unsuspected cancer is found on final pathology in a small but
   not insignificant number. Because diverticula are under-reported overall, the true tumour prevalence is
   probably considerably lower.
 • Histology: urothelial cell carcinoma (UCC) about 70–80%, followed by squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)
   20–25%.
 • Tumours occur almost exclusively in adults, with a peak at 65–75 years.
 • Lack of muscularis propria means these tumours are at greater risk of being high grade and invasive, with
   rapid transmural and extravesical extension and a poorer prognosis. It also risks early dissemination
   during biopsy or transurethral resection (TUR), and makes precise pathological staging difficult.
 • Prognosis was historically poor — attributed to delayed diagnosis and advanced stage — but may be
   better in selected patients.
 • Historically this justified prophylactic excision of all diverticula, including asymptomatic ones. It is now
   accepted that observation with periodic endoscopic surveillance is reasonable in asymptomatic patients
   without dysplasia, malignancy or complicating factors (for example upper tract deterioration).
 • Imaging (CT, MRI, ultrasonography) identifies tumours within diverticula, but whether it reliably predicts
   extravesical invasion is unclear; one small series suggests tumour length and width on CT correlate with
   survival.
 • Clinical stage at presentation is the most important prognostic factor: 5-year survival falls steeply from
   superficial to extradiverticular disease. Preoperative staging is nevertheless inaccurate, with substantial
   upstaging on final pathology.

Other Associated Conditions
 • Many diverticula sit at the UVJ, explaining the high incidence of ipsilateral ureteric abnormalities. A
   ureter entering a diverticulum lacks muscular backing, giving a functionally shortened intramural tunnel
   (Fig. 122.7); there is a very high prevalence of ipsilateral VUR with congenital diverticula.
 • Other considerations: stones within the diverticulum, ureteric obstruction, and rare but life-threatening
   perforation or rupture.

 KEY POINTS: DIAGNOSIS OF BLADDER DIVERTICULA
   • No defined, functional muscularis propria, so larger diverticula empty poorly at micturition.
   • In adults, usually associated with BOO or neurogenic vesicourethral dysfunction; many are
     paraureteral.
   • Most are diagnosed incidentally during evaluation of nonspecific LUTS or infection.
   • Evaluate for BOO and, if present, treat it before or with definitive treatment of the diverticulum.
   • Malignancy can arise within a diverticulum; consider early extravesical extension because muscularis
     propria is absent.

 • The algorithm is cancer first, upper tract second, urodynamics third: every branch passes through

   reserved for persistent symptoms, complications or HUN attributable to the diverticulum.
Observation and Nonsurgical Management
 • Adults with minimal symptoms and no complicating factors may choose observation with surveillance,
   after counselling about the possibly increased malignancy risk, the need for reassessment, the
   unpredictable and potentially aggressive course of any malignancy, and the signs of progression —
   increasing LUTS, haematuria and UTI.
 • Surveillance schedule is not well defined but consists of periodic reassessment of symptoms, urine
   studies including cytology, and endoscopy of the lower urinary tract.
 • Patients with poor emptying after relief of obstruction who remain symptomatic, or who cannot or will
   not undergo excision, can be managed with CIC or an indwelling catheter; ongoing CIC with surveillance is
   acceptable if no complicating factors arise.
 • Children: indications for diverticulectomy match adults, but with multiple diverticula in a chromosomal
   syndrome, observation and medical management are preferred — the connective tissue disorder impairs
   wound healing, raises perioperative risk and predisposes to recurrence.
Indications for Intervention
 • Many incidentally found congenital or acquired diverticula are managed expectantly with periodic
   surveillance.
 • Diverticula may be larger than the bladder (Fig. 122.11), but size does not correlate with symptoms or
   complications and is not an absolute indication for surgery.
 • Diverticulectomy is indicated for: LUTS related to the diverticulum that do not respond to medical
   therapy; persistent symptoms; chronic relapsing UTI; stones within the diverticulum; carcinoma or
   premalignant change; and upper tract deterioration from obstruction or VUR.
 • Because symptoms and complications arise from poor emptying and stasis, excision improves lower tract
   emptying provided the primary problem (BOO) has been addressed.
 • Excision is usually elective; merits versus surveillance are discussed individually. The patient should be a
   reasonable surgical risk: correct or optimise nutritional, renal, cardiac and pulmonary factors and treat
   preoperative UTI. Prohibitive-risk patients should not undergo excision but may suit CIC or endoscopic
   treatment.

Staged Management for Bladder Outlet Obstruction
 • Concomitant open, laparoscopic or robotic diverticulectomy with prostatectomy, or diverticulectomy with
   transurethral prostatectomy, is often performed; a staged approach is an alternative.
 • If the diverticulum is attributable to BOO and there are no complicating factors, treat the outlet first →
   reassess the diverticulum → if emptying is satisfactory, symptoms resolve, and VUR, infection, malignancy
   and stones are absent, ongoing surveillance may be all that is needed.
 • Emptying after relief of obstruction is assessed by VCUG, videourodynamics or cross-sectional imaging
   such as ultrasound.
Endoscopic Management
 • Candidates: older or debilitated patients, poor candidates for open surgery, and patients undergoing TUR
   of the prostate with an associated poorly draining diverticulum.
TUR of the diverticular neck (sequence): incise the neck with the resectoscope loop or a Collins knife →
carry incisions down to the bladder muscle fibres at the level of the ostium → circumferential resection of
the entire neck if needed → the enlarged neck loses its sphincter-like narrowing and the diverticulum
empties better during micturition.
 • Generally safe and well tolerated, but it can cause urinary retention through reversal of flow — bladder
   contents "vent" into the diverticulum during voiding. It is less successful in large diverticula.
 • Neck TUR combined with fulguration of the entire urothelial lining should obliterate the diverticulum or
   considerably reduce it. In a small short-term series it was reasonably effective for somewhat larger
   diverticula (≥4 cm) in patients unwilling or unable to undergo excision.
Operative Excision
 • Open, laparoscopic or robotic, with or without concomitant treatment of BOO. The technique of
   diverticulectomy is covered in Chapter 124.

FEMALE URETHRAL DIVERTICULA
 • Definition: female urethral diverticula (UD) are variably sized, urine-filled periurethral cystic structures
   adjacent to the urethra, within the confines of the pelvic fascia, connected to the urethra by an ostium.
   They are among the most challenging diagnostic and reconstructive problems in female urology.
 • Presentation ranges from asymptomatic lesions noted incidentally on examination or imaging to
   debilitating, painful vaginal masses with incontinence, stones, severe dyspareunia or tumours. Anatomical
   variation in location, size and complexity makes each case unique.
 • First described in 1805; the modern era began in the 1950s with positive pressure urethrography (PPU),
   after which reported cases rose sharply — one mid-century series roughly doubled the number reported
   in the preceding 60 years. Ultrasonography and surface-coil or endoluminal MRI have since refined
   understanding (Fig. 122.12).

Anatomy of the Female Urethra
 • A musculofascial tube about 3–4 cm long, from bladder neck to external meatus, suspended from the
   pelvic sidewall (tendinous arc of the obturator) by the urethropelvic ligament — two fused layers of pelvic
   fascia extending to the sidewall bilaterally (Fig. 122.13).
 • The ligament has an abdominal side (endopelvic fascia) and a vaginal side (periurethral fascia). The
   urethra and most UD lie within and between these two leaves.
 • The urethra is a rich, vascular, spongy cylinder surrounded by an envelope of smooth muscle, skeletal
   muscle and fibroelastic tissue.
 • Periurethral glands: tubuloalveolar glands in the thick vascular lamina propria (submucosa),
   posterolateral along the whole length but most prominent over the distal two-thirds, with most draining
   into the distal third (Fig. 122.14 — they drain distally but arborise proximally). Most acquired UD are
   thought to arise from pathology of these glands.
 • Skene (paraurethral) glands are the largest and most distal periurethral glands; they drain outside the
   lumen, lateral to the meatus.
 • Muscle layers (varying sphincteric function): inner longitudinal smooth muscle, outer circular smooth
   muscle, and a skeletal muscle layer that spans much of the length but is most prominent in the middle
   third. The skeletal layer is U-shaped (horseshoe), incomplete on the vaginal aspect where the urethra
   abuts the vagina.
 • The anterior vaginal wall lies ventral to the urethra, separated from it by the periurethral fascia.
 • Because the sphincters and UD overlap anatomically, sphincter location and competence matter when
   planning repair.
 • Innervation: pudendal nerve (S2–S4); afferents travel in the pelvic splanchnic nerves.

URETHRAL DIVERTICULA

Pathophysiology and Etiology
 • A UD is a cavity dissecting within the fascia of the urethropelvic ligament, usually an isolated cyst-like
   appendage with a single discrete ostium (Fig. 122.15). Complex patterns exist: extending partly around
   the urethra (saddlebag), anterior to the urethra, or circumferential (Fig. 122.16).
 • Congenital versus acquired: UD in children may be a different entity from adult UD. Congenital cases in
   infants are reported, and some in young females regress spontaneously. Congenital anterior UD in males
   are a well-described but entirely different entity. Congenital Skene gland cysts are extremely rare.
   Paediatric diverticula have been attributed to an ectopic ureter draining into a Gartner duct cyst and to a
   forme fruste of urethral duplication.
 • The vast majority of UD are acquired and diagnosed in adult females; two large series contained no
   patient under 10 years, arguing against a congenital cause. A congenital precursor that becomes
   symptomatic later remains unproven.

 • Huffman's anatomical work: serial 10-µm sections and wax models showed the periurethral glands lie
   mainly dorsolateral to the urethra, arborise proximally, yet drain through ducts in the distal third.
 • Initial infection and especially reinfection may come from Escherichia coli, other coliforms or vaginal flora.
 • Routh's concept (now the most widely accepted theory): recurrent infection of the periurethral glands →
   obstruction → suburethral abscess → rupture into the urethral lumen → continual filling and pooling →
   stasis, reinfection and epithelialisation of the cavity → permanent UD. Reinfection, inflammation and
   recurrent obstruction of the neck produce symptoms and enlargement.

 • Noncommunicating UD have been diagnosed on MRI; whether these are a forme fruste or UD with an

Young and Wahle's modern hypothesis (sequence): infection and obstruction of periurethral glands in the
submucosa of the distal two-thirds → repeated infection and abscess formation → expanding mass displaces
the spongy tissue → disrupts the muscular envelope → herniates into the periurethral fascia → dissects
within the leaves of the periurethral fascia and urethropelvic ligament, most commonly ventrally (the classic
anterior vaginal wall mass), sometimes laterally or dorsally → abscess ruptures into the lumen, creating the
communication.
Concept — The gland infection–abscess–rupture model predicts the posterolateral mid-to-distal ostium and
the ventral vaginal mass; its weak point is the proximal UD, which is why alternative mechanisms are still
entertained.

Prevalence
 • UD are found in proportion to how hard they are sought. Fewer than 100 cases were reported before
   1950; diagnosis rose with PPU.
 • True prevalence is unknown; reported up to 1–6% of adult females in some series. An accurate figure
   would require screening symptomatic and asymptomatic women in primary care, which has not been
   done.
 • Prevalence rises with symptom selection: low in autopsy and asymptomatic populations, higher in
   incontinent women, and highest in highly symptomatic or tertiary referral cohorts (about 10% on
   endorectal-coil MRI of women with LUTS) — the latter do not reflect the general population.
 • Surgical and population-based rates are far lower than the historical prevalence (population-based
   incidence under 0.02% per year), partly because UD may be asymptomatic, misdiagnosed or overlooked.
 • Race: older series found Black women up to six times as likely to have UD, historically ascribed to urban
   academic referral bias. One large institutional series found most diverticulectomies in White women, yet
   national inpatient UD surgery was threefold commoner in Black women.

Diverticular Anatomy and Histology
 • Lining may be urothelial, squamous, columnar, cuboidal or mixed; sometimes absent, the wall being
   fibrous tissue only. Two-thirds show inflammation.
 • Histology is usually benign, but about 10% of specimens show significant abnormality — metaplasia,
   dysplasia or carcinoma — requiring long-term follow-up or further treatment.

Presentation
 • Most patients present between the third and seventh decades. Symptoms and signs are protean (Box
   122.1).
 • The "three Ds" — dysuria, dyspareunia and (postvoid) dribbling — are historical: only about 5% of
   contemporary cases have all three, and individually or together they are neither sensitive nor specific.
 • Commonest symptoms: irritative LUTS (frequency, urgency), pain and infection.

Chapter 122 — Bladder and Female Urethral Diverticula                                                     Urethral Diverticula


 • Multiple bouts of recurrent cystitis should raise suspicion of UD (stasis in the sac).
 • Other complaints: pain, vaginal mass, haematuria, vaginal discharge, obstructive symptoms, retention.
   Gentle compression of a tender or nontender anterior vaginal wall mass may express urine or pus per
   meatus. Spontaneous rupture is extremely rare but can produce a urethrovaginal fistula.
 • Size and complexity do not correlate with symptoms: very large UD may be nearly silent while small,
   impalpable ones cause considerable distress. Symptoms wax and wane, perhaps with repeated infection
   and inflammation.
 • Misdiagnosis is common — treatment for years for interstitial cystitis, recurrent cystitis, vulvodynia,
   endometriosis or vulvovestibulitis. Diagnostic delay runs to years across many physicians, even though
   about half have a palpable mass — underscoring a baseline level of suspicion and a thorough pelvic
   examination.

Pregnancy
 • UD may present in pregnancy; whether pregnancy causes UD is unknown, but it may unmask symptoms.
 • Conservative management until after delivery — antibiotics, aspiration or incision and drainage — avoids
   precipitating premature labour. Drainage during labour can facilitate delivery; vaginal delivery is possible;
   definitive repair follows delivery. Successful surgery during pregnancy has been reported, and
   transvaginal aspiration of symptomatic lesions is an option.
Concept — Because UD symptoms are those of common lower urinary tract complaints and do not track
lesion size, diagnosis depends on suspicion and examination rather than on any symptom pattern — the
"three Ds" persist only as history.

Evaluation and Diagnosis
 • Diagnosis combines history, physical examination, urinalysis and culture, cystourethroscopy and
   selected imaging; urodynamics helps in selected cases.

History and Physical Examination
 • Record LUTS, signs (for example haematuria), prior investigations and response to previous therapy —
   the basis for counselling about likely symptom improvement and for suspecting UD in diagnostic
   dilemmas.
 • Prior pelvic and anti-incontinence surgery: bulking agents can appear as UD on imaging, and a previous
   sling can hinder access and repair.
 • Document incontinence — presence, pad use and type (urge vs stress vs postvoid dribbling) — and
   sexual function and dyspareunia, since vaginal reconstructive surgery can worsen or improve them.
 • Palpate the anterior vaginal wall for masses and tenderness; record location, size and consistency. Most
   UD lie ventrally over the mid and proximal urethra, i.e. 1–3 cm inside the introitus (Fig. 122.19).
 • Lesions near or distorting the meatus are more likely Skene gland cysts or abscesses; distinguishing them
   may require MRI.
 • UD may lie anterior to the urethra, extend partly or fully around it, or extend proximally to the bladder
   neck, distorting the outlet and trigone. Such lesions raise the risk of intraoperative bladder and ureteric
   injury and of postoperative voiding dysfunction and incontinence.
 • A particularly hard mass suggests a calculus or malignancy and mandates further investigation.
 • Stripping ("milking") the anterior vaginal wall distally to express pus or urine is often described, but it
   fails to produce meatal discharge in the majority.
 • Assess vaginal atrophy, rugation and elasticity: atrophic, poorly oestrogenised tissue is used for flaps, so
   preoperative topical oestrogen may improve it. Assess introital capacity — a narrow introitus may
   require episiotomy. Perform a provocative stress test and assess for pelvic organ prolapse.
Urine Studies
 • Urinalysis and culture are mandatory. Commonest organism E. coli; other gram-negative enterics, N.
   gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia, streptococci and staphylococci are also found.
 • A sterile culture does not exclude infection — many patients are on antibiotics at presentation. Cytology
   if irritative LUTS or suspected malignancy.
Cystourethroscopy
 • Aims: visualise the ostium and exclude other causes of LUTS.
 • A flexible cystoscope (no beak) or rigid female cystoscope (short beak) keeps the irrigant outflow beside
   the lens, distending the short female urethra for better luminal views.
 • Manoeuvre: compress the bladder neck while an assistant presses on the diverticular sac, or compress
   the UD digitally during urethroscopy — pus is often seen issuing from the ostium.
 • Ostium usually posterolateral at 4 and 8 o'clock at the mid-urethra, but often very hard to find (Fig.
   122.20); success ranges 15–89%.
 • Highly symptomatic patients often need anaesthesia for endoscopy.
 • A positive study helps planning; failure to find an ostium must not deter further investigation or repair.
Urodynamics
 • Useful for patients with incontinence or significant voiding dysfunction, including documenting stress
   urinary incontinence (SUI) before repair.
 • About one-third present with incontinence; up to 50% demonstrate SUI on urodynamics.
 • VUDS is optimal: it combines VCUG and urodynamics, consolidating evaluation and reducing
   catheterisations. It distinguishes genuine SUI (needs an additional anti-incontinence procedure) from
   paradoxic dribbling from the UD (cured by diverticulectomy alone).

 • A few patients show BOO from the obstructive or mass effect of the UD; SUI may coexist with obstruction,

 • High-quality preoperative imaging must both diagnose and map the anatomy — especially the

Diagnostic Contrast Radiography
 • No single study is the gold standard. Choice depends on availability, cost and radiologist expertise.
   Options: double-balloon PPU, VCUG, intravenous urography, ultrasonography and MRI with or without an
   endoluminal coil.
Double-balloon PPU (historically optimal; sequence): specialised Trattner catheter with two balloons several
centimetres apart → one balloon at the external meatus, one at the bladder neck → both inflated to seal the
urethral lumen → contrast infused under slight pressure through a side hole between the balloons (Fig.
122.21) → urethra distends and contrast is forced into the UD. Outstanding images and, unlike VCUG,
independent of voiding; but rarely performed and the catheter is not widely available.
 • VCUG (Fig. 122.22): widely available and familiar; sensitivity 44–95%. Patients often cannot void in the
   radiology suite (catheter pain, psychogenic inhibition), and without voiding the UD is often not seen — a
   VCUG without voiding or postvoid images that shows no UD is nondiagnostic. If voiding fails under
   fluoroscopy, the patient voids in private and a postvoid film is taken. Poor flow underfills the UD and
   underestimates size and complexity (Fig. 122.23).
 • Three-dimensional CT VCUG with reconstructions is novel and not yet widely used.
 • Intravenous urography or CT urography: when the upper tract must be delineated or a congenital ectopic
   ureter is suspected as the cause of an anterior vaginal wall mass; the postvoid film occasionally shows the
   UD.
Ultrasonography
 • Abdominal, transvaginal, translabial and transurethral approaches; transvaginal imaging often gives size
   and location. The UD is anechoic or hypoechoic with enhanced through-transmission.
 • Advantages: relatively noninvasive, no radiation, no need to void. Limitation: may not give detailed high-
   resolution surgical anatomy.
 • Contrast-enhanced sonourethrography (transvaginal or transurethral) improves accuracy but needs a
   catheter — as invasive as VCUG — and a skilled sonographer.
Magnetic Resonance Imaging
 • Relatively noninvasive, high-resolution, multiplanar. UD are low signal on T1 and high signal on T2.
   Distinct advantages over VCUG: wholly independent of voiding and free of ionising radiation.
 • Surface-coil (Fig. 122.24) and endoluminal MRI (eMRI) techniques exist. eMRI places the coil in a body
   cavity next to the area of interest — intravaginal or intrarectal — improving signal-to-noise ratio and
   resolution, with excellent sensitivity and specificity.
 • Surface-coil MRI and eMRI appear superior to VCUG and/or PPU, but are expensive and not universally
   available. Against PPU, MRI agrees well on the diagnosis, less well on complexity and location, and least
   well on demonstrating the urethral connection.
 • Pitfalls: several lesions, including periurethral bulking agents, can be misdiagnosed as UD, and MRI may
   be inadequate for detecting malignancy within a UD.
 • Contraindications are few: metallic foreign bodies, claustrophobia, intolerance of the endoluminal probe.

Vaginal leiomyoma
 • Benign mesenchymal tumour of vaginal smooth muscle (Fig. 122.25); uncommon (about 300 cases
   reported) and a small minority of periurethral masses; may be misdiagnosed as UD.
 • Symptoms relate to size — mass effect, obstruction, pain, dyspareunia; fourth to fifth decade.
 • Like uterine leiomyomas, oestrogen dependent and regress at menopause. Transvaginal excision or
   enucleation is often curative and is recommended to confirm the diagnosis, exclude malignancy and
   relieve symptoms.
Skene gland abnormalities
 • Cysts and abscesses are similar lesions differentiated clinically (Fig. 122.26): small cystic masses just lateral
   or inferolateral to the meatus, lined by transitional or stratified squamous epithelium. Abscesses are very
   tender and inflamed, and pus may be expressed from the ductal orifice.
 • Classically, unlike UD, they do not communicate with the urethral lumen.
 • Seen in neonates and young to middle-aged women; symptoms include dysuria, dyspareunia, obstruction
   and pain.
 • Clinical distinction: Skene lesions are distal and often distort the meatus; UD are usually mid or proximal
   and do not.
 • Treatments: aspiration, marsupialisation, incision and drainage, simple excision — excision is curative.
 • Skene gland adenocarcinoma: because of homology with the prostate, PSA may be raised and
   normalises with treatment.
Gartner duct abnormalities
 • Mesonephric remnants on the anterolateral vaginal wall from cervix to introitus. Being mesonephric,
   they may receive ectopic ureters from poorly functioning or nonfunctioning upper-pole moieties of
   duplex systems (Fig. 122.27 — a turgid, debris-filled ectopic ureter on the anterior vaginal wall in a
   woman with lifelong incontinence and recurrent pyelonephritis); single-system ectopia is much less
   common in females.
 • The proportion with ipsilateral renal abnormality is unclear, so upper tract evaluation is recommended.
   About 6% of patients with unilateral renal agenesis have a Gartner duct cyst; up to 50% with Gartner
   duct cyst and renal dysplasia have ipsilateral müllerian duct obstruction.
 • Treatment: asymptomatic with a nonfunctioning moiety → observe; aspiration with sclerotherapy has
   succeeded; symptomatic → simple excision or marsupialisation; functioning renal moiety →
   individualise.
Vaginal wall cysts
 • Usually small, asymptomatic anterior wall masses that may enlarge to cause LUTS or dyspareunia (Fig.
   122.28 — misdiagnosed as a cystocele for years).
 • Origins: mesonephric (Gartner), paramesonephric (müllerian), endometriotic, urothelial or epidermoid
   (inclusion). Specific diagnosis only on histology; subtype is usually of little consequence, but epidermoid
   cysts follow trauma or vaginal surgery. Müllerian cysts are commonest, then epidermoid, then
   mesonephric; the remainder are Bartholin-type, endometriotic or indeterminate.
 • Must be distinguished from UD; simple excision if symptomatic.

Urethral mucosal prolapse
 • Circumferential herniation or eversion of urethral mucosa at the meatus: a beefy red, doughnut-shaped
   lesion completely surrounding the meatus. May be asymptomatic or cause bleeding, spotting, pain or
   urinary symptoms.
 • Two populations: prepubertal and postmenopausal females. A predilection for young Black girls is not
   confirmed by recent series.
 • Children: often related to Valsalva voiding or constipation, with eversion through a pathologically loose
   attachment between the urethral smooth muscle layers. Postmenopausal: causation less clear,
   epidemiologically linked to oestrogen deficiency.
 • Medical: topical creams (oestrogen, anti-inflammatory) and/or sitz baths. Surgical: cauterisation, ligation
   around a Foley catheter, or complete circumferential excision with suture reapproximation of the
   remaining mucosa to the vaginal wall (few complications).
 • Bottom line: medical treatment often fails or recurs; surgical excision gives superior results to medical
   management or catheter ligation, with urethral stenosis a possible complication.
Urethral caruncle
 • Inflammatory lesion of the distal urethra, usually in postmenopausal women: a reddish, exophytic,
   mucosa-covered mass at the meatus. Often asymptomatic and incidental; when irritated causes
   underwear spotting or pain, less often voiding symptoms; rarely thromboses into a discoloured mass (Fig.
   122.29 — simple excision curative).
 • Aetiologically related to mucosal prolapse; chronic irritation produces haemorrhage, necrosis and
   inflammatory growth, matching the histology.
 • Atypical appearance or behaviour → excise to exclude coexisting or mimicking disease: intestinal
   metaplasia, tuberculosis, melanoma, lymphoma.
 • Evidence is sparse; initial conservative treatment with topical oestrogen or anti-inflammatory creams and
   sitz baths.
Simple excision of a large or refractory caruncle (sequence): grasp the tip → apply traction to expose the
base fully → excise → reapproximate the mucosa with absorbable suture if the defect is large → Foley
catheter for several days, around which the mucosa usually heals.

Urethral Diverticula and Stress Urinary Incontinence
 • SUI may coexist with UD. Significant de novo SUI occurs in 7–16% after diverticulectomy without a
   concomitant anti-incontinence procedure. When minor leakage is counted, de novo SUI approaches half,
   but most is minor and few need further surgery.
 • Risk factors for de novo SUI: size ≥30 mm, proximal location, circumferential configuration and shorter
   urethral length. A complex circumferential configuration on MRI carried more postoperative SUI than a
   simple UD in one series, but this has not been found consistently.
 • Not every UD patient needs simultaneous SUI surgery: extra time, cost and morbidity must be weighed
   against convenience. About 50% of SUI improves or resolves after diverticulectomy alone, so routine
   concomitant surgery risks overtreatment.
 • Bothersome symptomatic SUI → a concomitant anti-incontinence procedure can be offered (Fig.
   122.30); same-setting repair is well described. No or mild non-bothersome SUI → diverticulectomy alone,
   then reassess after recovery.
 • Synthetic material (mid-urethral polypropylene mesh) must not be used synchronously with UD surgery
   — increased risk of urethral erosion and infection. Whether it is safe months or years later is unknown.
 • Autologous pubovaginal fascial slings give satisfactory results as a concomitant procedure.
 • Staged approach: most persistent or de novo SUI after transvaginal UD excision resolves with 12 months
   of conservative measures.
Concept — The tissue dissected at diverticulectomy is the continence mechanism, so surgery can unmask,
create or cure SUI. Hence only bothersome SUI is treated at the same sitting, and only with autologous tissue
next to a freshly reconstructed urethra.

Surgical Repair of Female Urethral Diverticula
Indications for Repair
 • Not all UD mandate excision. Some are asymptomatic or incidental; some patients are unwilling or
   medically unfit. The natural history is unknown — whether untreated UD progress in size, symptoms or
   complexity is not known. Surgical risks include de novo incontinence, urethral stricture and vaginal
   scarring.
 • However, carcinomas arising in UD are repeatedly reported, some asymptomatic and not identified on
   imaging — so patients choosing nonoperative management must be counselled.
 • Nonoperative management: low-dose antibacterial suppression and digital stripping of the anterior
   vaginal wall after micturition to prevent dribbling and reduce stasis-related UTI. Whether long-term
   cancer surveillance is needed is unknown.
 • Symptomatic patients — dysuria, refractory bothersome postvoid dribbling, recurrent UTI, dyspareunia,

Techniques for Repair
Alternative techniques
 • Since the original 1805 description (transvaginal incision and packing with lint): transurethral and open
   marsupialisation, endoscopic unroofing, fulguration, incision and obliteration with oxidised cellulose or
   polytetrafluoroethylene, coagulation, and excision with reconstruction — the last now most common.
 • Transvaginal marsupialisation (Spence-Duckett) of distal lesions may reduce operative time, blood loss
   and recurrence.
 • Risks: extending the incision too far proximally causes vaginal voiding or damages the proximal and distal
   sphincter mechanisms (SUI); in sexually active women a pseudoseptum may form between urethra and
   anterior vaginal wall, causing dyspareunia.
 • Therefore applicable only to UD of the distal third in women who are not sexually active, in very select
   cases; not commonly performed.
Excision and reconstruction
 • The commonest modern approach; principles are set out in Box 122.2. Minor areas of disagreement:
   vaginal incision (midline vs inverted U vs inverted T), whether the entire mucosalised portion must be
   removed, and drainage (urethral catheter alone vs urethral plus suprapubic).

BOX 122.2 PRINCIPLES OF TRANSVAGINAL URETHRAL DIVERTICULECTOMY
 No.     Principle
 1       Mobilisation of a well-vascularised anterior vaginal wall flap(s)
 2       Preservation of the periurethral fascia as a separate layer

Preoperative preparation
 • Urine must be sterile; do not operate in acute UTI. Prophylactic antibiotics help ensure sterility;
   preoperative parenteral antibiotics, especially with recurrent or persistent UTI.
 • Postvoid stripping of the anterior vaginal wall empties the UD and prevents stasis and UTI — not possible
   with noncommunicating or painful UD.
 • Topical oestrogen for several weeks in postmenopausal atrophic vaginitis improves tissue for dissection
   and flap mobilisation.
 • Counselling is paramount: pain, dyspareunia, voiding dysfunction, UTI and incontinence may persist
   despite technically successful surgery.
 • Symptomatic SUI → offer simultaneous anti-incontinence surgery. Preoperative VUDS maps the UD,
   assesses bladder neck competence, confirms SUI, and separates true SUI from postvoid dribbling, vaginal
   voiding and paradoxic incontinence from a urine-filled UD.
Procedure (Fig. 122.31)
 1. Position and exposure: dorsal lithotomy, pressure points padded, adjustable padded stirrups; vaginal
    antiseptic preparation; weighted vaginal speculum and Scott (ring) retractor with hooks, Allis clamps. A
    posterolateral episiotomy occasionally helps, but the mid-urethral location of most UD usually makes it
    unnecessary.
2. Drainage: urethral Foley catheter. If wanted, place a suprapubic tube at the start (Lowsley tractor, or
   percutaneously under cystoscopic guidance) — never at the end, when it would traverse and risk
   disrupting the fresh urethral suture line.
3. Incision: mark an inverted U — base at the distal urethra, limbs to the bladder neck or beyond, widening
   proximally to preserve vascularity at the distal lateral flap margins. Compared with the inverted T, the
   inverted U gives excellent lateral exposure at the mid-vagina and extends proximally as needed. Saline
   infiltration aids dissection.
4. Vaginal flap: raise the anterior vaginal wall flap in the plane between vaginal wall and periurethral fascia,
   with countertraction; preserve the fascia and avoid entering the UD.
5. Fascia: incise the periurethral fascia transversely; develop proximal and distal fascial flaps without
   entering the UD.

 6. Excision: grasp the UD and dissect it back to its origin within the fascial leaves, opening it if necessary;
    identify the ostium; remove the entire mucosalised wall, including small adherent or inflamed portions of
    urethral wall at the ostium, so that no mucosa remains to cause leakage or recurrence. Historical aids
    (urinary or Fogarty catheters in the UD, gauze packing, methylene blue, silicone or cryoprecipitate casts)
    are usually unnecessary.
 7. Urethral closure: the catheter is often visible after excision. Close over a catheter as small as 12 Fr
    without long-term stricture risk — watertight, tension-free, absorbable. A circumferential UD may
    require segmental urethral resection and complex reconstruction.
 8. Fascial closure: reapproximate the periurethral fascial flaps perpendicular to the urethral suture line to
    minimise overlap and fistula risk, obliterating all dead space.
 9. Optional Martius flap: a fibrofatty labial flap over the fascia as an extra layer. Indications are not agreed,
    but it is favoured with poor-quality tissue, attenuated fascia or marked inflammation to reduce wound
    breakdown and urethrovaginal fistula; harvest complications are low (≤2%) with good cosmesis.
10. Vaginal closure: advance and reapproximate the vaginal flap with running interlocking absorbable suture
    — a three-layer closure (four with a Martius flap) — and place an antibiotic-impregnated vaginal pack.
 • Pseudodiverticulum (fascia attenuated or absent): reconstruct with an interpositional Martius flap or a
   graft such as a pubovaginal fascial sling. Preserving and reconstructing the fascia is paramount to prevent
   recurrence, close dead space and avoid fistula (Fig. 122.32).
 • Rare anterior UD without a posterior component: suprameatal, infrapubic incision, same principles.
   Surgery in the prone-jackknife position has also been described, reflecting the ergonomic difficulty of
   transvaginal surgery.
Postoperative care
Antibiotics for 24 hours → remove vaginal pack → discharge with closed catheter drainage; antispasmodics
liberally for bladder spasm → pericatheter VCUG at 14–21 days → no extravasation: remove catheters;
extravasation: repeat weekly until resolved — most resolve within several weeks conservatively.

 • Fistula prevention: meticulous technique, good haemostasis, avoiding infection, preserving the

Persistence or recurrence of symptoms after urethral diverticulectomy
 • Obstructive and irritative LUTS may resolve, but symptoms persist or recur in a substantial number
   regardless of UD complexity, approach or technical success. Symptoms caused by surgery may settle with
   time; otherwise investigate for recurrent UD, a new UD or urethral stricture.
 • Excision of a recurrent UD is less likely to resolve preoperative symptoms.
 • Causes of recurrence: incomplete removal, inadequate urethral closure, residual dead space, other
   technical factors. Long-term recurrence about 10–22%; pooled contemporary rate about 10%.
 • Risk factors: circumferential configuration (independently associated on multivariate analysis), complex
   configuration (simple UD rarely recur after one operation; complex ones often need several), proximal
   location and multiple UD. Horseshoe UD had low recurrence in one series.
 • Reoperation is difficult — altered anatomy, scarring, obscured planes — and fistula and recurrence are
   commoner in reoperative cases; secondary procedures are less successful at complete removal.

 KEY POINTS: MANAGEMENT OF FEMALE URETHRAL DIVERTICULA
   • Investigate concomitant incontinence thoroughly before surgery; strongly consider treating
     bothersome SUI at the time of diverticulectomy.
   • Transvaginal excision with urethral reconstruction is the commonest approach.
