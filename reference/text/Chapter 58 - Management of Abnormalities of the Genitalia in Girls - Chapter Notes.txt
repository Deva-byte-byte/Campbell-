FEMALE GENITAL EMBRYOLOGY
Cloacal division
 • The cloaca is an endoderm-lined confluence of hindgut (dorsal) and allantois (ventral); it receives the
   mesonephric ducts just before week 4.
 • The urorectal septum (appears week 4) separates the urogenital sinus ventrally from the anorectal canal
   dorsally. Two components:
    ◦ Tourneux fold — coronal fold growing caudally from the allantois–hindgut angle towards the cloacal
      membrane.
     ◦ Rathke plicae — lateral cloacal wall infoldings that coalesce in the midline and complete the septum
       caudally.
 • By weeks 6–7 the septum fuses with the cloacal membrane → ventral urogenital membrane + dorsal anal
   membrane.
 • The resulting fibromuscular node (perineal body) is the insertion of the perineal muscles and divides the
   primitive cloacal sphincter into urogenital diaphragm (anterior) and external anal sphincter (posterior) —
   hence both are supplied by the pudendal nerve.
Mesonephric duct and ureteric bud
 • Mesonephric ducts enter the urogenital sinus near the müllerian tubercle; the ureteric bud induces the
   metanephric blastema (secretory kidney).
 • The terminal ureteric bud–mesonephric segment is absorbed into the sinus wall → ureteric orifices at the
   lateral trigone.
Müllerian ducts and the vagina
 • Paramesonephric (müllerian) ducts form from coelomic epithelium lateral to the mesonephric ducts,
   cross medially and fuse → proximity explains paramesonephric anomaly + ipsilateral renal anomaly.
 • Caudal fusion with dissolution of the midline partition → common uterovaginal canal → uterus, cervix,
   proximal two-thirds of vagina. Failure of septal regression → müllerian duct anomalies.
 • Koff (1933): contact of the uterovaginal primordium with the sinus forms the müllerian tubercle, which
   induces paired endodermal sinovaginal bulbs (possibly terminal wolffian segments).
 • Sequence: sinovaginal bulbs proliferate → solid cord → vaginal plate → canalised caudal-to-cranial →
   distal vagina.
 • Sinus distal to the tubercle everts (exstrophies) to form the vestibule → separate urethral and vaginal
   openings.
 • Hymen = invagination of the posterior wall of the urogenital sinus separating vaginal lumen from sinus;
   normally ruptures perinatally.
Vestibular glands and cystic remnants
 • Skene (paraurethral) glands — homologues of the prostatic glands; Gartner duct — wolffian duct
   remnant; Bartholin (greater vestibular) glands — urogenital sinus outgrowths, homologues of the
   bulbourethral glands.

Skeletal development and caudal regression
 • Vertebrae form craniocaudally; lower limb buds from somites 25–29, differentiating weeks 4–8.
 • A segmentation disturbance at the caudal somites when the fetus is <10 mm (weeks 4–5) affects many
   systems → caudal regression syndrome (Duhamel, 1961): terminal bowel, kidney, bladder, müllerian
   system, lumbosacral spine.
 • Proposed mechanisms: disordered mesodermal migration, reduced proliferation, premature apoptosis;
   increased in infants of diabetic mothers; homeobox gene deletions reproduce the constellation.
 • Because somites differentiate cranial-to-caudal, earlier insults → higher anorectal malformations with
   more severe upper tract, genital duct and spinal anomalies than low imperforate anus.
 • Mesodermal disturbance can extend cranially to C1 and the tracheo-oesophageal anlagen (VATER,
   MURCS).

 KEY POINTS: FEMALE GENITAL EMBRYOLOGY
   • Common ontogeny of urogenital and external anal sphincters → pudendal nerve supplies both.
   • Müllerian–wolffian proximity → paramesonephric anomalies with ipsilateral renal anomalies.
   • Caudal somite segmentation disturbance in weeks 4–5 → urogenital, skeletal and gastrointestinal
     malformations = caudal regression syndrome.


Concept — Genital, renal, anorectal and spinal anomalies travel together because they are built from
adjacent tissue in the same week; the earlier and more cranial the insult, the more systems it takes with it.

EVALUATION AND CLASSIFICATION OF FEMALE GENITAL ANOMALIES
Examination
 • Differential of vulval anomalies is broad; age and ancestry narrow it, but physical examination is the
   most useful tool.
 • Reassure the child that examination is painless; frog-leg position. Record clitoral size, hymenal
   configuration, urethral location, character of any interlabial mass (smooth, lobulated, haemorrhagic).
 • Pull-down manoeuvre: labia majora grasped and drawn caudally and laterally → funnels introitus,
   displays distal vagina.
 • Define the mass relative to landmarks: lubricated cotton applicator posteriorly (vagina) and/or small
   feeding tube in the suspected urethra.
 • Awake inspection: otoscope, nasal speculum or paediatric vaginal speculum. Vaginal discharge or
   bleeding → examination and vaginoscopy under anaesthesia.
 • Renal–pelvic ultrasonography is a useful adjunct in selected disorders.

 • Ultrasonography: genital anatomy and screening for upper tract anomalies.
 • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) = gold standard for müllerian anatomy — especially presence of a
   cervix and functioning endometrium.
 • Complex cases: examination under anaesthesia, vaginoscopy, hysteroscopy, laparoscopy.
 • Obstructive anomalies → immediate intervention; non-obstructive → usually no surgery until
   reproductive age and only if intercourse or fertility is affected.
 • Most inclusive classification: American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM).
Concept — The operative decision turns on one question: is it obstructive? Obstruction mandates prompt
drainage; everything else waits for function to demand it.

CONGENITAL DISORDERS OF FEMALE EXTERNAL GENITALIA

Disorders of the Clitoris
Hypertrophied Clitoris
 • Commonest cause: congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) — androgenic metabolites from an adrenal
   steroid enzyme block; 21-hydroxylase or 11β-hydroxylase deficiency. Some clitoromegaly in nearly every
   girl with CAH.
 • Degree of clitoromegaly often but not always matches common urogenital sinus length.
 • Any newborn with clitoromegaly: serum electrolytes, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, karyotype.
 • Glucocorticoid + mineralocorticoid replacement → negative feedback removes further androgenic
   stimulation.

Diminutive Clitoris
 • Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (CAIS) → reduced clitoral length versus controls; clitoral-to-
   urethral distance unchanged.

Disorders of the Vestibule
Urethral Prolapse
 • Complete circumferential eversion of urethral mucosa at the meatus; first described by Solinger (1732).
 • Bimodal: prepubertal girls of African ancestry and postmenopausal White women.
 • Proposed causes: hypo-oestrogenism, abnormal connections between inner longitudinal and outer
   circular distal urethral muscle, episodic raised intra-abdominal pressure.
 • Commonest complaint: bleeding / spotting on underwear from oedematous friable mucosa.
 • Sign: doughnut-shaped mass with meatus at centre; if a catheter passes through the centre, no imaging
   is needed.
 • Treatment: observation, topical corticosteroid, conjugated oestrogen, sitz baths → spontaneous reduction
   possible but recurrence after non-operative treatment is high.
 • Procedure of choice: circumferential excision of redundant mucosa + suture of normal urethra to
   vestibule. Avoid ligation over a catheter (sloughing) and cryosurgery.

Urethral Polyp
 • Paediatric equivalent of the urethral caruncle; rare; presents as an interlabial mass.
 • Likely hamartomatous or inflammatory; benign, transitional and squamous epithelium. Include in the
   differential of any interlabial mass.

Vestibular Cysts
 • Three neonatal introital cysts: paraurethral (Skene), Gartner duct, covered ectopic ureter. Large cyst
   emanating from the vaginal introitus → renal–pelvic ultrasonography for duplication.
 • Paraurethral cyst: dilated periurethral gland (6–30 glands; two largest = Skene) responding to maternal
   oestrogen with mucoid secretion. Hallmark: displaced meatus → eccentric stream. Meatus clearly
   separate → no imaging. Usually self-limited, ruptures; persistent → bedside needle drainage.
 • Gartner duct cyst: incomplete wolffian regression; anteromedial vaginal wall.

 • Covered ectopic ureter: an ectopic ureter may end in a wolffian segment (= Gartner duct); usually
   ruptures before birth into the vagina; if epithelium persists → urine-filled covered cyst. Intraoperative
   contrast defines anatomy; incision relieves obstruction.
 • More ectopic insertion → more dysplastic segment, yet vaginal-inserting moieties may still make urine →
   upper pole heminephrectomy if significant urine output (incontinence) or infection after
   decompression.

Prolapsed Ureterocele
 • Ectopic ureterocele = cystic dilatation of the terminal ureter; ~90% subtend the upper pole of a duplex
   system.
 • Prolapse through the urethra during voiding, almost always in infancy → urinary retention; relative
   urological emergency. Colour pink → dusky purple with duration.
 • Bladder–renal ultrasonography: upper pole hydronephrosis in a duplex system.
 • Sequence: needle decompression or incision → reduction → urethral catheter; general anaesthesia often
   preferable (infant straining); contrast injection aids anatomy.

Ectopic Ureteral Insertion
 • In girls the ectopic ureter may enter distal to the urethral sphincter → diurnal incontinence despite
   normal voiding.
 • Usually upper pole of a duplex; single-system ectopia occurs and may mimic absent kidney → search for
   an ectopic kidney.

Labial Enlargement
 • Imaging: ultrasonography first, Doppler for vascularity, MRI as adjunct.
 • Commonest cause: inguinal hernia — usually patent processus vaginalis, but consider CAIS: a large
   proportion of CAIS girls undergo childhood hernia repair, and CAIS is found in a small but real fraction (~1–
   2.5%) of girls with hernia.
 • Excluding CAIS: uterus on preoperative ultrasonography, karyotype, or vaginoscopic cervix at surgery;
   prenatal cell-free DNA without a Y chromosome confirms 46,XX and may make these unnecessary.
 • If CAIS: preserve the testis (oestrogen via peripheral aromatisation of testosterone); place intra-
   abdominally or superficial to the fascia for easier monitoring.
 • Hernia + phallic enlargement → mosaic DSD, ovotesticular DSD, partial androgen insensitivity syndrome,
   5α-reductase deficiency.
 • Childhood asymmetric labium majus enlargement (CALME): physiological expansion of normal vulval
   tissue, no capsule; soft, fleshy, painless; fibrous tissue enclosing fat lobules; oestrogen receptor-positive,
   but low prepubertal oestrogen questions a hormonal role.
 • No well-delineated mass → observe; continued growth → other aetiologies: plexiform neurofibroma,
   sarcoma, vascular/lymphatic malformation, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour, metastatic
   (extraintestinal) Crohn disease. Well-demarcated mass → consider excision.

Disorders of the Vagina
Imperforate Hymen and Hymenal Skin Tags
 • Hymenal skin tags: almost always normal; excise if symptomatic (bleeding) to exclude malignancy.
 • Congenital hymenal variants range from imperforate to microperforate.
 • Imperforate hymen = probably the commonest congenital obstructive anomaly of the female tract.

Abnormalities of Vertical Fusion
General
 • Present after puberty with amenorrhoea, abdominopelvic pain, distension mimicking pregnancy. Delay
   → retrograde menstruation → endometriosis.
Transverse vaginal septum
 • Failure of fusion and/or canalisation of urogenital sinus and müllerian ducts; ~1 in 70,000 females.
 • Post-menarche: amenorrhoea, pelvic pain, urinary retention from haematocolpos; neonates/children:
   mucocolpos; or incidental.
 • Level: upper and middle third commonest, lower third least. Usually <1 cm thick, often a small central/
   eccentric perforation — obstruction and ascending infection still occur despite perforation.
 • Diagnosis: transperineal, transrectal, abdominal ultrasonography and MRI (level, thickness). MRI
   distinguishes a high septum from cervical agenesis — misdiagnosis causes serious morbidity.
 • If pain is controllable: defer surgery with gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist or continuous
   oral contraceptive → lower segment dilates, easing repair.
 • Techniques: simple incision; excision with approximation of upper and lower mucosal edges; Z-plasties.
 • Balloon-assisted division: ultrasound-guided 14-gauge angiocatheter through septum → aspirate thick
   blood → 0.035-inch guidewire → NephroMax balloon dilatation → excise remaining septum; thick
   septum → resectoscope loop excision.
 • Transabdominal approach: posterior wall of distended vagina opened vertically → septum perforated →
   Foley balloon left 2 weeks; used where sociocultural belief requires an intact hymen.
 • Commonest complication: stenosis at resection site. Z-plasty converts a contracting transverse line into a
   longitudinal one; postoperative mould further reduces stenosis.

Vaginal atresia (distal vagina)
 • Urogenital sinus fails to form the lower vagina; müllerian structures normal → uterus, cervix, upper
   vagina present (distinct from vaginal agenesis and CAIS).
 • Examination: very shallow dimple below the urethra; distended vagina palpable rectally distinguishes it
   from CAIS or agenesis.
 • Ultrasonography and/or MRI mandatory before intervention.
 • Sequence: transverse incision at hymenal ring → dissect through fibrous zone → reach distended upper
   vagina → drain → pull-through to introitus. Haematocolpos acts as a tissue expander. Gap bridged by
   perineal skin flaps and/or vaginal mobilisation; combined abdominoperineal approach aids exposure
   and anastomosis.

Vaginal agenesis (müllerian aplasia)
 • ~1 in 5000 live female births: congenital absence of the proximal vagina in a phenotypically,
   chromosomally (46,XX) and hormonally (normal luteinising hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone)
   intact female.
 • MRKH syndrome: sinovaginal bulbs fail to develop and form the vaginal plate, possibly from failed
   induction by the uterovaginal primordium; coincident mesodermal organogenesis explains associated
   findings. Associated with maternal galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase deficiency.
 • Hymenal fringe and small vaginal pouch present (urogenital sinus-derived) — unlike vaginal atresia.
 • Presents after expected menarche: second commonest cause of primary amenorrhoea after gonadal
   dysgenesis; minority have cyclic pain (functioning uterine remnant).
 • Inguinal hernia less common than in CAIS. Imaging: remnant müllerian structures, renal and skeletal
   anomalies.

Associated Findings
 • Variable absence/hypoplasia of cervix, uterus and tubes; ~10% have an obstructed normal or
   rudimentary uterus with functioning endometrium. Most lack a uterus or have a solid rudimentary one;
   tubes frequently rudimentary.
 • Ovaries always present and functional (occasionally cystic).
 • Renal anomalies in about one-third (first noted by Rokitansky, 1838) — almost exclusively type B; mostly
   unilateral renal agenesis or ectopia. Converse holds: girls with renal anomalies frequently have genital
   anomalies.
 • Skeletal anomalies 10–20%. Klippel-Feil (cervical vertebral fusion; ~1 in 30,000–40,000 births) → MURCS
   association (Duncan, 1977), generalised mesodermal defect in week 4; Klippel-Feil only in type B. Also
   scoliosis, hand and face anomalies.
 • Abnormal pelvic vasculature — awareness prevents surgical misadventure.
 • Not associated with lumbosacral spinal disorders or occult dysraphism (unlike anomalies with abnormal
   cloacal septation).
 • Type B proportion varies with ethnicity (high in White cohorts, lower in Chinese populations).

Vaginal Replacement Surgery
Creation of a skin neovagina
 • Helpful for the patient to speak with someone previously treated before choosing.
 • Frank (1938) dilation: graduated hard dilators pressed on the perineum → progressive invagination of the
   dimple; Ingram modification = bicycle-seat stool. Best when a dimple or pouch exists.
 • Progressive perineal dilation = first-line therapy in adolescents; a multicentre study found surgery not
   superior to dilation → non-surgical options always before vaginoplasty.
 • Vecchietti: Plexiglas “olive” against the dimple, traction sutures passed cranially through vesicorectal
   space and abdominal wall and progressively tensioned; laparoscopic assistance avoids laparotomy;
   healthy mucosa, near-normal length at 3 months; little used by urologists.
 • Abbe (1898), the first landmark: canal between rectum and urethra lined with split-thickness skin →
   popularised as the McIndoe procedure.
 • McIndoe sequence: full mechanical + antibiotic bowel preparation → split-thickness buttock graft
   (0.018–0.022 inch) tubularised over stent → transverse incision at dimple → dissect urethrorectal space
   to peritoneal reflection → insert graft and mould → labia minora sutured over stent → suprapubic
   catheter → strict bed rest 1 week.
 • Stents: packed gauze, condom-covered wood, silicone foam, acrylic, metal, inflatable. High postoperative
   stenosis → dilation required; satisfaction excellent.
 • Full-thickness graft (buttock) or labial flaps → less contracture than split-thickness. Williams (1964): labia
   majora pouch ± Frank dilation.

 • Buccal mucosa: hairless, moist, non-keratinised stratified squamous; dense elastic fibres, robust
   neovascularisation; also for revision and partial replacement after tumour excision; favourable long-term
   results.
 • Others: muscle flaps (pudendal thigh, rectus abdominis, buttock), pelvic peritoneum, amnion.
 • Robotic-assisted tubularised peritoneal vaginoplasty (RATPV): single peritoneal flap tubularised and
   joined to inverted non-hair-bearing perineal skin; good length and calibre; no bowel harvest, no laser hair
   removal; used mainly in transgender surgery, promising for agenesis.
 • Autologous cultured vaginal tissue with McIndoe technique: normal length and depth, satisfactory sexual
   function.
Creation of an intestinal neovagina
 • Baldwin (1907): U-shaped sigmoid loop with septum division; abandoned for morbidity and mortality
   until improvements from the 1970s. Sigmoid, caecum, small bowel all usable.
 • Sigmoid sequence: bowel preparation → frog-leg (Allen stirrups if older) → select distal sigmoid on a
   pedicle reaching perineum → divide distal end, anastomose to perineum → excise ~3 cm intervening
   segment (no overlapping suture lines → fewer fistulae) → fix to posterior peritoneum (prevents
   prolapse).
 • Modified Monti detubularisation: open along one side near mesentery, retubularise Heineke-Mikulicz →
   mesentery cranial, caudal end reaches further.
 • Intact cervix → bowel can be anastomosed to the vagina, allowing menstrual egress.
 • Ileum: detubularised, reconfigured conically for diameter; laparoscopic-assisted harvest avoids
   laparotomy.
 • Perineal dimple present → anastomose bowel directly to it; otherwise create an adequate rectovesical
   space (progressive Hegar dilators) to protect blood supply; pass segment inside a lubricated Penrose
   drain.
 • Results excellent: mucosal inversion → simple trimming; mild stenosis → Z-plasty at mucocutaneous
   junction; most achieve adequate coitus.

Special Considerations: Cervical Atresia
 • Uncommon; mimics other obstruction (primary amenorrhoea, cyclic or chronic pain). Wrong diagnosis/
   operation → disaster, possible death.
 • MRI = most useful modality.
 • Hysterectomy + vaginal replacement often best.
 • Uterovaginal anastomosis can succeed with pregnancies (caesarean delivery), but loss of the
   endocervical barrier → life-threatening ascending infection.
 • Continuity impossible → in vitro fertilisation with transmyometrial embryo transfer (caesarean delivery);
   meanwhile hormonal endometrial blockade to limit pain and endometriosis.

ACQUIRED DISORDERS OF THE FEMALE EXTERNAL GENITALIA

Rhabdomyosarcoma
 • Vaginal rhabdomyosarcoma: grapelike cluster from the posterior vestibule; mean age <2 years.
 • Best prognosis of all female genital tract primaries — embryonal predominance + early detection from
   bleeding.
 • Biopsy → staging: computed tomography (CT) of abdomen and chest + bone marrow biopsy → protocol
   stratification.
 • Chemotherapy advances have steadily reduced surgery across the Intergroup Rhabdomyosarcoma Study
   (IRS) trials.
 • Local resection may follow chemotherapy; wide excision of the organ only for persistent or recurrent
   disease.

Labial Adhesions
 • Also labial agglutination / synechia vulvae; commonest interlabial abnormality (0.6–1.8%); mainly first 2
   years, peak 13–23 months.
 • Labia minora fuse from the posterior fourchette towards the clitoris; distinguish from labia majora
   fusion (DSD).
 • Not seen in newborns (maternal oestrogen) → hypo-oestrogenism hypothesised, but circulating
   oestradiol is not different from controls → questioned. Oestradiol may aid re-epithelialisation (heparin-
   binding epidermal growth factor) — i.e., helps healing after separation.
 • Local irritation and trauma are important triggers; rarely sexual abuse (haematoma, lacerations).
 • Usually asymptomatic; may cause post-void dribbling (vaginal pooling), perineal irritation, contaminated
   urinalysis, difficult imaging.
 • Spontaneous resolution up to 80% within 1 year → observe if asymptomatic.

Female Circumcision (Infibulation)
 • Ritual female genital mutilation (FGM): Africa (Horn of Africa through central Africa to Nigeria), Middle
   East, Muslim populations of Indonesia and Malaysia. Practised in 19th-century Britain for “epilepsy to
   lesbianism”; now illegal in Western countries.
 • ~200 million females affected; ~3 million African girls annually.
 • Ethiopian data: associated with ethnicity/religion and higher socioeconomic status; inversely related to
   maternal education.
 • Age: birth to pre-marriage; typically 4–10 years, commonest age 7; usually without anaesthesia as a rite
   of passage. Predates Islam — not religious. Deinfibulation often before consummation.
 • Range: sunna (clitoral prepuce excision) → complete vulval excision. Pharaonic circumcision = infibulation
   by sutures, thorns or twigs; legs bound up to 40 days.

Condyloma Acuminata
 • Rising incidence in children; mostly perianal, occasionally vestibular.
 • High suspicion for sexual abuse, but perinatal transmission is a real mechanism.
 • Treatment choice by age, site, severity; variable success. Spontaneous resolution in over half.
 • Imiquimod — immune response modifier via Toll-like receptor 7; effective in rapidly progressive
   unresectable disease. Carbon dioxide laser: straightforward for anogenital lesions, harder at the vestibule.
 • Cause = human papillomavirus (HPV) → vaccination for primary prevention.

Inguinal Hernias
 • Female : male ≈ 1:5 to 1:10. Usually isolated, but may be the presenting sign of CAIS (~1%) → every
   phenotypic female with a hernia is assessed for genetic male status.
 • Karyotype definitive but costly (not routine); pelvic ultrasonography for vaginal stripe — also costly.
 • Vaginoscopy at hernia repair shows the cervix; CAIS also has a shorter vagina.
 • Inguinal laparoscopy: contralateral gonad and uterus; round ligament uniformly absent in genetic males.

 KEY POINTS: ACQUIRED DISORDERS OF THE FEMALE GENITALIA
   • Labial adhesions: no treatment unless symptomatic.
   • FGM: ~200 million females worldwide.
   • Condylomata: may be perinatally acquired.
   • Inguinal hernia in a girl → consider CAIS.
