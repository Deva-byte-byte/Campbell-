RENAL FUNCTION, FLUID, AND ELECTROLYTE HOMEOSTASIS

Renal Development
 • Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT) — the spectrum produced by abnormal or
   dysregulated renal development — are the commonest cause of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) in
   children.
 • Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) = volume of filtrate crossing the glomerular filtration barrier per unit
   time; renal blood flow is one determinant, and both rise together through fetal and postnatal
   development.
 • Renal blood flow rises from 3–4% to 12–16% of cardiac output in the first year: ↑ cardiac output +
   marked ↓ renal vascular resistance (relative to the cardiac output rise).
 • Mechanisms of falling vascular resistance: intrarenal flow redistributes from deep juxtamedullary
   glomeruli → outer cortical glomeruli; rising angiotensin II ↑ mean arterial pressure and constricts the
   efferent arteriole → ↑ glomerular capillary pressure → ↑ GFR.
 • Renin, angiotensin II and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) rise in late gestation and are higher in the
   first 2 weeks of life than in adulthood.
 • Prenatal and neonatal GFR is much lower than adult GFR. Creatinine in the first 48 h reflects maternal
   function; by day 7 it reflects the neonate's own (normally <0.5 mg/dL at term).
 • GFR doubles over the first 2 weeks in term infants, then rises steadily to adult levels by 2 years; tubular
   maturation runs in parallel.
 • Total body water 70–90% of body weight in term and preterm neonates (higher than older children).
 • Dilute well, concentrate poorly: neonates dilute to 50 mOsm/L but concentrating ability is limited →
   negative free-water balance; normal free-water loss 1–2% body weight/day over the first 5 days (more in
   preterm).
 • Why concentration is limited: ↓ responsiveness to antidiuretic hormone (ADH); short tubules (esp. thick
   ascending limb, which lengthens with age); low Na⁺-K⁺-ATPase activity (rises after birth, peak at 2–3
   weeks); low medullary interstitial urea, needed for the countercurrent multiplier.

HAEMATURIA (MICROSCOPIC AND GROSS)
 • Gross haematuria = red or "tea-coloured" urine. Microscopic haematuria = clear yellow urine, dipstick
   positive, >5 red blood cells (RBCs) per high-power field on freshly spun urine.
 • Isolated microscopic haematuria ≈1% of school-age children; <25% remain positive on repeat testing and
   most of those resolve within 1 year. Usually benign, yet a definitive diagnosis is reached in <30%.
 • Gross haematuria ≈ slightly >0.1% of paediatric emergency visits; prompts urgent evaluation and a cause
   is found in most.
 • History: painful/painless, intermittent/persistent; trauma, pain, fever, dysuria, voiding dysfunction, rash,
   arthralgia, swelling/weight gain, exercise, headache, visual change. Family: haematuria, bleeding
   disorders, hearing loss, stones, sickle cell, renal disease. Examine: blood pressure (BP), rash, joints,
   oedema, flank pain, masses, perineum.
 • Critical step = fresh urine with microscopy. Red/bloody → non-glomerular; brown/"tea-coloured" →
   glomerular.
 • Gross haematuria, heme-positive dipstick, no RBCs → haemoglobinuria or myoglobinuria.
 • Sediment: crystals → urolithiasis; white cells/bacteria → infection; RBC casts → glomerulonephritis (then
   quantify proteinuria).
 • Suspected glomerular disease, proteinuria, hypertension, oedema or systemic disease → renal function,
   electrolytes, full blood count, albumin, complement C3/C4, antistreptolysin O (ASO) or anti-DNase B →
   refer to paediatric nephrology.
 • Urinary tract infection = commonest cause of gross haematuria in children → culture if fever, abdominal
   pain, dysuria or frequency.
 • Trauma, flank/abdominal pain or clots → urological evaluation, often computed tomography (CT). Family
   history of stones, pain, passage of gravel → stone imaging. Renal cysts (mostly polycystic kidney disease)
   can bleed. Rare: obstruction, tumours, coagulopathy, vascular malformation.
 • Microscopic haematuria: routine ultrasound is low-yield; if persistent for months, image only on clinical
   grounds. Voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) and cystoscopy are not indicated for isolated microscopic

PROTEINURIA
 • Urine normally contains protein; children may show low-level dipstick proteinuria physiologically (filtered
   plasma + secreted tubular proteins).
 • Urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) normal: <0.7 mg/mg newborn, <0.2 mg/mg older children.
   Timed collection: <4 mg/m²/h. Adolescents/adults: <150 mg/24 h.

Nephrotic Syndrome
 • Clinical: dependent oedema (eyelids, scrotum, labia); hypertension atypical; microscopic haematuria
   ≈20%, macroscopic unusual.
 • Definition: proteinuria >40 mg/m²/h or UPCR >2.0 mg/mg + hypoalbuminaemia + hyperlipidaemia. In
   children it usually occurs in isolation rather than as part of nephritis.
 • Three groups: congenital/infantile, primary (inherited or idiopathic), secondary (≈10%). Mutations in ~30
   genes identified in inherited or sporadic primary disease.
 • CNS: onset within 3 months of birth; genetic or perinatal infection; proteinuria often begins in utero.
   Immunosuppression not recommended — unresponsive.

Glomerulonephritis
 • Classic tetrad: haematuria + proteinuria + hypertension + acute kidney injury (AKI); "tea/cola" urine with
   dysmorphic RBCs and RBC casts.
 • Systemic diseases may present as GN → look for multisystem signs. Initial labs: AKI severity, metabolic
   derangement, and complement — hypocomplementaemia sharply narrows the differential.

Hypocomplementaemic Glomerulonephritis
 • Acute post-streptococcal GN = commonest PIGN: pharyngitis or pyoderma several weeks before; school-
   age, rare <3 years; sporadic or epidemic.
 • AKI usually mild; severe cases → rapidly progressive GN (RPGN) needing renal replacement therapy
   (RRT).
 • Diagnosis: C3 usually <50% of normal + raised ASO or anti-DNase B.
 • Recovery timeline (conservative care): improvement within 1 week → oedema resolves 5–10 days →
   hypertension 2–3 weeks → C3 normal by 8–12 weeks → proteinuria up to 6 months → microscopic
   haematuria >1 year.
 • Persistently low C3 or atypical course → biopsy.
 • MPGN and C3GN: rare, late childhood, rare <5 years; biopsy required.
 • SLE: suspect in any child with systemic disease + GN + low C3 and C4; ranges from asymptomatic
   haematuria/proteinuria to RPGN; almost all hypocomplementaemic, ANA-positive and anti-double-
   stranded DNA-positive.

Normo-complementaemic Glomerulonephritis
 • IgA nephropathy (IgAN) = commonest primary GN worldwide, children included; 2nd–3rd decades;
   biopsy is the only definitive diagnosis.
 • Recurrent painless macroscopic haematuria concurrent with or immediately after upper respiratory
   infection (contrast the several-week latency of PIGN); resolves in days, microscopic haematuria/
   proteinuria may persist; occasional loin pain.
 • IgAN progression risk: persistent proteinuria, hypertension, raised creatinine; 20–30% reach ESKD,
   another 20% progressive chronic kidney disease (CKD).
 • HSP: self-limited vasculitis; purpuric rash, arthralgia, abdominal pain, GN — may appear asynchronously
   over days–weeks. Nephritis mostly asymptomatic haematuria + mild proteinuria; gross haematuria up to
   25%; nephritic/nephrotic presentation in a minority, RPGN rare.
 • HSP is a clinical diagnosis (no specific marker); biopsy only guides treatment in nephrotic syndrome or
   RPGN. Resolves in 1–2 weeks; recurrences common for 4 months; urinary abnormalities up to 3 months,
   and up to 2 years in 10–20%; most achieve full remission.
 • Alport syndrome: defect of type IV collagen α3/α4/α5 chains of the glomerular basement membrane;
   presents with persistent microscopic haematuria ± intermittent gross.
 • Alport hallmark = bilateral sensorineural deafness, high-frequency first, late childhood; family history of
   renal failure or deafness in a haematuric child is highly suggestive. Later: macular disease, anterior
   lenticonus; rarely leiomyomata (oesophagus, trachea, female genitalia).
 • Alport inheritance: mostly X-linked COL4A5; autosomal COL4A3/COL4A4 — heterozygous = dominant,
   biallelic = recessive. X-linked males: function preserved in 1st decade → proteinuria late childhood/
   adolescence → hypertension → renal insufficiency; ~50% ESKD by 25 years.
 • ANCA-associated GN: rare in childhood; aggressive; untreated 90% mortality within 2 years; intensive
   immunosuppression has transformed survival, but ~one-quarter reach ESKD. Eosinophilic GPA (Churg-
   Strauss) extremely rare.

TUBULOPATHIES
 • Isolated (e.g. distal RTA) or generalised (Fanconi); primary or secondary.
 • History: family history of renal/metabolic disease; failure to thrive, poor feeding, recurrent vomiting,
   dehydration, irritability, tetany, seizures, jaundice.
 • Initial tests: full blood count; metabolic panel with Ca, Mg, PO₄; venous/arterial blood gas; quantitative
   urine K, Na, Ca, PO₄, Mg.
 • Renal tubular acidosis (RTA): inappropriately high urine pH in metabolic acidosis; hallmark =
   hyperchloraemic (normal anion gap) metabolic acidosis, urine pH >5.5, raised urine anion gap.

CYSTIC RENAL DISEASE
 • Common cause of CKD and ESKD in children; found prenatally or during work-up of hypertension,
   haematuria, abnormal function, polyuria, or incidentally (abdominal pain, constipation). Imaging or family
   history often diagnostic; genetic testing preferred when possible.
 • Multicystic dysplastic kidney (MCDK): ultrasound = large reniform mass of non-communicating cysts;
   non-functional; usually unilateral — bilateral → near-universal neonatal death.
 • MCDK associations in up to 25%: renal hypoplasia/dysplasia, vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), ureteropelvic
   junction obstruction. Contralateral compensatory hypertrophy; MCDK involutes over months–years.
 • VCUG recommended, especially with hydronephrosis and/or hydroureter.
 • MCDK surveillance (renal function, BP, ultrasound): 3-monthly in year 1 → 6-monthly until involution, or
   at least to 5 years; emerging risk of early hypertension and CKD → growing consensus for annual review
   into adulthood.

UROLITHIASIS: MEDICAL MANAGEMENT
 • Medical and surgical management of paediatric urolithiasis is covered in Chapter 54.

HYPERTENSION
 • Definition: repeated BP >95th percentile for age, sex and height. Prevalence 3–4% — far below adults —
   but rising with childhood obesity.
 • American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP): ages 1–13 vs adolescents ≥13; normal = <90th percentile
   (children) or <120/80 mm Hg (adolescents).
 • White-coat hypertension: up to one-quarter with raised office BP are normal outside the office.
 • Staging sets urgency of work-up and predicts end-organ damage.

   aetiology.
 • First-line: therapeutic lifestyle change for stage 1 or primary hypertension without end-organ damage.
 • Drug therapy if: lifestyle failure in primary hypertension, stage 2, end-organ damage, symptoms, or
   secondary hypertension. No evidence-based first-choice drug in children (unlike adults).
 • Targets: end-organ damage occurs even at 90th–95th percentile → aim <90th percentile; adolescents
   <130/80; CKD or diabetes <50th percentile.

 KEY POINTS
   • Rising prevalence tracks obesity; one-quarter white-coat; lifestyle first without end-organ damage.


Concept — age predicts aetiology: the younger the child, the more likely a secondary (vascular, renal,
tumour) cause; obesity has shifted older children toward adult-type essential hypertension, so work-up
intensity scales inversely with age.

ACUTE KIDNEY INJURY
 • Definition: abrupt fall in function — creatinine rise ≥0.3 mg/dL (within 48 h) or ≥50% from baseline, or
   oliguria <0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥6 h; a continuum linked to outcome.

 • Staging logic: creatinine by fold-rise (1.5 → 2 → 3×); urine output by duration (6 → 12 → 24 h). The
   paediatric-only stage III criterion is a clearance <35, because no absolute creatinine defines paediatric AKI.
 • Epidemiology: ~4/1000 paediatric admissions; up to one-third of intensive care admissions; dialysis-
   requiring AKI 1–2% of critically ill children; mortality 15% vs 0.6% without AKI.
 • Causes have shifted away from primary renal disease → multiorgan failure, ischaemia, nephrotoxins,
   congenital heart disease, bone marrow transplantation, sepsis.
 • Creatinine is insensitive and late: normal values vary with age, muscle mass and size; ~50% GFR loss
   before creatinine rises; lags GFR by 24–72 h.

 • Complications and causes: hyponatraemia (free-water retention); hypernatraemia (sodium given);

 • AKI → CKD: >one-third have reduced function or dialysis dependence at discharge; ~two-thirds with long

       • AKI → markedly increased mortality; prerenal → vigorous isotonic fluid; CRRT = standard of care.

CHRONIC KIDNEY DISEASE
 • Definition: structural or functional abnormality persisting >3 months; Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality
   Initiative (KDOQI) stages by GFR because complications appear at set GFR levels regardless of cause.
 • Paediatric caveats: staging excludes <2 years (term infant GFR ≈40 mL/min/1.73 m², adult by ~2 years);
   the 3-month rule excludes infants <3 months.

 • Stages I–II need evidence of damage; GFR alone defines III–V. Stage V threshold (<15) is also the dialysis-

 • Causes differ from adults and by age: young children → CAKUT (hypo/dysplasia; obstruction — posterior

 • Complications worsen with stage; KDOQI and KDIGO issue stage-based care guidelines.

END-STAGE KIDNEY DISEASE: DIALYSIS AND RENAL TRANSPLANTATION
 • ESKD = needing chronic dialysis or transplantation to live. No absolute creatinine or BUN mandates
   dialysis.
 • Start dialysis: CKD stage V (GFR <15) or CKD with uncontrolled hypertension, volume overload, metabolic
   derangement, poor growth, or uraemic complications.

 • Size threshold: many centres wait for ≥10 kg and 65 cm (technical issues, graft thrombosis).
 • Urological prerequisite: repair or reconstruct urinary tract anomalies (CAKUT) before transplant;
   complete immunisation.
 • Native nephrectomy (peritransplant) for chronic/recurrent pyelonephritis, refractory hypertension,
   uncontrolled nephrotic syndrome.
 • Primary graft dysfunction <3%; delayed graft function and rejection by donor type — see table.
 • Immunosuppression: antibody induction (thymoglobulin or basiliximab) → maintenance CNI (tacrolimus
   or ciclosporin) + antiproliferative (MMF); trend to steroid-free maintenance in lower-risk children.
 • Infection: young children may lack immunity to cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus and BK virus.
   Malignancy (renal cell, thyroid, skin) risk ≈7-fold vs general paediatric population.
 • Antibody-mediated rejection increasingly recognised.
 • Chronic allograft nephropathy has no therapy → nearly all eventually need retransplantation or dialysis.

   • Peritoneal dialysis first <9 years/<20 kg; transplant improves growth, neurocognition, QoL; year-1

Concept — size and the bladder decide the pathway: small children get peritoneal dialysis because venous
access fails, transplant waits for 10 kg/65 cm, and the urologist's job is a safe, reconstructed lower tract
before the graft goes in; living donation wins on every graft metric.

SUMMARY
 • Effective partnership between paediatric urology and nephrology — each with full-spectrum expert
   teams — has improved outcomes and reduced disease burden for children with kidney and urinary tract
   disorders.
