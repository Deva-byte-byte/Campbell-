Chapter 163 — Focal Therapy of Prostate Cancer
Campbell-Walsh-Wein Urology · Chapter Notes

 • Global incidence about 30.6 per 100,000 per year; prostate cancer is the second most incident cancer in
   men worldwide.
 • In the United States it is the commonest solid-organ malignancy in men and the second leading cause of
   cancer death in men.
 • Incidence has risen over a 10-year period, particularly in countries with a high Human Development
   Index and per-capita gross domestic product.
 • Overdiagnosis and overtreatment controversy: radical treatment is oncologically effective, but more men
   than necessary bear its collateral damage to the cavernosal nerves, urinary sphincter and adjacent
   organs.
 • Three developments drive interest in gland-sparing targeted therapy: multiparametric magnetic
   resonance imaging (mpMRI), MRI-fusion targeted biopsy, and recognition that Gleason grade 4 and 5
   cancers are the biologically lethal subgroup.
 • Definition — focal therapy (FT): guided ablation of a cancerous lesion that is image defined and biopsy
   confirmed (or biopsy confirmed only), with a safety margin around the target.

Concepts and Historical Perspective
 • Model: breast conservation. The Halsted mastectomy gave way to local excision with reconstruction;
   prostate FT followed that paradigm shift.
 • Evolution: subtotal ablation sparing one or both neurovascular bundles → "male
   lumpectomy" (hemiablation), the first form of FT → true image-guided focal destruction with a margin
   of normal tissue.
 • Early ablations lacked advanced imaging; localisation ranged from standard transrectal biopsy to 5-mm
   transperineal template grids.
 • Treatment always balances cancer aggressiveness against life expectancy. FT aims to treat only the foci
   that threaten survival or quality of life, preserving surrounding tissue and so sexual and urinary function.
Concept — FT is the prostate's version of breast conservation: its legitimacy depends entirely on being able to
see, sample and ablate the lesion that matters.

Biologic Basis for Prostate Focal Therapy
Using Cancer Grade as an Indication of Aggressiveness
 • Best predictors of prostate cancer mortality: tumour grade, serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and
   clinical stage. In low-volume localised disease, grade is the earliest indicator of lethal potential.
 • Gleason grading (Donald Gleason, 1973), in modified form, remains the most effective practical
   prognosticator of aggressiveness.
 • Hanahan–Weinberg hallmarks of cancer (2000; expanded 2011): acquired self-sufficiency, immortality
   and invasive capability (Fig. 163.1). Gleason grade 4 and 5 carry many more hallmarks than grade 3.

Multifocality of Prostate Cancer Versus the Index Lesion Hypothesis
 • Most prostate cancer is multifocal (50–91%) in radical prostatectomy specimens. One Korean series is the
   exception, reporting a unifocal majority (Table 163.1).
 • Index lesion (IL) = the largest or highest-grade tumour focus. The index lesion hypothesis holds that the
   IL determines the course of the cancer.

CLINICAL APPLICATIONS OF FOCAL THERAPY

Treating the Index Lesion
 • It is increasingly accepted that the natural history is driven by a primary lesion rather than by secondary
   lesions.
 • Eradicating the dangerous foci gives oncological control while better preserving quality of life. The
   secondary lesions are placed on AS.

The Complementary Roles of Focal Therapy and Active Surveillance
 • The two largest AS cohorts now have 10–15 years of follow-up and show an extremely low metastasis
   rate in D'Amico low-risk disease.
 • In the more inclusive cohort (up to low-volume 3+4), most men who progressed had Gleason 3+4.
 • Prostate Intervention Versus Observation Trial (PIVOT): the survival benefit of prostatectomy over
   observation was seen in the intermediate-risk subgroup.
 • Hence grade 4 is the therapeutic target. The strategy is FT to intermediate- or high-grade foci plus AS for
   the low-grade remainder, with focal retreatment of any new higher-grade focus. The aim is to turn
   prostate cancer into a chronic disease, in keeping with its long natural history.
 • Multidisciplinary team sequence (uro-oncologists, radiologists, pathologists): map disease extent by
   advanced imaging and biopsy → confirm benefit and compliance with follow-up → completely ablate
   the IL(s) → monitor with imaging and biopsy → retreat persistent or de novo disease focally, or convert
   to whole-gland treatment.
Concept — FT and AS are not rivals but two halves of one strategy: ablate the grade 4 disease that kills, and
watch the grade 3 disease that does not.

PATIENT SELECTION
 • Patient selection is the fundamental tenet of FT. The aggressive lesion must be identified and its extent
   delineated for complete ablation.
 • Recurrence and persistence are more likely than after radical therapy, so the patient must be compliant,
   willing and psychologically suited to close surveillance.
 • The lesion is identified histologically and/or radiologically. mpMRI shows the whole gland and may
   predict aggressive histology, but consensus requires biopsy confirmation.
 • Imaging is imperfect at excluding aggressive cancer, so many experts add a systematic biopsy.

Biopsy-Based Lesion Identification
 • Early work aimed at lateralisation for hemiablation, largely in low-grade disease that would now go to AS.
   It still shows how well biopsy maps cancer.
 • Transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS)-guided biopsy unilaterality is the strongest predictor of pathological
   unilaterality. Sensitivity and negative predictive value (NPV) are high, but specificity and positive
   predictive value (PPV) are poor.
 • Extended cores beat sextant cores on specificity and NPV, but even 24-core TRUS leaves specificity low.
   Adding transrectal cores has a ceiling because sampling is random and the anterior zone is poorly
   accessible transrectally.
 • Transperineal mapping biopsy (TMB), 1990s: equal access to anterior and posterior gland, and a 5-mm
   grid or template gives consistent core density and distribution.

 • Against TMB, staging TRUS biopsy misses almost half of the disease relevant to hemiablation. More than
   half of men with unilateral cancer on TRUS are disqualified after TMB.
 • Predictive models are weak. Only PSA ≤4 ng/mL and unilateral cancer on biopsy predicted unilateral
   disease; another model reached only chance-level discrimination (area under the curve, AUC, 0.52). More
   than 2 positive TRUS cores predicted persistence or biochemical recurrence after focal treatment.
 • These data aimed to locate all cancer. Current interest is in preferential detection of Gleason 4/5,
   because detecting more pattern 3 disease drives overtreatment.

Advanced Imaging Techniques in Prostate Cancer
Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI)
 • High-field magnets, contrast agents and better protocols added functional to anatomical imaging, giving
   high diagnostic accuracy. mpMRI is the key enabler of FT. It can do the following:
    1. Preferentially detect high-grade lesions. Dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) gadolinium imaging picks
       up vascularised, larger, higher-grade foci. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) maps water mobility and
       shows the high cellularity of lost glandular architecture.
    2. Detect anterior-zone cancer, which is 15–33% of cancers. These tumours are diagnosed late because
       TRUS biopsy misses them, but are readily seen on mpMRI. Caught early, they are ideal for FT:
       complete ablation sparing both neurovascular bundles.
    3. Detect EPE: accuracy 62–74%, sensitivity 35–63%, specificity 73–91%, PPV 36–85%, NPV 57–92%.
       mpMRI outperforms the Partin tables and adds limited pelvic staging.
    4. Identify the IL. Sensitivity is modest overall but highest for tumours >1 cm, Gleason ≥7 and index
       tumours. IL location agrees closely with the prostatectomy specimen. Significant cancers that mpMRI
       misses are mostly nonindex foci with <20% pattern 4.
 • PROMIS (mpMRI against 5-mm grid TMB, 11 centres with varied equipment and expertise) for clinically
   significant prostate cancer (csPCa): sensitivity 93%, specificity 41%, PPV 51%, NPV 89%.
 • PROMIS implications: mpMRI lesions need histological confirmation; systematic biopsy of MRI-negative
   areas cannot yet be omitted; and a strategy relying heavily on mpMRI risks a small but real amount of
   outfield disease.
 • Technical dependence: 3 tesla is more accurate than 1.5 tesla, and an endorectal coil is recommended at
   1.5 tesla. Protocol matters too. Reader experience determines IL identification and EPE interpretation.
 • mpMRI underestimates histological tumour volume by 5–30% (after allowing for shrinkage) and
   underestimates the axial diameter.
 • Treatment margin (counting only pattern 4/5): 5 mm ablates >95%, 6 mm ablates 98.6% of csPCa. Lesion-
   only ablation is therefore hard, and complete ablation may require hemi- or zonal ablation.

MRI-Targeted/MRI-Fusion Biopsy
 • mpMRI has high sensitivity but low specificity, so histological confirmation of suspicious lesions is
   critical.
 • Prostate Imaging–Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS): from the American College of Radiology and the
   European Society of Urogenital Radiology; now version 2 (2015); a 5-point Likert scale for csPCa. Higher
   PI-RADS scores go with higher Gleason scores.
 • Histological Gleason score is still the decision marker. 2016 consensus: Gleason 3+4 is the "sweet spot"
   for FT. Higher grades raise concern about EPE outside the image-guided target zone, which may need
   radical treatment.

 • MRI-targeted transperineal vs transrectal biopsy in the same patients: no difference in csPCa detection.
 • Tumours are identified accurately for FT only by an experienced multidisciplinary team of radiologists

Role of the Systematic Biopsy
 • Experts agree that a concurrent systematic biopsy is necessary to find MRI-invisible csPCa. The required
   intensity is disputed.
 • Reducing saturation biopsy (about 22 cores) to fewer systematic cores progressively lowers csPCa
   detection and changes the FT planning template.
 • About 90% of outfield csPCa lies within 1 cm of the target (the lesion's "penumbra"), so biopsies should
   be taken within and around the MRI lesion.
 • With limited systematic biopsy there is a small but significant risk of untreated csPCa. This affects biopsy-
   based FT outcomes, but the long-term impact is unclear.
Concept — Imaging finds the lesion, but only biopsy can grade it, and only a systematic biopsy can vouch
for the rest of the gland. That is why every consensus has kept both.

Promising Imaging Modalities
Positron Emission Tomography
 • Positron emission tomography (PET) with choline tracers or fluorodeoxyglucose has been investigated for
   staging.
 • Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA): a type 2 transmembrane protein expressed 100- to 1000-
   fold more in prostate cancer than in normal cells.
 • The early antibody agent ¹¹¹In-capromab pendetide failed to localise cancer reliably for quadrant ablation.

 • Small-molecule ligands to the external epitope, labelled with ¹⁸F or ⁶⁸Ga, give high tumour uptake and
   rapid clearance, so contrast is high.
 • PSMA PET is not degraded by biopsy haemorrhage or inflammation, whereas mpMRI is transiently
   impaired.
 • mpMRI has greater sensitivity; PSMA PET (¹⁸F-DCFBC) has greater specificity, including for Gleason 8–9
   disease.
 • Choline-PET and mpMRI targets can both be fused to three-dimensional (3D) TRUS, combining
   anatomical and functional information for biopsy.

Multiparametric Ultrasound
 • Rationale: mpMRI is resource intensive and fusion is imperfect. Ultrasound is real-time and point-of-care,
   and a multiparametric ultrasound approach avoids MRI–ultrasound registration error.

Contemporary Patient Selection Criteria
 • Criteria have evolved with biological understanding. Early FT cohorts included low-risk men now
   managed by AS.
 • Treatment choice is patient-centric, so there are few comparative trials between modalities. Practice
   rests on consensus (Table 163.2).
 • Evolution of the stated aim: 2009 — eradicate all known cancer and preserve genitourinary function →
   2010 — treat cancer, leaving the benign prostate and surrounding structures intact → 2013 — treat the
   dominant lesion or IL; acceptable residual disease first quantified as untreated 3+3 up to 5 mm core
   length (biopsy type and intensity not addressed) → 2016 — completely ablatable 3+4 is the "sweet
   spot"; 4+3 the maximum grade; most panellists reluctant to treat high grade → 2019 — treatment and
   retreatment to delay or avoid radical therapy.
 • Assessment evolution: mpMRI moved from "state-of-the-art centres only" to "whenever
   possible" (2016). TMB was state of the art in 2010 and 2012; MRI–TRUS fusion biopsy was
   recommended by 2016. The latest consensus still requires systematic biopsy of the MRI-"normal" gland
   because mpMRI has a small but significant false-negative rate.
 • Sexual function (2013 trial-design consensus and 2016 consensus): erectile dysfunction is not a
   contraindication to FT. Potential preservation of erections is an important reason to choose FT, but most
   experts would not deny FT to an impotent man, given the urinary, rectal and other toxicities of
   indiscriminate whole-gland therapy.

ABLATION PATTERNS AND CURRENT TECHNOLOGIES

Types of Ablation
 • Many ablation patterns have been called FT, which makes the literature heterogeneous. "Hockey-stick"
   ablations belong under partial prostate ablation, not FT (Fig. 163.3).
 • International consensus on definitions: FT = lesion ablation, quadrant ablation and hemiablation.
 • Lesion treatment is further split into image-targeted lesion ablation (image-detected, biopsy-confirmed)
   and non-image-targeted ablation (biopsy information only).

Ablation Approach
 • Routes: transrectal, transperineal, or (less commonly) transurethral. Choice depends on tumour
   location, available technology, physician experience and patient anatomy or preference.
 • Transrectal gives easy access to the posterior zone; transperineal gives better access to the anterior

Focal Cryotherapy
 • Thermal ablation by extraction of heat to lethal cold. Cell death results from intra- and extracellular ice
   (membrane disruption), recrystallisation, vascular stasis and ischaemia, apoptosis and immune-
   mediated effects.

 • Four developments make cryotherapy suited to FT:
    1. Third-generation cryoprobes using the Joule–Thomson effect, as fine as 17 gauge.
    2. Variable ice-length adjustment, so the ice ball is contoured to the lesion.
    3. Ice edge visible on TRUS, computed tomography (CT) or MRI, giving real-time monitoring of the
       ablation zone and margin.
    4. Urethral warmer, which has greatly reduced urethral cold injury.
 • Thermocouples are placed in the ablation zone or its margin and along vital structures: external
   sphincter, neurovascular bundles, Denonvilliers fascia.
 • Anterior focal cryoablation sequence (Fig. 163.4): biplanar ultrasound probe in the rectum → variable-
   length cryoprobes set to prostate measurements → four probes placed in the anterior prostate → ice
   formed, with a hyperechoic leading edge monitored → console confirms lethal temperature at the
   anterior apex and safe temperatures at the external sphincter and right neurovascular bundle.
 • Series from 2008 to 2023 (Table 163.3) mostly used a hemiablation template, the original model of FT.
 • Over a median follow-up of 6–85 months: biochemical progression-free survival (BPFS) 71–98%; 3-year
   BPFS 56–62.5%; 5-year BPFS 62–70%.
 • In cohorts mandating rebiopsy at 6–12 months, the positive biopsy rate was 7.1–38.9%, about a quarter
   of it clinically significant.
 • Selection and biopsy criteria varied. Most patients were D'Amico low or intermediate risk.
 • Pad-free continence 95.1–100%; potency 46.8–100%. About half of men potent after hemiablation use
   phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (PDE5I).
 • Clavien–Dindo grade ≥3 complications ≤4% in every series. Urinary retention is the commonest adverse
   event (reactive oedema), avoided by keeping a urethral catheter for a few weeks. Retention 0–9%; recto-
   urethral fistula (RUF) up to 0.8%. Others: haematuria, haematospermia, urethral sloughing, stricture.
 • No comparison with radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy exists. The comparisons are partial vs whole-
   gland cryotherapy in the Cryo On-Line Data (COLD) registry:
     ◦ Low risk (matched pairs): no difference in 5-year BPFS; erectile recovery significantly better with
       partial ablation.
     ◦ Intermediate risk (propensity matched): no difference in early BPFS; partial ablation roughly twice as
       likely to restore erections at 12 months. This reflects contemporary selection.

Focal Salvage Cryotherapy
 • Local recurrence after definitive radiotherapy is hard to manage: salvage must achieve cancer control
   and limit salvage morbidity.
 • Focal salvage cryotherapy: about half are biochemically free at 5 years, with slow further decline to 10
   years, and a favourable side-effect profile.
 • Focal and whole-gland salvage ablation give similar oncological control; focal is better functionally. In
   the largest cohort, about three-quarters were free of biochemical progression at 2 years, and about half
   of initially potent men kept erections.
 • The most feared complication of salvage therapy is RUF: about 1.4% in the largest cohort (Table 163.4).

Focal High-Intensity Focused Ultrasound
 • A transducer focuses multiple ultrasound beams on a preset point, reaching ≥55 °C → coagulative
   necrosis. The transducer sits in the transrectal probe, which also plans and monitors treatment.
 • Posterior-zone lesions are well suited. Anterior lesions are harder: energy dissipates across intervening
   tissue, and anterior targets are displaced as progressive oedema develops.

   steeply by 10 years, with a high positive biopsy rate.

MRI-Guided Transurethral Ultrasound Ablation
 • In-bore MRI-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS): MR thermometry confirms lethal temperature in the
   target and safe temperatures at the urethra and sphincter (Fig. 163.5). Phase II: a slight, non-significant
   EPIC urinary dip at 1 month that recovered by 3 months, with no change in other EPIC domains over 2
   years.
 • MRI-guided transurethral ultrasound ablation (TULSA): made possible by miniaturised HIFU transducers
   (Fig. 163.6). It combines in-bore thermometry and anatomical resolution, and the transurethral route
   reaches the anterior prostate efficiently.
 • Whole-gland TULSA phase I (multicentre): safe; spatial control of ablation within 1.3 mm; no change in
   functional scores; grade 3 complications about 3%; no grade 4–5.
 • TULSA in low- and intermediate-risk disease: about three-quarters free of biochemical progression and of
   salvage at 3 years, but csPCa on 12-month biopsy in about a third.

Focal Laser Ablation
 • Focal laser ablation (FLA): coagulative necrosis from an interstitially placed laser fibre. Destruction is
   limited to a fixed distance from the active fibre tip, so the ablation zone is predictable and can be used
   safely close to vital structures.
 • Disadvantages: longer procedures or extra fibres for larger lesions, and particular care is needed to
   obtain an adequate margin.
 • Histology: neovascularisation and increased tumour mitotic activity at the edge of the ablation zone
   reinforce the need for a safety margin (Fig. 163.7).
 • Fibre placed transperineally or transrectally via a delivery trocar.
 • In-bore MR-guided FLA technique (phase II, Fig. 163.8): 14-gauge titanium introducer with
   polytetrafluoroethylene catheter into the lesion → 17-gauge laser applicator through each catheter →
   60–120 s at 6–15 W, repeated as needed → target ≥60 °C, with ablation extending >5 mm beyond the
   lesion. The cohort was mostly low risk; there was some infield and more outfield cancer at 12 months,
   with IIEF unchanged.
 • Office-based FLA under local anaesthesia with a modified MRI–ultrasound fusion biopsy device (mainly
   Gleason 7) was safe but had higher recurrence, so attention to the margin is needed.
 • The longest follow-up (about 6 years) showed a high failure-free rate but infield recurrence in about
   40%. The authors called the infield recurrence and need for salvage "disconcerting" and urged close
   monitoring and better energy delivery.
 • Across series (Table 163.7), over a median follow-up of 6–72.5 months: continence 95–100%; minimal
   change in IIEF or Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM) scores; infield recurrence 11–50%, outfield
   16.7–60%.
 • Severe adverse events are rare. There was one grade III event by the Common Terminology Criteria for
   Adverse Events (CTCAE), a urinary tract infection. Grade II events included two RUFs, both healing with
   urinary catheterisation within 4–6 weeks.

Focal Irreversible Electroporation
 • Irreversible electroporation (IRE) is non-thermal: short pulses of direct current create irreversible
   membrane pores → cell death.
 • It claims to preserve connective tissue architecture, a possible scaffold for regrowth.
 • Ablation occurs between two or more probes placed 10–20 mm apart (Fig. 163.9).
 • Delivery sequence: 20 test pulses characterise the current between each probe pair → settings adjusted
   → deep anaesthesia to prevent muscle depolarisation → 70 pulses per probe pair for the final ablation.
   Field strength must exceed 1600 V/cm between every probe pair.
 • Three early-phase studies (about 100 men) used hemi- or anterior-zone ablation or template-grid focal
   ablation. They showed no grade 4–5 complications and continence almost universally maintained, with
   some fall in erectile scores.
 • Ablate-and-resect study (resection at 1 month) found fibrinoid necrosis of the unilateral neurovascular
   bundle and denudation of the prostatic urethra in most patients. Whether the surviving connective
   tissue permits later regrowth and functional recovery is unknown (Fig. 163.10).
 • Larger cohorts (three series, 451 men, mainly low and intermediate risk): csPCa infield 1–9.8% and
   outfield 5–16% at 6–12 months.
 • Matched-pair IRE vs robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) (no head-to-head trial): IRE
   significantly better for pad-free continence and erections sufficient for intercourse, but some IRE
   patients had residual csPCa requiring salvage.

Focal Vascular-Targeted Photodynamic (VTP) Therapy
 • Low-power near-infrared laser light of a specific wavelength activates an intravascular photosensitiser
   preferentially taken up by tumour. Reactive oxygen species then damage cell membranes and small
   arterioles irreversibly, causing tumour necrosis. VTP is regarded as both a drug and a device.
 • TOOKAD Soluble (WST11, padeliporfin), a bacteriochlorophyll derivative: highly soluble, stays within the
   vasculature even at high dose, and is rapidly cleared by the kidney and liver.
 • Treatment sequence: single intravenous dose of 4 mg/kg → transperineal intraprostatic fibre → 753-nm
   light at 150 mW/cm and 200 J/cm → about 2 hours of anaesthesia → protective eyewear and low-level
   light for the following hour.
 • Three phase II trials (Gleason 3+3): safe. Infield positive biopsy was lower with hemiablation than overall.

Focal Radiofrequency Ablation
 • Radiofrequency ablation (RFA): heat of about 100 °C from molecular friction and agitation denatures
   proteins and membranes, causing coagulative necrosis.
 • Generator up to 100 W, 450–1200 kHz; probes are monopolar or bipolar; thermocouples give real-time
   temperature monitoring; control is impedance- or temperature-based.
 • Key advantage: limited conduction through fat, which shields adjacent structures.
 • Bipolar focal RFA (low and intermediate risk): csPCa on 6-month targeted biopsy in a fifth. At 12 months,
   most men were continent and able to penetrate. There were many adverse events, but none CTCAE grade
   ≥3; urinary obstruction resolved without sequelae.
 • A second series found infield csPCa in almost a third at 6 months, with one grade 3 event (gross
   haematuria needing only diagnostic cystoscopy). Continence was preserved, but IIEF fell significantly.
   Technique and margin planning need to improve (Table 163.11).

Focal Brachytherapy
 • Treatment-simulation studies show that brachytherapy can deliver an adequate focal dose.
 • Its advantage is a sharp fall-off in dose within a few millimetres of the source. High-dose-rate (HDR)
   dose can be further sculpted by adjusting source dwell through the interstitial catheters.
 • Low-dose-rate (LDR, seed) brachytherapy vs HDR brachytherapy: LDR urinary toxicity is low, depending
   on baseline symptoms and urethral dose. HDR carries a urethral stricture rate up to 8.2% within 2 years,
   at the prostatic apex or bulbar urethra.
 • Mid-term (4-year) focal HDR in low- and intermediate-risk disease gave poor results: biochemical
   disease-free survival 70%, outfield csPCa in a third, and deterioration to moderate or severe erectile
   dysfunction in half. There was no grade 2 genitourinary or gastrointestinal toxicity. The authors blamed
   poor patient selection.
 • Unique follow-up: mandatory biopsy at 24 months in published series. This delays confirmation of
   success and makes early comparison with non-radiation FT difficult.

Other Modalities
Focal Intensity-Modulated Radiation Therapy
 • External-beam advances allow a highly focused intraprostatic dose that spares vital structures.
 • Randomised phase II trial, urethral-sparing intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) vs standard
   IMRT (low risk): no genitourinary toxicity benefit, and worse oncological outcome at median 4.7 years.
 • In contrast, another series reported good 3-year BPFS across low, intermediate and high risk, with mostly
   grade 1 genitourinary toxicity.
 • Focal stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT): dosimetric feasibility has been shown but there is no clinical
   trial. A five-patient pilot showed better dosimetry, minimal toxicity and an early favourable PSA
   response. The results are preliminary, and radiation toxicity appears late, so long-term multicentre data
   are needed.

Focal Nanoparticles
 • Photothermal excitation of nanoshells that accumulate preferentially in the fenestrated perivascular
   spaces of tumours → preferential cancer-cell death. A drug and device therapy.
 • An open-label multicentre trial of a single dose of AuroLase nanoparticle-directed focal excitation (low to
   intermediate risk) reported about three-quarters success on 12-month biopsy.
 • PRX302: a genetically modified pore-forming protein (aerolysin) activated by enzymatically active PSA
   within the prostate. In a pilot study a single MR-fusion transperineal dose was well tolerated, with some
   complete and some partial histological responses.
 • NanoPac: sterile paclitaxel nanoparticles given by intratumoral injection (phase IIa). In men with Gleason
   ≥7, it was injected under TRUS guidance with mpMRI into the IL and its lobe, followed by radical
   prostatectomy at 4 weeks.
     ◦ The proportion of adenocarcinoma fell in some specimens. At 15 mg/mL, lesion volume on mpMRI and
       PSA density both fell.
     ◦ No drug-related serious adverse events, prostatitis or dose-limiting toxicity occurred, so the dose was
       escalated to 15 mg/mL.
     ◦ Paclitaxel persisted in the prostate and lymph nodes more than 30 days after injection.

Adjuvants to Focal Therapy
 • Many proponents think successful FT will rest on a combination of device and adjunct, not on a device
   alone.
 • SBRT with a boost to the MR-visible tumour (5–10 fractions): no biochemical recurrence at 2 years in a
   pilot study; acute genitourinary/gastrointestinal toxicity 8%/2%; late grade 3 toxicity 6%/0%. The added
   value of non-radiation focal ablation plus low-dose external beam is undetermined.
 • DOTA-AR, a bombesin-antagonist peptide targeting the gastrin-releasing peptide receptor (GRPr),
   conjugated to a radioisotope and combined with WST11, delayed tumour growth in GRPr-positive
   xenografts.

POST–FOCAL THERAPY FOLLOW-UP
 • PSA is less informative after FT. It should be undetectable after radical prostatectomy, and after
   radiotherapy a defined rise is needed for biochemical recurrence. After FT the fall is variable, 50–80%,
   because residual epithelium keeps producing PSA.
 • Proposed thresholds: nadir + 1.0 ng/mL at 12 months, or nadir + 1.5 ng/mL at 24–36 months, gave high
   sensitivity and specificity for csPCa on for-cause biopsy.
 • Digital examination is unreliable because of scarring and contraction of the tumour site. On mpMRI,
   TRUS and contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), the ablation zone first enlarges and then the gland
   shrinks through fibrosis and deformation.
 • 2015 International Consultation on Urological Diseases (ICUD)–Société Internationale d'Urologie (SIU)
   definition of success:

CONCLUSIONS AND AREAS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH
 • Willet Whitmore: "Is cure possible when necessary and necessary when possible?"
 • Longer life expectancy, awareness and PSA screening mean more cancers are diagnosed, and better
   imaging and biopsy find them earlier. In a subset, FT may cure or control the cancer for a long time. Given
   its slow biology, prostate cancer can be converted from an acute to a chronic disease.
 • FT + AS of the untreated gland avoids overtreatment and preserves genitourinary function. Many men
   value quality of life as highly as cancer control, and image-guided ablation can deliver both. It remains a
   work in progress.
 • Three open problems:
     1. Can untreated satellite "insignificant" cancers safely be left? The answer is unknown, a question AS
        shares. Small cancers may progress, and the randomised VTP trial's progression-free benefit in low-
        risk disease suggests value in treating lower-grade lesions. Clinical, imaging and genetic biomarkers
        are needed, possibly with functional and metabolic imaging. Meanwhile, close surveillance continues.
     2. Long-term cost-effectiveness is unknown. Up to half of men on AS eventually have radical treatment,
        and many FT patients will need further focal or radical treatment. Patients must accept possible repeat
        treatment. The cost and morbidity of repeated FT, and of radical treatment after FT, are undefined.
     3. Adjuvants may be needed. Wide excision for breast cancer is routinely paired with adjuvant
        radiotherapy, but no prostate trial has tested focal ablation with whole-gland irradiation. Several
        adjuvants are in early development.
Concept — The breast analogy that launched FT also shows its gap. Lumpectomy succeeds because the rest
of the organ receives adjuvant treatment; prostate FT instead relies on surveillance and retreatment.
