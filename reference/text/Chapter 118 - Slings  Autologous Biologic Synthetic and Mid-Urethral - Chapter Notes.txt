PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF STRESS URINARY INCONTINENCE AND SLING MECHANISM
OF ACTION
Two theories dominate the explanation of how a sling works.

DeLancey (1994) — the Hammock Hypothesis
 • The healthy female urethra rests on a "suburethral hammock" attached laterally to the arcus tendineus
   fasciae pelvis (ATFP).
 • In stress urinary incontinence (SUI) this supporting layer is deficient, manifesting clinically as
   hypermobility of the bladder neck and urethra.
 • Inferior descent of these structures produces unequal transmission of intra-abdominal pressure, and
   hence leakage.
 • Therapeutic corollary: a pubovaginal sling (PVS) sited at the bladder neck abolishes SUI by supplying a
   strong tissue layer that gives dynamic compression of the urethra during rises in intra-abdominal
   pressure.

The Mid-Urethral Theory
 • Ingelman-Sundberg (1953): the pubococcygeus muscles insert at the level of the mid-urethra, just
   outside the vaginal epithelial wall, and are integral to the mid-urethral continence mechanism.
 • Asmussen and Ulmsten (1983), Westby (1982): maximum urethral closure pressure (MUCP) occurs at the
   mid-urethra in continent females — attributed to the combined contribution of the anatomical structures
   sited there.

Petros and Ulmsten (1990) — the Integral Theory
 • A unifying concept: continence depends on adequate function of three components — the pubourethral
   ligaments, the suburethral vaginal hammock, and the pubococcygeus muscles.
 • Injury to any one of the three — by surgery, parturition, ageing, or hormonal deprivation — impairs mid-
   urethral function and produces urinary incontinence (UI).
 • Supporting evidence (Ulmsten et al., 1987): skin and round ligament biopsies showed incontinent
   females had substantially less collagen — classically quoted as 40% less — than continent controls, the
   inference being that collagen loss weakens the connective tissue supporting the urethra.

THE ADVENT OF THE MID-URETHRAL SLING
 • Ulmsten and Petros (1995) applied the integral theory to build the first retropubic synthetic mid-urethral
   sling (MUS), named intravaginal slingplasty; the initial report gave good cure rates with no reported
   complications.
 • Ulmsten et al. (1998) renamed the procedure the tension-free vaginal tape (TVT) and reported high cure
   rates in a multicentre study.
 • Complications in that TVT series established the characteristic complication profile: transient urinary
   retention, one case requiring a small adjustment of the sling through a vaginal incision, haematomas, and
   mesh perforation of the bladder recognised intraoperatively; vaginal mesh exposure could not be

 • This initial trial was the first to report the two complications unique to and defining of MUS surgery:

 • Delorme (2001) described the transobturator approach specifically to minimise perforation of the

 • 2006: the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the first single-incision MUS
   (SIMS) — performed entirely vaginally, with no skin entry.
 • Sling procedures of all types have been consistently shown to be efficacious and safe for female SUI; over
   the same period regulatory and professional bodies have raised concerns about mesh exposure and
   perforation specific to synthetic mesh, biologic grafts, and the specialised placement tools. Detailed
   discussion is in Chapter 119.

Guideline Positions
 • American Urological Association / Society for Urodynamics and Female Urology (AUA/SUFU) Guideline
   on the Surgical Treatment of Female SUI (Kobashi et al., 2017, 2023):
 • Strong Recommendation — physicians may offer MUS to index patients considering SUI surgery.
 • Conditional Recommendation — either retropubic or transobturator MUS may be offered to index
   patients electing surgery.
 • Both rest on grade A evidence.
 • SIMS outcome data accumulating over recent years produced a Conditional Recommendation in index
   patients on grade B evidence in the 2023 AUA/SUFU Update.

 KEY POINTS: INTRODUCTION TO SLINGS
   • Early theories of SUI centred on unequal pressure transmission to bladder and urethra, urethral
     hypermobility, and an excessively caudal urethral position.
   • Later theories emphasised the pubourethral ligaments, suburethral vaginal hammock and
     pubococcygeus muscle as the vital supports of the proximal and mid-urethra.
   • Consequent surgical principle: PVS are placed under minimal tension at the bladder neck to re-
     establish the suburethral hammock; MUS are placed loosely under the mid-urethra to prevent
     movement of the posterior urethral wall and assist urethral coaptation.


PREOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT
The purpose of evaluating the incontinent female is to characterise the type of incontinence, assess the
contribution of comorbid medical conditions, elucidate the differential diagnoses, and obtain prognostic
information guiding treatment selection.

The Initial Evaluation
AUA/SUFU Clinical Principle — five components in a female with SUI desiring surgery
 • Focused history, including assessment of bother (e.g. validated questionnaire)
 • Focused physical examination, including pelvic examination

 • Objective demonstration of SUI with a comfortably full bladder — Valsalva or cough stress test (CST)
 • Assessment of post-void residual urine volume (PVR) — any method acceptable
 • Urinalysis
The European Association of Urology (EAU) has separately published guidelines on assessment, on non-
surgical treatment (Nambiar et al., 2018) and on surgical treatment of urinary incontinence (Lucas et al.,
2013).
History
 • Define the onset, frequency, character and severity of the incontinence, plus other storage and voiding
   symptoms.
 • Identify aggravating factors — notably sexual intercourse and exercise.
 • Accurately assess baseline urinary urgency: this specific symptom correlates with poorer outcomes
   after sling surgery (Richter et al., 2008).
 • Cover factors affecting bladder and urethral function: neurological disease, prior surgery, pelvic
   radiation, current medications.
 • Ask specifically about faecal incontinence and defaecatory problems.
 • Because incontinence affects quality of life (QoL) rather than quantity of life, formal assessment of
   bother is integral, not optional.
Examination
 • A focused neurourological and pelvic examination is mandatory in any female complaining of urinary
   incontinence.
 • It may uncover neurological or musculoskeletal disorder, and correctable anatomical abnormalities such
   as vesicovaginal fistula or vaginal epithelial irritation.
 • Standard components: vaginal anatomy — urethral meatus and vaginal support, i.e. pelvic organ
   prolapse (POP); quality of vaginal tissue — atrophy, irritation;
degree of urethrovesical hypermobility; associated findings — fistulae, foreign material.
 • Valsalva during examination to reveal or exaggerate POP, urethral hypermobility and SUI.
 • Cotton-tipped applicator (Q-tip) test, Bergman and Bhatia (1987): hypermobility is defined as
   movement >30 degrees from baseline on straining — though the test's usefulness is controversial.
 • If a supine stress test with a full bladder is negative, repeat it standing.

Guideline Positions on Further Testing
Baseline
 • Urinalysis and PVR in all females; more extensive imaging is not part of the initial evaluation of SUI.
Additional evaluation SHOULD be performed (Expert Opinion)
 • Inability to make a definitive diagnosis from symptoms and initial evaluation
 • Inability to objectively demonstrate SUI
 • Known or suspected neurogenic lower urinary tract (LUT) dysfunction — e.g. multiple sclerosis
 • Abnormal urinalysis — unexplained haematuria or pyuria
 • Urgency-predominant mixed urinary incontinence (MUI)
 • Elevated PVR by clinician judgement
 • High-grade POP — pelvic organ prolapse quantification (POP-Q) stage ≥3 — with SUI not demonstrated
   on POP reduction
 • Significant voiding dysfunction

Additional evaluation MAY be performed
 • Concomitant overactive bladder (OAB) symptoms; failed previous anti-incontinence surgery; previous
   POP surgery.
Modalities of additional testing
 • Upper tract radiography where there is concomitant haematuria or suspected neurogenic dysfunction.
 • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of head, spine and pelvis to detect spinal cord or brain lesions.
 • Cystoscopy should NOT be performed in evaluating SUI in the index patient (Clinical Principle). It
   becomes integral, however, where there is concern for neoplasia or foreign body, or after previous
   pelvic surgery.

The Urodynamics Controversy
 • Preoperative urodynamic evaluation — filling cystometrogram, leak point pressures, electromyography,
   pressure-flow studies — remains an area of controversy.
 • AUA/SUFU Conditional Recommendation, grade B evidence: urodynamics may be OMITTED in the
   index patient when SUI is clearly demonstrated.

Landmark Trial — ValUE (Nager et al., 2012)
 • Multicentre randomised non-inferiority trial, 630 females with SUI or stress-predominant MUI,
   randomised to urodynamics versus office evaluation alone before anti-incontinence surgery.
 • Conclusion: preoperative office evaluation alone was non-inferior to evaluation with urodynamics for
   one-year outcomes in uncomplicated, demonstrable SUI.
 • Secondary analysis (Norton et al., 2016): urodynamics carried substantial per-patient cost, in excess of
   US$300, extrapolating to tens of millions of dollars of annual United States expenditure — cited
   alongside the invasiveness of the studies as further argument against routine use.
Criticism of the trial's design
 • The overwhelming majority of screened females were ineligible for inclusion — taken to indicate that
    most females presenting with SUI do NOT have uncomplicated SUI, so the trial population is
    unrepresentative of practice.
 • Almost all treating physicians were fellowship-trained, arguably reducing the need for rigorous
   urodynamic testing in that setting and limiting generalisability to non-specialist practice.

The Counter-Argument: Assessment of Urethral Function
 • The chief goal urodynamics uniquely serves is evaluation of urethral function, indexed by low MUCP and
   by abdominal leak point pressure (ALPP) / Valsalva leak point pressure (VLPP).
 • McGuire (1981): MUCP <20 cm H2O indicates type 3 SUI — now termed intrinsic sphincter deficiency
    (ISD).
 • Sand et al. (1987), in females undergoing modified Burch colposuspension: MUCP ≤20 cm H2O predicted
    a markedly higher failure rate than MUCP >20 cm H2O.

 • MUCP ≤20 cm H2O has since been used generally to characterise severe urethral dysfunction.

 • McGuire et al. (1993) — the ALPP thresholds: ALPP <60 cm H2O signifies ISD; ALPP >90 cm H2O signifies
    little or no ISD.
 • The definition of ISD has since evolved from strict urodynamic criteria to a clinical diagnosis.
 • Conceptually essential: every female with stress incontinence has some component of ISD. ISD is not a
   separate disease entity but a matter of degree.

 • Urodynamics remain the only way to definitively diagnose ISD and low MUCP/ ALPP/VLPP
   preoperatively.
Where the evidence sits
 • No study has shown that urodynamic evaluation definitively predicts postoperative outcome in
   uncomplicated SUI.
 • Multiple authors report that low MUCP or VLPP predicts poorer outcome after MUS, and specifically
   after the transobturator approach.
 • Others found no predictive value of low MUCP or VLPP after MUS (Costantini et al., 2008, 2009).
 • Further refinement proposed: only ISD in the presence of a hypomobile urethra portends poorer
   success (Wlazlak et al., 2017) — ISD remains an incompletely understood concept.

Urodynamics in the Non-Index Patient
Expert Opinion — urodynamics MAY be performed
 • Prior anti-incontinence or POP surgery
 • Mismatch between subjective and objective measures
 • Significant voiding dysfunction
 • Urge-predominant MUI, urgency urinary incontinence (UUI), or OAB
 • Elevated PVR
 • Unconfirmed SUI
 • Neurogenic LUT dysfunction
 • The decision turns on the patient's willingness to undergo the study and on whether the result would
   alter or better define treatment options.
 • In significant POP, urodynamics must be performed both with and without POP reduction — using a
   sponge stick, speculum or pessary.
Bladder-side information from urodynamics
 • Beyond outlet assessment, filling and emptying pressures give prognostic information about bladder
   function.
 • Detrusor underactivity and Valsalva voiding may predict postoperative voiding difficulty (Gracely et al.,
   2021) — more prominently in non-index patients.
 • Detrusor overactivity (DO) found on filling may change the choice of treatment — the single most
   consequential bladder finding.
The two-way relationship between SUI and detrusor overactivity
 • Mechanism proposed for stress-induced DO: stress leakage of urine into the proximal urethra (Kuru,
   1965) and traction on pelvic nerves when raised abdominal pressure acts on weakened pelvic
   supportive tissue (Serels et al., 2000).
 • Therefore pre-existing DO may IMPROVE after anti-incontinence surgery where the DO is stress-induced.
 • But existing DO and urgency may equally WORSEN after otherwise uncomplicated anti-incontinence
   surgery.
 • Practical consequence: in known DO, consider alternative or complementary treatments before
   proceeding to sling surgery.
 • Low cystometric bladder capacity and decreased compliance may also worsen sling outcomes and must
   be weighed.
 • The overall value of urodynamics in predicting sling outcomes remains debatable (Nager et al., 2008,
   2011).

 KEY POINTS: PREOPERATIVE ASSESSMENT
   • Minimum evaluation of a female with UI: focused history, physical examination, and objective
     confirmation of SUI, plus the basic clinical tests — urinalysis and PVR.
   • Cystoscopy should not be performed in uncomplicated SUI, but cystoscopy and radiographic imaging
     may be performed in haematuria, prior pelvic surgery, or suspected urinary tract abnormality.
   • Urodynamic studies (UDS) may be omitted in uncomplicated SUI and stress-predominant MUI
     desiring surgery; they prove useful where the diagnosis is unclear or where complicating factors
     coexist — significant storage and voiding symptoms, prior LUT surgery, suspected neurogenic bladder
     dysfunction, or significant POP.
   • Slings treat both SUI and stress-predominant MUI effectively, but thorough characterisation of
     preoperative symptoms and bother guides counselling and treatment choice.


ALTERNATIVE TREATMENT OPTIONS
 • Because UI rarely affects quantity of life, non-surgical options must be discussed with every patient.
 • AUA/SUFU Expert Opinion: treatment should be tailored to the individual, and patient bother taken
   into account in deciding on therapy.

Initial Non-Surgical (Conservative) Therapy
 • Patient education · behavioural and dietary modification · judicious fluid restriction · weight loss · pelvic
   floor muscle training (PFMT).
Weight loss
 • Subak et al. (2009a): randomising females with obesity between two weight-loss programmes, greater
   weight loss produced correspondingly greater reduction in SUI episodes — a dose-response
   relationship between weight lost and continence gained.
 • A recent review concluded weight loss benefits both SUI and OAB symptoms (Fuselier et al., 2018).
Pelvic floor muscle training
 • Kegel exercises and formal PFMT act by improving pelvic floor muscle strength and coordination.
 • Cochrane systematic review (Dumoulin et al., 2018), pooling randomised and quasi-randomised trials of
   PFMT in female SUI: PFMT should be recommended to most patients BEFORE surgical treatment. Versus
   no treatment, PFMT gave markedly higher rates of cure or improvement of SUI, and roughly double the
   rate of cure or improvement of incontinence of any type.
 • Adherence is central to both short- and long-term symptom improvement — the limiting factor in
   practice.
Devices
 • Pessaries and other vaginal and urethral devices help SUI in selected individuals.
Periurethral bulking agents
 • Submucosal injection via cystoscope — a minimally invasive option with variable success and frequent
   need for retreatment.
 • Cochrane review (Kirchin et al., 2017): limited evidence of benefit.
 • Nonetheless remains an option for females not ready for a more morbid and invasive procedure.
   Detailed in Chapter 120.

PUBOVAGINAL SLING
Defining contrast: the PVS is placed under the BLADDER NECK; the MUS is placed under the MID-URETHRA.
Indications — the versatility of the PVS
  • Primary and recurrent SUI, associated with both urethral hypermobility AND ISD.
 • Pure SUI and stress-predominant MUI.
 • Concomitant antero-apical POP.
 • Complex SUI associated with neurological conditions.
 • Neurogenic SUI, e.g. myelodysplasia — effective for leakage occurring between clean intermittent
   catheterisations (CIC), but only once urodynamics have confirmed sufficient bladder capacity and
   compliance.
 • Urethral reconstruction after damage from trauma, synthetic mesh or graft perforation, and iatrogenic
   hypospadias.
 • Interposition during repair of urethrovaginal fistula or urethral diverticulum.
 • Recurrent SUI after failed retropubic suspension or failed MUS.
Of the many materials and techniques described, the PVS using AUTOLOGOUS FASCIA remains the gold
standard for management of ALL forms of SUI.

Anatomy and Mechanics of a Pubovaginal Sling
Mechanism of stress leakage
 • The female urethra lies beneath the pubic symphysis; the pubourethral ligaments suspend the anterior
   urethral wall to the pubic arch.
 • In urethral hypermobility, a rise in intra-abdominal pressure makes the posterior urethral wall slide
   away from the anterior wall, thereby opening the bladder neck and proximal urethra.
 • Uneven pressure transmission plus bladder neck opening (funnelling) produces the stress leak.
 • ISD is a different defect entirely — it arises within the urethra proper, so the sphincter cannot coapt or
   generate sufficient resting urethral closing pressure.
The four-layer urethra and the components of continence
 • The female urethra comprises four separate tissue layers that keep it closed. The middle muscular layer
   compresses to maintain the resting urethral closure mechanism; the outer seromuscular layer augments
   that closing pressure.
 • Normally the resting urethral closing pressure of the internal sphincter exceeds the resting or Valsalva
   pressure of the bladder.
 • External sphincter fibre types: fast-twitch fibres → sudden voluntary contraction; slow-twitch fibres →
   continuous passive control via the involuntary guarding reflex during filling.
 • Levator ani maintains the integrity of the pelvic diaphragm for continence control.
 • Support structures: urethropelvic ligament → supports the bladder neck; pubocervical fascia →
   supports the undersurface of the bladder.
How the sling works
 • The bladder neck PVS gives dynamic urethral compression WITHOUT obstruction during rises in intra-
   abdominal pressure.
 • Goal: adequate urethral coaptation plus increased urethral responsiveness to abdominal pressure —
   balanced against ischaemia, retention and erosion from unnecessary tension.

 • Blaivas and Olsson (1988) described the current version of the PVS: a SHORT FREE GRAFT of rectus
   fascia whose tension could be adjusted.
 • Critical mechanistic point: it is incorporation of the sling into the endopelvic fascia, with subsequent
   fixation and fibrosis — NOT entry into the retropubic space — that prevents the sling giving way under
   raised intra-abdominal pressure.
 • Sling width <2 cm: wide enough to give the necessary urethral compression, with cross-sectional area
   sufficient to avoid a narrow constricting band.

Pubovaginal Sling Materials
The ideal and the reality
 • Materials used across the last century fall into four classes: autologous, allograft, xenograft, synthetic.
 • The ideal material gives long-lasting suburethral support with minimal adverse effects, and
   incorporates into the host with minimal tissue reaction.
 • In reality, most materials work by promoting organised fibrosis, reinforcing the sphincteric mechanism
   through improved suburethral support.
 • The fibrosis paradox: theoretically greater fibrosis → better clinical results, but inflammatory infiltration
   causes rapid sling degradation and possible tissue destruction with erosion.
 • Autologous slings are completely biocompatible and rarely associated with urethral perforation.
   Biologic grafts and synthetics gained past popularity only for reduced operative time, morbidity, pain and
   hospital stay.
 • Most allograft, xenograft and synthetic sling materials are no longer available; further discussion of
   synthetics for bladder neck sling (BNS) construction is in Chapter 119.

Autologous Pubovaginal Slings
The two commonest autologous materials
 • Rectus abdominis fascia — harvested from the abdominal wall.
 • Fascia lata — harvested from the lateral thigh.
Histological behaviour
 • FitzGerald et al. (2000): rectus fascial grafts undergo extensive remodelling after placement, with
   abundant fibroblasts and connective tissue on biopsy.
 • Woodruff et al. (2008), comparing synthetic, autologous, allograft and xenograft slings histologically:
   autologous materials showed the greatest host fibroblast infiltration and neovascularisation, with
   minimal inflammatory or foreign-body reaction.
 • Explants examined up to 65 months after placement showed autologous fascial grafts consistently
   intact with minimal gross degradation.
 • Autologous tissue carries a negligible risk of urethral erosion (Webster and Gerridzen, 2003).
 • Disadvantages of harvest: increased operative time, longer hospital stay, more postoperative pain, and
   harvest-site complications.
Rectus fascia — the practical point
 • Even where the harvest site is scarred and thickened by prior operations, graft quality is not necessarily
   compromised.
 • Prior ventral hernia repair is the situation in which fascia lata becomes the preferred autologous
   material.
Fascia lata — the comparison with rectus fascia
 • Similar: completely biocompatible, minimal tissue reaction, similar mechanical properties.

 • Better: shorter reported recovery time; no risk of abdominal harvest-site complications.
 • Worse: requires patient repositioning, additional operative time, and operating in an area unfamiliar to
   most pelvic surgeons; thigh pain is a short-term sequela.
 • Harvest technique matters for thigh pain: early series reported pain on walking in the majority at one

 • Thigh muscle herniation is width-dependent: a 10–20 cm wide graft produced herniation in roughly
   half; a narrow graft, 1.5 cm × 12–15 cm, produced NONE.
Vaginal epithelium (vaginal wall sling)
 • Raz et al. (1989) described the use of in situ vaginal wall.
 • Drawbacks: possibly insufficient tensile strength; risk of epithelial inclusion cyst formation; vaginal
   shortening.
 • The absence of retropubic space dissection may mitigate the overall efficacy of the procedure.

 KEY POINTS: PUBOVAGINAL SLING MATERIALS
   • The ideal PVS material is sterile, biocompatible, non-carcinogenic and consistent in quality.
   • The implanted material should incorporate into the host with minimal tissue reaction, giving long-
     lasting suburethral support with minimal complications.
   • Autologous materials remain the gold standard for PVS — no adverse tissue reaction and negligible
     chance of urethral perforation. Other biomaterials were adopted only to shorten operative time,
     hospital stay and recovery.
   • Allografts: tissue-processing techniques vary and may disrupt the microstructure, affecting ultimate
     graft strength.
   • Xenografts: in situ, less tensile strength than allografts, and the highest propensity to encapsulate;
     processing likewise varies significantly.
   • Synthetics: significant inflammatory and foreign-body reaction, with higher rates of graft infection
     and perforation — SUBOPTIMAL for placement at the bladder neck.


AUTOLOGOUS PUBOVAGINAL SLING OPERATIVE PROCEDURE

Counselling
 • Cover the global risks of surgery in informed consent, then the sling-specific risks.
 • Counsel explicitly on transient AND permanent voiding dysfunction: postoperative difficulty emptying
   the bladder, and de novo urgency and frequency.
 • Preoperative instruction in CIC in selected patients may avoid an indwelling urethral catheter
   postoperatively.
 • Uberoi et al. (2021) — expectation management is a distinct counselling task: focus-group participants
   expected sling surgery to resolve other pelvic floor issues such as bowel symptoms, and described
   persisting non-SUI symptoms as negative outcomes even when their SUI had improved.

Anaesthesia
 • Spinal or general — decided by patient, surgeon and anaesthetist consensus.

Antibiotic prophylaxis
 • Sling surgery is a CLEAN-CONTAMINATED case.
 • Prophylaxis must cover Staphylococcus aureus, streptococci, enterococci, vaginal anaerobes and skin
   flora.
 • AUA Best Practice Policy Statement: a single preoperative dose of a first- or second-generation
   cephalosporin. Second-generation agents (cefoxitin, cefotetan) give better anaerobic cover in principle
   than first-generation cefazolin — however, cefazolin proved equivalent for vaginal anaerobes in sling
   procedures (Lightner et al., 2020).
 • Alternative single-dose regimens: ampicillin/sulbactam · aminoglycoside or aztreonam with
   metronidazole · aztreonam with clindamycin · clindamycin alone.
 • Systematic review (Temtanakitpaisan et al., 2022): prophylactic antibiotics had little or no effect on
   surgical site infection, urinary tract infection (UTI), bacteriuria, postoperative fever, or vaginal mesh
   exposure — but the included trials were small and the conclusion must be interpreted with caution.
Thromboprophylaxis
 • Bilateral lower-limb intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) devices routinely applied BEFORE
   induction of anaesthesia.
 • AUA Best Practice Policy Statement for Prevention of Deep Venous Thrombosis in Urologic Surgery
   (Forrest et al., 2009): for incontinence surgery, the choice between IPC, low-dose unfractionated
   heparin and low-molecular-weight heparin is made on individual patient and procedural risk factors —
   no blanket recommendation.
Positioning and nerve injury
 • Dorsal lithotomy, with deliberate care to minimise lower-limb neural injury.
 • Bohrer et al. (2009): overall incidence of peripheral nerve injury is low. Commonest nerves injured:
   lateral femoral cutaneous and femoral (equal, and jointly predominant), then common fibular
   (common peroneal), ilioinguinal/ iliohypogastric, saphenous and genitofemoral.
 • Prognosis is excellent: complete resolution of neuropathic symptoms in all but one patient, at a median
   of approximately one month (range one day to six months).
Skin and vaginal preparation
 • Prepare and drape the abdomen from the umbilicus down, and the vagina.
 • Povidone-iodine is the commonest vaginal antiseptic in the United States; practice differs elsewhere.
 • American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) committee opinion, September 2013:
   chlorhexidine gluconate solutions containing 4% alcohol or less are safe and effective for vaginal
   preparation — an off-label use.
Set-up
 • Weighted speculum in the vagina; 18-Fr Foley catheter per urethra.
 • Moderate Trendelenburg; a headlight aids vaginal dissection.
 • A vaginal ring retractor retracts the labia majora, and later the incision, improving exposure.
Additional set-up for fascia lata harvest
 • IPC device positioned BELOW the patella on the harvest side.
 • Knee elevated and supported on a 1-litre fluid bag or cushion.
 • Limb internally rotated at the hip and taped to the table below the operative site with 3-inch tape.
 • Thigh prepared to expose the anterolateral aspect from greater trochanter to patella.
 • Landmarks marked: greater trochanter and lateral femoral condyle — these denote the proximal and
   distal attachments of the fascia lata (Dwyer and Kreder, 2008).

Graft Harvest for Autologous Pubovaginal Sling
Rectus fascia harvest
 • Pfannenstiel incision 6–7 cm, sited approximately 2 cm above the pubic symphysis → carried down to
   the rectus fascia.
 • Mark a 2 × 8 cm graft on the rectus fascia, transverse or longitudinal.
 • Harvest with scalpel or electrocautery.
 • If the fascial incision is transverse, stay ≥2 cm away from the pubic symphysis to permit tension-free
   fascial closure.
 • Freeing the fascial edges off the underlying rectus muscle aids tension-free closure but may
   theoretically weaken the fascia — the trade-off to state.
 • Close the fascia with running No. 1 polydioxanone (PDS). Alternative in patients with minimal
   subcutaneous fat: figure-of-eight 0-0 polyglactin 910 (Vicryl).
 • If the fascia cannot be closed adequately — rare — interpose a segment of synthetic mesh or biologic
   graft.
Graft preparation
 • Place the graft in 0.9% normal saline, with or without antibiotic.
 • On a sterile side table, clean off overlying fat and perifascial tissue.
 • Secure a separate No. 1 PDS or 1-0 polypropylene suture to EACH end of the graft.
 • Place each suture perpendicular to the sling fibres (transverse harvest only), run it across the end of the
   graft, and tie down.
 • Leave sutures long; return the graft to saline until needed.
Fascia lata harvest
 • Mark a 3 cm longitudinal incision beginning just above the patella, over the iliotibial band.
 • Dissect to the fascia lata; make two parallel longitudinal incisions 2 cm apart.
 • Bluntly lift the graft off the underlying muscle; clamp as distally as possible with a right-angle clamp (3–
   4 cm) and transect, creating one free end.
 • Secure the free end with No. 1 PDS.
 • Lift the proximal fascia lata off the muscle belly with a thin malleable retractor, passing it both
   superficial and deep to the fascia lata to separate it from adipose tissue and muscle fibres.
 • With the free distal end under tension, use a Crawford fascial stripper to extend the fascial incision
   proximally and divide it before removal.
 • Secure a second No. 1 PDS to the other free end; store the sling in saline.
 • Length: classically a full-length strip of 20 × 2 cm; shorter lengths (8 cm) are now commonly used
   (Karram and Bhatia, 1990).
Thigh closure
 • Apply immediate compression to constrict perforating vessels.
 • Inspect carefully for arterial bleeding before closure.
 • Irrigate and close in three layers, WITHOUT closing the fascia lata itself.
 • Apply a compressive wrap and replace the IPC device.
 • Keep the compressive bandage on for 8 hours postoperatively; encourage early ambulation.

Pubovaginal Sling Vaginal Dissection
Incision
  • Inject 0.9% sterile normal saline into the vaginal epithelium at the mid-urethra and bladder neck →
    hydrodistention aids tissue dissection.
 • Preferred incision: inverted U — it gives excellent exposure of the urethra at the bladder neck and
   direct access to the endopelvic fascia and thence the retropubic space.
 • Top of the incision approximately 2 cm below the urethral meatus; an Allis clamp placed immediately
   below the meatus improves exposure.
 • Arms of the inverted U extend to the level of the bladder neck, located by palpating the Foley balloon.
 • 15-blade knife through the vaginal epithelium, staying ABOVE the periurethral and pubocervical fascia
   — this avoids bleeding and injury to urethra and bladder.
 • Alternative: a midline vaginal incision from the bladder base proximally to the mid-urethra distally.
 • Create THICK vaginal epithelial flaps with Allis clamp and Metzenbaum scissors; retract with the vaginal
   ring retractor.
 • Keep the arms of the inverted U wide to avoid devascularising the flap.
Entering the retropubic space
 • Perforate the endopelvic fascia beneath the easily palpable ischiopubic rami.
 • The bladder must be adequately drained before this manoeuvre, and again before passing Stamey
   needles or larger clamps.
 • Metzenbaum scissors angled toward the ipsilateral shoulder, tips pointing upward; perforate directly
   medial and immediately under the ischiopubic ramus at the superior margin of dissection.
 • Perforation is in a SUPEROLATERAL direction; spread the scissors widely to prepare the next step.
 • Blunt finger dissection of the retropubic space on either side of the urethra — this connects the
   infrapubic and retropubic dissection planes.
 • Endpoint of adequate dissection: the posterior surface of the pubic symphysis is easily palpable with
   very little intervening tissue, permitting free movement of the sling and easy tensioning.
 • Even with the abdominal fascia closed, simultaneous finger palpation through the abdominal and
   vaginal incisions should be possible, with gentle medial palpation of the bladder.
 • Do NOT attempt aggressive medial mobilisation — this risks bladder injury. Haemostasis with bipolar
   cautery.
The reoperative case
 • After prior urethral suspension or sling, more aggressive SHARP dissection is required.
 • In difficult cases the safest plane into the retropubic space is immediately adjacent to the periosteum
   of the pubis, and dissection should be as sharp as possible to minimise injury to pelvic viscera.

Needle passage
 • Stamey or Cobb-Ragde needles are passed from ABOVE, through the abdominal incision, guided
   carefully behind the pubis.
 • Keep the needles in contact with the pubis until they emerge lateral to the bladder into the vaginal
   incision.
 • Alternative instruments for suture advancement: larger clamps such as tonsil clamps.
 • The bladder must be completely drained before passage of any suture passer.

Cystoscopy — mandatory
 • Perform cystoscopy with a 70-degree lens after needle passage, following the needle course while an
   assistant moves the needles downward and medially toward the bladder.
 • Cystoscopy is regarded as essential to eliminate the serious complication of sling perforation of the
   bladder.
 • If a small bladder injury or transvesical needle passage occurs: remove the needles and repass them
   MORE LATERALLY.

 • After extravesical passage is confirmed, observe brisk ureteric efflux bilaterally to confirm patency;
   intravenous methylene blue or sodium fluorescein, or ureteric catheters, may be used.
 • Replace the Foley catheter for completion of the case.

 • Pass the graft suture ends through the Stamey/Cobb-Ragde needle eyelets.
 • Mark the CENTRE of the graft with a clamp, remove the needles, and bring the suture ends out through
   the abdominal incision, tagged with hemostat clamps.
 • Suture the distal aspect of the graft to periurethral tissue with two simple 4-0 polyglactin 910.

 • Vaginal packing and urethral catheter both removed on the morning of postoperative day 1.

Autologous Pubovaginal Slings
Primary SUI and the complex population
 • Reintroduced in the late 1970s (McGuire and Lytton, 1978), the autologous PVS has remained
   reproducible and effective at long-term follow-up.
 • The earliest series (McGuire et al., 1987; Blaivas and Jacobs, 1991) deliberately included a complex
   population — pelvic radiation, diabetes, spinal cord injury, pelvic trauma.
 • Even patients with "proximal urethral function loss", defined as MUCP <4 cm H2O, achieved a high cure
    rate — the striking demonstration that severe ISD is not a contraindication to PVS.
 • Concomitant anterior colporrhaphy with PVS gives excellent results in grade 3–4 cystocele with SUI
   (Cross et al., 1997).
Recurrent SUI
 • Groutz et al. (2001), comparing primary with recurrent SUI (the latter after one to three failed anti-
   incontinence procedures): cure was significantly higher in primary SUI, but ALL patients in both groups
   were cured or improved.
 • Richter et al. (2001), long-term QoL after PVS, most having recurrent SUI after failed bladder neck
   suspension: the large majority felt the sling improved QoL and would undergo surgery again; despite
   postoperative voiding dysfunction, all were satisfied with SUI resolution and none elected urethrolysis.
 • Petrou and Frank (2001): high cured or improved rate for REPEAT PVS after failed suburethral sling.
 • Athanasopoulos et al. (2011) — one of the earliest and largest series of PVS after failed MUS; roughly a
   third had prior MUS, all underwent partial sling excision at the time of PVS, with a high cured or
   markedly improved rate.

Landmark Trial — SISTEr (Albo et al., 2007)
 • Stress Incontinence Surgical Treatment Efficacy Trial: multicentre randomised trial of rectus fascia PVS
   (326) versus Burch colposuspension (329).
 • Two primary outcomes: resolution of overall UI — no subjective or objective incontinence, increase of
   <15 g on 24-hour pad weight, and no medical or surgical treatment for incontinence; and resolution of
   SUI — no subjective SUI and a negative cough stress test.
 • Result: PVS gave a higher SUI cure rate (47% vs 38%, p < 0.001), but significantly higher rates of
   postoperative voiding dysfunction and cystitis.
 • All 20 subsequent procedures for bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) occurred in the PVS group.
 • Long-term extension (Brubaker et al., 2012): 5-year continence 31% (PVS) versus 24% (Burch), p = 0.002.
   Satisfaction at 5 years higher after PVS (83% vs 73%), declining with time in both arms but remaining
   higher in the sling group. Adverse event rates were similar (Burch 10% vs PVS 9%).
 • Interpretation: the low absolute success rates reflect a STRICT definition of success and vigilant follow-
   up, not a poor operation.
 • The key conceptual observation: subjective satisfaction diverges from strict objective cure — patients
   can be satisfied without being completely dry.

 • Mason and Roach (1996): a shorter 4 × 2 cm modified rectus fascia PVS used successfully in ISD.

   of PVS is usually a storage-symptom problem, not a stress problem.
Fascia lata outcomes
 • Intermediate-term outcomes appear comparable to rectus fascia in retrospective series.
 • Beck et al. (1988): high cure by postoperative CST in recurrent SUI. Govier et al. (1997) and Brown and
   Govier (2000): lower, questionnaire-derived satisfaction-based cure — illustrating that the measure

 • Peng et al. (2020), rectus fascia versus fascia lata: similar freedom from SUI at 1 month and 1 year;
   wound complications made up almost a third of postoperative complications in the rectus group; the

Cochrane systematic review (Saraswat et al., 2020)
 • 34 trials, most with follow-up <24 months.
 • PVS versus MUS (14 trials): PVS are probably NO BETTER, and may be LESS effective, than MUS for
   continence at 1 to 5 years. Not all PVS were autologous.
 • MUS was associated with shorter operating times, fewer perioperative complications EXCEPT bladder
   perforation, and less postoperative voiding and storage dysfunction.
 • PVS versus PVS (9 trials): results could NOT be pooled owing to clinical heterogeneity of sling types and
   materials.
PVS with mesh complication surgery
 • Shah et al. (2013): PVS at the time of urethral repair for MUS exposure — continence in most, no major
   intraoperative complications, and complete resolution of mesh-related symptoms in all.
 • Milose et al. (2015): staged autologous sling after prior urethral diverticulectomy, urethral mesh
   exposure and/or urethrovaginal fistula — cure of SUI in about two-thirds of the whole cohort, but NONE
   of the previously diverticulectomised or mesh-excised subgroup was completely continent. The
   damaged urethra is the poor-prognosis group.

OUTCOMES OF AUTOLOGOUS PUBOVAGINAL SLING IN PATIENTS WITH MIXED
URINARY INCONTINENCE
 • SUI with concomitant urgency or UUI is a complicated treatment problem, usually requiring combined
   pharmacological therapy and surgery.
 • Medical therapy for MUI resolves the urgency component in only about two-thirds of patients (Nordling
   et al., 1979; Stephenson and Mundy, 1994).
 • Pharmacological therapy does not address the bladder outlet, and therefore cannot be expected to
   achieve dryness where there is a significant stress component.
 • Anti-incontinence surgery may cure, improve, OR aggravate storage symptoms — the three-way
   outcome is the point.
 • Even an uncomplicated sling may produce de novo urgency or UUI. This aspect of anti-incontinence
   surgery is fundamentally unpredictable and is a major reason for patient dissatisfaction.
 • No consistent clinical or videourodynamic parameter predicts urgency outcomes after PVS in MUI.
 • Residual urgency after PVS behaves similarly to de novo urgency, and increasing episodes of urgency
   and UUI may correlate with surgical failure.
 • Overall the PVS remains effective in MUI, with cure rates similar to those in pure SUI.

COMPLICATIONS OF PUBOVAGINAL SLINGS

Voiding Dysfunction From Bladder Outlet Obstruction After Pubovaginal Sling
Incidence
  • Voiding dysfunction after PVS: 2.5–35%, exceeding rates after Burch colposuspension.
 • SISTEr confirmed significantly more postoperative voiding dysfunction, UTI and postoperative UUI after
   PVS than after Burch.
 • First AUA SUI Clinical Guidelines Panel meta-analysis (Leach et al., 1997): urinary retention beyond 4
   weeks after PVS occurs in 8%, and the risk of PERMANENT retention generally does not exceed 5%.
 • Morgan et al. (2000): prolonged retention in only 2.4% at four-year follow-up — taken as the
   appropriate rate when slings are not intentionally overtensioned.
Presentation — deliberately variable
 • Purely voiding symptoms: decreased force of stream, positional emptying, elevated PVR, urinary
   retention.
 • Purely storage symptoms: urgency, UUI. Or a combination of both.
 • Obstruction may also present as recurrent UTI, prolonged suprapubic pain, or painful voiding EVEN
   WHERE emptying is complete and PVR is low — the trap in relying on PVR alone.
 • Nitti et al. (2002): a sixth of patients requiring urethrolysis after PVS had NO voiding symptoms and no
   retention.
 • Cross et al. (1998b): persistent postoperative urgency or UUI (around a quarter of patients) is a
   COMMONER presentation of obstruction than frank retention (around 8%).
Mechanism — the tension dilemma
 • Iatrogenic obstruction is usually technical: sutures or sling material placed under excessive tension.
 • There is a fine line between inadequate and excessive tension, and no consistent way of determining
   ideal tension for the individual patient.
 • Too loose → inadequate support of bladder neck and proximal urethra → persistent SUI.
 • Too tight → excessive elevation of the bladder neck toward the pubic bone → hypersuspension,
   overcorrection of the urethrovesical angle → obstruction.
Host factors predisposing to postoperative emptying failure
 • Pre-existing voiding dysfunction.


                                                 page 18 of 48
Chapter 118 — Slings                                                         COMPLICATIONS OF PUBOVAGINAL SLINGS

 • Subclinical impaired detrusor contractility — becomes symptomatic as relative obstruction once outlet
   resistance rises (Weinberger and Ostergard, 1996).
 • Dysfunctional voiding — failure of relaxation of the external (striated) urethral sphincter (FitzGerald and
   Brubaker, 2001).
 • Habitual voiding by abdominal straining — theoretically problematic because the sling provides
   DYNAMIC resistance that increases with intra-abdominal pressure, so the straining meant to empty the
   bladder instead tightens the outlet.
 • A thorough history establishing chronology, character and bother of the predominant symptom is
   invaluable, given the variability of presentation.
Evaluation of suspected post-sling obstruction
 • Physical examination may show abnormal urethral angulation, a foreshortened non-pliable vagina, or a
   non-mobile urethra — but hypersuspension may not be evident on examination.
 • PVR is very important, though NO clear cut-off value for obstruction exists (Siddighi and Karram, 2007).
 • Cystoscopy to exclude bladder pathology, sling perforation, and a hypersuspended urethra.
 • Videourodynamics in selected cases at the physician's discretion — but the classic high-pressure, low-
   flow pattern pathognomonic of obstruction is frequently ABSENT in females.
 • The single most important criterion for deciding to incise a sling or perform urethrolysis is the
   TEMPORAL RELATIONSHIP between onset of symptoms and the operation — not a urodynamic number.
Attempts to predict postoperative voiding dysfunction
 • Weinberger and Ostergard (1996): impaired detrusor contractility predicted retention after synthetic PVS.
 • Miller et al. (2003): females voiding with minimal or no rise in detrusor pressure had significantly higher
   retention risk after allograft PVS; none with a normal detrusor contraction developed retention.
 • McLennan et al. (1998): did not confirm the association.
 • Mitsui et al. (2007): a trend toward prolonged CIC in those with preoperative PVR >100 mL or maximum
   flow rate (Qmax) ≤20 mL/s — a substantial minority needed CIC for up to 40 months.
 • Lemack et al. (2008), within SISTEr: urodynamic findings did NOT predict postoperative voiding
   dysfunction.
 • Practice point: low detrusor pressure and Valsalva voiding preoperatively should NOT exclude a patient
   from an anti-incontinence procedure.
Prolapse as a cause of post-sling obstruction
 • POP uncorrected at surgery, or arising de novo afterwards, is a key factor.
 • Prolapse of sufficient size may kink, angulate or externally compress the urethra, and POP in ALL THREE
   compartments (apical, anterior, posterior) must be excluded as a cause of obstruction.
 • Kobashi et al. (2002), combined cystocele repair and PVS using a single piece of cadaveric fascia: at one
   year, only one patient developed persistent obstruction requiring urethrolysis.
 • Barnes et al. (2002), concurrent PVS with prolapse repair in grade 3 or 4 POP with occult SUI: no
   permanent retention, two de novo UUI, and preoperative UUI resolved in nearly half. Conclusion:
   concurrent surgery had little negative effect on postoperative emptying.
 • There is a paucity of literature on the emptying and voiding effects of concurrent PVS and prolapse
   repair.
The causality problem
 • Preoperative storage symptoms after PVS may resolve, improve, remain unchanged, or worsen — and
   may appear de novo.

 • Postoperative urgency and UUI are strongly related to failure, yet no preoperative risk factor
   consistently predicts them.
 • Consequently assigning causality — and deciding whether to investigate and treat BOO rather than the
   storage symptoms themselves — is genuinely difficult (Gomelsky et al., 2022). Two reasons:
 • The effect of sling loosening or urethrolysis is unpredictable: storage symptoms may improve or persist,
   and stress continence may be lost in a patient who is currently dry.
 • In a patient who does not undergo loosening for incomplete BOO, the long-term consequence for the
   detrusor is unknown — the effect of long-standing BOO on the bladder is well characterised in males but
   not in females.
 • Therefore a detailed risk–benefit discussion about sling loosening or urethrolysis must be held with the
   patient.

Surgical Management of Voiding Dysfunction After Pubovaginal Sling
Timing — the governing rule
 • Transient retention is common; most females return to spontaneous efficient voiding within the first 10
   days.
 • Obstruction after autologous PVS usually improves or resolves with time, so most clinicians wait up to 3
   MONTHS before considering surgical intervention.
 • Initial conservative management: temporary indwelling catheter · CIC · timed and double voiding ·
   biofeedback · PFMT · anticholinergic therapy.
Early intervention in complete retention
 • If in complete retention, revision may be undertaken sooner.
 • Within the first 6 weeks after autologous PVS the sling may be loosened in theatre under spinal or
   general anaesthesia: insert a cystoscope into the bladder, then apply gentle inferior pressure to the
   urethra.
 • This manoeuvre is NOT advised with synthetic slings.
 • Urethral dilation with downward traction after autologous PVS has only anecdotal support, and results
   range from temporary relief to worsening urethral rigidity from periurethral fibrosis.
 • Transurethral resection or incision of the bladder neck is likely to FAIL, because the sling is
   EXTRALUMINAL; it also risks damage to sphincter or bladder neck and periurethral fibrosis, causing
   worsened incontinence or bladder neck contracture (Ghoniem and Elgamasy, 1995).
 • After 6 weeks, or when conservative measures fail: sling incision or formal urethrolysis is indicated.
Urethrolysis — approaches and results
 • Three approaches: retropubic, transvaginal, suprameatal. Overall success 65–93%.
 • Foster and McGuire (1993): transvaginal urethrolysis succeeded in only half of PVS obstructions —
   lower than for needle suspension or retropubic urethropexy. Their explanation: transvaginal LATERAL
   dissection is insufficient to relieve the DIRECT SUBURETHRAL COMPRESSIVE force of a sling.
 • Petrou et al. (1999): the suprameatal approach is superior to transvaginal because it allows access to
   and division of the LATERAL WINGS of the sling; most of their patients succeeded.
 • Petrou and Young (2002): retropubic urethrolysis resolved obstruction in most, though a minority
   subsequently developed SUI. Carr and Webster (1997): significant or complete resolution in the large
   majority after retropubic urethrolysis.
 • Recurrent SUI after formal urethrolysis: 0–19%. Outcomes of urethrolysis and sling revision for BOO are
   summarised in Table 118.4.

Sling incision versus formal urethrolysis
 • Sling incision gives comparable success (84–100%) with SHORTER operative time and LESS morbidity
    than formal urethrolysis — the practical argument for trying it first.
 • Midline or lateral sling incision WITHOUT graft interposition has been reported successful by several
   authors.
 • Sling incision WITH autologous graft interposition between the cut ends was described to prevent
   recurrent SUI (Ghoniem and Elgamasy, 1995), but others report suboptimal outcomes.
 • Vaginal wall interposition relieved obstruction in almost all patients in two series — yet SUI recurred in
   a quarter of each. The interposition does not reliably protect continence.
The four takeaways from the sling-incision literature
 • A LATERAL incision theoretically minimises urethral injury where the sling can be identified — but the
   dissection plane between urethra and sling cannot be established consistently or safely.
 • Approximately 20% develop recurrent SUI, though not all seek further treatment.
 • A minority still benefit from subsequent formal urethrolysis after an initial failure to relieve BOO.
 • No preoperative or urodynamic parameter consistently predicts success or failure of urethrolysis.
Contested predictors of urethrolysis failure
 • Foster and McGuire (1993): DO predicted higher failure — contradicted by later studies.
 • Carr and Webster (1997): the only predictor of success was NO PRIOR urethrolysis.
 • Nitti and Raz (1994): failure related to higher PVR. None of these has been confirmed by others.
Causes of persistent symptoms and repeat urethrolysis
 • Four causes of persistent storage or voiding symptoms after urethrolysis: persistent or recurrent
   obstruction · detrusor overactivity · impaired detrusor contractility · learned voiding dysfunction.
 • Recurrent obstruction may arise from periurethral fibrosis and scarring, or from intrinsic urethral
   damage caused by previous urethrolyses.
 • The commonest reason for failure is likely INSUFFICIENT dissection and lysis of the urethra — an
   inadequate operation, not an unfavourable patient.

Pubovaginal Sling Perforation and Exposure
 • Incidence depends on sling composition. Synthetic slings perforate into the urethra 15 times more
   often, and are exposed in the vagina 14 times more often, than autologous, allograft and xenograft
   slings (Blaivas and Sandhu, 2004), based on the meta-analysis of 287 peer-reviewed articles (Leach et al.,
   1997).
 • Subsequent studies confirm that most perforations and exposures involve synthetic slings, particularly
   WOVEN POLYESTER slings. Cases of autologous and allograft perforation and exposure have been
   published, but they are rare.
 • Most urethral perforations are diagnosed between 1 and 18 months after the original surgery, with a
   mean of 9 months. Perforation from synthetic slings is discussed in Chapter 119.
 • Urinary tract perforation by an autologous PVS is rare: only four documented cases of bladder and
   urethral erosion in the peer-reviewed literature.
 • Contributing factors: missed bladder perforation after Stamey needle passage · excessive tension ·
   traumatic urethral instrumentation after sling placement — e.g. for clot evacuation or surveillance
   cystoscopy.
 • Management: urethral perforation → excise the perforated portion of the sling and repair the urethra.
   Bladder erosion → treat endoscopically. Rarely, a Martius flap is required for complex cases.

Non-urologic Pubovaginal Sling Complications
 • Most PVS complications are urinary tract related, but significant non-urologic complications occur.
 • 2009 update of the AUA guideline for SUI surgery (Appell et al., 2009), by meta-analysis: the
   commonest non-urologic complications are, in order, PULMONARY, CARDIOVASCULAR, NEUROLOGIC
   and GASTROINTESTINAL (bowel injury).
 • Across all anti-incontinence procedures — retropubic and transvaginal suspensions, anterior
   colporrhaphy and PVS — the estimated death rate is approximately 3 per 10,000 procedures.
 • Anger et al. (2007), sling surgery (mostly MUS) in Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 and over: within 3
   months, about an eighth developed surgical or urologic complications, and a third were diagnosed with
   UTI. At one year, non-urologic complications recorded were pulmonary (the commonest), cardiac, bowel
   injury or obstruction, and thromboembolic. On multivariate analysis, non-White race was a risk factor
   for both urologic and non-urologic complications, and women aged 65–69 were significantly LESS likely
   to have non-urologic complications, or to need treatment for BOO or recurrent incontinence, than
   those over 75.
 • Chughtai et al. (2016), in over 21,000 female Medicare beneficiaries: adverse events within 90 days were
   rare, the sole exception being urinary retention, recorded in about one in nine.
 • SISTEr complication data: serious adverse events 13% after autologous PVS versus 10% after Burch. In
   the sling group, deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and serious bleeding each occurred in well under 1%, and
   serious wound complications in a small minority.
 • Wound complications specific to autologous harvest: the commonest are harvest site infection, seroma,
   and incisional hernia. Blaivas and Chaikin (2011), in over 500 patients: wound infection and incisional

MID-URETHRAL SLINGS

Mechanics of the Mid-Urethral Sling
Theoretical basis
 • Integral theory (Petros and Ulmsten, 1990): continence depends on adequate function of the
   pubourethral ligaments, the suburethral vaginal hammock, and the pubococcygeus muscle. Injury to
   any one — from surgery, parturition, ageing or hormonal deprivation — impairs mid-urethral function
   and causes SUI.
 • Conceptually critical: urethral hypermobility is a SIGN of damage to the normal supporting structures —
   NOT a cause of SUI.
Imaging observations
 • Preoperative ultrasonography and MRI show the proximal urethra in females with SUI is often OPEN AT
   REST.
 • Regardless of urethral mobility, movement of the anterior and posterior urethral walls during stress
   produces a SHEARING effect that opens the urethra and causes leakage.
How the MUS works — three proposed mechanisms
 • It impedes movement of the posterior urethral wall above the sling, redirecting its motion
   anteroinferiorly or anteriorly.
 • Inward movement of the posterior urethral wall against the sling compresses and narrows the urethral
   lumen.
 • Some authors propose the MUS additionally KINKS the urethra dynamically during stress events, as
   seen on ultrasound (Lo et al., 2001).
 • The unifying concept: securing the posterior urethral wall, with or without compression during stress.
Anchoring
 • Retropubic MUS anchors to the ENDOPELVIC FASCIA; transobturator MUS anchors to the OBTURATOR
   INTERNUS AND EXTERNUS muscle and fascia.
 • Ideally either is placed LOOSELY at the mid-urethra; function does not require the sling to be tight.
 • Over time the mesh becomes fixed at its anchoring site and provides support along its ENTIRE course
   inferior to the pubic symphysis and ischiopubic rami — not merely in the midline behind the urethra.
 • This broad support is the likely explanation for why 50–93% remain continent after MUS INCISION for
   obstruction — cutting the midline does not abolish the whole support.

Urethral Mobility as a Prognostic Factor
Retropubic and transobturator MUS — the standard rule
 • Lack of urethral mobility BEFORE surgery predicts lower SUI cure rates.
 • Decreased urethral mobility AFTER surgery is associated with persistent incontinence.
 • Conversely, persistent significant urethral mobility postoperatively is NOT associated with a worse
   outcome.
 • Rationale: absent mobility signifies a FIXED urethra and potentially ISD, and females without
   hypermobility do not respond as well to MUS surgery.
 • The ideal combination is a loosely placed MUS with a MOBILE urethra — allowing compression during
   Valsalva while remaining non-obstructive at rest.
SIMS — the exception
 • Unlike retropubic and transobturator MUS, RESTRICTION of urethral mobility after SIMS may be
   associated with BETTER outcomes.
 • Martan et al. (2009), using perineal ultrasonography, cough test and validated questionnaires: the sling
   restricted urethral mobility, and a greater degree of restriction predicted a higher likelihood of cure —
   but the restrictive effect weakened within the first 3 months.
 • Spelzini et al. (2013): outcomes after SIMS were WORSE in those with persistent urethral hypermobility.
 • Proposed explanation: SIMS efficacy depends on restricting mobility, and that restriction is required to
   secure the posterior urethral wall, achieve adequate kinking, or compress the lumen.

Does the Mid-Urethral Sling Obstruct? — The Controversy
 • Despite tension-free placement, it is disputed whether tension or resistance is ultimately created at the
   bladder outlet.
 • Evidence AGAINST obstruction: Meschia et al. (2001), over 400 retropubic MUS: increased voiding time
   postoperatively, but no difference in flow rate, urethral closure pressure (UCP) or functional urethral
   length. Lo et al. (2001): no statistical difference in average flow rate (Qave), Qmax, PVR, MUCP or
   functional urethral length.
 • Evidence FOR obstruction: Gateau et al. (2003): consistent decrease in Qmax, increased mean detrusor
   pressure at maximum flow (PdetQmax), increased mean urethral resistance and elevated PVR —
   concluding retropubic MUS produces obstructive changes at the bladder outlet. Sander et al. (2002),
   one year after retropubic MUS: both subjective and objective voiding-phase changes — most patients
   reported more difficult voiding, with significant falls in Qmax, corrected Qmax and Qave, and
   significantly increased PVR, though never exceeding a quarter of capacity.

The modern standard
 • Almost all current MUS products are soft, loosely woven, POLYPROPYLENE MONOFILAMENT mesh with
   pore size exceeding 75 µm.
 • Why >75 µm matters: it permits migration of host inflammatory cells (leukocytes and macrophages)
   into the mesh for infectious surveillance and host wound healing (imbibition and inosculation).
 • The same material is optimal for inciting fibrous tissue ingrowth.
 • This is TYPE 1 MESH in the Amid classification, described in the general surgical literature as favourable
   for its mechanical properties — stretch and elasticity. Further discussion is in Chapter 119.
Transobturator product evolution — a cautionary sequence
 • UraTape (Mentor-Porgés) — the FIRST transobturator MUS (Delorme et al., 2003): polypropylene,
   MICROPOROUS, with a central SILICONE core.
 • Replaced by ObTape because of a high rate of vaginal exposure, likely due to the silicone core — but
   vaginal exposures also occurred with ObTape, probably because of its semi-microporous nature (<50
   µm).
 • Second-generation Aris transobturator tape: larger 200 µm pore size, allowing improved tissue
   ingrowth with LESS encapsulation.
 • BioArc — unique in having a BIOLOGIC (porcine dermis, InteXen) graft sutured at either end to
   polypropylene mesh.
 • Many retropubic and transobturator MUS are off the market; all available products are monofilament,
   MACROPOROUS, woven polypropylene.

Mid-Urethral Sling Patient Counselling
 • Because MUS surgery implants a synthetic prosthesis, counsel thoroughly about the PERMANENT nature
   of these products and about unique and sometimes serious complications, though infrequent.

Surgical Approach for Retropubic Mid-Urethral Sling
The kit
 • Two curved trocars of 5 mm diameter, and a polypropylene mesh segment 40 cm long × 1.1 cm wide —
   dimensions vary slightly by manufacturer.
 • The sling is covered by a clear plastic sheath, which protects the mesh from contamination and eases
   passage through host tissues.
 • For the down-up (bottom-top) technique, a rigid catheter guide is placed in the urethra with an 18-Fr
   Foley to deflect the bladder away from the trocar path. An ergonomic handle attaches to the trocar.
Local anaesthetic infiltration
 • ~5 mL into the vaginal epithelium and into the planned suprapubic skin sites.
 • 20 mL along the posterior aspect of the pubic bone to the level of the urogenital diaphragm, to
   anaesthetise the retropubic space.
 • 10 mL on EITHER side of the urethra to the level of the urogenital diaphragm.
Incisions and dissection
  • Two suprapubic stab incisions just above the symphysis pubis, approximately 2 cm lateral to the
    midline.
 • One midline vaginal incision, approximately 1.5 cm long, sited 1.5 cm proximal to the external urethral
   meatus.
 • Minimal dissection with Metzenbaum scissors under the vaginal flaps on either side, elevating vaginal
   epithelium off periurethral tissue to the level of the pubocervical (endopelvic) fascia.
 • UNLIKE the PVS, the endopelvic fascia is NOT perforated during dissection.
Bottom-top trocar passage
 • Place the trocar in the dissection tunnel immediately beneath the vaginal epithelium on one side of the
   urethra, tip close to the lower rim of the pubic ramus.
 • With controlled pressure, elevate the trocar through the endopelvic fascia → into the space of Retzius
   → through the rectus muscles → out through the suprapubic skin incision.
 • Keep the trocar in close contact with the INFERIOR SURFACE OF THE PUBIC BONE throughout, to avoid
   perforating the lower urinary tract and to avoid intraperitoneal entry.
 • Tactile contact with bone plus slow graded pressure ensures metal-to-bone apposition and avoids
   bladder injury.
 • Deflect the lower urinary tract simultaneously with the catheter guide and catheter. Repeat
   contralaterally.
Top-down trocar passage — the differences
 • A catheter guide with Foley is typically NOT used.

 • Once cystoscopy is clear, drain the bladder, bring the mesh through the incisions, and tension the sling.

 • Excise redundant mesh at the level of the suprapubic skin incisions.
 • Close the vaginal incision with delayed absorbable suture; skin incisions may be left open or closed by

How surgeons actually tension — Borazjani et al. (2017) survey
 • 596 surgeons from 56 countries (roughly two-thirds gynaecologists, one-third urologists), more than
   30% performing over 50 slings annually.
 • Objective intraoperative tension assessment is uncommon: intraoperative stress test used by a small
   minority, cystoscopic evaluation of urethral coaptation by fewer, and the cotton-tipped applicator test by

   transobturator, p = 0.15).
 • AUA SUI Guideline, grade A evidence, Conditional Recommendation: in index patients selecting MUS
   surgery, physicians may offer EITHER retropubic or transobturator MUS.
Inside-out versus outside-in transobturator
  • Short- and medium-term subjective cure are similar.
 • Voiding dysfunction is MORE frequent inside-out; vaginal perforation is LESS frequent inside-out.
 • Either approach is acceptable, based on surgeon experience.

Outcomes for Single-Incision Mid-Urethral Sling
 • First FDA approval 2006: TVT-Secur (Gynecare, Ethicon).
Neuman (2008) — the learning-curve study
 • 100 consecutive hammock-style SIMS, comparing the first 50 with the last 50 at 12 months.
 • Objective failure fell from 20% to 8%, achieved by placing the sling CLOSER TO THE URETHRA.
 • Vaginal perforation with the inserter occurred in the first group and was eliminated in the second by
   WIDENING THE SUBMUCOSAL TUNNEL.
 • Mesh exposure fell from 12% to 8% by creating DEEPER submucosal tunnels.
 • No bladder or urethral perforation, UTI or wound infection; one self-remitting paravesical haematoma.
Dmochowski et al. (2009) — one of the largest SIMS trials
 • 642 patients across 29 sites in 8 countries, effectiveness by standing cough stress test and the
   Incontinence Quality-of-Life instrument (I-QOL).
 • Sling position: about two-thirds hammock, one-third U-position. Two-thirds pure SUI, one-third stress-
   predominant MUI.
 • Intraoperative complications were rare: bladder perforation and bleeding over 200 mL each well under
   1%.
 • Postoperative retention, UTI, voiding dysfunction, de novo urgency and mesh erosion were all low

 • Quality-of-life gains at 3 months were sustained at 12 months; objective continence 89% at 6 months

Cochrane Systematic Review (Nambiar et al., 2017)
 • 31 randomised or quasi-randomised trials, 3,290 females, comparing SIMS with other anti-incontinence
   procedures.
 • SIMS devices included: TVT-Secur, MiniArc, Ajust, Needleless, Ophira, Tissue Fixation System,
   CureMesh.
 • No studies compared SIMS against no treatment, conservative treatment, colposuspension,
   laparoscopic procedures, or traditional PVS.
 • Findings against SIMS: more persistent or recurrent incontinence than retropubic MUS (41% vs 26%);
   higher risk of de novo urgency; higher incontinence than INSIDE-OUT transobturator MUS (30% vs
   11%); worse adverse event profile, with higher vaginal exposure and markedly higher bladder and/or
   urethral erosion.
 • Findings favouring SIMS: less postoperative pain, with marginally lower long-term pain of questionable
   clinical significance.
 • The decisive subanalysis: the excess incontinence was principally attributable to TVT-Secur, which has
   since been WITHDRAWN from the market.
 • With TVT-Secur removed, evidence was INSUFFICIENT to show any difference in incontinence between
   other SIMS and inside-out or outside-in transobturator MUS, and insufficient to demonstrate a
   difference in adverse events. The only significant differences remaining were lower postoperative and
   long-term pain, and shorter operating time, marginally favouring SIMS.
 • Comparing one SIMS against another: insufficient evidence of any difference between products.
Post-Cochrane meta-analyses
 • SIMS appears NON-INFERIOR to transobturator MUS on both objective and subjective criteria, at short-
   and medium-term follow-up.
 • Limited data suggest retropubic MUS outcomes are BETTER than SIMS. Adverse events after SIMS are
   low.
 • As with other slings, SIMS efficacy may decline with longer follow-up: Han et al. (2012) found subjective
   cure after TVT-Secur fell significantly between years 1 and 3 (85.4% to 72.9%).
 • Masata et al. (2012), randomising SIMS against transobturator MUS at 2 years: SIMS had significantly
   lower objective (69% vs 93%) and subjective (75% vs 90%) cure.
 • AUA SUI guidelines, grade B evidence, Conditional Recommendation: physicians may offer SIMS to
   index patients undergoing MUS surgery.

 • Cure is typically higher in pure SUI than in MUI, but MUS is an effective treatment where urgency or

 • Over half of patients with preoperative MUI experience improvement or resolution of urgency or UUI.
 • Urge-predominant MUI is more likely to require COMBINATION therapy addressing both stress and
   urge components.
 • De novo urgency and UUI may develop after ANY sling procedure.

Outcomes of Mid-Urethral Sling for Intrinsic Sphincter Deficiency
 • ISD is an important risk factor for failure of conventional anti-incontinence procedures, but the LACK OF
   A UNIVERSALLY ACCEPTED DEFINITION hinders evaluation of surgical efficacy.
 • Urodynamic definition as before: ALPP <60 cm H2O or MUCP <20 cm H2O.

Evidence that ISD predicts failure
 • Lo et al. (2022), in over 600 MUS patients of whom 8% had urodynamic ISD: at one year, ISD patients had
   markedly lower objective and subjective cure (around 50%) versus overall cure of around 86–88%. Sling
   type made no difference, and on multivariate analysis reduced urethral mobility and lower MUCP were
   associated with higher odds of failure.

 • Paick et al. (2004): cure was significantly lower with VLPP under 60 cm H2O than with higher VLPP

The counter-argument — mobility, not pressure
 • Several authors hold that LOSS OF URETHRAL MOBILITY, rather than low ALPP or MUCP, is the true risk
   factor for failure.
 • Therefore low leak point pressures are NOT in themselves a contraindication to retropubic MUS; in the
   MUS era, leak point pressures should be correlated with the physical examination and used to counsel
   about reduced chance of success.
 • Success is clearly lower in those with BOTH a fixed urethra and low leak point pressures.
 • A preoperatively FIXED URETHRA is more predictive of no cure than a low leak point pressure.
Which sling in ISD
 • Ample evidence supports retropubic MUS in ISD.
 • Evidence also exists for non-inferior transobturator MUS outcomes in ISD — but O'Connor et al. (2006)
   found the odds of continued SUI after transobturator MUS were TWELVE TIMES greater with low VLPP.
 • Comparative studies and meta-analyses indicate RETROPUBIC MUS IS MORE EFFECTIVE THAN
   TRANSOBTURATOR MUS IN FEMALES WITH ISD.
 • TOMUS, though not designed for this question, performed preoperative urodynamics on all patients
   and found VLPP and MUCP had NO effect on sling outcomes — but urethral mobility was not recorded,
   which is precisely the omitted variable.
 • Limited evidence suggests SIMS performs similarly to transobturator MUS in ISD (Hwang et al., 2022).
 • MUS is beneficial in SUI with ISD PROVIDED there is preoperative urethral mobility, and absolute cure
   and improvement rates in ISD fall within the range reported for other procedures.
 • AUA/SUFU Expert Opinion: in patients with SUI and a FIXED, IMMOBILE urethra who wish treatment,
   physicians may offer retropubic MUS, PVS, bulking, or adjustable retropubic MUS.

Outcomes of Mid-Urethral Sling for Recurrent Stress Urinary Incontinence
 • Recurrent or persistent SUI after failed anti-incontinence surgery is a genuine dilemma: there is NO
   level 1 evidence defining the best next step after a failed MUS.
 • A Cochrane review concluded there were insufficient data to assess the effects of ANY management
   strategy (Bakali et al., 2019).
 • No published paper reports exclusively on females whose FIRST operation was a MUS — the population
   of interest has never been studied in isolation.
 • British Society of Urogynaecologists database review of 2,938 records: repeat MUS is by far the
   commonest procedure performed (77.3%) (El-Hamamsy and Tincello, 2021).
 • Comparison across studies is hampered by differing definitions of cure and improvement, differing
   outcome criteria, and short follow-up; many series are further biased by including patients in whom
   bulking agents had failed.
Choice of procedure
 • Retropubic MUS performs BETTER than transobturator MUS for recurrent SUI.
 • Sabadell et al. (2020): retropubic MUS after transobturator MUS failure gave a cured or improved rate
   of 67% at 10 years.
 • Abdel-Fattah et al. (2017b): transobturator MUS for recurrent SUI gave 62% success by Patient Global
   Impression of Improvement at 9 years, with clinically significant quality-of-life improvement in 84%.

 • Stav et al. (2010a): repeat MUS was significantly LESS effective at curing incontinence than a primary

Outcomes of Mid-Urethral Sling in Patients With Pelvic Organ Prolapse
The three possible continence states in prolapse
 • Overt SUI — subjective and objective, symptomatic.
 • Occult SUI — demonstrated only with POP reduction.
 • Neither.
 • Critical caveat: absence of symptomatic, overt or occult SUI may mean there is no SUI — or may simply
   reflect the LIMITATION of demonstrating SUI with prolapse reduction. Rarely, the patient may
   misinterpret SUI as UUI.
The concurrent-versus-staged dilemma
 • Concurrent sling: reduces postoperative symptomatic SUI, but increases sling-related complications —
   obstruction, de novo or worsened storage LUTS, and mesh-related adverse events.
 • Staged sling: treats SUI only if it develops and minimises sling complications during the POP repair —
   but commits the patient to a second operation if SUI does occur.
 • Giugale et al. (2019): preoperative SUI RESOLVED in about 30% after POP repair alone; about half were
   treated for persistent SUI, and about a third underwent a staged MUS, most within 12 months.
Evidence supporting a concomitant sling for symptomatic or occult SUI
 • Baessler et al. (2018), meta-analysis of 19 randomised trials and 2,717 females: a concomitant MUS
   probably improves postoperative subjective SUI rates and probably decreases the need for further
   continence surgery. Illustratively: if the risk of SUI after POP surgery alone is 39%, the risk with a MUS
   falls to between 8% and 19%.

Theoretical advantages and risks of a MUS at prolapse repair
 • Advantage over autologous PVS or retropubic suspension: reduced operative time and blood loss from
   the sling portion.
 • Increased dissection increases graft exposure → greater infection, perforation or vaginal exposure.
 • Increased blood loss or anatomical distortion from the POP procedure → sling migration, voiding
   dysfunction, obstruction.
 • Appropriate TENSIONING is challenging in occult SUI, where the leak is only demonstrable with the
   prolapse reduced.
Outcomes in practice
 • Studies are mostly small and short-term, but MUS can be added to POP surgery with minimal morbidity
   and high success (73–93%) — and the same applies to MUS for occult SUI.
 • Meltomaa et al. (2004), at three years by questionnaire: no statistical difference in SUI cure or urgency
   between MUS alone and MUS combined with other vaginal surgery. Transient retention was commoner
   after combined surgery, but urethrolysis rates were low and not statistically different.
 • Bakas et al. (2018) at 13 years: anterior colporrhaphy cured POP in 92%, and cured cystocele with
   urodynamic SUI in 84% when combined with TVT-O; TVT-O alone gave objective cure of 81%.
 • AUA/SUFU Conditional Recommendation, grade C evidence: in patients undergoing concomitant
   surgery for POP and SUI, physicians may perform ANY of the incontinence procedures — MUS, PVS, or
   Burch colposuspension.
The "prophylactic" sling — prolapse with NO symptomatic, overt or occult SUI
 • Stewart et al. (2021), comparing MUS for documented SUI, prophylactic MUS, and no MUS: no significant
   difference in postoperative retention in those without a sling; persistent SUI in 7% of the documented-
   SUI group, de novo SUI in 5% of the prophylactic group, and de novo SUI in 12% of the no-MUS group.
 • Lo et al. (2019), hysterectomy and transvaginal mesh with MUS versus POP repair without MUS: de novo

 • Matsuoka et al. (2015), systematic review of seven randomised trials: an anti-incontinence procedure at

Outcomes of Mid-Urethral Sling in Elderly Females
Why age matters
 • Higher rates of DO, UUI, ISD, and emptying abnormalities from impaired contractility.
 • More likely to have had prior incontinence procedures → higher rates of URETHRAL FIXATION, itself a
   predictor of sling failure.
 • Long-standing oestrogen deficiency → greater risk of poor or delayed healing and mesh exposure.
 • Greater anaesthetic risk from medical comorbidity, affecting the surgery, its outcome and the
   postoperative course.
Gordon et al. (2005) — 460 consecutive MUS, about a third aged over 70
 • Significantly FEWER bladder trocar injuries in the elderly.
 • Postoperative fever, UTI, wound infection and haematoma were similar between groups.
 • More perioperative medical morbidity in the elderly — pulmonary embolism, cardiac arrhythmia, DVT
   and pneumonia — against a single arrhythmia in the younger group.
 • NO increased risk of vaginal exposure. Voiding dysfunction requiring catheterisation beyond one week
   was low and similar in both groups.
 • Cure rates essentially identical: 93% elderly versus 94% younger. De novo UUI was significantly greater
   in the older patients (18% vs 4%).
Other de novo urgency data
 • Sevestre et al. (2003): a similar rate of de novo UUI (around 18%) in elderly patients.
 • Allahdin et al. (2004): postoperative urgency in as many as 60% of older females, of whom 44%
   developed it de novo — the highest figures reported.
 • Athanasiou et al. (2020), 72 patients aged over 75, mean follow-up 13.7 years after TVT-O: objective cure
   80.5%, subjective 84.7%, patient-reported success 91.7%. De novo urgency 23.7%; aggravation of pre-
   existing urgency in a further 26.5% — distinct endpoints, both to be counted.
Urethral mobility as the decisive variable — Liapis et al. (2006)
 • Cotton-tipped applicator displacement <30 degrees: only 42% became continent.
 • Displacement >30 degrees: 90% continent.
 • Angle <10 degrees: 80% remained INCONTINENT.
Cure data and health burden
 • Jha et al. (2009): age did NOT significantly affect SUI reduction or quality-of-life improvement after
   retropubic MUS.
 • Postoperative voiding dysfunction and de novo urgency do appear commoner in older females, and
   may affect them more significantly.

 • Emphasis has shifted from chronological age to FRAILTY as the risk factor for surgical complications. Full

 • Persistent SUI after retropubic or transobturator MUS is higher in the elderly, potentially due to

 • Despite some initial increase in perioperative morbidity, MUS surgery IS safe in the elderly.
 • The literature is insufficient to support one anti-incontinence procedure over another in this group.
 • Elderly patients should NOT be excluded from potentially curative MUS surgery on the basis of age

Outcomes of Mid-Urethral Sling in Obese Patients
 • The prevailing link between obesity and SUI is INCREASED INTRA-ABDOMINAL PRESSURE. Neurological
   and metabolic mechanisms have also been proposed, supporting a multifactorial pathophysiology.
 • Both retropubic and transobturator MUS achieve success exceeding 80% in obese females, varying with
   the definition of cure.
 • Success is nonetheless LOWER than in the non-obese, and several long-term observational studies
   identify OBESITY AS A RISK FACTOR FOR TVT FAILURE. Brennand et al. (2017), 5-year secondary analysis
   of a randomised trial: objective cure by 1-hour pad test 87.4% non-obese versus 65.9% obese (p =
   0.003); subjective cure 76.7% versus 53.6% (p = 0.002).
 • SIMS data in obesity are CONFLICTING — one study found worse outcomes in the obese, another did
   not. Both are methodologically compromised: the negative study used TVT-Secur in nearly half its
   patients, and the positive study used a BMI cut-off of 25 kg/m², thereby grouping overweight women
   with the obese.

The regulatory context
 • Over the past decade there has been a 27% increase in the rate of surgery for SUI, largely attributable
   to the growing popularity of the MUS (Jonsson Funk et al., 2012).
 • 2010: the FDA released a revised statement alerting the public to safety concerns about synthetic mesh
   for POP and SUI surgery.
 • The FDA became aware of the problem through the Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience
   (MAUDE) database.
 • MAUDE data, 2008–2010: 1,371 voluntary and involuntary self-reported medical device reports of
   MUS-related complications, a significant proportion being mesh perforation and exposure.
 • Full discussion of mesh complications, and of the regulatory and legal issues, is in Chapter 119.

Mid-Urethral Sling Trocar Injury to the Urinary Tract
 • Trocar injury = passage of the trocar into the urethra or bladder at the time of sling placement. This is
   DISTINCT from perforation and exposure, which are late events discussed in Chapter 119.
 • Postoperative quality of life may be affected as much by voiding difficulties, UTIs and other adverse
   events as by cure or improvement of SUI.
 • Causes: a combination of patient and technical factors — body habitus, previous pelvic or vaginal
   surgery, iatrogenic injury, and surgeon technical error.
Incidence
  • Early national registries soon after TVT introduction: bladder trocar injury 2.7% (Finland) and 3.8%
    (Austria) — consistent with current literature.
 • Crane et al. (2023), 1,500 MUS from a single centre: 2.3% trocar puncture rate, ALL in retropubic cases.
   At 34 months, no significant difference in de novo storage or emptying symptoms between punctured

    and non-punctured patients, and cystoscopy in the punctured group showed NO bladder mesh

 • Ford et al. (2017): bladder puncture 4.5% retropubic versus 0.6% transobturator — the rate is lower
   with transobturator because of the direction of trocar passage.
 • Within the transobturator approach, injuries may be commoner with the OUTSIDE-IN route (Tamussino
   et al., 2007).
Management — the critical asymmetry
 • BLADDER trocar injury is generally BENIGN. No study has linked trocar injury to perioperative
   haemorrhage, haematoma, or subsequent mesh perforation of bladder or urethra.
 • Once recognised intraoperatively: re-pass the trocar safely with cystoscopic confirmation, and
   CONTINUE the case. An indwelling catheter may be left overnight or up to 72 hours, though some
   describe removal in the recovery room with no untoward sequelae.
 • URETHRAL trocar injury is a CONTRAINDICATION to continuing MUS surgery. AUA/SUFU Clinical
   Principle: physicians should NOT place a mesh sling if the urethra is inadvertently injured at the time of
   planned MUS.
 • Re-evaluate for STAGED anti-incontinence surgery after catheter drainage and complete healing of the
   urethral puncture.

Other Trocar Injuries and Bleeding After Mid-Urethral Sling
 • Serious injuries are extremely rare: the Finnish registry reported serious vascular complications in
   0.07%, the Austrian registry bowel perforation in 0.04%.
 • Deng et al. (2007), reviewing MAUDE: of 928 reported complications, 161 (17.3%) were considered
   MAJOR — including vascular injuries, bowel injuries, and 10 deaths.
 • Bleeding is inconsistently reported. Large registries and randomised trials: severe bleeding or
   haematoma in approximately 2–3%, usually manageable with observation or local compression.
 • Lin et al. (2021), systematic review of 55 studies: haemorrhage rates of about 1.6% retropubic and 1.5%
   transobturator.
 • Bleeding complications occur more commonly after RETROPUBIC MUS: Flock et al. (2004) reported
   haemorrhage of ≥300 mL requiring surgical intervention in about 4%.
 • Tseng et al. (2005), performing ultrasonography on every patient after MUS: about an eighth had
   significant retropubic haematomas over 5 cm on the first postoperative day; only one required
   evacuation, and all others had resolved by one month.
 • The important conceptual point: a postoperative haematoma is a relatively COMMON and
   ASYMPTOMATIC event after trocar passage — its detection does not mandate intervention.

Urinary Tract Infections
 • UTI is arguably the commonest complication after MUS surgery.
 • Bonus et al. (2022), national surgical quality analysis of 16,491 MUS: UTI was the commonest
   complication (3.4%).
 • Dallas et al. (2017), from Californian statewide data, 28,635 outpatient MUS as a sole procedure: within
   30 days, 5.7% had at least one unplanned hospital visit; among emergency department diagnoses, UTI
   was second only to urinary retention and catheter problems.
 • Berger et al. (2022), 13,404 MUS procedures from a large managed care organisation: in the first 12
   postoperative months, 23% developed a UTI and 4% developed de novo recurrent UTI.

 • Timing: UTIs were most frequently diagnosed in the FIRST MONTH, with the SEVENTH POSTOPERATIVE

 • Vigil et al. (2017), reviewing 9,022 MUS procedures: age over 65, BMI over 40 kg/m², and hospital

Groin and Thigh Pain After Mid-Urethral Sling
 • Groin and thigh pain are associated with the TRANSOBTURATOR approach; suprapubic pain with the
   RETROPUBIC approach.
 • Laurikainen et al. (2007), Finnish randomised trial: groin pain in 16% after inside-out transobturator
   versus 1.5% after retropubic MUS.
 • Teo et al. (2011): a randomised trial comparing the two routes was ABORTED because of a 26%
   incidence of leg pain in the transobturator arm (versus 1.7% retropubic).
 • Ford et al. (2017), from 18 trials and 3,221 females: pooled groin pain after transobturator MUS 6.4%.
   Groin pain may also PERSIST longer after transobturator MUS.
 • Doo et al. (2006): persistent suprapubic pain in about 2% at 5 years after retropubic MUS.
 • The majority of groin pain is TEMPORARY. De Leval (2003): the symptom, present in 16%, resolved after
   the SECOND postoperative day.
 • Krauth et al. (2005): most affected patients responded to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.
   Proposed mechanisms: subclinical haematoma, or a transient neuropathic phenomenon.
 • Lim et al. (2006): early groin pain in about a quarter after transobturator MUS, but persistent groin pain
   at 12 months in only 3.7% after conservative management — the trajectory to counsel.
 • Roth (2007) and Duckett and Jain (2005): steroids and local anaesthetic effective, without side effects,
   for persistent groin pain at 3 months. Rest and physical therapy may also help.
 • The extreme case: Wolter et al. (2008) reported refractory medial thigh pain requiring medial thigh and
   transobturator EXPLORATION by orthopaedic surgery, with sling excision.

Wound Infections After Mid-Urethral Sling
 • Wound-related complications range from minor superficial cutaneous infection to pelvic abscess.
 • Ward and Hilton (2002): vaginal wound infection in 2% after retropubic MUS. Richter et al. (2010): 0.7%
   in BOTH retropubic and transobturator arms.
 • Rare cases of NECROTISING FASCIITIS have been reported, associated with obesity, hypertension and
   diabetes.
 • Severe infection is rare, but diagnosis requires a HIGH INDEX OF SUSPICION and may be DELAYED BY UP
   TO SEVERAL YEARS. It may be associated with mesh exposure; treatment is discussed in Chapter 119.
 • Abscess and ADDUCTOR MYOSITIS have been reported, presenting as leg pain, difficulty ambulating,
   and cellulitis.
Mesh excision for chronic pain and severe infection
 • Where chronic pain or severe infection follows a mesh MUS and non-operative therapy fails, complete
   mesh excision FROM BOTH SIDES OF THE BONE may be necessary.

Emptying Dysfunction After Mid-Urethral Sling
 • Voiding symptoms — slow stream, hesitancy, intermittency, frank retention — are typically the result of
   BOO from the sling being placed too TIGHTLY, in the WRONG LOCATION (too PROXIMALLY), or
   associated with POP that was unrecognised preoperatively or arose de novo.
 • Some patients have voiding dysfunction WITHOUT evidence of obstruction.
 • Originally all slings were thought to carry a similar risk; it is now believed the RETROPUBIC MUS confers
   a HIGHER risk.
 • TOMUS: voiding dysfunction necessitating surgery or permanent catheterisation 2.7% retropubic versus
   0% transobturator. Urinary retention, defined as catheterisation beyond 6 weeks, was also higher
   retropubic (3.7% vs 0.7%).
 • Ford et al. (2017), from 37 trials and 6,200 females: postoperative voiding dysfunction significantly LESS
   frequent after transobturator MUS.
 • Small studies have associated postoperative voiding dysfunction with preoperative Qmax and PdetQmax,
   addition of other pelvic surgery, increasing age, DECREASING BMI, postoperative UTI, and previous anti-
   incontinence surgery — but there is NO consensus on preoperative factors predicting voiding
   dysfunction after MUS.
Evaluation
 • The optimal evaluation is poorly defined in the literature.
 • The decision to perform urethrolysis or sling incision rests on a CLEAR TEMPORAL RELATIONSHIP
   between symptom onset and the operation.
 • Careful pelvic examination must exclude progression of ANTERIOR AND APICAL POP, which can render
   a non-obstructive sling obstructive TEN YEARS OR MORE after placement.
 • Urodynamics are useful in selected cases at the physician's discretion — but NOT all patients will show
   obstruction urodynamically.
 • Cystoscopy is useful to exclude bladder pathology, urethral mesh perforation, and a hypersuspended
   bladder neck.
Management — conservative first
 • Obstruction after MUS is FREQUENTLY TRANSIENT and can be managed with short-term CIC or
   continuous catheter drainage.
 • If voiding volitionally but inefficiently: timed voiding, biofeedback, PFMT and selective medical therapy
   have all had some success.
 • Finnish database (Kuuva and Nilsson, 2002, 2003): of 34 patients with urinary retention, most (20)
   resumed normal voiding after fewer than 14 days of conservative management; two took a further 5
   and 6 weeks; only ONE required midline sling lysis, after which voiding was normal. Separately, 111
   patients had voiding dysfunction WITHOUT retention: in 13 it lasted up to 4 months, and only 2
   required surgical transection of the sling.
 • Long-term retention after retropubic MUS is RARE; incision, revision or removal of the sling usually
   improves symptoms.

Sling incision — technique and timing
 • Within the first 3 months: midline division of the MUS through a SINGLE VERTICAL VAGINAL INCISION is
    the preferred method for persistent obstructive voiding dysfunction.
 • After 3 months the sling is fixed along its ENTIRE COURSE, and midline incision may not achieve enough
   relaxation to resolve the dysfunction. More extensive sling excision is then required, including the
   LATERAL portion — extending to the inferior portion of the pubic symphysis for retropubic MUS, and to
   the ischiopubic ramus for transobturator MUS.
 • Rarely, a full urethrolysis as described for the autologous BNS is required.
 • Author's observation from hundreds of revisions: OBSTRUCTIVE SLINGS ARE MOST OFTEN FOUND
   CLOSER TO THE BLADDER NECK THAN THE MID-URETHRA — malposition, too proximal, is the usual
   finding.
 • Most studies report 70–100% remain continent after sling lysis, though one large series reported only
   50%.
 • Klutke et al. (2001), from 600 retropubic MUS: 2.8% required reoperation for retention and persistent
   obstructive symptoms, performed at a mean of 64 days. The sling was either released with 1 cm of
   downward traction or cut in the midline. One urethral injury, repaired without sequelae. Symptoms
   resolved in ALL patients, all voided to completion, and nearly all remained continent.
 • Most authors recommend waiting AT LEAST 2 WEEKS before sling incision for urethral obstruction;
   although one series reported return to normal voiding after 6 weeks of conservative management, the
   majority of studies do NOT support waiting beyond 4 weeks.
 • Pinsard et al. (2023), comparing early loosening with delayed section or excision in patients with PVR

   different, but at median 9 months significantly MORE patients had resolved voiding dysfunction in the
   EARLY group (63.2% vs 31.3%, p = 0.01), and persistent or recurrent SUI was HIGHER in the DELAYED
   group (21% vs 43.7%, p = 0.04). On multivariate analysis, the main predictive factor for recurrent SUI
   was DELAYED SECTION.
 • Author's position: the sling should be incised WITHIN 4 WEEKS in cases of significant obstruction or
   retention. Author's clinical observation: patients who return to "normal voiding" with conservative
   management often retain persistent urgency and frequency.

Storage Symptoms After Mid-Urethral Sling
 • Storage symptoms — urgency, frequency, UUI — are common after MUS, either as persistent or
   worsened symptoms in preoperative MUI, or de novo after surgery for pure SUI.
 • Pergialiotis et al. (2017), systematic review of 32 studies and 3,139 patients across all three MUS types:
   de novo OAB in 11.5% of non-randomised studies and 6.4% of randomised studies. By device the rates
   are strikingly similar: SIMS 9.7%, inside-out transobturator 11.2%, outside-in transobturator 8.7%, TVT
   9.8% — no route protects against de novo OAB.
 • A broader literature review estimates de novo OAB syndrome at 15–30% (Marcelissen and Van
   Kerrebroeck, 2018).

 KEY POINTS: MID-URETHRAL SLING STORAGE AND EMPTYING DYSFUNCTION
   • Long-term urinary retention and obstructive voiding dysfunction are both RARE after MUS.
   • Definitions of retention and indications for management vary, but MOST patients may be managed
     conservatively initially.
   • URETHRAL DILATION IS POTENTIALLY HARMFUL AND SHOULD NOT BE PERFORMED.
   • For persistently elevated PVR with bothersome symptoms refractory to conservative management,
     TRANSVAGINAL SLING RELEASE consistently resolves symptoms while MAINTAINING CONTINENCE in
     the majority. Recommended waiting period: at least 2 to 4 weeks.
   • Sling release should be attempted through a SMALL MIDLINE VAGINAL INCISION using MINIMAL
     DISSECTION. Patients obstructed longer than 3 months may require more extensive excision.
   • Worsened or de novo storage LUTS should raise STRONG SUSPICION OF PARTIAL BOO. Treatment
     mirrors that for the autologous BNS, but outcomes of pharmacological and surgical treatment of de
     novo storage LUTS are limited.

ADJUSTABLE SLINGS
The Remeex readjustable sling — device description
 • A short suburethral polypropylene mesh sling with non-absorbable suspension sutures attached to a
   tensioning device called a VARITENSOR.
 • The mesh is placed under the mid-urethra as in a retropubic MUS.
 • The varitensor is a SCREW attached to a ROTATING REEL, implanted ABOVE THE RECTUS FASCIA through
   a small suprapubic incision.
 • Postoperatively the screw is accessed PERCUTANEOUSLY with a MANIPULATOR functioning like a
   screwdriver; turning it tightens suburethral sling tension, allowing INDIVIDUALISED tensioning.
 • The manipulator is then withdrawn, leaving the varitensor in the subcutaneous tissue over the fascia
   and under the skin.
 • Proposed indication: COMPLEX SUI — ISD, fixed or hypomobile urethra, and reoperations — precisely
   the population in which conventional MUS performs worst.
Outcomes
 • Giberti et al. (2011), in predominantly ISD patients at 5 years mean follow-up, cure defined as dry on
   cough stress test with pad weight of 0–1 g: cure in about 86%, with substantial reduction in mean pad
   weight. Readjustment of tension was needed in a small minority, and persistent retention, seroma and
   de novo urgency each occurred infrequently.
 • Giberti et al. (2017) at 7 years mean follow-up: cure in 90%, with readjustment again needed in a small
   minority; main adverse events were bacterial cystitis, de novo urgency, persistent retention and
   seroma.
 • Ko et al. (2017), in patients with SUI and concomitant DETRUSOR UNDERACTIVITY: treatment success in
   about four-fifths — but despite being dry, a quarter wanted the Remeex system REMOVED because of
   persistent postoperative retention within a year. Dryness is not the same as satisfaction in this group.
 • Ros et al. (2022), in 120 females: subjective success 70%, defined as improvement by one or more
   severity levels on the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire — Urinary Incontinence
   Short Form (ICIQ-UI-SF). Greater BMI and greater presurgical 24-hour pad weight predicted WORSE
   results.
 • Kim et al. (2019c), by contrast: subjective success above 90% in normal-weight, overweight and obese
   groups alike, with high and statistically indistinguishable satisfaction.
Complications and position
 • Overall complication rates 32–51%, but MOST are Clavien class II and III.
 • Bladder injury 6.7–14%. De novo OAB 7.8–12.3%. Readjustment rates vary very widely, from about 14%
   to 43%.
