CLASSIFICATION AND ANATOMIC DESCRIPTION

General Patterns
 • Ureteral developmental abnormalities — ectopic ureters and ureteroceles — are a large component of
   clinical urology and continue to challenge management despite wide recognition and well-defined
   surgical strategies.
 • The difficulty arises from a wide spectrum of clinical significance and a variable pattern of presentation,
   which is why a working grasp of normal and abnormal lower urinary tract embryology is required rather
   than a list of operations.
 • Ectopic ureter and ureterocele are taught as distinct entities but share many features and probably share
   underlying developmental mechanisms, with slight variations producing the obvious differences in
   appearance.
 • Management is approached similarly with only slight variation; clinical cases exist that lie between the
   two entities, suggesting a developmental continuum rather than two separate diseases.
Concept — The two entities are best treated as one developmental defect of ureterotrigonal connection
expressed along a continuum; the differences in appearance dictate the technical variation in management,
not the strategy.

Ectopic Ureter
 • Definition: any ureter, single or duplex, that does not enter the trigonal area of the bladder.
 • A ureter merely near the bladder neck is not technically ectopic — ectopia requires entry at or distal to
   the bladder neck.
 • In a duplex system the ectopic ureter is inevitably the upper pole ureter, because it buds later and more
   cephalad from the mesonephric duct than the lower pole, with later incorporation into the developing
   urogenital sinus.
 • Association with a Gartner duct cyst — the remnant of the wolffian duct from which the ureter buds,
   which may itself show cystic dilation. The duct runs parallel alongside the vagina (the müllerian
   structure); rupture of the cystic duct into the vagina establishes communication. This is the anatomical
   basis of incontinence in the female.
 • Male presentation therefore never includes incontinence; instead hydronephrosis, flank pain, urinary
   tract infection (UTI), or epididymitis/orchitis.
 • Zinner syndrome = ectopic ureter with ipsilateral renal absence and seminal vesicle cyst. A retrograde
   injection study may demonstrate the ureterocele or ureter communicating with the seminal vesicle and
   vas deferens, the two structures joining just above the seminal vesicles.
 • Single-system ectopic ureters and ureteroceles may mimic the ectopic upper pole but may also present
   with an apparently absent kidney — usually a failure of ultrasound to identify a small, poorly functioning
   unit, prompting computed tomography (CT) with contrast or renal function studies.
 • Either single or duplex systems may show severe hydroureteronephrosis reflecting distal obstruction;
   obstruction may be severe enough to have impaired renal development to the point of non-function.
 • Bilateral single-system ectopic ureters (rare) are associated with a hypoplastic bladder, poorly developed
   trigone and bladder neck, and bilateral renal dysplasia.

 KEY POINTS: ECTOPIC URETER
   • An ectopic ureter is any ureter, single or duplex, that does not enter the trigonal area of the bladder.
   • In a duplex system it is inevitably the upper pole ureter, because budding from the mesonephric duct
     occurs later and more cephalad than the lower pole bud.
   • In females it may enter anywhere from the bladder neck to the perineum, and into the vagina, uterus
     and even rectum; continuous wetting is a classic symptom.
   • In males it always enters above the external sphincter or pelvic floor, usually into wolffian structures;
     the presentation does not include incontinence.


Ureterocele
 • Definition: a version of the ectopic ureter with cystic dilation of the distal ureter, sited either wholly
   within the bladder or spanning the bladder neck and urethra.
 • As with ectopic ureter, may be single or duplex; in duplex systems always the upper pole.
 • Ureteroceles may extend into the urethra but never form entirely within it, and never attach to wolffian
   ductal structures — the point of separation from the ectopic ureter.
 • The most clinically useful classification separates intravesical from extravesical (ectopic) ureteroceles.

Intravesical Ureterocele
 • Entirely within the bladder and above the bladder neck.
 • Includes the so-called "simple" ureterocele of the adult with mild or no upper tract dilation; the term
   "simple" should be discouraged in favour of "single intravesical ureterocele".

 • Intravesical ureteroceles can still obstruct, producing severe hydronephrosis and clinically significant
   sequelae.

Ectopic (Extravesical) Ureterocele
 • Some portion is permanently situated at the bladder neck or urethra. The orifice may be in the bladder,
   at the bladder neck, or in the urethra.
 • This must be distinguished from an intravesical ureterocele that merely prolapses into the urethra with
   voiding.

Morphological Variants and Functional Grading
 • Stephens classification: cecoureterocele, stenotic, sphincteric, sphincterostenotic, blind, and non-
   obstructed.
 • Cecoureterocele — the critical subgroup: the orifice lies within the bladder, but the ureterocele cavity
   extends submucosally beyond the bladder neck into the urethra, as a long tongue beneath the ureteral
   submucosa; the orifice communicating with the bladder lumen is large and incompetent. Often not
   characterised preoperatively, and its complexity creates surgical difficulty, particularly with endoscopic
   incision.
 • Ureterocele disproportion (non-obstructive ureterocele with duplication): duplex kidney in which the
   upper pole is non-dilated and so dysplastic that it is not detected on most imaging, with no ureteric
   dilation beyond the bladder — a typical-appearing ureterocele in the bladder with an apparently normal
   ipsilateral kidney.
 • Churchill functional classification grades by impact on the upper urinary tract across all renal units:
   upper pole alone in jeopardy → entire ipsilateral kidney threatened → contralateral system also at risk
   through reflux or bladder outlet obstruction (BOO). The last group comprised roughly a quarter of the
   reported cases.
 • Ureterocele type shows some association with therapeutic outcome, but the predictions have not
   proved as reliable as first reported.

Concept — The ureterocele is the same distal ureteric maldevelopment with a retained, distensible distal
segment; the defining questions in both conditions are always the same — where is the orifice relative to the
bladder neck, and what does the distal segment do to the bladder outlet.

 KEY POINTS: URETEROCELE
   • A ureterocele is a version of the ectopic ureter with cystic dilation of the distal ureter, located within
     the bladder or spanning the bladder neck and urethra.
   • Several classification systems exist, but the most useful distinction for clinical practice separates
     intravesical from extravesical ureteroceles.
   • In a cecoureterocele the orifice of the affected ureter is within the bladder, but the cavity extends
     submucosally beyond the bladder neck into the urethra.


Embryology and Aetiology
 • The specific mechanisms remain undefined, but understanding of normal and abnormal ureterotrigonal
   development is the likely source of insight.
 • Pathogenesis: ureteral ectopia, with or without ureterocele, produces renal maldevelopment through
   defective ureterotrigonal connections — the renal lesion is a consequence of the connection defect, not
   a separate event.
 • A growing number of genetic pathways critical to distal ureter connection have been identified in
   humans and rodents; mouse models have clarified how proper distal connections are generated.
 • Classic model: the common nephric duct (CND) forms the trigone.
 • Revised model: the CND regresses by apoptosis, and the trigone forms from endoderm, like the bladder.
 • Chwalle's classic explanation of ureterocele formation: distal ureteral expansion results from failure of
   rupture of a distal membrane at the ureteral orifice — the Chwalle membrane.
 • Because the ureteral bud derives from the wolffian duct, the ectopic ureter does not insert directly into
   müllerian structures (vagina, cervix, uterus) but is associated with them through the Gartner duct, the
   wolffian remnant running alongside the mature müllerian structures.

 • Weigert-Meyer rule: the inverse relationship of duplex ureteral orifices — the ectopic or ureterocele-
   bearing upper pole orifice is caudal to the lower pole orifice.
Concept — Two mechanisms generate the whole chapter: a mis-timed, mis-sited ureteral bud, which fixes
the orifice position and therefore the Weigert-Meyer inversion and every ectopic site, and a failure of distal
orifice canalisation, which fixes the ureterocele. Renal dysplasia is downstream of both, not parallel to them.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

Imaging
Prenatal Detection
 • The majority of ureteroceles and ectopic ureters are detected by prenatal ultrasound, often only
   through the identification of hydronephrosis; the specific diagnosis is frequently not made until after
   birth.
 • Prenatal patterns are identical to postnatal ultrasound patterns but more easily misinterpreted.
 • A duplex system is difficult to identify unless one moiety is dilated.
 • An upper pole "cyst" in a fetus should be read as upper pole hydronephrosis until proven otherwise.
 • Minor hydronephrosis may be missed, though change over time prompts recognition; expected ureteric
   dilation may not be obvious but can often be traced down to the bladder.
 • The bladder must be inspected in every such case to identify a ureterocele, and it may be necessary to
   await bladder filling — a large ureterocele can fill the bladder and mask its own presence.
 • Record upper pole parenchymal thickness and echogenicity — these findings inform later clinical
   decisions.
 • The ectopic ureter looks identical to a ureterocele except at bladder level: dilated upper pole, tortuous
   ureter, no intravesical component. A large ectopic ureter may impinge on the bladder and appear
   intravesical — a pseudoureterocele. Recognition requires an experienced maternal–fetal sonographer.
 • With a tentative diagnosis, evaluate the other renal units and the bladder: ipsilateral lower pole or
   contralateral dilation suggests reflux, or less commonly obstruction.
 • BOO from a ureterocele can obstruct all renal units; oligohydramnios from this cause is very unusual
   but does occur. Contralateral renal dysplasia may coexist with reduced amniotic fluid.
 • The need for prenatal intervention or early delivery is exceptional and unlikely to confer significant
   benefit.
 • A single-system lesion is suggested by dilation of the entire kidney and ureter; this may be
   indistinguishable from obstructive megaureter or severe reflux, but the distinction has little immediate
   prenatal consequence and is made postnatally.
Concept — Prenatally the diagnosis is made at the bladder, not at the kidney — upper tract appearances of
ectopic ureter and ureterocele are interchangeable, so the entire diagnostic yield lies in imaging a filled
bladder.

Incidental Postnatal Detection
 • Sometimes diagnosed from hydronephrosis found on imaging performed for unrelated reasons —
   trauma, abdominal pain.
 • Some studies performed for general abdominal pain not thought to be renal prove to be of renal origin.
 • A dilated ureter may not be recognised as such: presumed ovarian cysts have proved to be markedly
   dilated ureters. Always include the dilated ureter in the differential of a cystic pelvic structure.

Infection
 • Infection remains a significant reason for clinical presentation of both entities, at any age, with a highly
   variable pattern.
 • Generalised urosepsis may be the presenting scenario, and renal–bladder ultrasonography will usually
   provide the diagnosis.
 • It has been reported that acute ultrasound is unnecessary in a child with febrile UTI and a normal
   prenatal ultrasound after 30 to 32 weeks; the authors consider this tenuous given the variable quality of
   prenatal imaging, and hold that ultrasound should be obtained in all children with urosepsis.
 • In febrile UTI there is rarely urgency if the child is responding clinically; the value of early detection is the
   possibility of a simple drainage procedure.
 • Sepsis not responding rapidly to appropriate therapy → urgent transurethral incision (TUI) of an
   infected ureterocele, or percutaneous or open drainage of an ectopic ureter; open drainage in this
   setting is usually an end ureterostomy.
 • Ectopic ureters frequently present less acutely, with ongoing low-grade fever and periodic spikes.
 • A negative urine culture does not exclude infection — the infected ectopic system may not drain into the
   bladder.
 • In young females, parents may describe purulent discharge from the perineum; this pattern should
   prompt ultrasound, usually revealing a dilated upper pole or whole system.
 • The unobstructed ectopic ureter rarely presents with infection but can present with incontinence.
 • In young males the subacute pattern occurs, but epididymitis is the more common presentation — true
   bacterial epididymitis or infected urine.
 • In any young male with suspected bacterial epididymitis, perform upper tract ultrasonography to
   exclude an abnormality.
 • Older males present the same way, often after recurrent episodes of epididymitis before the cause is
   found; the reason for the delay is unclear.
Concept — Infection in these anomalies is infection in a compartment that does not communicate with the
bladder; that single fact explains the negative culture, the perineal discharge, the epididymitis, and the need
to image rather than treat empirically.

Incontinence
 • Incontinence may be caused by an ectopic ureter in a female but never in a male; untreated
   ureteroceles are not associated with incontinence.
 • The toilet-trained female with verified continuous urinary leakage must be evaluated for an ectopic
   ureter.
 • Imaging may not immediately detect it because the affected moiety may not be dilated — suspicion
   must be driven by history and occasionally physical examination.
 • Before toilet training the pattern is hard to detect, though some parents note persistent dribbling during
   changing; in early training the wetting is often falsely attributed to lack of motivation.
 • The characteristic history is persistent low-volume dampness at all times, day and night, that does not
   relent, sometimes worsening with activity.
 • The discriminating question: can the child be dry for 30 to 60 minutes? With an ectopic ureter the
   answer is no.
 • Caution in questioning: parents of children wetting for other reasons will also say the child is always wet
   when dry intervals in fact exist.

 • Rarely the leakage is intermittent, perhaps from intermittent leakage through a membrane of the
   Gartner duct.
 • In older undiagnosed children, symptoms may be misattributed to dysfunctional voiding, laziness, or
   even sexual abuse; seek voiding postponement, posturing and constipation to assess whether voiding
   dysfunction explains the wetting.

Pain
 • Pain is uncommon with either entity.
 • Exceptions: pain from acute infection, episodic obstruction of the ectopic ureter, and bladder pain from
   an obstructing ureterocele.
 • Intermittent drainage of an ectopic ureter in older children: abdominal pain followed by perineal
   drainage of urine or purulent material, presumed to reflect accumulation behind an obstructed orifice
   with subsequent release.

Prolapse
 • Ureterocele prolapse is unusual but distinctive and still confuses clinicians.
 • Appearance: smooth, congested, mucosa-covered interlabial mass protruding from the urethra, distinct
   from the vagina, often with difficulty voiding.
 • It is not lobulated — that appearance would suggest sarcoma botryoides.
 • Bladder ultrasound usually confirms the diagnosis, with renal images supporting it. The accompanying
   voiding cystourethrogram characteristically shows the ureterocele prolapsing into the urethra with lower
   pole reflux and the classic "drooping lily" appearance of the lower pole displaced by the dilated upper
   pole.

Late Presentation
 • Presentation in the teenager or adult is reported, usually with infection or abdominal pain, rarely
   incontinence.
 • The non-obstructing ureterocele, often single-system, is well recognised in the adult — frequently
   containing a stone.
 • Vaginal wall prolapse has been associated with an ectopic ureter; ureterocele prolapse after incision has
   been reported in adults.
 • There is usually little or no function in the affected moiety. Upper pole heminephrectomy in duplex
   systems has been the mainstay, but upper-to-lower pole ureteroureterostomy may also be considered.
Concept — Presentation is determined by sex, by whether the system drains into the bladder, and by
whether the distal segment obstructs the outlet — continuous wetting, occult infection, and an interlabial
mass are three expressions of the same anatomical question.

 KEY POINTS: CLINICAL PRESENTATION
   • The majority are detected by prenatal ultrasound on the basis of hydronephrosis, although a specific
     diagnosis is often not made until after birth.
   • Ectopic ureters frequently present with a less acute pattern of low-grade fever with periodic spikes;
     urine cultures may be negative because the infected system does not drain into the bladder.
   • Young males with an ectopic ureter may present subacutely but often have associated epididymitis.
   • Incontinence may be caused by an ectopic ureter in young females but not in young males, and the
     toilet-trained female with verified continuous leakage must be evaluated for one.

EVALUATION
Management rests on a thorough assessment of the affected anatomy and of the functional implications of
the condition, together with the operative and postoperative care anticipated. Both renal and bladder
function are affected by the developmental abnormality and may ultimately require complex surgical
intervention; voiding, bladder emptying, vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), renal drainage and kidney function
are all elements of the management plan.

Anatomic Assessment
Physical Examination
 • Provides invaluable information in both conditions.
 • The prolapsed ureterocele is diagnostic and dramatic but unusual; further anatomical study is still
   necessary.
 • Detecting an ectopic perineal orifice in a child is unusual but highly informative as to the character of
   the problem and the best treatment — a search for it is worthwhile in any child with continuous wetting
   or a known leaking ectopic ureter. When found it lies between the urethral orifice and the vagina and
   can be cannulated with an angiocatheter.
 • Rarely a dilated Gartner duct cyst is detected on careful perineal examination; orifices or cystic
   structures found can then be imaged by contrast injection.
 • The dilated upper pole may be palpable in a relaxed infant, but rarely in an older child.

Ultrasonography
 • Usually provides the anatomic diagnosis and permits inference of functional status.
 • Typical findings: dilated upper pole with ureteric dilation, or a dilated single system.
 • Parenchymal character is more readily seen postnatally; if it appears healthy, the true need for
   functional upper tract imaging is controversial — it may be argued unnecessary if it will not change
   treatment.
 • The difference between functioning and non- or poorly functioning upper poles is usually apparent.
 • Above the level of the bladder there are no features that distinguish a dilated ectopic ureter from a
   ureterocele — so the bladder images are critically important, because management differs greatly.
 • Ureterocele on bladder views: a thin-walled cystic dilation within the bladder that does not extend
   beyond the bladder walls.
 • Laterality is usually apparent but may appear midline if the ureterocele is large; a lobulated ureterocele
   may appear as two structures, but careful examination should demonstrate communication.
 • Extension of a cecoureterocele into the bladder neck is difficult to visualise — non-visualisation is not
   diagnostic.
 • Confusing patterns: a large ureterocele may completely fill the bladder with no urine visible; a full
   bladder with an effaced ureterocele may be mistaken for an ectopic ureter — observation over time
   avoids both errors.
 • Pseudoureterocele: a very dilated ectopic ureter impinging on the bladder wall, distinguished because
   the wall is bilaminar and much thicker than a ureterocele wall, and the lumen extends well outside the
   bladder walls.

Voiding Cystourethrogram
 • The voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) gives excellent evaluation of the bladder, urethra and often the
   distal ureters.
 • Reflux is most often into the lower pole of the duplicated kidney, identified by the orientation of the
   calyces; reflux may also be contralateral and/or into the ureterocele itself.
 • Because the presence of reflux determines initial treatment for some practitioners, VCUG is frequently
   obtained.
 • Where a ureterocele is significantly obstructing on ultrasound and early incision is planned, the initial
   VCUG may be optional; with BOO or severe bilateral upper tract obstruction, VCUG findings would not
   alter treatment and transurethral puncture can proceed on ultrasound alone.
 • After incision, a post-puncture VCUG should be considered, as ipsilateral or contralateral reflux guides
   subsequent decisions.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging
 • Magnetic resonance (MR) urography gives the most detailed images of the affected tract.
 • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in young children usually requires sedation or anaesthesia and
   typically adds nothing to cheaper methods.
 • Its value rests with patients in whom other imaging cannot define complex anatomy: a duplex system
   with a non-dilated upper pole, one with massive dilation obscuring whether duplication exists, or one
   with significantly distorted anatomical relationships.
 • Where such delineation is needed, MRI adds functional information as well.

Functional Assessment
Renal Function — Nuclear Imaging
 • Radionuclide renal imaging remains the gold standard for renal functional assessment, usually best
   afforded by dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA) imaging, though obtainable with a mercaptoacetyltriglycine
   (MAG3) diuretic renogram.
 • The affected upper pole is the principal focus, but the health of the other moieties must be determined,
   particularly with lower pole reflux or hydronephrosis of any unit.
 • There are no criteria defining "normal" function of an upper pole associated with an ectopic ureter or
   ureterocele, and no objective parameters for the level of functional contribution worth preserving.
 • Functional assessment therefore offers only the dichotomy of some function versus no function —
   generally clear, but always with ambiguous cases.
 • Preoperative assessment cannot predict function after decompression.
 • Where observation is contemplated, a diuretic renal scan replaces the DMSA scan, giving both function
   and drainage; this has value where drainage is adequate and hydronephrosis has been reported
   ultimately to resolve.
 • A dilated ectopic ureter is only rarely suitable for observation, so obstruction assessment has limited
   value there.

Renal Function — Intravenous Pyelogram
 • The intravenous pyelogram (IVP) may occasionally give adequate functional information with
   anatomical delineation without the complexity of MRI.
 • Functional assessment is qualitative only, and the IVP is a less useful baseline study except where
   anatomy is uncertain or an ectopic ureter with no dilation is suspected in the setting of incontinence.

Bladder Function
 • The bladder is not the principal focus but its function cannot be ignored.
 • Impairment may arise from BOO by a ureterocele, or impaired continence from inadequate bladder neck
   function with either entity.
 • In bilateral single-system ectopic ureters, bladder development may be severely impaired because of
   the absence of fetal bladder filling.

Ultrasound imaging interpretation
 • A simple bladder ultrasound is very useful: waiting for filling and emptying yields bladder emptying
   efficiency and wall thickness, the latter indicating possible outlet obstruction.
 • The character, location and size of the ureterocele can usually be assessed, and the ectopic nature of a
   dilated ureter is sometimes identifiable.

Ureterocele and the bladder outlet
 • The appearance of the bladder base with filling and voiding on VCUG guides therapeutic decisions:
   significant eversion indicates a weak trigonal floor more likely to require surgical repair.
 • Prolapse into the urethra, or demonstration of a cecoureterocele, is important in planning and
   performing TUI and may better predict the need for secondary surgery.
 • A patulous bladder neck with either lesion may be complicated by later incontinence, though
   continence is hard to predict at presentation.

   into it.

Endoscopic Evaluation
 • Endoscopy is the final element of evaluation — not always essential, but most cases come to operation
   and concurrent endoscopic evaluation is important.
 • When endoscopic incision is to be performed, thorough endoscopy beforehand is critical to select the
   most effective site.
 • Document the character of the urethra, bladder neck and trigone relative to the lesion, and the location
   of the other ureteral orifices.
 • Seek the orifice of the affected ureter; if not seen in the bladder, examine the urethra carefully.
 • Technique as a sequence: start with little bladder filling → increase volume slowly → watch the
   ureterocele flatten. Its true limits are best appreciated with limited filling, which exposes the lowest
   portion — probably the best site for incision.
 • Detect extension into the urethra (cecoureterocele), which signals the need to ensure that section does
   not obstruct after incision.
 • Retrograde contrast injection can confirm ureterocele disproportion — a narrow proximal ureter with
   no filling of an upper pole segment.
 • Unusual connections with the genital ducts may be demonstrated — communication between ureter,
   vas deferens and seminal vesicles.
 • An ectopic ureteral orifice may be very difficult to find endoscopically; if at the bladder neck it is often
   patulous.
 • Some ectopic ureters have only a very thin layer of tissue separating them from the urethra, appearing
   more like a ureterocele; these may be associated with bladder neck defects and incontinence, and their

   endoscopic appearance supports a decision for more extensive removal of the patulous segment with
   bladder neck reconstruction.
Concept — Endoscopy is not a diagnostic test here but a site-selection exercise: everything it adds — the
lowest point of the ureterocele, urethral extension, a patulous bladder neck — exists to determine where to
cut and what will be left obstructing afterwards.
Concept — Functional evaluation answers two separate questions with two separate tools: how much renal
parenchyma is worth saving, answered by nuclear imaging only as "some" or "none", and how competent
the bladder outlet is, answered by VCUG and videourodynamics — and it is the second, not the first, that
predicts continence and the need for secondary surgery.

 KEY POINTS: EVALUATION OF ECTOPIC URETERS AND URETEROCELES
   • Ultrasound usually provides the anatomic diagnosis and permits an inference of renal function;
     typical findings are hydroureteronephrosis in a single system, or a dilated upper pole with ureteric
     dilation in a duplicated system.
   • The ureterocele is characterised by a thin-walled cystic dilation within the bladder that does not
     extend beyond its walls; a very dilated ectopic ureter impinging on the bladder and mimicking one is a
     pseudoureterocele.
   • Radionuclide imaging is the gold standard for renal functional assessment, best provided by DMSA; a
     MAG3 study with a loop diuretic can be used where both function and degree of obstruction are
     needed.
   • The affected upper pole is the principal focus, but the health of the other moieties must be
     determined.
   • VCUG provides the most definitive evaluation of the bladder and urethra, and the appearance of the
     bladder base with filling and voiding is useful in decision making — massive eversion indicates a
     weak trigonal floor more likely to require repair.
   • Endoscopy should document the character of the urethra, bladder neck and trigone relative to the
     ureterocele or ectopic ureter.


CLINICAL MANAGEMENT

Management Goals
 • Although definitive management differs between the two entities, the goals of management are identical
   and the approaches often are too.
 • The goals of therapy: preservation of renal function; elimination of infection, obstruction and reflux;
   and maintenance of urinary continence.
 • Minimisation of overall procedural morbidity is itself a goal, not an afterthought.
 • The goals are agreed; the means of achieving them remain controversial.
 • One area of general agreement: early institution of a daily prophylactic antibiotic reduces UTI risk in
   patients with significant hydronephrosis.

Fetal Management
 • Widespread fetal ultrasonography means a large proportion are diagnosed prenatally; duplicated
   systems without hydronephrosis may not be noted, but hydronephrosis is usually easily seen.
 • There is no role for fetal intervention in the ectopic ureter — identification serves only to plan postnatal
   management.
 • With prenatal suspicion: obtain a postnatal ultrasound, consider a VCUG, and often start prophylactic
   antibiotics at birth to limit early UTI risk.
 • Ureteroceles are likewise not an indication for fetal intervention, except when large and obstructing all
   renal units.
 • In those rare cases, percutaneous needle aspiration of the ureterocele or cystoscopic laser ablation of
   the fetal ureterocele has been reported — still experimental and performed only in selected centres.

Urgent Management
 • Child with sepsis of urinary origin and obstruction: antibiotics are usually sufficient to stabilise.
 • If the child does not respond promptly and appropriately → TUI of an obstructing ureterocele, or
   nephrostomy tube drainage of the ectopic ureter.
 • A cutaneous distal ureterostomy can be performed in either setting to establish drainage promptly and
   reliably.
 • Once a child has proven prone to infection by presenting this way, a drainage procedure should be
   undertaken non-urgently after stabilisation to prevent future infections, even if not required acutely.
Concept — The neonatal algorithm turns on one variable — is the segment obstructed and is the
parenchyma at risk — with antibiotics buying time and a drainage procedure becoming obligatory once
infection has declared the system vulnerable.

General Management Principles
 • For both lesions in a duplicated system, the principal concern is preservation of functional renal
   parenchyma, achieved by correcting obstruction and preventing reflux with its risk of infection-
   mediated parenchymal damage.
 • The two goals can conflict: relieving obstruction may induce reflux in either or both poles; the same
   action may equally cause existing lower pole reflux to resolve.
 • Decision making on when and how to intervene is quite subjective, and how much renal function is
   worth preserving must be decided individually.
 • For the ectopic ureter: ureteral reimplantation, or in a duplex kidney common sheath reimplantation, or
   ureteroureterostomy, either low or proximal near the renal pelvis.
 • Newer option for the young child not yet old enough for formal reimplantation: a side-to-side non-
   dismembered refluxing anastomosis with the bladder, performed in early infancy and intended as
   definitive in a population at low risk of UTI — long-term data are still required to establish safety and how
   many children need further procedures.
 • For the ureterocele: TUI, or ureterocele excision with common sheath reimplantation, or
   ureteroureterostomy.

 • Acute decompression, by entity: ectopic ureter → end ureterostomy near the bladder; ureterocele →
   TUI is usually effective.
 • Few objective criteria indicate how much residual function is worth preserving; VUR in either ureter
   must be considered, and every effort made to correct it with the drainage procedure.
 • These lesions are overwhelmingly dealt with postnatally; fetal decompression is reported only for severe
   BOO from a ureterocele.
 • Although earlier relief of obstruction might be expected to improve recovery of upper pole function, the
   evidence shows limited recovery even with early decompression. The counterpoint is an early
   endoscopic puncture series, which demonstrated function that had not been detected before drainage.
 • Impaired upper pole function occurs equally in those diagnosed prenatally and postnatally.
 • In most instances the upper pole contributes little to overall renal function, and there are no criteria for
   normal upper pole function.
Concept — Every operative choice in this chapter is a trade between obstruction and reflux across a moiety
of unknown and unpredictable worth; because function cannot be predicted before or after decompression,
the decision is empirical and individual, not algorithmic.

 KEY POINTS: CLINICAL MANAGEMENT — GOALS OF TREATMENT
   • The goals are preservation of renal function; elimination of infection, obstruction and reflux; and
     maintenance of urinary continence, with minimisation of overall procedural morbidity as a further
     goal.
   • For both entities in a duplicated system, a primary concern is preservation of functional renal
     parenchyma.
   • Decision making for parenchymal preservation is largely empirical, with few objective criteria for how
     much residual function is worth preserving.
   • For an ectopic ureter this can mean common sheath reimplantation or ureteroureterostomy, either

   • For a ureterocele it can mean TUI, or ureterocele excision with common sheath reimplantation or

   • Observational management in carefully selected patients is reasonable, with the potential for

Observational Management
 • Several groups report non-operative management of ureteroceles meeting criteria: no obstruction of the
   ipsilateral lower pole or contralateral kidney and limited reflux to the lower pole (grade III or less); no
   function of the upper pole; or no obstruction on diuretic renography.
 • A described subset had ureteroceles with multicystic dysplastic kidney in the associated upper segment,
   with low-grade or no reflux and no ureteric dilation; these prenatally diagnosed patients were managed
   non-operatively with a benign clinical course.
 • Some children do well without intervention, and resolution of upper pole dilation and lower pole reflux
   has been reported.
 • Counselling is difficult: the potential for later, unpredictable acute presentation is real, and for that
   individual it is clinically very significant even at a low statistical incidence.
 • The long-term risk, the relative balance of morbidities and the robustness of clinical predictors are all
   undetermined.

 • Carefully considered, observational management is an appropriate strategy with careful selection and
   parental education.

Total Reconstruction
 • Advocated by some as the most definitive procedure for ureteroceles.
 • Components: upper pole heminephrectomy + ureterocele excision + reimplantation of the lower pole
   ureter.
 • Definitive, but an extensive operation performed through two incisions.
 • Success rates are good, but it remains uncertain whether it is appropriate in most children when long-
   term relief from obstruction and reflux can be achieved with one, or at most two, lesser procedures.
 • Best candidate: the older child with a massive ureterocele, a non-functioning upper pole, and
   significant lower pole reflux.
 • Contemporary execution: partial nephrectomy laparoscopically or retroperitoneoscopically, with a lower
   Pfannenstiel incision for bladder reconstruction and reimplantation.

Upper Pole Partial Nephrectomy
 • Long the preferred treatment where there is clearly no upper pole function, and where there is concern
   about how effective a drainage procedure will be because of massive dilation.
 • Numerous series advocate different approaches with results varying by degree of reflux, age and
   underlying pathology, but no data indicate any one approach is definitively better as a universal choice.
 • The surgical methods are similar whether open, conventional laparoscopic or robotic, and apply to both
   ectopic ureters and ureteroceles in duplex systems.

Open Partial Nephrectomy or Heminephrectomy
 • A standardised procedure with relatively little recent evolution.
 • Access: a flank approach usually offers better exposure to the upper pole vessels; dorsal lumbotomy is
   effective and less morbid than a muscle-dividing flank incision, but more challenging in older children.
 • The primary concern is to avoid damaging the viable lower pole.
 • Sequence: retract the kidney gently, avoiding vascular injury from traction and small-vessel spasm →
   transect the upper pole ureter and place a traction stitch on its proximal portion as a handle → dissect
   the ureter both below and above the vessels → pass the ureter behind the main renal vessels → dissect
   carefully around the renal vessels to protect the lower pole → sequentially ligate the upper pole
   vessels, most often two or three in number → demarcation of the upper pole parenchyma becomes
   apparent → resect the upper pole with electrocautery or another cutting device → strip the capsule off
   the upper pole in continuity for use in closure → close with several mattress sutures incorporating a
   pedicle of retroperitoneal fat.
 • Adjuncts: atraumatic clamping of the renal pedicle allows a bloodless field; an intravenous osmotic
   diuretic such as mannitol immediately before and after clamping helps prevent acute tubular necrosis;
   topical vasodilators such as papaverine should be available for vasospasm. In general, vascular control
   is not essential, because the polar vessels can be identified and controlled.
 • Upper pole ureterectomy: maintain the dissection immediately on the wall of the upper pole ureter to
   preserve the blood supply of the remaining lower pole ureter.
 • If obstruction is accompanied by reflux into the ectopic ureter, some recommend a second (Gibson)
   incision to resect the ureter in its entirety; care must be taken to avoid injury to the vas deferens in
   males.

 • To avoid common sheath complications, the back wall of the upper pole ureter can be left attached to
   the lower pole ureter, removing the remainder — this prevents damage to the lower pole ureteric blood
   supply, which courses between the two ureters. Resection proceeds to the bladder, where sutures close
   the upper pole ureteral hiatus.
 • Alternative: ligate the distal refluxing stump and leave it in situ — few subsequent problems are
   attributed to the small volume of reflux. Larger-diameter stumps carry a higher risk of late infection. The
   refluxing, obstructed ectopic ureter raises the most concern, so remove as much ureter as possible
   without injuring the bladder neck.
 • A Penrose drain, through a separate counterincision or at the incision edge, drains the renal fossa and the
   ureteral dissection.
 • Postoperative evaluation with Doppler sonography to demonstrate normal anatomy, absence of a
   urinoma, and normal blood flow to the lower pole; a nuclear renal scan is optional if lower pole
   viability is in doubt.

Laparoscopic and Robotic Partial Nephrectomy
 • Transabdominal or retroperitoneal, with robotic assistance increasingly common.
 • Advantages: less postoperative pain, earlier return of gastrointestinal function, earlier discharge,
   quicker parental return to work; plus enhanced visualisation and magnification, improved cosmesis, and
   avoidance of the second incision often needed for distal ureterectomy.
 • Feasible in very small infants, with operative time falling as experience and skill increase.
 • Newer energy devices allow resection of the upper pole in a bloodless field.
 • Transperitoneal sequence: grasp the pathological ureter as a handle and dissect closely on its wall to
   spare the normal ureter's blood supply → pass the upper pole ureter behind the vessels and use it to
   facilitate upper pole dissection → ligate the polar vessels with clips or divide with electrocautery,
   producing clearer demarcation → develop the plane between the upper pole collecting system and
   lower pole parenchyma bluntly → identify and transect the upper-to-lower pole attachments → remove
   the polar element with electrocautery or another energy source → check for collecting system leakage
   with intravenous methylene blue → close the defect over a pedicle of local fatty tissue.
 • Closure of the defect may limit postoperative fluid collections; the described alternative is fibrin glue
   and haemostatic agents on the cut surface covered with Gerota fascia.
 • Robotic advantages over standard laparoscopy: augmented magnification, greater instrument dexterity
   around the renal pedicle and polar vessels, and three-dimensional vision. Patient and port positioning
   are the same as for pyeloplasty.
 • Vascular control may be by absorbable or metal clips or suture ties — ties may be preferable, reducing
   the risk of avulsing a clip during later dissection.
 • The defect is usually closed with a tongue of retroperitoneal fat and two or three mattress sutures of
   3-0 polydioxanone suture (PDS) or Vicryl.
 • The robotic approach has been described for both upper and lower pole heminephrectomy with
   successful outcomes.
 • Laparoendoscopic single-site surgery nephrectomy has been described for a single-system ectopic
   ureter, using one 22 mm multitrocar port recessed in the umbilicus for the entire procedure; single-site
   robotic platforms offer robotic precision with still less morbidity.

Outcomes
 • Results of upper pole removal for both entities are in general very good.
 • For the ectopic ureter, the residual stump is rarely problematic.
 • For ureteroceles with pre-existing lower pole reflux, resolution may be expected in up to 20%.
 • New reflux appears in 15–50% of patients who had none preoperatively.
 • The overall secondary surgery rate after primary upper pole nephrectomy for ureterocele is 40–50%.
 • The literature is hard to interpret because indications for secondary surgery vary — in some series any
   postoperative reflux was an indication for bladder-level surgery, in others reflux was monitored and
   treated only for clinical problems such as UTI. Neither approach has been validated by long-term data.

Complications
 • The most significant complication is loss of lower pole function.
 • It may be a long-term issue and may not be recognised immediately; clinical signs — fever, increasing
   pain, haematuria — may appear in the first week after surgery.

   • The primary concern is to avoid damaging the viable lower pole.
   • Separation of the duplicated ureters during intravesical dissection is discouraged, because it injures

   • Postoperative evaluation is best performed with Doppler sonography — anatomy, urinoma, and

   • Definitive cure by partial nephrectomy for ectopic ureters is usually successful, the residual stump

   • For ureteroceles with lower pole reflux, resolution may be expected in up to 20%, and new reflux in

Lower Tract Reconstruction
 • A definitive reconstruction at the bladder is suitable for both the ectopic ureter and the ureterocele.
 • Advantage: it relieves obstruction and corrects reflux in one operation.
 • Disadvantages: potential injury to the bladder neck and vagina, and the complexity of the procedure.
 • If clinically significant reflux persists after other procedures, lower tract reconstruction may be
   necessary.

Ureterocele Excision and Common-Sheath Reimplantation
 • Sequence: transverse incision of the ureterocele between two stay sutures → develop a plane
   proximally between the ureterocele wall and the bladder wall → dissect the ureterocele off the bladder
   to the point where it joins the lower pole ureter → dissect the two ureters as a unit → taper the upper
   pole ureter as needed → reimplant both ureters submucosally → dissect the distal ureterocele in the
   same plane to the level of the bladder neck and resect it → plicate the detrusor if its backing appears
   insufficient to prevent reflux → raise bladder mucosal flaps to cover the area of the removed
   ureterocele.
 • Separation of the duplicated ureters during intravesical dissection is discouraged — it injures the
   common blood supply running longitudinally between the two ureters.
 • Plication of the detrusor underlying the ureterocele may be needed to reinforce areas of muscle
   deficiency.
 • The distal portion of the ureterocele may extend below the bladder neck — extreme care is needed
   here to avoid injury to the sphincter mechanisms.
 • If the entire ureterocele cannot be excised, it can be carefully fulgurated and closed over in two layers.

 • Cecoureteroceles present a unique challenge: the distal aspect can create an obstructive flap-valve with
   voiding, acting like an obstructing windsock within the urethra and causing bladder outlet obstruction.
   Options: resection by gentle retraction if it is not very large; closure of the opening with two layers of

Marsupialisation
 • The thin intravesical aspect is removed and the edges oversewn, with no attempt to dissect the back
   wall or reinforce the posterior bladder wall, on the empirical observation that this is not always needed.
   Reported results have been satisfactory.
 • Advantages: avoids dissecting out the whole ureterocele and avoids risk of injury to the underlying
   vagina.
 • Potential risks: diverticulum formation and altered bladder dynamics postoperatively in the wrong
   patient population.
 • Best for patients without a posterior detrusor defect, and perhaps smaller ureteroceles.
 • The authors prefer complete excision with formal trigonal reconstruction — definitively correcting the
   anatomical and functional defect at the first open operation. If marsupialisation is used, it must be used
   with care.
 • Results are very good, although persisting reflux can be an issue in 5–10% of patients, more commonly
   when ureteral tapering is needed. Although its clinical significance has been questioned, persisting
   reflux in this context must be viewed with caution.
Concept — Bladder-level reconstruction is the only procedure that addresses obstruction and reflux
simultaneously, and it pays for that with proximity to the bladder neck, sphincter and vagina — which is why
every technical caution in the section concerns what lies beneath and beyond the ureterocele, not the
ureterocele itself.

   • Cecoureteroceles present a unique challenge: the distal aspect can create an obstructive flap-valve
     with voiding, acting like a windsock in the urethra.
   • Marsupialisation is an alternative, in which the thin intravesical aspect is removed and the edge

   • Results of excision and common sheath reimplantation are very good, although persisting reflux
     occurs in 5% to 10%.
   • Total reconstruction — upper pole nephrectomy with ureterocele excision and lower pole

Pyeloureterostomy and Ureteroureterostomy
 • Indicated when the upper pole of either an ectopic ureter or ureterocele is to be maintained, because of
   function or surgeon preference.
 • Increasingly used even in non-functioning upper poles, because concerns about long-term hypertension
   have not been substantiated and the procedure can be completed through a small incision, often no
   larger than a herniorrhaphy incision.

Open Procedure
 • Distal ureteroureterostomy: end-to-side anastomosis between the upper pole ureter and the lower pole
   ureter, performed via an inguinal open approach.
 • It is critically important to identify the recipient lower pole ureter correctly — cystoscopically place a
   stent into the lower pole ureter at the start of the procedure.
 • Proximal alternative: ureteroureterostomy or pyeloureterostomy, draining the upper pole system into
   the lower pole system. High anastomoses may be preferable to a distal anastomosis with a dilated
   upper pole, because the latter may produce more urinary stasis.
 • The clinical significance of the "yo-yo" phenomenon remains uncertain, but a proximal anastomosis is
   preferred when there is significant ureteral size mismatch.
 • Technique: limit dissection to an absolute minimum, especially medially, to prevent disruption of either
   ureter's blood supply → make a generous longitudinal pyelotomy or ureterotomy in the lower recipient
   pole to overcome size disproportion → anastomose end-to-side → aspirate the distal upper pole ureter
   with a feeding tube to decompress it → resect the distal upper pole ureter as far inferiorly as possible,
   staying directly on its wall to avoid the adjacent lower pole ureter's vasculature.
 • If there is no reflux: resect as distally as possible and the remnant lower portion may be left open.
 • If reflux is present in an ectopic ureter: take it as close to the bladder neck as possible.
 • If associated with a ureterocele: resection of the ureterocele may be avoided as long as it is well
   decompressed.

Laparoscopic Procedure
 • Both ureteroureterostomy and pyeloureterostomy can be readily performed laparoscopically, aided by
   robotic control, and are carried out at the level of the iliac vessels for the ectopic ureter presenting with
   incontinence.
 • A stent is placed in the recipient ureter only, not across the anastomosis.
 • Robotic-assisted laparoscopic ureteroureterostomy (RALUU) versus open ureteroureterostomy:
   comparable operative times and complication rates, with slightly shorter hospitalisation for robotic
   cases; in robotic cases cystoscopy with lower pole stenting was consistently performed, stent type
   varying by surgeon preference.
 • A further RALUU series used an external ureteral stent removed on postoperative day 1, showing
   complete resolution of hydronephrosis postoperatively.
 • Laparoscopy-assisted open ureteroureterostomy: an umbilical camera port for visualisation, then a
   Babcock passed through the small incision to grasp the ureters from the retroperitoneum and deliver

Transurethral Incision for Ureterocele
 • The simplest means to decompress an obstructed upper pole or single-system ureterocele.
 • Reported relief of obstruction: 78–97%.
 • TUI versus multiple punctures (watering can technique): no difference in decompression rates, although
   multiple punctures are reported to induce less reflux into the affected ureter.
 • In acute sepsis TUI is the most appropriate intervention, though percutaneous nephrostomy is a viable
   option.
 • The trade-off: TUI reliably relieves obstruction but risks inducing reflux, which may predispose to upper
   tract infection and the need for reconstruction. The balance between TUI's minimal morbidity and its
   uncertainty is the basis of the clinical controversy about its utility.
 • Preferred method: a transverse incision through the full thickness of the ureterocele wall using cutting
   current, made as distally on the ureterocele and as close to the bladder floor as possible to lessen the
   chance of postoperative reflux.
 • Instruments: a Bugbee electrode or an angled-tip wire; the angled-tip wire is favoured — finer tip, more
   precision, and the angle allows easier manipulation. In older children, the resectoscope with the Collins
   hot knife may be used. Laser incision has equivalent results; cold knife incision or puncture has also
   been used.
 • The ureterocele should be incised deeply, because ureteroceles may be thick-walled.
 • Adequacy is confirmed either by the escape of a jet of urine from the ureterocele or by viewing the
   urothelium on its inside.
 • For an ectopic ureterocele extending into the urethra: adequate drainage by either a longitudinal
   incision extending from the intravesical portion down into the urethral portion, or two separate
   punctures — one intravesical, one urethral. Retrograde incision of the intraurethral component has also
   been described for these more challenging cases.
 • Aftercare: in the uninfected well child, no bladder catheter is left and most are treated as outpatients.
   Follow-up ultrasonography at 4 to 6 weeks assesses decompression; a voiding cystogram in the same
   period determines lower pole reflux status and whether new reflux into the ureterocele has been
   created.

 • The natural history of lower pole reflux after TUI is incompletely defined.
 • An observational approach has been taken in some series, with resolution reported at variable follow-

 • Cure — improvement of hydronephrosis with absence of VUR — was several times more likely in single-

Ureterostomy for Ectopic Ureter
 • The ectopic ureter in a neonate with sepsis or massive dilation may be best managed with a temporary
   end ureterostomy, though endoscopic ureteral incision with stenting, or a nephrostomy tube, are
   alternatives.
 • Diversion permits acute decompression to manage sepsis and allows later assessment of any function in
   the affected unit before definitive management.
 • End ureterostomy allows the ureter to decompress and positions it for reimplantation if the unit proves
   salvageable.
 • No resection of redundant ureter is performed — it will shorten in time, and length will be needed for
   reimplantation.
 • The stoma should be positioned at what would be the lateral end of a Pfannenstiel incision.
 • Percutaneous ureterostomy has also been reported.
 • Assessment of functional status is performed shortly before definitive surgery, usually at 4 months or
   after 6 months of age. DMSA scanning is probably the most efficient means, with differential urine
   collection an option.
 • If no function is appreciable, partial or total nephrectomy is performed with removal of the entire
   ureter to the stoma; a drainage procedure through ureteroureterostomy remains an option.
 • Endoscopic incision of the bladder and ectopic ureter to create direct drainage into the bladder has
   been described, analogous to TUI of a ureterocele; open ureterovesicostomy for drainage has also been
   reported. Whether either is a long-term solution remains unclear.

Summary of Clinical Decision Making
Ectopic Ureter
 • Decision making is much more straightforward than for ureteroceles and rests on whether to maintain
   the upper pole of a duplex system.
 • If the upper pole is maintained, the approach depends on lower pole reflux:
     ◦ Lower pole reflux present → common sheath reimplantation, or lower pole reimplantation with
       distal upper-to-lower ureteroureterostomy.
     ◦ No lower pole reflux → either proximal or distal ureteroureterostomy.
 • If the degree of function is ambiguous → a temporary end ureterostomy permits assessment after the
   acute presentation, particularly with a massively dilated ureter.
 • If reflux is present, the need for lower tract surgery to correct it may influence the choice to avoid upper
   tract surgery altogether.
 • If removal is chosen — for degree of dilation, or preference to remove non-functioning dysplastic tissue
   → upper pole nephrectomy.
 • For the single-system ectopic ureter, preservation versus removal rests on degree of function and
   surgeon preference; there are no objective outcome data favouring either.

Ureterocele in a Duplex System
 • The availability of endoscopic incision creates more options.
 • TUI is an operation of a totally different nature from upper pole nephrectomy or lower tract
   reconstruction — it is inappropriate to equate them comparatively.
 • Because significant lower pole reflux and upper tract dilation may in some patients be treated with a
   brief outpatient procedure with documented long-term results, it is reasonable to offer TUI before
   moving to complex upper or lower tract reconstruction.
 • Even if half these patients need subsequent surgery, it can be safely deferred until the child is older.
 • Further appeal: TUI may make subsequent surgery less complex by decompressing a dilated upper pole
   ureter — reimplantation may be much more effective and may not require excisional tapering, reducing
   overall morbidity.
 • The appeal of TUI is less in the older child with a massive upper pole, in whom removal may be
   preferable and definitive surgery can be performed at diagnosis.

Voiding Dysfunction After Ureterocele Repair
 • Voiding dysfunction after lower urinary tract reconstruction for ureterocele has been reported in some
   series, but with a low incidence; in other series there was no evidence of incontinence or significant
   bladder dysfunction.
 • One series reported infrequent voiding with the possibility of infection in roughly half of patients, and
   incontinence in only a small minority; a similar observation was made with a lower incidence after
   complex bladder surgery for duplication anomalies.
 • The bladder dysfunction has been attributed to an intrinsic bladder abnormality rather than to the
   surgical procedures.
 • In the child with recurrent infections or incontinence after repair, consider the type of surgery and the
   possibility of inadequate trigonal support.
 • Inadequate trigonal support leads to a weak bladder base, ballooning of the trigone posterior to the
   bladder neck, and an obstructive process — with inadequate bladder emptying, infection, and upper
   tract dilation.
 • This can also occur after TUI of a ureterocele with a urethral component, as with a cecoureterocele.
 • Assessment: VCUG to assess bladder anatomy with voiding, and videourodynamics if needed. Lateral
   views during voiding are essential to identify the bladder pathology. Antegrade cystoscopy via a
   suprapubic puncture has been used to better assess the bladder neck. Recurrent VUR may occur,
   particularly with obstructed voiding.

OTHER URETERAL ANOMALIES

Anomalies of Number
Bifid Ureters and Ureteral Duplication
 • Ureteral duplication may be associated with ectopia or a ureterocele but is equally compatible with a
   normally functioning renal system — provided both ureters enter the bladder orthotopically, or the
   duplication is partial.
 • Incidence: approximately 1 in 125 people (0.8%) on autopsy series, which are less selective than clinical
   series.
 • Slightly commoner in females, approximately 1.6:1.
 • Unilateral duplication is six times more frequent than bilateral, but it is clinically prudent to look
   carefully for contralateral duplication whenever unilateral duplication is documented — this matters
   most in non-hydronephrotic upper poles and ureteral ectopia causing incontinence, where the second,
   occult system is the cause of persistent wetting after treating the first.
 • Right and left sides are affected similarly in unilateral duplication.
 • Definitional distinction: a bifid renal pelvis includes only a single ureter, whereas confluence of the
   upper and lower ureters below the ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) constitutes partial duplication, or bifid
   ureter.
 • An increased incidence of ureterorenal pathology is documented with duplication anomalies, and the
   clinical implication depends largely on the ureteral insertion.

operations — upper-to-lower for the obstructed upper pole, lower-to-upper for the refluxing lower pole.

 • Triplication of the ureter, complete or partial, is very rare, and typically presents with infection or

 • Associations: ureteroceles; ectopia to the bladder neck, urethra or vagina; lower and mid-pole UPJ

 • Surgical management is tailored to the specific anatomy, and minimally invasive techniques have been
   reported effective.

Quadruple Ureters
 • Ureteral quadruplication is rarer still, and most reported cases have been in adults.
 • Three recent cases involved four ureters draining into a large ureteral cyst and connecting to the
   bladder through a single ureter.
 • One report of a young adult with incontinence described three of four ureters merging and entering the

Concept — As ureteral number increases, the Weigert-Meyer law degrades from rule to tendency — it still
typically holds in triplication and is explicitly violated in quadruplication. The law describes the ordering of
two sequential buds; it has no authority over anatomy generated by additional, unordered budding events.

Fibroepithelial Polyps
 • Presentation: flank pain, haematuria, or incidental detection of hydronephrosis.
 • The commonest site of attachment is the UPJ, although they may originate from any part of the ureter.
 • As a cause of UPJ obstruction they are very uncommon — reported in 0.5% of all UPJ patients
   undergoing pyeloplasty.
 • A very large majority of patients are male (89%), and a majority of polyps are left-sided (78%).
 • Bilaterality is uncommon but reported, with clinical effects on both kidneys; polyps may also be

   significantly distal to the UPJ.

Vascular Anomalies Involving the Ureter
 • A variety of vascular lesions can cause ureteral obstruction, and in all of them the vascular system,
   rather than the urinary system, is the anomalous one.
 • With the exception of accessory renal blood vessels, all are relatively uncommon, although all have
   clinical relevance.

Preureteral Vena Cava (Circumcaval or Retrocaval Ureter)
Terminology
 • Commonly known to urologists as circumcaval or retrocaval ureter — terms that are anatomically
   descriptive but misleading as to development.
 • "Preureteral vena cava" is the more accurate term, because it emphasises that the lesion results from
   altered vascular, not ureteral, development.

Anatomy
 • Involves the right ureter, which deviates medially behind (dorsal to) the inferior vena cava, winds about
   it and crosses in front of it from medial to lateral, then resumes a normal distal course to the bladder.
 • The renal pelvis and upper ureter are typically elongated and dilated in a J or fishhook shape before
   passing behind the cava.
 • The collecting system is not inevitably obstructed.

Embryology
 • The definitive inferior vena cava develops on the right from a plexus of fetal veins. Retroperitoneal
   venous pathways are initially symmetrical: posterior cardinal and supracardinal veins dorsally,
   subcardinal veins ventrally, forming a collar on each side through which the ascending kidneys pass.
 • Normal course: the left supracardinal veins and the lumbar portion of the right posterior cardinal vein
   atrophy → the subcardinal veins become the internal spermatic veins → the definitive right-sided
   inferior vena cava forms from the right supracardinal vein.
 • The anomaly: if the lumbar portion of the subcardinal vein fails to atrophy and becomes the primary
   right-sided vein, the ureter is trapped dorsal to it.
 • Double right vena cava: when the definitive cava forms normally and the ventral portion of the
   primitive ring also persists, both the dorsal and the ventral right-sided components survive, and the
   right ureter is trapped between the two limbs.
 • Bilateral or left-sided vena cava can occur, and a bilateral circumcaval ureter has been described in situs
   inversus. In bilateral vena cava with a circumcaval ureter, the circumcaval ureter has been reported only
   on the right — indicating the right cava developed abnormally from a persistent subcardinal vein while
   the left developed normally from the left supracardinal vein.

Incidence and presentation
 • Autopsy incidence about 1 in 1500 (0.07%); a recent meta-analysis indicated a prevalence of 0.13%.
 • About three times commoner in male than female cadavers.
 • The symptoms are those of obstruction. Although the lesion is congenital, most patients do not present
   until the third or fourth decade.

Diagnosis
 • Flank or abdominal pain, or infection — or incidental discovery during other radiological tests.
 • Excretory urography often fails to visualise the ureter beyond the J hook, the segment extending behind
   the cava.
 • Retrograde ureteropyelography demonstrates an S curve to the point of obstruction, with the retrocaval
   segment lying at the level of L3 or L4.
 • Ultrasonography, CT and MRI have all been useful in defining the vascular malformation.
 • CT urography may be the procedure of choice, confirming the diagnosis and avoiding retrograde
   ureteropyelography.
 • Nuclear renal furosemide scanning can assess the severity of obstruction but may not identify the
   anomaly itself.
 • MRI can demonstrate the course of the preureteral cava and may be more detailed and less invasive
   than CT and retrograde pyelography.

Treatment
 • Surgical correction: ureteral division, with relocation and ureteroureteral or ureteropelvic
   reanastomosis, usually with excision or bypass of the retrocaval segment, which can be aperistaltic.
 • Be mindful of the ureteric blood supply — from the renal artery and aorta superiorly and the iliac
   vessels inferiorly.
 • The preferred approach for the obstructed ureter is ureteral division and relocation.
 • Laparoscopic and robotic reconstruction, by both transperitoneal and retroperitoneal approaches, has
   been described in children.
Concept — The name carries the teaching: the cava is the anomaly, the ureter is merely trapped. That
explains why the lesion is right-sided, why it presents in adulthood despite being congenital, why the
retrocaval segment is excised or bypassed rather than simply released, and why it is the classic mimic of UPJ

Other Anomalies of Position
 • Several instances of horseshoe kidney have been reported in association, as have a variety of left renal
   anomalies — agenesis, hydronephrosis, malrotation and hypoplasia.
 • Mimics of circumcaval ureteral obstruction: an obstructing branch of the right spermatic vein, a lumbar
   vein, and an anomalous tendon of the iliopsoas muscle.

Preureteral iliac artery (retroiliac ureter)
 • A ureter coursing behind the common iliac artery is rare; either side may be involved, and bilateral
   cases have been reported.
 • Obstruction occurs at the level of L5 or S1 as the ureter is compressed behind the artery.
 • Coexisting anomalies are common, particularly vasal anomalies.
 • Like the preureteral cava, it is considered to be of vascular origin without definitive proof.
 • Embryology: normally the primitive ventral root of the umbilical artery is replaced by a more dorsal
   branch developing between the aorta and the distal umbilical artery; persistence of the ventral root
   while the dorsal root fails to form traps the ureter dorsally.
 • Ureteral or mesonephric duct ectopia is often present. Reported combinations include an ectopic ureter
   emptying with the ipsilateral vas deferens via a persistent common mesonephric duct into the proximal
   posterior urethra; bilateral retroiliac ureters with bilateral ectopic termination of the vasa deferentia
   into the ureters; and ectopic vaginal termination with urometrocolpos from an imperforate hymen.
 • A retro–internal iliac artery ureter causing left ureteral obstruction has been reported in an otherwise
   normal young female.

Vascular obstruction of the distal ureter
 • Obstruction of the distal ureter by uterine, umbilical, obturator and hypogastric vessels close to the
   bladder has been described.
 • It is not always clear that vascular impressions on a dilated ureter are causal — the finding may be an
   artefact when a ureter dilated by intrinsic obstruction is secondarily compressed against an adjacent
   vessel.

Herniation of the ureter
 • Extremely rare. The classic series covered inguinal, scrotal and femoral herniations.
 • Most were paraperitoneal — a loop of herniated ureter alongside a peritoneal hernial sac; only a
   minority were extraperitoneal, with no hernial sac.
 • In paraperitoneal ureteral hernias, the ureteric loop is always medial to the peritoneal sac.
 • Of six scrotal hernias, four had no peritoneal sac. When the ureter extended into the scrotum it was
   more likely to be dilated, causing upper tract obstruction.
 • In children, herniated ureters have presented with hydronephrosis, associated with megaureters and
   with persistent hydronephrosis after posterior urethral valve ablation.
 • Internal hernias of the ureter are even more exceptional: reports exist of a sciatic hernia containing a
   ureter, herniation between the psoas muscle and the iliac vessels, and lumbar triangle herniation.
 • Ureteral herniation with obstruction has been reported as a rare complication of renal transplantation.

 • The table's organising row is level of obstruction, which alone distinguishes the two lesions radiologically.

Concept — Every lesion in this section shares one mechanism — a vessel that should have regressed did not,
and the ureter was caught on the wrong side of it. The treatment principle follows directly: move the ureter,
not the vessel, and excise the segment rendered aperistaltic by the entrapment.

 KEY POINTS: OTHER URETERAL ANOMALIES
   • Ureteral duplication is compatible with a normally functioning renal system if both ureters enter
     orthotopically or the duplication is partial.
   • It is clinically prudent to look for contralateral duplication when unilateral duplication is
     documented.

   • An increased incidence of ureterorenal pathology is documented with duplication anomalies.
   • Conditions routinely affecting a single-system kidney affect the lower pole — UPJ obstruction and
     VUR; the upper pole is more likely affected by conditions of abnormal ureteral formation — ectopia
     and ureterocele.
   • Polyps of the ureter may present with flank pain, haematuria or incidental hydronephrosis, and the
     commonest site of attachment is the UPJ.
   • UPJ polyps have mostly been treated with pyeloplasty, although whether endoscopic resection would
     suffice is unsettled; ureteroscopic removal is the recommended therapy away from the UPJ.


 KEY POINTS: PREURETERAL VENA CAVA
   • The lesion involves the right ureter, which deviates medially behind the inferior vena cava, winds
     about it and crosses in front from medial to lateral before resuming a normal course to the bladder.
   • If the lumbar subcardinal vein fails to atrophy and becomes the primary right-sided vein, the ureter
     is trapped dorsal to it.
   • It may present with flank or abdominal pain or infection, or be discovered incidentally.
   • Surgical correction involves ureteral division, with relocation and ureteroureteral or ureteropelvic
     reanastomosis, usually with excision or bypass of the aperistaltic retrocaval segment.
