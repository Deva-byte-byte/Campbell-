Chapter 43 — Exstrophy-Epispadias Complex
 • One spectrum of genitourinary malformation: from glanular epispadias (mildest) through classic bladder
   exstrophy to cloacal exstrophy (an overwhelming multisystem defect).
 • Five entities (Fig. 43.1): cloacal exstrophy; male classic bladder exstrophy; female classic bladder
   exstrophy; male epispadias; female epispadias.

HISTORICAL ASPECTS
 • Assyrian-Babylonian cuneiform tablets (first–second millennia BCE) recorded birth anomalies as omens;
   genital anomalies were frequent, renal and bladder anomalies rare and uninterpretable — a definitive
   description of bladder or cloacal exstrophy was not corroborated.
 • First epispadias: attributed to Byzantine emperor Heraclius (CE 610–641). First cloacal exstrophy:
   Schenck, 1595.

Incidence and Inheritance
 • Bladder exstrophy incidence: 2.15 per 100,000 live births (United States); about 1 in 46,000 in an
   international prospective study. German live prevalence: whole complex about 1 in 11,000; exstrophy
   about 1 in 30,675.
 • Male-to-female ratio 2.3:1 across series; some series report 5–6:1.
 • Recurrence in a family about 1 in 100. Offspring of an affected parent: 1 in 70 live births — about 500-
   fold the general population. No affected offspring of affected parents were reported until the 1980s.
 • Twin data: concordance is inconsistent even in monozygotic twins, so the cause is multifactorial, not
   simple Mendelian. Multiplex families and consanguinity occur but are rare.
 • Other anomalies in relatives are mostly midline defects and oral clefts. Cleft lip and palate are also
   prevalent in probands.
 • Parental factors: older parents on average; many probands are first pregnancies, which may reflect later
   first pregnancy and more use of assisted reproduction.
 • Phenotype correlates (a multicentre study of mild, intermediate/classic and severe/cloacal forms): males
   over-represented in every group; maternal first-trimester smoking → cloacal exstrophy; preconception
   folic acid → mildest phenotype (epispadias).
 • Hormonal exposure: high-dose early first-trimester progesterone (about tenfold rise) and in vitro
   fertilisation (IVF; about 7.5-fold rise) both increase risk, which implicates a hormonal role. In a large
   female cohort, parental age, reproductive history, alcohol, drugs, radiation and infection showed no
   association, but periconception smoking was more common in cloacal exstrophy.
Genetics
 • CASPR3 (chromosome 9): breakpoint disruption in the 5′ region — the first suggestion of a genetic basis.
 • 22q11.2 duplication: the most common genetic variant associated with bladder exstrophy–epispadias
   complex (BEEC). No single coding gene within the locus is duplicated in isolation.
 • Risk loci on seven chromosomes.
 • p63 (p53 family): the ΔNp63 isoform drives stem-cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis, and is implicated
   in development of the abdominal and ventral bladder wall. It is under-expressed and dysregulated in
   exstrophic urothelium, but no genomic p63 mutation has been found.

 • Expression profiling: candidate genes cluster in cellular assembly, musculoskeletal development and
   connective tissue; desmosomal/cytoskeletal genes are largely under-expressed. The most downregulated
   genes are desmin and desmuslin, which interact with desmoplakin (overexpressed).
 • ISL1 (5q11.1): a LIM/homeobox transcription factor, confirmed by genome-wide association as a
   susceptibility gene for classic bladder exstrophy (CBE). It regulates genital tubercle factors (Bmp4, Fgf10,
   Wnt5a). Variants may impair its transcription and its interaction with oestrogen receptor α.
Concept — The epidemiology points to a multifactorial, partly heritable and hormone-sensitive field defect:
familial clustering and a single dominant locus both fail to explain it, while environmental modifiers (smoking,
folate, IVF) shift where on the spectrum a fetus lands.

EMBRYOLOGY
 • Core defect: the cloacal membrane fails to be reinforced by mesodermal ingrowth. This bilaminar
   membrane at the caudal germinal disc occupies the infraumbilical wall. Mesenchyme growing between its
   ectoderm and endoderm normally forms the lower abdominal muscles and pelvic bones.
 • Normal sequence: mesenchymal ingrowth → urorectal septum grows caudally and divides the cloaca
   (bladder anterior, rectum posterior) → the septum meets the membrane remnant, which perforates to
   form the urogenital and anal openings → paired genital tubercles migrate medially and fuse cephalad to
   the membrane (Fig. 43.2, 4–16 mm stages).
 • Timing and extent of rupture decide the variant. Classic exstrophy is more than 50% of the complex.
   Cloacal exstrophy is the earlier defect of the same developmental field.

Anatomic Considerations
Bony Pelvis and Spinal Defects
 • Three-dimensional (3D) computed tomography (CT) showed that the pelvis is not simply widened at the
   symphysis.
 • Rotational anomalies: external rotation of the posterior pelvis/iliac wings (mean 12° each side) →
   external rotation of the anterior segment (mean 18° each side) → coronal rotation of the sacroiliac joint
   (about 10° larger than normal; pelvis about 15° more inferiorly rotated) → acetabular retroversion →
   convergence of the iliac wings → femoral retroversion.
 • Dimensional anomalies: increased pubic diastasis; anterior pubic segment shortened by 30%;
   intertriradiate distance increased by about 31%. The posterior segment length is normal. The sacrum has
   larger volume and surface area.
 • Clinical consequences: the rotation contributes to the short, pendular penis; lateral displacement of the
   innominate bones causes wide hips, a waddling gait and outward rotation of the limbs. These cause little
   disability and improve with growth (foot progression angle 20–30° beyond normal early on).
 • Bone histology and endochondral ossification are normal in fetal exstrophy pelves, so the 30% shortfall is
   unexplained. Restoring a closed pelvic ring may normalise growth and load distribution.
 • Cloacal exstrophy: greater deformity, plus right–left asymmetry, sacroiliac malformation and occasional
   hip dislocation.
 • Spine: clinically insignificant variants (spina bifida occulta, lumbarisation or sacralisation) are fairly
   common; scoliosis and dysraphism (myelomeningocele, lipomeningocele, scimitar sacrum,
   hemivertebrae) are uncommon; neurological dysfunction is exceptional.

Pelvic Floor Defects
 • Puborectal sling supports twice the normal cavity area. The levator ani is displaced posteriorly (most of
   it behind the rectum), rotated outward, and flattened from its normal cone into a flat, less dome-shaped
   sheet. Muscle length and thickness are normal (Fig. 43.5: paucity of anterior pelvic floor musculature
   before closure).
 • Levator curvature does not track the size of the diastasis.
 • Postclosure imaging: continent patients have the shortest intersymphyseal distance, the most normal
   levator divergence and the deepest bladder neck. In dry adults the puborectalis angle is under 65°.
 • Closure reshapes the pelvis from a box into an inwardly rotated hammock → shifts levators into the
   anterior compartment → smooths the floor contour. This supports aggressive posterior placement of the
   vesicourethral unit, osteotomy and pelvic fixation.

Abdominal Wall Anomalies
 • Triangular fascial defect containing the exstrophied bladder and posterior urethra. It is bounded
   inferiorly by the intrasymphyseal band (the divergent urogenital diaphragm), which tethers the posterior
   vesicourethral unit to the pubic ramus. The anterior rectus sheath fans behind the urethra and bladder
   neck to insert into this band.
 • No striated sphincter is found in the band. Bladder muscle extends laterally and interdigitates with rectus
   fascia to form a fibrous urogenital diaphragm. Radical incision of these fibres down to the levator hiatus
   is essential; they are often found intact at reclosure of failed repairs.

Chapter 43 — Exstrophy-Epispadias Complex                                                     Classic Bladder Exstrophy
 • Umbilicus sits at the apex, well below the iliac crest line, with a shortened umbilicus-to-anus distance.
   Umbilical hernia, if present, is small and repaired at closure. Omphalocele is rare in CBE (common in
   cloacal exstrophy).
 • Indirect inguinal hernia is common, especially in males, and often bilateral. Causes: patent processus
   vaginalis, large rings, lack of canal obliquity. Repair at closure (excise the sac and repair the transversalis
   fascia and muscle) and explore the contralateral side. Osteotomy at closure lowers both hernia incidence
   and recurrence.

Anorectal Defects
 • Short, broad perineum; the anus is displaced anteriorly, directly behind the urogenital diaphragm, at the
   posterior limit of the fascial defect.
 • Colorectal anomalies about 2% — low, but about 72-fold the general population. Commonest:
   imperforate anus → rectal stenosis → congenital rectal prolapse.
 • The divergent levators and distorted external sphincter give imperfect early anal continence.
 • Rectal prolapse: before closure, prolapse is transient, reducible, resolves with closure and pubic
   apposition, and is an indication to proceed with closure. Prolapse after closure → suspect posterior
   urethral or outlet obstruction → urgent cystoscopy.

Male Genital Defect
 • Anterior corporal length about 50% shorter than controls; posterior corporal length normal; anterior
   corporal diameter about 30% greater. The corpora separate in parallel, so the intercorporal angle is
   unchanged (Fig. 43.7).
 • The penis therefore looks short because of both the pubic diastasis and a true congenital deficiency of
   anterior corporal tissue.
 • Surgical anatomy: triangular, separated corpora; a long convex ventral surface and a short wedge-shaped
   dorsal surface; the lie of the neurovascular bundle on each corpus sets its length.
 • Prostate: in infants, larger transverse and anteroposterior dimensions and volume. In adults, normal
   volume and weight but never circumferential — the urethra lies anterior to the prostate (Fig. 43.8:
   intersymphyseal fibrous band runs along the whole prostate). Periprostatic vessels and sensory bundles
   are externally rotated but follow normal landmarks.
 • Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is measurable but below the age-specific upper limit.
 • Vasa, ejaculatory ducts and seminal vesicles are normal.
 • Cavernous nerves are displaced laterally and almost always preserved (potency is retained). Retrograde
   ejaculation may follow closure or bladder neck reconstruction.
 • Testes: fertility is not thought to be impaired by testicular dysfunction (harvested sperm for IVF had
   normal parameters). Testes often appear undescended but are mostly retractile, with enough cord length
   to reach the scrotum without orchidopexy.

Female Genital Defects
 • Simpler reconstruction than in the male (Fig. 43.9). The short vagina, anterior cervix, stenotic anterior
   orifice, bifid clitoris and divergent labia are as in the Key Points. Tubes and ovaries are normal.
 • At primary closure: join the clitoral halves, and join the labia minora to form a fourchette. In adulthood,
   vaginal dilation or episiotomy may be needed for intercourse.
 • Levator hiatus: normal anteroposterior length but almost twice normal width. This predisposes to
   uterine prolapse — usually after childbirth but also in nulliparous women (mean age of onset in one
   series 16 years), sometimes needing suspension.

 • Osteotomy is not protective; adult diastasis width is the only predictor of prolapse.
 • Clitoral body lies mostly anterior to its pelvic attachment in females with CBE, unlike normal females and
   males with CBE (Fig. 43.10).

Urinary Defects
 • Newborn mucosa is usually normal but may carry hamartomatous polyps. Irrigate with saline and cover
   with a protective membrane to prevent cystic or metaplastic change.
 • Size, distensibility and neuromuscular function of the plate, and the size of the fascial defect, decide
   whether repair is attempted.
 • The newborn exstrophic bladder is immature, not neuropathic:
     ◦ Muscarinic receptors: normal density and affinity.
     ◦ Collagen-to-smooth-muscle ratio: raised (type III collagen increased threefold, type I unchanged). It
       falls after successful infection-free closure and approaches normal by adulthood, and stiffness falls
       with it.
     ◦ Myelinated nerves: reduced through loss of small fibres, with large fibres preserved.
     ◦ Immunohistochemical neural markers (vasoactive intestinal polypeptide, neuropeptide Y, substance P,
       calcitonin gene–related peptide, protein gene product 9.5, NADPH diaphorase): no dysinnervation.
     ◦ → a maturational delay with potential for normal development after successful closure.
 • Small, fibrotic, inelastic, polyp-covered plate → functional repair may be impossible (Fig. 43.11). Polyps
   are fibrotic or oedematous; about half carry squamous metaplasia; von Brunn nests, cystitis cystica and
   cystitis glandularis occur, the last more often in secondary closures → adenocarcinoma risk → urine
   cytology and cystoscopy into adulthood. Polyps after a failed closure impede growth.
 • A bladder that is invaginated or bulges through a small defect signals good potential capacity. True size is
   judged only under anaesthesia, because pelvic depth cannot otherwise be assessed.
 • Urodynamics: most continent patients have normal cystometrograms, and most bladders are compliant
   and stable before bladder neck reconstruction. After bladder neck reconstruction compliance and
   stability fall, and only a minority keep normal detrusor function. Series disagree, probably because
   bladder neck techniques are not standardised.
 • Ultrastructure (Fig. 43.12): caveolae (cell–cell signalling) are normal after successful closure but deficient
   in bladders that later need augmentation; failed closures show collagen deposition and degenerating
   muscle.
 • Smooth-muscle cell biology: actin and myosin are normal. Exstrophy cells migrate more, driven by
   transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) and not by calcium; proliferation is equal. Findings on
   intracellular calcium and contractile force conflict between studies. Delayed-closure cells show less
   proliferation (Ki67) and more autophagy and apoptosis.
 • Urothelium: squamous metaplasia, inflammation and loss of transitional differentiation. Uroplakin-II (the
   barrier plaque protein) is reduced, most in delayed and repeat closures, and metaplasia is commoner
   after delayed closure → argues for early closure. Even after successful infant closure only a minority show
   a fully differentiated phenotype (uroplakin 3a, cytokeratins 13 and 20, claudin 4). Tight-junction barrier
   formation in culture is variable, and squamous lines usually fail — a possible transcriptional/genetic basis.
 • Upper tracts usually normal; renal anomalies are uncommon (commonest: duplex system; also
   hypoplastic or absent, pelvic, ureteropelvic junction obstruction, multicystic dysplastic), but more
   frequent than in the general population.

 • Ureteric course: an enlarged, deep pouch of Douglas forces the ureter laterally and down, so it enters
   from below and lateral with little or no obliquity → vesicoureteric reflux (VUR) in 100% of closed
   bladders → reimplantation at bladder neck reconstruction.
 • Excess outlet resistance at closure → recurrent infection despite prophylaxis → reimplantation and/or
   outlet revision may be needed before bladder neck reconstruction.
 • Staged reconstruction does not harm renal function.
Concept — Every anatomical abnormality traces to an open, externally rotated pelvic ring with an anteriorly
displaced floor: short penis, wide hips, anterior anus, flat levators, oblique-less ureters and prolapse all
follow, which is why closing the ring deep in the pelvis is the recurring surgical theme. The bladder itself is
immature, not defective — its fate is set by what happens after birth.

 • Additional quantitative signs: umbilical cord insertion-to-genital tubercle distance below the 5th centile
   for gestation; pubic diastasis over 10 mm.

SURGICAL RECONSTRUCTION OF BLADDER EXSTROPHY
 • Evolution: 1950s staged approach (iliac osteotomies 4–6 days before closure; separate epispadias repair;
   intrasymphyseal band wrapped round the urethra) → 1970s three stages: closure → bladder neck
   reconstruction with antireflux → epispadias repair.
 • Modern staged repair of exstrophy (MSRE): newborn bladder, abdominal wall and posterior urethral
   closure well onto the penile shaft (± bilateral innominate and vertical iliac osteotomy) → epispadias repair
   at 6–12 months → bladder neck reconstruction with antireflux at 4–7 years, once capacity is adequate
   and the child can join a voiding programme.
 • Alternatives: combined closure with penile repair in the newborn (complete primary repair); staged
   repair without osteotomy using radical soft-tissue mobilisation (Kelly); total newborn repair (Erlangen);
   ureterosigmoidostomy with closure (Mainz).

Evaluation and Management at Birth
 • Mucosa is smooth but easily denuded. Sequence: tie the cord with 2-0 silk close to the wall (a clamp
   traumatises mucosa) → cover with non-adherent plastic wrap → at each nappy change remove it, irrigate
   with sterile saline and replace it (Fig. 43.15). Keep it covered during transfer.
 • Counselling starts prenatally, from a surgeon experienced in exstrophy. The team includes paediatric
   orthopaedics, paediatric anaesthesia, social work, specialist nurses and a psychiatrist or psychologist.
   Parent support association available.
 • Cardiopulmonary assessment in the first hours; renal ultrasound suffices before closure. Modern
   transport keeps any neonate within hours of a full centre.

Selection of Patients for Immediate Closure
 • An experienced exstrophy surgeon decides at birth. Detrusor size and functional capacity drive success,
   but apparent size is not potential capacity: a small plate that bulges on crying or indents easily under
   anaesthesia may be adequate, and extra bladder may be found behind the fascia.
 • Once relieved of surface trauma, a small bladder enlarges under minimal outlet resistance. Robust infant
   + good template + experienced team → strongly consider newborn closure.
 • Uroplakin-II is higher after newborn than delayed closure → early closure may potentiate bladder
   growth.

The Inadequate Bladder Template Unsuitable for Newborn Closure
 • Inadequate = under 3 cm diameter, polyp-covered, or inelastic/fibrotic. Examination under anaesthesia is
   often needed because oedema, excoriation and polyps develop after birth.
 • Conditions precluding primary closure: penoscrotal duplication; hypoplastic bladder; significant bilateral
   hydronephrosis; ectopic bowel within the plate (relative contraindication).
 • Delayed primary closure (DPC) for small templates: all such patients had osteotomy and closure
   succeeded. Continence procedures were as frequent and as successful as in controls, but the first
   continence procedure came later, continence came later, and a catheterisable channel was needed far
   more often. Capacities were somewhat smaller, but annual growth rates were similar.
 • Longitudinal data: delayed closure gives lower capacities, with the steepest losses at 4–6 months and
   beyond 9 months. Short-term complications (transfusion, infection, dehiscence) rise slightly but are
   manageable.
 • Timing: close as early as possible; if a delay is needed for growth, close at 6–9 months. Waiting 6 months
   or more is safer than closing a small plate badly (dehiscence → future incontinence). Delay, when
   indicated, does not jeopardise growth.
 • No adequate growth by 6–12 months → excise the bladder and use a non-refluxing colon conduit or
   ureterosigmoidostomy.

Pelvic Osteotomy
 • Mean pubic diastasis 4.8 cm, an open pelvic ring. Larger template → wider diastasis → greater need for
   osteotomy. Hip function is normal; external foot rotation lessens with growth even without osteotomy.
 • Aims: close the ring, relieve tension on the abdominal closure, improve later reconstruction, and reduce
   primary and recurrent inguinal hernia. First described in 1958 (bilateral posterior iliac osteotomy 1–3
   weeks before closure), it lowered dehiscence rates.
 • When: at any stage if the diastasis blocks the urological goals.
     ◦ Newborn: not routine unless the diastasis is over 4 cm or the pelvis is poorly malleable (judged jointly
       by a senior paediatric urologist and a paediatric orthopaedic surgeon, under anaesthesia).
     ◦ Closure after 72 hours of age → osteotomy.
     ◦ Under 72 hours with malleable pubes that meet on medial rotation of the greater trochanters →
       closure without osteotomy. If in doubt, osteotomise.
     ◦ Sacroiliac ligament laxity allows tension-free closure in the first 48–72 hours.
 • Newborn osteotomy is safe: orthopaedic complications are rare. Newborns with osteotomy fail at a rate
   similar to carefully selected newborns without it.
 • Always required after a failed closure with wide diastasis, in large templates and in cloacal exstrophy.
   Extreme diastasis (over 6 cm) may need staged pelvic closure after osteotomy.

Complications of Osteotomy and Immobilisation Techniques
 • Inadequate immobilisation → failed closure, bladder prolapse, loss of suprapubic tubes and stents. Pain
   and movement control are equally important: tunnelled epidural for 2–3 weeks while callus forms.
 • Combined osteotomy complications: transient left femoral nerve palsy (full recovery by 12 weeks; none
   on the right despite the same surgeon; managed with bed rest then a knee immobiliser), delayed iliac
   union, wound and pin-site infection, transient abductor weakness and peroneal palsy. Pin-site
   inflammation is almost universal, especially at the iliac crest, and is controlled with oral antibiotics.

 • Partial recurrence of diastasis even after osteotomy, by two mechanisms: (1) early pin loosening before
   healing, mostly in infants (denser bone in older children gives more rigid fixation); (2) undergrowth of the
   ischiopubic segment (a third smaller in adults) with growth. Uncorrected diastasis widens with growth, so
   correction is still net positive. Classic exstrophy gains more correction than cloacal.
 • The main role of osteotomy is to relieve tension on the bladder, posterior urethra and abdominal wall
   during healing. It is used less in newborns (ligament laxity) and is essential for failed repairs, cloacal
   exstrophy, and newborns with a wide diastasis and an excellent template. In combined closure with
   epispadias repair it lets the corpora be brought over the urethra.

Kelly Repair
 • Developed to avoid osteotomy and improve on an earlier soft-tissue technique. The neourethra is made
   from the urethral plate dissected off the corpora (as in penile disassembly), but the meatus is always
   placed at the penoscrotal junction first and moved distally later.
 • Single-stage delayed closure with Kelly mobilisation without osteotomy: all closed without dehiscence,
   but fistula or stenosis was common. Kelly plus anterior component separation can close large defects
   without osteotomy.

Complete Repair: Complete Primary Repair of Exstrophy
 • Aim: fewer procedures, and continence without formal bladder neck reconstruction.
 • Penile disassembly (Fig. 43.24): penis separated into right corpus with hemiglans, left corpus with
   hemiglans, and urethral wedge (plate with its spongiosum). Sequence: ventral start (as in Cantwell-
   Ransley) → medially above Buck fascia down to tunica albuginea → preserve spongiosum → carry
   posteriorly to the bladder neck → urethra between the corpora into a hypospadiac position → corpora
   sutured in the midline → hypospadias-style skin closure → proximal dissection into the pelvis for
   concomitant closure, plus bladder neck tailoring.
 • Now often modified — the plate is left attached to the glans (resembling modified Cantwell-Ransley)
   because of soft-tissue loss after complete disassembly.
 • Females: more urinary retention when CPRE is done after 72 hours (longer plate, narrower neck; the
   urethra is compressed at pubic approximation, risking ischaemia). Delayed CPRE → a gradual tapered
   transition with a wider bladder neck and a shorter plate.

 • Perineal membrane dissected off the pubis to set the unit deep — a principle common to all good

 • Drawbacks: most boys are left hypospadiac; about half need ureteric reimplantation in the first year.

Schrott-Erlangen Approach
 • The most evolved single-stage approach (details in the table). Long-term (about 20-year) follow-up: most
   objectively continent, with mostly normal renal function.

Mainz Repair
 • Began with failed closures and small plates, then applied to all newborns from 1964 regardless of
   template. In girls: genital reconstruction plus anterior uterine fixation. Usually only one later cosmetic
   operation.
 • Principles common to every method: (1) radical mobilisation of the posterior vesicourethral unit; (2)
   combined closure with epispadias repair only in very carefully selected patients; (3) tension-free closure
   of the abdominal wall and pelvis, with osteotomy if needed; (4) strict selection criteria for newborn
   closure.

Modern Staged Reconstruction of Bladder Exstrophy
 • Objective: convert exstrophy into complete epispadias with the urethra on the proximal or mid shaft.
   Balanced posterior outlet resistance preserves the kidneys and stimulates bladder growth.
 • Epispadias repair about 6 months, after testosterone; bladder neck repair at 4–5 years with adequate
   capacity and, above all, readiness for a voiding programme. Europe is trending towards staged repair.
 • Trap: Erlangen, Kelly and CPRE were claimed to avoid bladder neck repair, but almost all patients still
   need an outlet procedure.

Bladder, Posterior Urethral, and Abdominal Wall Closure
Sequence (Fig. 43.20): outline a 2-cm-wide mucosal strip from the distal trigone to below the
verumontanum and on to the mid-shaft (male) or to the vaginal orifice (female) → incise above the

fibres → divide the urogenital diaphragm fibres sharply with cautery down to the levator hiatus → mobilise
the plate up to the prostate if left in continuity → free the corpora from the suspensory ligaments and

 • Tapering purpose: narrow and lengthen the unit so it sits deep — enough resistance to aid adaptation
   and prevent prolapse, not enough to harm the upper tracts. This becomes the site of the later bladder
   neck repair.
 • Stents 10–14 days (up to 2–3 weeks): closing a small bladder causes swelling that can obstruct the ureters
   and cause transient hypertension. The urethra is not stented because secretions accumulate and cause
   necrosis.
 • Broad-spectrum antibiotics before and during surgery convert a contaminated field to a clean one.
 • The commonest error: inadequate dissection of the urogenital diaphragm. Most failed closures,
   including repeatedly failed ones, have intact fibres. The unit is then pulled anteriorly when the pubes
   close, which increases prolapse risk and gives a poor position for later epispadias repair. 3D MRI-guided
   dissection improves accuracy and safety (Figs. 43.21–43.22: fibres run along the medial and

Combined Bladder Closure and Epispadias Repair
 • First tried in the 1960s and abandoned in the 1970s for complications. Revived for failed closures and,
   later, for newborns. It combines closure with a modified Cantwell-Ransley repair, suits delayed closures
   and failed closures, and does not create hypospadias.
 • Limit to boys older than 6 months because of the risk of penile and corporal tissue loss. Select by phallic
   size and length, depth of the urethral groove, template size (delayed closure) and perivesical and urethral
   scarring (failed closure). Not for the occasional exstrophy surgeon.

Management After Primary Closure
 • MSRE leaves mid-shaft epispadias with incontinence. About 3 days in intensive care; tunnelled epidural
   lidocaine plus patient-controlled analgesia.
 • At 4 weeks, before suprapubic tube removal: calibrate the outlet (catheter or sound) → full ultrasound →
   clamp the tube to estimate residual → urine culture. Continuous antibiotic prophylaxis, since all have
   VUR.
 • Ultrasound surveillance: 3 months after discharge, then every 6–12 months for 2–3 years. Yearly
   cystoscopy and cystography under anaesthesia for reflux and growth — capacity in an awake, crying
   infant is unreliable.
 • Capacity under 30 mL by 1–2 years → warn parents that a continence procedure may not be feasible.
   Best predictors: template size at birth plus a successful, infection-free primary closure. A useful
   continence interval after closure alone is unusual.
 • Outlet too tight (retention, reflux, dilatation, infected urine): urethral dilation or intermittent
   catheterisation → transurethral incision of a posterior stricture → early antireflux surgery (6–12 months)
   → surgical revision (skin-flap advancement or patch) only if these fail.
 • Recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI) plus distended bladder → cystoscopy looking for intrapubic stitch
   erosion into the posterior urethra → remove it transurethrally or through a small suprapubic incision.
 • Upper tracts stay acceptable with prophylaxis and residuals kept under 50 mL. Glomerular filtration rate
   (GFR) after bladder neck reconstruction is no worse than before closure.
 • Diversion is seldom needed if an adequate outlet was built and follow-up is meticulous.

Selected Technical Aspects of Other Methods of Closure
Kelly Repair
Stage 1: close the bladder to the posterior urethra with no pubic apposition. Stage 2 (radical soft-tissue
mobilisation): incise round the old closure with parallel cuts on the plate to halfway between the ureteric
orifices and the verumontanum → excise lateral mucosa → expose the dorsal penile nerves and corporal
attachments → move the plate beneath the corpora (penoscrotal hypospadias) → enter the extraperitoneal
space behind the rectus and retract the vas → cut a “flake” of superior pubis carrying the corpora and
urogenital diaphragm remnants → incise the levator through to its pubic insertion → free the pudendal
vessels and nerves in the ischiorectal fossa → bring the urethral and bladder neck muscles together without
tension (Fig. 43.23).

Mitchell Repair
 • Often done after a few months of age. The urethra is freed from the corpora and pelvic diaphragm at the
   first stage, which Mitchell argues places the bladder neck deeper. Originally thought sufficient for
   continence — most still need bladder neck repair.
 • The penis is disassembled into three parts on the premise that each corpus has its own blood supply, but
   the plate depends on the spongiosum → ischaemia risk. It is now modified to keep the plate attached to
   the glans.
 • Intersymphyseal band incised medial to the inferior pubis down to the levator hiatus (as in MSRE) →
   bladder neck tailored → urethra taken towards the tip. If it falls short (most patients), the meatus is left
   hypospadiac at the base.
 • Females: bladder neck, urethra and vagina mobilised as one unit.
 • Osteotomy (as MSRE, unlike Kelly) if over 3 days old or the diastasis is excessive.

Epispadias Repair
 • Order reversed historically: bladder neck reconstruction used to precede urethroplasty. The discovery
   that epispadias repair increases bladder capacity (via outlet resistance) moved it earlier (Fig. 43.19:
   marked capacity gain after urethroplasty).
 • Now performed at 6–10 months in all. Completion before 12 months gives better eventual capacity.
 • Testosterone before every urethroplasty (small penis, scarce skin): testosterone enanthate 2 mg/kg
   intramuscularly, 5 weeks and 2 weeks before.
 • Current techniques: Cantwell-Ransley (1989), modified Cantwell-Ransley (1995), Mitchell–Bägli penile
   disassembly (1996).
 • Four key concerns: (1) correct dorsal chordee; (2) reconstruct the urethra; (3) reconstruct the glans; (4)
   close penile skin.
 • Formal elongation and chordee release are always needed at urethroplasty: excise suspensory ligament
   remnants and old scar, and free the corpora further from the inferior rami (surprisingly little is achieved at
   initial closure). Gains are apparent, not true.
 • Complete disassembly: in over two-thirds the plate does not reach the tip → hypospadias needing
   another operation.

Chordee
 • Options: dorsomedial corporal incision and anastomosis; dermal graft lengthening of the dorsum.

Urethral Reconstruction
 • Abandoned methods: tubularisation of the dorsal groove in place; free genital or extragenital skin grafts;
   ventral transverse island flaps; double-faced island flaps.
 • Modern principle: tubularise the plate and move it beneath the corpora → fewer fistulae, a downward
   penile deflection and an easily catheterisable channel.
 • Hypospadias repair after disassembly: reported as easy by some, but a large European series found a

 • Preferred repair if the groove is long enough: modified Cantwell-Ransley (urethra already closed well up

Modified Cantwell-Ransley sequence (Fig. 43.25): transverse glans traction stitch → parallel incisions

 • Result: the urethra is displaced ventrally → downward deflection, with some added length from dorsal
   rotation of the corpora.

Penile Skin Closure
 • Z-plasty at the penile base prevents contraction and upward tethering. Ventral foreskin is split into lateral
   preputial flaps brought dorsally (asymmetry staggers the suture line, which helps) or buttonholed and
   transposed to the dorsum.

Postoperative Problems
 • Bladder spasm control is paramount — spasms cause extravasation and fistula. Caudal epidural plus
   oxybutynin from the end of surgery. Discharged with the plastic dressing intact, opiates, antispasmodics
   and broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Female Exstrophy
 • Principles: (1) radical dissection of bladder and urethra keeping the vaginal connection; (2) free the
   lateral vesicourethral–vaginal unit from the urogenital diaphragm; (3) tension-free closure of abdomen,
   bladder–urethra and genitalia; (4) judicious osteotomy and proper immobilisation.
 • Osteotomy if pubes over 4 cm apart or not malleable. The diastasis is narrower in females even with a
   large template → closure is easier and osteotomy is needed less often than in males.

Continence and Antireflux Procedure
 • Many patients arrive still incontinent after newborn repair alone or after repeated bladder neck
   injections; most groups recommend some outlet procedure.
 • Yearly cystoscopy and gravity cystogram under anaesthesia after closure. Expected growth after
   newborn closure is about 54 mL over 24 months. Median capacity about 100 mL is typical in those who
   end up completely dry; they are usually 5–7 years old and developmentally ready.
 • Pre-bladder-neck programme (nurse practitioner, nurses, child psychologist) starts at least 6 months
   before; completing it is a prerequisite for surgery.
 • Reflux is managed conservatively with prophylaxis until bladder neck reconstruction; earlier
   reimplantation for pyelonephritis, recurrent UTI, worsening hydronephrosis or falling renal function.
Modified Young-Dees-Leadbetter (YDL) sequence (Fig. 43.26): open by a transverse incision at the bladder

   bar; the bar may be divided for exposure (repair with 2-0 polydioxanone) and abduction then restricted

 • Ureteric stents through the bladder wall; suprapubic tube 3 weeks; at 4 weeks clamp for no more than 1

Concept — Staged reconstruction is a sequence of controlled outlet resistances: enough after closure to

MODERN INITIAL REPAIR OF BLADDER EXSTROPHY: OUTCOMES AND RESULTS
 • Functional reconstruction transformed outcomes; early newborn closure with or without osteotomy is
   well established, with acceptable continence and preserved renal function in most.
 • Two most reliable predictors of eventual continence: template size at birth and a successful primary
   closure. Referred patients whose primary closure elsewhere succeeded mostly became continent after
   bladder neck repair by a second surgeon → success of the primary closure matters more than who
   performed it.

Initial Closure
 • Successful primary closure (with or without osteotomy) brings quicker and higher continence.
 • Each failed closure erodes potential: after two closures, well over half still reach adequate capacity but
   fewer than 1 in 5 gain voided continence; after three, adequate capacity drops to half. Most multiply-
   closed patients had no osteotomy at their first closure.
 • Closure before 1 year: far fewer need eventual augmentation than when closed later.
 • CPRE complications are comparable in frequency to MSRE but more serious: fascial dehiscence,
   vesicocutaneous fistula, early reimplantation for upper-tract change (prompting calls for reimplantation
   at closure), major prolapse or dehiscence even with osteotomy, and soft-tissue loss — partial or
   complete glans loss, loss of the urethrovaginal septum.
 • Erlangen: mild complications (occasional fistula, minimal hydronephrosis, rare severe hydronephrosis
   needing surgery), no prolapse or dehiscence, with no osteotomy but a sophisticated obturator-foramen
   coaptation technique.
 • Volume and training: high-volume hospitals (more than 5 closures a year) have lower costs. A successful
   newborn closure costs far less than a reclosure. Reclosures by fellowship-trained paediatric urologists
   achieve better success and continence than those by general urologists, non-fellowship paediatric
   urologists or paediatric surgeons.

Epispadias Repair
 • Incontinence is the biggest problem, but genital appearance is the greatest concern for males; many
   adolescents rate an odd-looking penis as worse than incontinence.
 • Modified Cantwell-Ransley outcomes (CBE and epispadias): most penises horizontal or downward on
   standing; early fistula about 1 in 6, falling by 3 months; proximal anastomotic strictures and dorsal skin
   separation occasional; a negotiable, catheterisable channel in all; a few need further straightening;
   sexually active adults report orgasm and ejaculation with a straight erection. From 1994 the plate is
   detached from the corona except the distal 0.5–1 cm within the glans, to bury the urethra deeper.
 • Ransley's reverse MAGPI concept (1989): dorsomedial corporal incision and anastomosis plus distal
   meatotomy. Long-term fistula and stricture rates are low (about 5%).
 • Why coronal fistula is rare: dissecting the strip inside the glans places the urethra ventrally, beneath raw
   glans tissue apposed over it (unlike the Young repair). Fistulae occur at the penile base, where the
   urethra rises between the corpora. With scarce tissue (failed closure or repair, recurrent fistula) → tunica
   vaginalis flap.
 • Paraexstrophy skin flaps cause strictures → reserved for when standard lengthening fails; most surgeons
   preserve the plate at closure.
 • Mitchell–Bägli disassembly: fistula and stricture rates similar, but a large share are made hypospadiac
   (reports range roughly from over a third to over four-fifths). Ischaemic loss of glans, plate and corpora is
   reported; the largest series links it mainly to closure without osteotomy → pudendal vessel compression

 • Urodynamics: continent patients have higher capacity and compliance. Unstable contractions occur in
   both continent and incontinent groups. High end-filling pressure is linked to non-obstructive
   hydronephrosis → lifelong follow-up.

Failed Genitourethral Reconstruction
 • Urethrocutaneous fistula is the common complication (roughly 4–19%). Tortuosity or stricture impeding
   catheterisation is uncommon.
 • Penile disassembly introduced graver losses: glans, corpora, penile skin and plate (Fig. 43.29C) — from

 • Older patients (scars, short phallus): excise scar and close primarily, or use flaps or full-thickness grafts.

Alternative Techniques of Reconstruction
 • Indications: plate too small or significant hydronephrosis at presentation, a failed closure leaving a small
   bladder, or failed continence surgery.

Ureterosigmoidostomy
 • First diversion popularised for exstrophy. Upper tracts are initially normal, so normal-calibre ureters can
   be reimplanted non-refluxing. Metabolic problems fell with better techniques. Attraction: no stoma.
 • Prerequisites: normal anal continence and informed family.
 • Complications: pyelonephritis, hyperchloraemic acidosis, rectal incontinence, ureteric obstruction,
   delayed malignancy.
 • Long-term: many remain in place and continent decades later with mostly normal renal function, but
   colorectal cancer occurs — almost all poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, typically about three
   decades after diversion. Rates differ between European series → lifelong vigilance.
 • Mainz sigma pouch: non-refluxing reimplants; abdominal wall reconstructed; bladder kept as a seminal
   receptacle above the prostate; penis reconstructed later. Continence in a rectal reservoir is excellent.

Continent Urinary Diversion in the Exstrophy Patient
 • Incontinent diversion is rarely needed. Bladder too small to close or to reclose → non-refluxing colon
   conduit (protects kidneys from VUR; undiversion possible later).
 • Criteria for bladder neck reconstruction not met (small capacity, cannot follow a voiding regimen) →
   defer. Failed bladder neck reconstruction → augmentation plus continent diversion, typically with
   bladder neck closure, often ileal or sigmoid cystoplasty with an appendiceal channel. Catheterised
   continence is achieved in the great majority; bladder stones are the commonest complication.
 • Failure modes: nipple-valve de-intussusception, inadequate appendiceal tunnel, persisting bladder neck
   incompetence — nearly all repairable. Good outcomes even after many prior operations.
 • Early diversion (5 years or younger) is driven by upper-tract change and social factors (early wish to be
   dry, severe hydronephrosis, pyelonephritis). It is safe and gives good continence; all stoma patients are dry
   on catheterisation.
 • Unlikely to reach capacity for bladder neck repair → move early to continent diversion.
 • Malignancy after continent diversion is low unless there are co-existing carcinogens (tobacco, chronic
   immunosuppression) or the inherent exstrophy risk.

 KEY POINTS: CONTINENT URINARY DIVERSION IN THE EXSTROPHY PATIENT — INDICATIONS
   • Failed closures that do not grow after reclosure
   • Newborn closures whose bladders do not grow
   • No dryness or inadequate capacity after bladder neck reconstruction
   • Upper-tract decompensation after bladder neck reconstruction or primary closure

Concept — Failure compounds: each failed closure lowers the capacity ceiling, which makes bladder neck
failure likelier, which ends in augmentation and a stoma. Management therefore pivots on recognising early
which bladders will not grow and diverting them before the upper tracts or the child pay the price.

TRANSITIONAL EXSTROPHY PATIENT: ADOLESCENT AND ADULT CONCERNS
 • Lifelong functional and psychological needs: sexuality and continence are common to the whole
   complex; cloacal exstrophy adds neurological and orthopaedic disability.
 • Renal preservation is good after functional reconstruction but can suffer after diversion. Temporary
   diversion for decompression is usually followed by undiversion to continent pouches; long-term diversion
   is now infrequent.

CONTINENCE
 • Early era: cystectomy with ileal conduit or ureterosigmoidostomy. Ureterosigmoidostomy fell out of
   favour because of lethal colonic cancer, although renal function was mostly preserved.
 • Now: continent catheterisable pouches in some centres; mostly augmentation cystoplasty with bladder
   neck reconstruction or closure. With 10 or more years' follow-up, most show no GFR decline, and
   declines had remediable causes → augmentation does not compromise long-term renal function.

CANCER RISK
 • Exstrophy patients with augmentation develop multifocal adenocarcinoma disproportionately → an
   inherent exstrophy risk. DNA methylation studies do not show a strong epigenetic mechanism.
 • Smoking and post-transplant immunosuppression increase risk. Endoscopic surveillance from 5 years
   after reconstruction is suggested, though early lesions are hard to see in an augmented bladder.
 • Faecal incontinence persisting into adulthood has been reported (not confirmed by others) → keep
   evaluating.

MALE CONCERNS

Sexuality
 • Main concerns: length, appearance and chordee. Functional length is gained by degloving, excising all
   residual scar and releasing suspensory ligament remnants.
 • Skin cover: tissue expansion (pre-planned, 4–6 weeks) or full-thickness grafting (Fig. 43.30).
 • Residual dorsal chordee: dorsal grafts (dermis, tunica vaginalis, human acellular dermis — preferred as
   available, durable and easy). Open the penis dorsally for half its circumference. Measure with artificial
   erection before grafting, because saline leaks through suture lines afterwards and misleads.
 • Cloacal exstrophy: tiny corpora → flap phalloplasty; retain all native corporal and glans tissue as an
   anchor.
 • After assisted lengthening or neophalloplasty: penile perception and sexual activity rise; most are
   satisfied; erectile scores and satisfaction are similar across techniques.
 • Libido and sexual function are normal; erections are preserved after epispadias repair; orgasm is
   satisfactory in most; partners are satisfied; absent ejaculation mainly after cystectomy.
 • Trap: questionnaire studies show more erectile dysfunction (worse after multiple continence operations)
   and lower orgasmic scores, yet desire and satisfaction match peers. Most men are sexually active.
 • Adult care: transition to adult urology. PSA runs below age norms (changes may be muted) → routine
   digital rectal examination and PSA screening as for other men, plus prostatic hyperplasia assessment.

Male Fertility
 • Paternity is historically rare. Recent surveys show about a quarter of men achieve paternity, half of them
   with assisted reproductive technology (ART). Antegrade urethral milking has achieved pregnancy.
 • Functional closure vs diversion: normal sperm counts are commoner after diversion → iatrogenic
   verumontanum injury during closure or bladder neck reconstruction, plus retrograde ejaculation.
 • Low ejaculate volume, azoospermia and oligoasthenospermia are frequent; raised follicle-stimulating
   hormone in some. Genital reconstruction with urethral closure carries high infertility risk. Fewer bladder
   neck operations → better sperm counts.
 • Gamete intrafallopian transfer (GIFT) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) → successful
   pregnancies with no exstrophy in offspring.

 • Usually confined to genital deformity, pubic diastasis and a deficient continence mechanism. Renal

Surgical Management
 • Aims: continence with upper-tract preservation, and acceptable genitalia. Incontinence management
   mirrors closed exstrophy. First cure of male complete epispadias incontinence: Young, 1922.
 • Good capacity → epispadias repair and bladder neck reconstruction can be done in one stage.
 • Small bladder → urethroplasty and penile elongation first, since a small incontinent refluxing bladder is a
   poor setting for bladder neck repair. After repair capacity rises substantially within about 18 months, and
   continence after the later bladder neck repair is high.
 • Capacity is the dominant predictor: adequate vs inadequate pre-repair capacity gives markedly different

   bladder flaps, Marshall-Marchetti suspension): they raise resistance but do not correct the anatomy.
 • A third of incontinent epispadias bladders are under 60 mL. Augmentation, polytetrafluoroethylene
   injection and combined neck reconstruction with augmentation have been tried. Primary urethral closure
   increases capacity without hydronephrosis.
 • Timing: urethral and genital reconstruction at 6–12 months; bladder neck reconstruction at 4–5 years
   (capacity growth and toilet-training readiness).
 • Small series: genitoplasty with percutaneous bladder neck suspension (the neck moved intra-
   abdominally) had mixed results; urethroplasty with double-breasted perineal musculature gave
   impressive continence.

Surgical Results
 • Continence about 87.5% in the authors' girls, comparable with other series of adequate-capacity
   bladders. All primary patients exceeded 80 mL; after prior urethral and genital reconstruction, mean
   capacity at bladder neck repair about 120 mL → no augmentation or catheterisation needed.

   months when urethroplasty came separately → no speed advantage for staging, but the capacity gain
   outweighs it. Delay may reflect pelvic muscle development.
 • Female epispadias vs CBE: epispadias girls are reconstructed later, but bladder growth, procedure

CLOACAL EXSTROPHY
 • Primarily an anterior abdominal wall defect; incidence 1 in 200,000–400,000. Sex ratio previously 2:1
   male, but probably 1:1.
 • Mostly sporadic (occasional unbalanced translocations; sibling recurrence suggests multifactorial causes).
   Associations: ART, preterm labour, multifetal pregnancy, maternal smoking. Mothers were more
   compliant with preconception folate.
 • OEIS complex = Omphalocele, Exstrophy, Imperforate anus, Spinal defects.
 • Survival is now into adulthood → focus has shifted to QoL.

Anatomic Considerations
 • Classic constellation: bladder exstrophy as two hemibladders, complete phallic separation, wide diastasis,
   exstrophied terminal ileum between the hemibladders, rudimentary hindgut, imperforate anus,
   omphalocele; often spinal and lower-limb defects. Urogenital anomalies resemble CBE but are more
   severe.

Neurospinal Abnormalities
 • Spinal cord or vertebral anomalies in 85–100%: lumbar myelodysplasia about 80%, thoracic about 10%,
   the rest sacral. Types: lipomeningocele (commonest), myelomeningocele, spina bifida, isolated tethering.
 • Image every patient: MRI suggested for all; spinal ultrasound is comparable in infants. Only about 1 in 5
   dysraphisms shows a sacral skin sign.
 • Mechanism: disruption of dorsal mesenchyme (or mechanical pulling apart of cord and vertebrae), not
   neural tube non-closure.
 • Function ranges from near-normal to wheelchair-bound. A significant neurological deficit sharply
   reduces the chance of voided continence.
 • Autonomic bladder innervation arises more medially → at risk during dissection → neuropathic bladder
   after reconstruction. Corporal nerves arise from the sacral plexus, run in the midline, perforate the pelvic
   floor and pass medial to the hemibladders (Fig. 43.36: also caval duplication). Neural elements are
   histologically abnormal compared with CBE.

Skeletal System Abnormalities
 • Universal. The most extreme diastasis in the complex: posterior segment angled further back, greater
   anterior external rotation, asymmetry, sacroiliac malformation. Bone segment lengths are similar to CBE
   and bone histology is normal (growth potential normal) → most need osteotomy.
 • Other: vertebral anomalies without myelodysplasia; limb absence or shortening; clubfoot (commonest
   limb defect); tibial and fibular deformity; congenital hip dislocation; excess hip abduction.

Intestinal Tract Abnormalities
 • Omphalocele in 88–100%, containing small bowel and/or liver → close in the neonatal period to prevent
   rupture.
 • Associated gastrointestinal anomalies common (malrotation, duplication, anatomically short bowel
   equally). Hindgut remnant in most.
 • Short gut syndrome in about a quarter, even with normal small-bowel length (absorptive dysfunction)
   → preserve all large bowel. An unused hindgut is kept for urogenital reconstruction. Modern techniques
   have reduced short gut.

Genitourinary Abnormalities
 • Müllerian anomalies in most females: uterine (commonest) and cervical duplication, often bicornuate;
   vaginal duplication, agenesis, or atresia/hypoplasia; rarely lateral vaginal displacement; complete uterine
   and tubal duplication. Imaging may miss structures found at surgery. Treatment includes vaginoplasty,
   septal incision, colporrhaphy and hysterectomy. Preserve all müllerian duplications for lower-tract
   reconstruction.
 • Upper-tract anomalies 41–60%; males more often; abnormal müllerian anatomy → about tenfold renal
   anomaly risk → mesonephric duct dysfunction as a common culprit. Solitary kidney commonest, then
   ectopic and malrotated; less often multicystic dysplastic, UVJ obstruction, duplex and fusion. Pelvic kidney,
   agenesis, hydronephrosis and hydroureter in up to a third. Ectopic ureters to vas, uterus, vagina or tube;
   duplication, congenital stricture, megaureter.
 • Male genitalia: completely separated, often asymmetric phallic halves and split scrotum; testes often
   undescended; inguinal hernias common. Females: widely divergent clitoral halves.

Cloacal Exstrophy Variants
 • Standard form: everted bowel between hemibladders, omphalocele, blind tailgut with imperforate anus,
   wide diastasis, bifid phallus, split scrotum or labia; bifid vagina with two uteri. Treatment has moved from
   palliating an almost universally fatal disorder to complex reconstruction with a near-normal lifespan.
 • Variants (mostly skin-covered; some with bowel and hemibladder duplication) need combined
   genitograms, retrograde ureterograms and bowel continence studies to define anatomy. They have
   fewer spinal anomalies and better faecal continence (pelvic floor innervation permits a Peña pull-
   through) and fewer comorbidities → treatment can markedly improve QoL.
 • Intravesical phallus (Fig. 43.38): a rare variant in genotypic males — paired, single or corporal-like tissue
   anywhere on the bladder plate (usually caudal), with erectile-type histology. Apparent aphallia → inspect
   the plate and biopsy any phallic structure before resecting.

Prenatal Diagnosis
 • Early ultrasound criteria: large infraumbilical wall defect, lumbosacral myelomeningocele, non-visualised
   bladder. Only about 15% are diagnosed prenatally.
 • Mean gestational age at diagnosis about 22 weeks (range 15–32). Criteria are graded by frequency, not
   severity (major = present in over half of cases).

SURGICAL RECONSTRUCTION OF CLOACAL EXSTROPHY
 • First successful reconstruction: 1960. Survival was about 50% in the 1970s and almost 100% by the 1980s
   with intensive neonatal care, but is compromised without subspecialty access.

Evaluation and Management at Birth
 • Medical stabilisation first, then a full anatomical inventory for short- and long-term plans, including
   gender assignment. Keep bowel and bladder moist under plastic.
 • Neurospinal defect → immediate neurosurgical evaluation. Consult social work and paediatric
   orthopaedics.
 • Gender assignment team: paediatric urologist, paediatric surgeon, paediatrician, paediatric
   endocrinologist, child psychologist or psychiatrist, with parental counselling and involvement.
 • Medical concerns or hemibladders too small → intestinal diversion first, delayed closure.

 KEY POINTS: EVALUATION OF THE NEWBORN WITH CLOACAL EXSTROPHY
   • Neurospinal anomalies
   • Size of the omphalocele
   • Size and quality of the bladder templates
   • Width of pubic diastasis
   • Other associated anomalies


Gender Assignment
 • Historical policy: universal female assignment of 46,XY infants (small, widely separated corpora) with
   bilateral orchidectomy, clitoral reconstruction and vaginoplasty.
 • Evidence against it: reassigned 46,XY children show a marked male shift in psychosexual development
   despite no pubertal hormones; one converted patient virilised from an ectopic testis.
 • Evidence that is mixed: no difference in social, behavioural or psychological problems by assignment;
   masculine childhood behaviour with feminine identity in some.
 • Current practice: assign gender congruent with karyotype where possible (Fig. 43.39; most paediatric
   urologists favour this). It is supported by normal neonatal testicular histology (even when cryptorchid)
   and by modern phalloplasty.

Immediate Surgical Reconstruction
 • Individualised plans. Myelocystocele → neurosurgical closure once stable, then lifelong watch for cord
   tethering (symptomatic in up to a third). Spina bifida is common; most walk-age children ambulate fully,
   some with aids, a few use wheelchairs.
 • Neonatal omphalocele closure combined with intestinal diversion.
 • Hindgut: formerly ileostomy with hindgut resection; now always use the hindgut for fluid absorption.
   Tubularised cecal plate with end colostomy reduces short gut; the remnant enlarges and absorbs more
   water. It may be anastomosed iso- or antiperistaltically for formed stool.
 • Anal stenosis (not atresia) → potential for continence → pull-through. An unused hindgut stays as a
   mucous fistula for later augmentation or vaginoplasty.
 • Gastrointestinal reconstruction with bladder closure → pubic approximation, usually with osteotomy.
   Some defer bowel reconstruction 1–2 years: near-normal colon, or short colon that still forms solid stool
   → pull-through with bowel management. Cannot form solid stool → permanent stoma. Bowel-
   lengthening procedures improve absorption.
 • Closure not feasible at omphalocele repair → approximate the hemibladders in the midline without
   further dissection, converting to bladder exstrophy; abdominal distension then enlarges the plate for later
   closure.

Role of Osteotomy
 • Indicated in all cloacal exstrophy at bladder closure (wide diastasis); medically stable infants may be
   closed early. It reduces dehiscence and ventral hernia: significant complications were almost universal
   without osteotomy and uncommon with it, in otherwise similar groups. Osteotomy does not improve
   eventual continence.
 • Success by type: combined (about 90%) > posterior (about 76%) > anterior (about 61%). Osteotomy
   raises the odds of successful closure nearly sixfold. Combined osteotomy avoids repositioning and avoids
   back-closure complications.
 • Extreme diastasis (over 10 cm) → osteotomy → gradual fixator closure over 1–2 weeks → then bladder
   and wall closure. Outcomes and complications are comparable with a single-stage combined approach.
 • Immobilisation: Buck traction and external fixation perform best; spica is declining. An interpubic
   titanium bar or plate maintains reduction (Fig. 43.40). Care is needed with asymmetric pelves and limb
   anomalies, which make traction difficult.
 • Risk factors for failed urinary reconstruction (failed vs successful): closure in the first week, no
   osteotomy, spica or mummy wrap. Successful closures used Buck traction with fixation (or Bryant ±
   fixation), intersymphyseal plates, and staged fixator cranking over 2–3 weeks. Nearly all successful
   reclosures had osteotomy.
 • Complications: more orthopaedic complications (mainly superficial pin-site infection) than in CBE, with no
   excess of grade 3 or higher. External hip fixation is used more and may contribute.

Bladder Closure in Cloacal Exstrophy
Modern Staged Reconstruction
 • Follows bladder exstrophy staging. Independent predictors of successful closure: staged closure, pelvic
   osteotomy, immobilisation over 4 weeks, and closure by a paediatric urologist.
 • Dual-stage pathway (DSP) adoption: closure success rose to 100% with a similar complication rate, even
   though referred patients now have wider diastases.
 • Technique: approximate the hemibladders posteriorly → free the lateral edges from the wall → join in the
   midline → place bladder and posterior urethra deep in the pelvis. An acellular dermal patch reduces
   interpubic stitch erosion and penopubic fistula. Repair inguinal hernias at closure. Genital reconstruction
   follows the assigned gender.
 • Genitalia: postnatal reconstruction congruent with assigned gender. Adequate unilateral or bilateral
   corpora → male assignment favoured → epispadias repair about age 1. Earlier phallic reconstruction
   disappointed, but phalloplasty (various flaps) now lets most genotypic males be raised male. If female
   assignment is needed, the phallic halves are joined as a clitoris.
 • Vagina: early in genetic females; delayed in converted males; from a preserved hindgut or expanded
   perineal skin; long-term dilation may be needed.
 • Fixation and traction 6–8 weeks.

Single-Stage Reconstruction
 • Single-stage repair resembles CPRE; it is deferred if the omphalocele is large or medical issues exist.
   Single-stage failure approaches half vs about 15% for staged closure → staged closure is recommended.
   Small single-stage series: dry intervals before toilet training, some needing bulking, occasional
   augmentation.

Abdominal Wall Reconstruction
 • One of the hardest steps: diastasis is about twice CBE. Options: non-absorbable mesh, component
   separation, bioprosthetic material.
 • Sequence: pelvic osteotomy → relaxing fascial incisions (preferred over muscle techniques because the
   musculature is poor) → if still too wide, bioprosthetic in preference to synthetic (less chronic infection,
   superficial infection treatable without explanting, fewer adhesions and fistulae).
 • About a third of staged closures need bioprosthetic material, all without early herniation. Those closed
   without it are younger. Defect size has not been shown to predict need.

Failed Cloacal Exstrophy
 • Primary closure fails in about 31–43%. Staged closure gives about four times the odds of success of one-
   stage closure.

 • Failure mode: mainly bladder prolapse, then dehiscence. Predictors of successful secondary closure:
   older age at reclosure, osteotomy, Buck traction with external fixation.
 • Costs are large and rise sharply after failure; secondary closure complication rates are similar to primary.

Role of Osteotomy in Failed Cloacal Exstrophy
 • The same principles as primary closure: osteotomy (nearly sixfold odds of success; associated with
   successful reclosure) plus secure immobilisation — Buck traction with external fixation is the gold
   standard, clearly better than spica, which dominates among failed secondary closures. Recommended:
   combined bilateral anterior innominate and vertical iliac osteotomy with 6 weeks of Buck traction and
   external fixation; spica is not recommended.

Techniques to Create Urinary Continence
 • Continence is possible in most but usually needs augmentation and intermittent catheterisation. A
   median of two procedures (range one to four) is needed for a dry interval over 3 hours, often from
   revisions of the continent diversion. Median age at urinary continence about 11 years.
 • Augment with hindgut (if available), ileum or stomach. Gender-converted males → continence hardest
   → continent stoma. Genetic females: YDL can work, but most need catheterisation. YDL success tracks
   the neurological status.
 • Components: orthotopic urethra from local tissue, vagina, ileum, stomach or ureter; catheterisable ileal
   stoma if bowel length and fluid balance allow. Delay the continent reservoir until the child can self-
   catheterise.
 • Urethra vs abdominal stoma depends on outlet adequacy, dexterity and interest, and orthopaedic status
   (spine, hips, braces, ambulation). More than half achieve continence, most via a continent stoma, the rest
   voiding or catheterising urethrally.
Concept — In cloacal exstrophy closure success is an orthopaedic problem and continence is a neurological
one: staging, osteotomy and rigid fixation secure the closure, but the spinal lesion and the gender-assignment
path decide whether the child voids, catheterises a urethra, or uses a stoma.

LONG-TERM ISSUES IN CLOACAL EXSTROPHY
 • Survival is almost universal → the aim is QoL through function, with individualised management.
 • The main determinant of long-term disability is the level of the neurological defect → early aggressive
   neurological management and lifelong tethering surveillance. Minimal deficit → aim for bowel pull-
   through and voided continence.
 • Ricketts continence score (6-point bowel plus bladder; 6 best, 0 worst): most children score poorly
   (colostomy with an incontinent bladder); very few reach an enema programme with a continent bladder.
 • Permanent ileostomy vs terminal colostomy: ileostomy has more early morbidity, but bowel adapts by
   about 3 years, and with aggressive parenteral nutrition growth is similar. Modern bowel reconstruction
   avoids long-term short gut in most.
 • Phallic reconstruction: past lengthening failed (tiny corpora, wide separation) → neophalloplasty
   (forearm or other flap). Satisfaction with penile reconstruction is about 60% and does not differ by
   method.
 • Fertility: male fertility compromised across the complex, although paternity occurs spontaneously or
   with ART. Female fertility typically preserved with little ART → caesarean section with a urologist
   present. Cervical prolapse is worse than in bladder exstrophy.

SUMMARY
