 and Related Disorders
 A BRIEF HISTORY AND OVERVIEW
   • 1876 — Samuel Gross coined “interstitial cystitis”: chronic bladder inflammation of unknown
     aetiology.
   • Guy Hunner (1915, 1918) described “classical” interstitial cystitis (IC): bladder pain on filling with
     cystoscopically inflamed areas of the bladder wall, later termed Hunner lesions (HLs).
   • Over the following 50 years, most patients with bladder pain had no bladder-wall abnormality on
     routine cystoscopy.
   • 1987 National Institutes of Health/National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases
     (NIH/NIDDK) criteria: an expert set of inclusion/exclusion criteria built to give a homogeneous
     population for clinical trials — a research definition, not a clinical one.
   • Applied in clinical practice, the NIDDK criteria excluded about 60% of patients who would otherwise
     have been diagnosed clinically. The definition therefore had to broaden, and bladder pain syndrome
     (BPS) is now the most commonly used term; it meets medical pain-taxonomy standards.
   • Current clinical definition (adopted by the American Urological Association [AUA] Guideline for over a
     decade): “an unpleasant sensation (pain, pressure, discomfort) perceived to be related to the urinary
     bladder, associated with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) of more than 6 weeks’ duration, in the
     absence of infection or other identifiable causes.”
   • IC/BPS is an umbrella term for a heterogeneous syndrome — a form of chronic pelvic pain spanning
     gross inflammatory bladder disease (HLs) at one end and bladder pain with multiple non-urological
     complaints and a normal-looking bladder at the other.
   • Mounting evidence that BPS is distinct from disease with HLs. The authors propose “IC” for patients
     with HLs — defined by symptoms plus cystoscopic and histological evidence of visible end-organ
     inflammatory disease. Some regard IC as a disease distinct from BPS, the latter being a clinical
     syndrome or symptom complex.
   • US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) meeting: asked whether IC and BPS should be combined in
     trials (implying two conditions), it unanimously chose to study them together — their symptoms are
     indistinguishable.
   • Heterogeneity confounds treatment development; better phenotyping may improve outcomes
     through precision-medicine approaches.
   • Figure 28.1: urological chronic pelvic pain syndrome (UCPPS) contains IC/BPS (both sexes) and chronic
     prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS, males). Within IC/BPS, IC with HLs is a true
     inflammatory end-organ disease; BPS without HLs is a syndrome or symptom complex.
 Concept — The history is a progressive widening of the definition from a cystoscopic lesion to a symptom
 complex; the chapter now narrows it again by splitting off the HL phenotype, because lesion-defined
 disease and symptom-defined syndrome behave differently.

 Concept — Every name encodes an assumption about where the pain arises: “cystitis” implies bladder
 inflammation, “bladder pain syndrome” a symptom complex, “UCPPS” a regional pain disorder, and
 ICD-11’s “chronic primary” label classes the pain itself as the disease.

 EPIDEMIOLOGY
   • Three estimation methods: self-reported history, symptom identification, clinician diagnosis.
     Prevalence estimates rise as the case definition broadens; methodological differences explain
     between-study variation.
   • RAND Interstitial Cystitis Epidemiology (RICE) study — the most sophisticated population-based study:
     3.3–7.9 million US females and 2.1–4.6 million US males have symptoms consistent with IC/BPS.
     Mean age of onset 30–40 years.

 Prevalence in Males
   • Female:male ratio 5:1 in clinic-diagnosis studies but 2–3:1 in population studies — men are under-
     diagnosed.
   • In men, IC/BPS prevalence exceeds that of CP/CPPS. Many men labelled CP/CPPS have IC/BPS-type
     bladder symptoms: in MAPP, most men (largely enrolled as CP/CPPS) reported “painful bladder filling”

Chapter 28 — Interstitial Cystitis/Bladder Pain Syndrome and Related Disorders                                 EPIDEMIOLOGY
     (pain worsening with filling) and/or “painful urgency” (strong urge to void because of pain, pressure or
     discomfort).
   • Practice: ask men about bladder pain and urinary symptoms, not only genital (penile, testicular,
     perineal, ejaculatory) pain; re-evaluate for IC/BPS when CP/CPPS treatment fails. CP/CPPS is covered
     in Chapter 27.

 Prevalence in Children
   • No prevalence data; considered uncommon.
   • Paediatric voiding dysfunction with bowel and pelvic-floor disturbance is common and produces
     pelvic and urethral pain easily confused with IC/BPS.
   • Recall-based studies: adult female IC/BPS patients report more childhood urgency and recurrent
     urinary tract infections (UTIs). Those with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)-like symptoms recalled
     needing to push to defaecate, voiding only once or twice daily, and painful urination in childhood.
   • With the high prevalence of high-tone pelvic floor dysfunction (HTPFD) in IC/BPS, this suggests linked
     bladder–bowel–pelvic-floor dysfunction dating from childhood.
   • A small paediatric cohort with bladder pain, frequency and sensory urgency without infection,
     evaluated by urodynamics (UDS), cystoscopy and distension, had findings consistent with IC/BPS:
     median onset about 5 years; most had long-term remission after bladder distension. They must be
     distinguished from dysfunctional voiding.

 Prevalence of Hunner Lesions
   • Why HL epidemiology is difficult: medical codes do not separate HL-positive from HL-negative patients;
     most US clinicians do not perform cystoscopy or biopsy; data come mainly from tertiary or research
     centres (limited generalisability); and no pictorial atlas of HLs existed until 2020.
   • Conventional figure: about 20% of IC/BPS patients have HLs; reported range among cystoscoped
     patients 3.5–56% (Table 28.1).
   • HL prevalence rises with age, in both sexes (Fig. 28.2, two clinical series and the MAPP cohort):
     significantly higher at ≥50 years than below 50; uncommon under 30 and present in over half of
     patients aged over 60–70.
   • Progression from non-HL to HL disease has never been documented.
   • Limited studies suggest a possible genetic susceptibility to IC/BPS.

 NATURAL HISTORY, EFFECTS ON QUALITY OF LIFE, AND ECONOMIC BURDEN

 Natural History
   • Assessed mainly by patient recall. Phenotype differs by age at diagnosis:
  Age at diagnosis         Characteristic features
  <30 years                More severe urological and non-urological pain, urgency, frequency, dysuria, dyspareunia,
                           genital pain
  30–60 years              More chronic overlapping pain conditions (COPCs) such as fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS),
                           widespread pain, somatic symptom burden, depressive symptoms
  >60 years                More nocturia, urinary incontinence and HLs

   • Prodromal pelvic pain and irritative voiding often precede diagnosis by decades. Painless frequency is
     common among those with prodromes; these patients more often have non-bladder syndromes (e.g.
     FMS) and a poorer prognosis — consistent with the recalled childhood voiding complaints.
   • Mild symptoms at onset → stable at 3 years; chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS) at onset → progression.
   • MAPP predictors of worsening pain and urinary symptoms at 12 months: extent of widespread pain,
     somatic symptom burden and poorer overall health. Not predictive: baseline anxiety, depression,
     general mental health. Pain catastrophising and perceived stress predicted worse pain.
   • Follow-up to 9 years: pain and urinary symptoms remarkably stable — a chronic condition with a
     stable overall course.
   • Remission is under-captured, because asymptomatic patients stop attending. In the Interstitial Cystitis
     Database, about a third had at least one remission of ≥3 months; a minority of these lasted ≥6 months.
   • Early improvement in longitudinal studies reflects regression to the mean and an intervention effect
     (more follow-up and care). Hence the need for randomised controlled trials (RCTs), ideally with a pre-
     enrolment run-in. Regression to the mean, placebo effect and selection bias explain why treatments
     promising in uncontrolled series fail in RCTs.
   • Flares (MAPP): reported by almost all female UCPPS patients and by every patient meeting IC/BPS
     criteria. Highly variable in duration (seconds to months), frequency (several a day to once a year or
     less), type (pain vs urinary) and intensity. Commonest duration: multiple days; longer flares bring more
     pain, urinary symptoms, activity disruption and bother.
   • Case-crossover study: sexual activity was the only consistent trigger of flare onset; subgroups were
     triggered by dietary factors and abdominal exercise. Intervene when a trigger is identified, but no rigid
     flare-prevention plan.

 Quality of Life
   • As with all chronic pain: decline in mental and physical quality of life (QoL), poor coping, anxiety,
     depression and high stress.
   • Sexual dysfunction in both sexes profoundly affects QoL — identify and treat it.

   • Catastrophising (believing the worst will happen and that one could not tolerate it) is common and
     associated with worse depressive symptoms, mental health, social functioning, vitality and pain;
     negative affect and catastrophising partly explain disability.
   • Depression and catastrophising (helplessness) predict suicidal ideation. Cognitive behavioural therapy
     (CBT) addressing catastrophising and mood may play an important role.
   • Work: IC/BPS patients are six times more likely than the general population to reduce work time for
     health reasons, but half as likely as patients with arthritis. In RICE, the impact of bladder symptoms on
     life activities was the most consistent predictor of work outcome → role for behavioural therapy to
     build coping and self-management.

 Abuse and Childhood Trauma
   • Boston Area Community Health (BACH) Survey: emotional, sexual and physical abuse is a risk factor
     for IC/BPS-type symptoms, confirmed by case–control and health-maintenance-database studies and
     by MAPP (more adverse childhood experiences, including sexual and physical violence, than healthy
     controls).
   • History of sexual abuse → more pain, fewer voiding symptoms, more widespread pain outside the
     pelvis; more likely to worsen and less likely to improve over 12 months.
   • Caveat: data are mostly self-report and recall, so subject to bias. Draw no conclusion about abuse in
     an individual, but remain sensitive to the possibility in all pain patients, and in BPS particularly.
   • Multiple diagnoses → higher rate of previous abuse; such patients may need referral to a traumatic-
     stress centre.
   • Neuroimaging: childhood adversity alters brain functional networks and structures (e.g. the
     amygdala, which modulates response to threat and stress) — the neurobiological basis of its effect.

 Medical Costs
   • Direct costs amount to several thousand US dollars per patient-year, excluding lost wages, pre-
     diagnostic costs, alternative therapies and misdiagnosis; Medicare expenditure per beneficiary over 65
     is higher still.
   • In the first year after diagnosis, IC/BPS adds substantially to total health-care costs, mostly
     outpatient; HL patients use more resources than non-HL patients.
 Concept — IC/BPS is chronic but not progressive: symptoms flare around a stable baseline, so the burden
 is set less by the bladder than by widespread pain, catastrophising and coping — which is why those, and
 not baseline mood, predict outcome.

 ETIOLOGY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
   • Unknown and probably multifactorial, acting through one or more pathways to produce a common
     symptom set (Fig. 28.3: pain beyond the bladder, regional and widespread; HL-positive = IC, HL-
     negative = BPS). Many theories; sparse confirmatory clinical evidence.
   • Proposed mechanisms: “leaky” urothelium with defects of the glycosaminoglycan (GAG) layer; mast-
     cell activation with histamine release; altered neuroplasticity; neurogenic inflammation; pelvic organ
     cross-sensitisation; dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis or stress response —
     possibly combining into a self-perpetuating process of chronic bladder pain and voiding dysfunction.
   • IBS, FMS, CFS and other chronic pain disorders may precede or follow IC/BPS — not inevitably; their
     causal relationship is unknown.

   • Neural cross-talk in the dorsal root ganglia, spinal cord and brain → central sensitisation and altered
     functional brain connectivity. This may explain the absent physical findings and non-response to local
     therapy in many BPS patients — the mechanism is systemic.
   • Infection: no organism analogous to Helicobacter pylori in gastritis has ever been consistently
     identified, including with microbiome and culture-independent DNA/RNA techniques; negative studies
     far outnumber positive ones. A double-blind placebo RCT of 18 weeks of sequential antibiotics
     (detailed under Oral Therapies) showed minimal benefit and frequent new non-urinary side-effects.
     Active infection is unlikely to drive the process, and antibiotics have no role when urine culture is
     negative.
   • Not a primary autoimmune bladder disorder in the classic sense.
   • Small-fibre polyneuropathy (SFPN): a newer theory. Found in a proportion of both HL and non-HL
     patients; bladder capacity and HL status do not differ by SFPN status. Causal role unknown — FMS and
     CFS also show SFPN, and studies are few.

 PATHOLOGY
   • No pathognomonic histology — unsurprising in a syndrome defined by visceral pain; this reinforces the
     need for phenotyping.
   • Confounders: disease duration, recent trauma (e.g. hydrodistension), prior bacterial cystitis, and biopsy
     site and method. Appearances vary widely between patients and between serial biopsies from the
     same patient.
  Biopsy method                            Typical finding in IC/BPS
  Transurethral resection (TUR)            Mucosal ruptures, submucosal haemorrhage, mild inflammation
  biopsy
  Cold-cup forceps biopsy                  Normal in about half

   • Normal biopsy is common in symptomatic patients and does not exclude IC/BPS. Conversely,
     inflammatory features appear in 24–76% of non-HL patients. Epithelial denudation, submucosal
     oedema, congestion and ectasia, and inflammatory infiltrate are more frequent than in controls, yet
     over half of IC/BPS specimens are indistinguishable from controls.
   • IC and BPS differ strikingly in histopathology, chiefly in severity of inflammation. With no evidence of
     temporal transition from BPS to IC, this argues for distinct phenotypes, perhaps distinct disorders.
   • Classic comparative series: the HL group showed mucosal ulceration and haemorrhage, granulation
     tissue, intense infiltrate (mainly plasma cells and lymphocytes), raised mast-cell counts and perineural
     infiltrates. The non-HL group, despite equally severe symptoms, had relatively unaltered mucosa with
     sparse inflammation; the main feature was multiple small mucosal ruptures and suburothelial
     haemorrhages (glomerulations). Specimens were taken immediately after hydrodistension, so the
     minimal non-HL findings may be iatrogenic; the same group later found completely normal biopsies
     common in non-HL patients.
   • Severe lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate in almost all HL biopsies versus few non-HL. In HL patients,
     epithelial denudation extends beyond the lesion — a field change of the bladder surface.
   • Most studies find few inflammatory events in non-HL disease, although one reported more bladder-
     wall fibrosis and mast-cell infiltration.
   • Severely abnormal pathology may predict a poor prognosis — not consistently.

 Mast cells
   • Mast-cell proliferation, activation and degranulation may contribute: histamine and pro-inflammatory
     mediators cause pain, hyperaemia and fibrosis — all features of IC/BPS.
   • In IC, mast cells lie predominantly in the detrusor, with a second population in the lamina propria
     and epithelium having distinct staining, physiological responses and secretion. This epithelial
     population is absent in controls and not involved in BPS.
   • Mast-cell activation is far more pronounced with HLs, together with prominent epithelial and detrusor
     inflammation; in BPS it is sparse → the basic pathological processes may differ.
   • Mast-cell density as a diagnostic marker has failed: results disparate because of method. Tryptase
     immunohistochemistry showed higher subepithelial and detrusor density in HL disease but low
     predictive value, and non-HL density equalled that of overactive bladder (OAB). Digital quantitative
     analysis: IC resembles “chronic cystitis”; BPS resembles normal controls; density tracks overall
     lymphoplasmacytic density.
   • Mast-cell counts may matter in pathogenesis but have no role in diagnosis.
   • Role of histopathology: limited to HL patients, to exclude bladder cancer and carcinoma in situ (CIS),
     eosinophilic cystitis, tuberculous cystitis, ketamine cystitis and any other specific tissue diagnosis.
 Concept — Histology separates the two phenotypes rather than diagnosing the syndrome: HL disease is
 a genuine inflammatory lesion with mast-cell activation and a field change, whereas BPS is often
 histologically normal — so biopsy exists to exclude (CIS above all), not to confirm.

 ASSOCIATED DISORDERS
   • COPCs — IBS, FMS, CFS, migraine, temporomandibular joint disorder (TMJD) — are functional pain
     syndromes accounting for part of the 75–80% of patients with pain beyond the bladder. Regional
     pelvic disorders — HTPFD and vulvodynia — often coexist. These “pain generators” overlap with
     bladder symptoms, worsen pain and complicate diagnosis and management; the associations also hint
     at aetiology and aid phenotyping (Fig. 28.4, Table 28.2).
   • Pain distribution: 75–80% have pain beyond the pelvis, 38% widespread in multiple body regions;
     only 20–25% pelvis-only. “Pelvic pain and beyond” brings more sensory-type pain, depression, anxiety,
     stress and sleep disturbance, and worse QoL.
   • Key question in widespread pain: is the bladder the primary disease (less likely) or a bystander — one
     involved organ in systemic pain?
   • True somatisation disorder is rare, despite extrapelvic symptoms.
   • IBS, FMS and CFS are more prevalent than in controls. In UCPPS, over a third had at least one COPC:
     IBS alone was the commonest pattern, followed by multiple COPCs, with FMS alone and CFS alone less
     common. COPCs mean more severe and longer-standing symptoms, more depression and anxiety;
     multiple COPCs imply a centralised pain component.
   • IBS — the commonest associated condition (over 30% of IC/BPS), with the greatest excess over the
     general population of any functional pain syndrome. IBS patients feel intestinal pain at lower gas
     volumes than healthy people — strikingly similar to pain on bladder distension. Visceral organ cross-
     talk, via convergent sensory pathways in the dorsal root ganglion, spinal cord and/or brain, may explain
     the link.
   • FMS: chronic widespread non-articular musculoskeletal pain with fatigue, non-refreshing sleep and
     stiffness. Shares female predominance, stress triggers, associated symptoms and response to tricyclic
     compounds with IC/BPS. The “poster child” of centralised (nociplastic) pain — central sensitisation
     and altered brain pain processing.

   • Two pain phenotypes: “pelvic pain only” and “pelvic pain and beyond”. Widespread, possibly
     centralised pain may not respond when only local therapy is applied → tailor treatment to phenotype.
   • Vulvodynia: vulvar pain of at least 3 months without clear cause; prevalence in female IC/BPS ranges
     from about one in six to almost all. Shares comorbidities with IC/BPS (IBS, FMS, orofacial pain).
   • Endometriosis: evaluated by imaging and/or laparoscopy. The concordance of non-infectious
     inflammatory syndromes in tissues derived from the embryonic urogenital sinus, with overlapping
     functional syndromes, argues for a common cause.
   • Autoimmune disease: IC/BPS is not a classic primary autoimmune disorder. Crohn disease, systemic
     lupus erythematosus (SLE), Sjögren syndrome (SS) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) are infrequent in
     absolute terms but show an astoundingly high relative excess. The most frequent is inflammatory
     bowel disease (IBD)/Crohn disease, about 100-fold the general population (single study,
     uncorroborated).
   • SLE: tens-fold higher prevalence; bladder symptoms may predate the diagnosis of SLE. The critical
     distinction is association versus lupus cystitis: lupus cystitis runs a more virulent course — gross
     inflammation, bladder-wall fibrosis, hydroureteronephrosis. In a few, bladder symptoms arise from
     myelopathy of the sacral cord.
   • SS: autoimmune exocrinopathy with female preponderance — dry eyes, dry mouth, arthritis, and
     possibly fever and gastrointestinal (GI) and lung involvement. SS (keratoconjunctivitis sicca with focal
     lymphocytic sialoadenitis) was found in a substantial minority of IC patients; IC/BPS symptoms occur in
     up to 5% of SS. SS patients may have detrusor overactivity (DO) → evaluate individually before
     diagnosing IC/BPS.
   • HTPFD: tender, hypertonic pelvic floor and associated muscles in about 80% of IC/BPS. Higher
     intravaginal electromyography (EMG) signal correlates with higher IC symptom and problem indices
     and a shortened pelvic-floor muscle length. Symptom overlap makes both diagnoses harder (Figs. 28.3,
     28.5).
   • HTPFD aetiology unknown: possibly childhood voiding dysfunction; a guarding response to bladder
     pain causing obstructive and irritative voiding; bidirectional viscerosomatic and viscerovisceral cross-
     talk. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) shows altered activity and connectivity
     of pelvic-floor sensorimotor cortical control regions in women with IC/BPS.
   • Phenotype groups (Fig. 28.4): the smallest group is bladder symptoms only; others derive pain from
     regional (HTPFD, vulvodynia, endometriosis, IBS) or widespread (FMS, CFS, migraine, TMJD) syndromes,
     often several; autoimmune/immune-mediated disease (SLE, RA, IBD) may form a separate group. No
     evidence that IC/BPS progresses from bladder-centric to regional to widespread pain. Heterogeneity
     supports phenotyping for precision treatment.

 DIAGNOSIS

 Symptoms
   • Clinical syndrome per the AUA definition; the wording deliberately includes pressure and discomfort as
     well as pain, because patients voice them.
   • Pain typically suprapubic and worse with filling (“painful bladder filling”), temporarily relieved by
     emptying → frequent voiding day and night to relieve pain.
   • Dyspareunia common; isolated dysuria or urethral pain uncommon.
   • Associated LUTS: frequency, nocturia, “urgency”. The International Continence Society (ICS) urgency
     definition — “a sudden compelling desire to pass urine, which is difficult to defer” — suits OAB but is
     poorly understood by patients and clinicians. The overlap has prompted the view that IC/BPS and OAB-
     dry (urgency without urgency urinary incontinence [UUI]) form a spectrum of bladder hypersensitivity.
     Qualitative differences remain (Fig. 28.5):

 Basic Evaluation
   • The hardest part is excluding other identifiable causes of pelvic pain; in the authors’ experience,
     failure to diagnose other pain generators or confusable disease is among the commonest reasons for
     treatment failure. IC/BPS is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion (Table 28.3).

Chapter 28 — Interstitial Cystitis/Bladder Pain Syndrome and Related Disorders                                           DIAGNOSIS
   • Identify and treat all pelvic pain generators, usually stepwise — the principle of the AUA-derived
     diagnostic algorithm (Fig. 28.6).
   • History, examination, post-void residual (PVR) and urine studies suffice in almost all uncomplicated
     presentations.
   • History and examination as in Box 28.1 — above all, ask about ketamine, palpate the pelvic floor for
     HTPFD, exclude vaginitis and pelvic mass, and perform a cotton-swab (Q-tip) vulvar test for vulvodynia
     in women and genital examination with digital rectal examination (DRE) in men.
   • AUA 2022 basic studies: PVR (bladder scanner), urinalysis, urine culture. Urine cytology is considered
     when there are risk factors for urothelial malignancy — smoking history, degree of haematuria, age
     >40. Microscopic haematuria is evaluated per the AUA/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine
     and Urogenital Reconstruction (SUFU) Microhematuria Guideline (2020).
   • Symptom instruments: O’Leary–Sant Interstitial Cystitis Symptom and Problem Index (ICSI/ICPI);
     Genitourinary Pain Index (GUPI, male and female); Bladder Pain/Interstitial Cystitis Symptom Score
     (BPIC-SS); pain visual analogue scale (VAS) 0–10; Global Response Assessment (GRA).
   • Score pain and urinary symptoms separately — not as one composite (as ICSI/ICPI does), because an
     individual’s pain and urinary trajectories diverge over time and after treatment.
   • Widespread pain: self-administered body map (Fig. 28.8) — widespread = 3 or more of 12 regions in

 Ketamine Cystitis
   • Ketamine: N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist; anaesthetic and newly popular
     antidepressant. Recreationally a “club drug” — rapid onset, short action, and dissociative anaesthesia
     (unconscious, trance-like state with profound analgesia); many street names (e.g. “special K”, “ket”,
     “vitamin K”).
   • Recreational use began in Asia and spread internationally; substantial US lifetime use, and common
     among young club-goers (slightly more in men).
   • Ketamine and/or its urinary metabolites (e.g. hydroquinone) probably cause the pathology.
   • Clinically almost indistinguishable from IC/BPS. Early disease may be LUTS alone; progression can be
     rapid after about a year of use: dysuria, worsening frequency, gross haematuria, pelvic pain. Severity
     of symptoms and inflammation tracks duration, frequency and dose.
   • Continued use → gross inflammation resembling HLs but more widespread → irreversible bladder-
     wall fibrosis, hydronephrosis (about 30%) and renal insufficiency (about 10%).
   • Management: early identification and cessation — the most important and hardest part;
     rehabilitation is essential; cessation improves LUTS.
   • A history of ketamine use is strongly suggestive, but often not volunteered. Be especially wary of the
     young patient (<30) with an IC/BPS picture and visible bladder-wall inflammation.

 High-Tone Pelvic Floor Dysfunction
   • Synonyms: pelvic-floor hypertonicity, non-relaxing pelvic floor dysfunction, pelvic floor spasm, pelvic
     floor myalgia. Common across urological, gynaecological and colorectal pain syndromes.
   • Symptoms: pelvic pressure, persistent urge, sensation of incomplete emptying (poor emptying in
     severe cases), obstructive voiding (hesitancy, weak stream), constipation, dyspareunia (often felt the
     next day), perineal, penile and ejaculatory pain. Storage symptoms may be mistaken for OAB.
   • UDS not essential: shows voiding dysfunction; video-UDS shows poor relaxation of the external
     sphincter.
   • Suspect HTPFD with dysfunctional voiding + bowel disturbance + sexual pain, often dating from
     childhood.
   • Hallmarks: hypertonic pelvic and adjacent muscles, muscle banding, myofascial trigger points —
     tender “knots” in a taut band that reproduce pain. Tender, hypertonic pelvic floor in about 80% of IC/
     BPS; trigger points in a similar proportion, multiple in most.
   • Standardised six-site examination (Fig. 28.7): left and right anterior levator ani (pubococcygeus) → left
     and right posterior levator ani (iliococcygeus) → left and right obturator internus (lateral). Women
     vaginally in lithotomy; men transrectally, bending over.
   • HTPFD magnifies IC/BPS pain or is the sole cause; missing it commonly produces a suboptimal
     response, because the treatments differ → refer for evaluation by a pelvic-floor physiotherapist.
   • Multimodal programme: physical therapy; behavioural modification (avoid straining, “reverse Kegels”,
     stress reduction); topical heat; skeletal-muscle relaxants; control of constipation; anaesthetic or
     onabotulinumtoxinA (BTX-A) trigger-point injections.

 Beyond the History, Physical Examination and Urine Studies
 Frequency Volume Chart
   • One 24-hour chart of times and volumes usually suffices, for diagnosis and follow-up.
   • IC/BPS shows frequent low-volume voids with a narrower range than OAB, but the diary cannot
     distinguish IC/BPS from OAB.
   • Pelvic pain with infrequent or high-volume voids (or high cystometric capacity) → search for a non-
     bladder source of pain.

 Urodynamics
   • AUA: not part of the standard evaluation in uncomplicated cases. Its value is to identify other
     functional pathology that confounds diagnosis. Useful when empirical therapy fails, the presentation
     is complicated or confusing (pain with significant incontinence, high PVR); complex cases may need full
     video-UDS.
   • Cystometry in IC/BPS: normal detrusor function, reduced capacity, hypersensitivity (strong desire to
     void at low volume); compliance normal, because hypersensitivity prevents filling to the point of non-
     compliance. Pain on filling that reproduces symptoms is very suggestive. An end-stage fibrotic bladder
     has reduced capacity and compliance.
   • Uroflow or pressure–flow in men to evaluate outlet obstruction. Bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) is
     common in women with IC/BPS, presumably from pelvic-floor dysfunction; typical voiding-dysfunction
     symptoms merit dedicated therapy, and UDS adds little that changes care.
   • DO coexists in 15–19%; DO on UDS does not exclude IC/BPS. Using UDS to exclude DO as a cause of
     storage symptoms (not pain) is debatable: empirical treatment is justified first-line, and amitriptyline’s
     anticholinergic action helps storage symptoms anyway.

 Office Cystoscopy
   • AUA 2022 change: cystoscopy for patients in whom HLs are suspected — specifically those over 50,
     where prevalence is higher. Rationale: easy cystoscopic recognition, coupled with therapies specific
     and unique to this phenotype.
   • The only way to identify HLs is direct visualisation. HL extent correlates with symptom severity and
     capacity, but HLs cannot be predicted from the clinical presentation or urine studies.
   • Office flexible cystoscopy: minimal discomfort; often immediate reassurance about bladder cancer;
     most HLs visible without formal hydrodistension under anaesthesia. A 2020 pictorial atlas aids
     recognition (Fig. 28.9).
   • Some patients prefer anaesthesia; gentle distension under anaesthesia may reveal additional HLs.
   • Appearance is variable: most show a classic stellate pattern — vessels radiating from a central area of
     erythema or coagulum with inflammation; some are blanched and non-inflamed with a white stellate
     look; lesions may bleed with filling as a red “waterfall”, disrupting centrally; fibrous bridging between
     lesions further restricts capacity.
   • Map lesions early; avoid overdistension, which causes bleeding that obscures them. Lesion suspicious
     for CIS → biopsy. HLs rarely occur at the trigone — biopsy any trigonal lesion. Narrow-band imaging
     may reveal subtle lesions.
   • Also consider cystoscopy after failed conventional therapy, with a complicated presentation or
     previous abnormal cystoscopy; offer it to exclude stones, cancer, CIS or suspected BOO, and as part of
     haematuria work-up.

   • Intravesical local anaesthetic localises pain to the bladder, as opposed to adjacent generators (pelvic
     floor, GI, gynaecological); performed after cystoscopy or as a stand-alone office test.
   • Authors’ mixture: 15 mL 0.5% bupivacaine + 15 mL 2% lidocaine (30 mL); other lidocaine ±
     bupivacaine combinations acceptable.
   • Improvement → bladder-centric pain; no response does not exclude IC/BPS. Responders may be
     offered intravesical therapy.

 Potassium Sensitivity Test
   • Intravesical potassium chloride (KCl) challenge: compares the sensory-provocative effect of sodium
     with that of 0.4 M KCl; pain or symptom provocation = positive. Whether a positive result reflects
     epithelial permeability or sensory hypersensitivity is unclear. It was meant to diagnose IC/BPS without
     cystoscopy and hydrodistension.
   • Not recommended; perform a local anaesthetic challenge instead. With a symptom-based diagnosis, a
     painful provocative test is no longer needed.
   • Neither sensitive nor specific: negative in up to a quarter of patients meeting strict NIDDK criteria;
     positive in about a third of asymptomatic men and of a fixed population of textile workers; positive in a
     quarter with OAB, virtually all with radiation or acute bacterial cystitis, and most men with CP/CPPS;
     non-specific in gynaecological chronic pelvic pain and urethral syndrome. Does not predict response to
     oral pentosan polysulfate (PPS).

 Cystoscopy With Hydrodistention and Biopsy as a Diagnostic Procedure
   • Cystoscopy with hydrodistension (CHD) was therapeutic long before it was diagnostic. Under
     anaesthesia, sufficient distension reveals glomerulations — submucosal pinpoint petechial
     haemorrhages (Fig. 28.10A).
   • Sequence: fill to 80 cm H₂O for 1–3 minutes → drain (terminal effluent often blood-tinged) → refill →
     reinspect: glomerulations develop throughout the bladder and may not be seen without anaesthesia.
     Mucosal tears may develop, often near the bladder neck (Fig. 28.10B).
   • Glomerulations are not specific: seen after radiotherapy, with carcinoma, after toxic chemicals or
     chemotherapy, often in dialysis patients or after urinary diversion (bladder unfilled for long periods),
     in most men with prostate pain syndromes, in up to a fifth of men undergoing transurethral resection
     of the prostate (TURP) for LUTS, and in normal individuals after overdistension. Conversely, IC/BPS

   • Over a third of IC/BPS patients have widespread pain outside the pelvis; easily identified with an

   • Intravesical therapy is directed at bladder-centric pain; temporary relief with an anaesthetic challenge
     makes intravesical anaesthetic a viable treatment.
   • Low anaesthetic bladder capacity (<400 mL) at hydrodistension → worse pain and frequency, more
     bladder inflammation, fewer reports of depression, IBS, endometriosis and systemic pain — a more
     bladder-centric picture.

     measurement is standardised → value in phenotyping currently unknown; how to treat <400 mL
     differently from ≥400 mL is unclear. Many with <400 mL have HLs and many with ≥400 mL have
     systemic pain, so the categories overlap with the HL and widespread-pain phenotypes.

 pelvic-floor-centric disease physiotherapy, and centralised pain systemic therapy — and a trial negative in
 all-comers can conceal a positive result in the right phenotype.

 TREATMENT OF INTERSTITIAL CYSTITIS/BLADDER PAIN SYNDROME

 General Principles of Therapy
 Setting Expectations
   • No “home run” therapy exists — a consequence of heterogeneity and unknown aetiology. Few
     treatments give significant durable benefit to most patients, leaving a trial-and-error approach that
     breeds frustration.
   • Frustration and secondary depression are reduced by setting goals and realistic expectations, working
     as partners, and reassessing periodically. No single treatment cures IC/BPS; the reasonable goal is
     acceptable QoL while coping, not complete remission.




                                                              page 16 of 33
Chapter 28 — Interstitial Cystitis/Bladder Pain Syndrome and Related Disorders                                   TREATMENT OF IC/BPS
   • Most patients have persistent symptoms with a stable course and intermittent flares; balance
     tolerating residual symptoms against pursuing invasive therapies of uncertain added benefit. Build a
     trusting relationship; agree a flare plan (e.g. anaesthetic instillation) to reduce stress and uncertainty.

 Shared Decision Making
   • Decide after informing the patient of risks, benefits and alternatives; allow latitude in choosing
     treatments.
   • Conservative first, escalating for poor symptom control and unacceptable QoL (Fig. 28.6). Initial
     aggressive or multimodal therapy (behavioural + oral + intravesical) is appropriate for severe
     symptoms unlikely to respond to conservative measures; concomitant treatments address different
     aspects of the symptom profile and may reduce the need for aggressive options.
   • Placebo effect and regression to the mean are common in the first 3–4 weeks. If not improving → re-
     evaluate for other pain sources or misdiagnosis.
   • Stop ineffective treatments after a fair trial, and discuss stopping long-standing therapy of doubtful
     benefit, even temporarily.

 Multidisciplinary Approach
   • CBT from experienced mental-health professionals for catastrophising.
   • Suspected pudendal neuralgia → pudendal nerve blocks by trained clinicians — therapeutic and
     diagnostic.
   • Anaesthetic or BTX-A trigger-point injections (BTX-A not FDA-approved for this) when directed physical
     therapy has plateaued.
   • Chronic opioid therapy is not routinely recommended; carefully selected patients may benefit under
     pain specialists using “universal precautions”.
   • Early gynaecological, gastroenterological and rheumatological consultation, especially with
     widespread pain or COPCs (endometriosis, vulvodynia, IBS, FMS, CFS).

 Overview of Treatment Algorithm
   • AUA 2022 update: rule out confusable conditions (UTI, OAB, vulvodynia); complicated presentations
     may need extra tests (e.g. UDS).
   • HL → fulguration and/or triamcinolone injection (Guideline Recommendation); refractory HL → trial
     of oral ciclosporin A (CyA).
   • Pelvic-floor tenderness/HTPFD → myofascial physical therapy (Guideline Standard).
   • No longer first- to sixth-line tiers; treatments are grouped as behavioural/non-pharmacological, oral,
     intravesical, procedures and major surgery → individualised care with shared decision-making. Patients
     may move to more aggressive therapy (except major surgery) or multimodal therapy without first
     failing “lower-tier” options.

 Treatments for Interstitial Cystitis Patients With Hunner Lesions
   • Systematic review and meta-analysis: HL and non-HL patients differ in demographics, presentation,
     urinary markers and treatment response. Only cystoscopy identifies HLs; most are seen at office
     cystoscopy.
   • AUA Recommendation: fulguration and/or triamcinolone injection into the lesions. Biopsy at the
     surgeon’s discretion if never biopsied, to exclude CIS.
   • Patients declining anaesthesia may have behavioural, oral and/or intravesical therapy after shared
     decision-making. HL patients respond as well as non-HL patients to amitriptyline, dimethyl sulfoxide
     (DMSO), BTX-A and sacral neuromodulation (SNM).
   • Most HL patients (75–90%) improve significantly after fulguration and/or triamcinolone. Mean
     interval to re-treatment 7–12 months after triamcinolone, 11–29 months after fulguration.
     Recurrence after months is expected → counsel that periodic re-treatment may be needed.
   • Effect of repeated treatment on capacity is uncertain: one series showed no fall with repeated
     fulguration; another showed about a 20% fall after repeated fulguration or resection combined with
     hydrodistension.
   • TUR of HLs relieves symptoms (initial disappearance of pain in all in one series), but carries perforation
     risk with anterior lesions.
   • Neodymium:yttrium–aluminium–garnet (Nd:YAG) laser gives similar results, but forward scatter
     makes bowel injury an ever-present danger; with fulguration/triamcinolone safe and effective, lasers
     are hard to justify.
   • Energy applied to glomerulations or normal-looking tissue does not help.

     centre — not outward, because erythema and oedema keep expanding peripherally as inflammation
     propagates with cautery.
 Ciclosporin A for refractory Hunner lesions
   • Oral CyA, an immunosuppressant/immunomodulator, is an option for HL disease refractory to
     fulguration and/or triamcinolone.
   • Early trial: frequency fell, voided volumes rose, pain fell or disappeared; symptoms recurred on
     stopping. Long-term follow-up (mean about 5 years): most refractory IC patients became pain-free and
     capacity more than doubled; relapse after stopping responded to restarting.
   • CyA far superior to PPS at 6 months in an open-label RCT. Multicentre retrospective data: success in
     most HL patients but few non-HL → HL is the target subgroup; judge on a 3–4-month trial. Similar
     results in patients failing two prior therapies, and most HL patients in a recent series were moderately
     or markedly better.
   • Side-effects in 30–55%, may force discontinuation: nephrotoxicity, hypertension, immunosuppression
     and infection, lymphoma, gingival hyperplasia, alopecia, tremor; many drug and food interactions.

     hypomagnesaemia and malignancy (skin cancer, lymphoma): full blood count, renal and hepatic panels,

 Treatments for IC/BPS Patients With Pelvic Floor Tenderness/High-Tone Pelvic Floor
 Dysfunction
   • Biofeedback, soft-tissue massage and other physical therapies relax the pelvic floor — reasonable given
     the strong HTPFD association.
   • AUA Guideline Standard: offer appropriate manual physical therapy to patients with pelvic-floor
     tenderness, if trained therapists are available (Figs. 28.6, 28.11).
   • Landmark multicentre RCT (FitzGerald et al., 2012): 10 sessions of myofascial physical therapy vs
     global therapeutic massage → moderate or marked improvement at 3 months in 59% vs 26%.
   • Goals: relax pelvic-floor, abdominal and hip trigger points; lengthen contractures; release myofascial
     and connective-tissue restrictions and hypertonicity — many tender patients show hypertonicity or
     non-relaxation.
   • Avoid pelvic-floor strengthening (Kegel) exercises — they may exacerbate pain.

 Conservative Therapies
   • Minimal risk, so an intuitive start: stress reduction, exercise, warm baths, maintaining a normal
     lifestyle. In a large patient survey, dietary change, fluid management, heat or cold and stress reduction
     helped most respondents.
   • Stress worsens pain and urgency in IC but not in controls; acute stressors exacerbate symptoms.
     Mindfulness-based stress reduction is promising; yoga, meditation and relaxation techniques (deep
     breathing) may help; maladaptive coping worsens symptoms. Catastrophising — the irrational,
     consuming fear of disaster — amplifies pain and is modifiable with CBT. A small RCT found guided
     imagery better than rest.
   • Patients learn to avoid their own triggers. Non-dietary flare triggers: UTIs, stress, tight clothing. Self-
     help for flares: heat or cold to abdomen/pelvis, sitz bath, meditation, visualisation, hypnosis, rest.
   • Acupuncture: disappointing — mostly no better than placebo, sham or standard care. An open-label
     study improved multiple parameters for 3 months, but symptoms returned to baseline by 1 year
     (placebo and bias). A small single-blind RCT found electro-acupuncture no better than minimal
     acupuncture → needs sham-controlled trials with standardised needle placement.

 Diet
   • Dietary triggers are reported by a majority of patients (53–93%); surveys are methodologically weak
     but remarkably consistent: caffeine, alcohol, artificial sweeteners, hot peppers and acidic drinks such
     as cranberry juice.
   • Evidence conflicts: a small placebo-controlled study found no diet–symptom relationship; a Japanese
     controlled trial of intensive dietary manipulation (restricting tomatoes, soy and tofu, spices, excess
     potassium, citrus and acid-inducing foods) improved symptoms up to 1 year; a case-crossover study
     linked flares to tomatoes, yoghurt, spicy food, alcohol and caffeinated carbonated drinks.
   • Commonest flare self-management: drinking extra fluid — whether as natural hydrodistension or
     dilution of irritants is unclear; also rest, dietary change, heat or cold, relaxation.



                                                              page 19 of 33
Chapter 28 — Interstitial Cystitis/Bladder Pain Syndrome and Related Disorders                            TREATMENT OF IC/BPS
   • Urine pH hypothesis: acid-sensing ion channels are expressed, and some upregulated, in IC/BPS
     bladders. But a double-blind crossover instillation of urine at pH 5.0 vs 7.5 showed no difference in
     pain → manipulating urine pH has little effect. Orange and grapefruit juices, rich in potassium and
     citrate, raise urine pH, yet patients avoid them — either triggers are misattributed or another process
     (e.g. bladder–bowel cross-talk) operates. Alkalinising is still worth trying — calcium glycerophosphate
     (Prelief, an over-the-counter food-acid reducer) or baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) — without
     controlled trials.
   • Elimination diet — sequence: diary of food, voiding and pain → bland diet from compiled food lists
     (Box 28.4) → slowly reintroduce suspect items, recording the effect → avoid only confirmed triggers.
     Triggers are individual, some patients have none, and the method prevents unnecessary restriction
     and nutritional deficit. A short validated questionnaire captures baseline sensitivities.
   • Education and behavioural modification programme (EBMP), the control arm of the landmark NIH
     amitriptyline RCT (Foster et al., 2010), in newly diagnosed patients, covered four areas: (1) symptom
     control, (2) fluid control, (3) diet, (4) bladder training and urge suppression, plus behavioural stress
     and pain management. EBMP alone: 45% moderately or markedly improved at 12 weeks; adding
     amitriptyline (55%) was not significantly better. Separately, hydrodistension followed by bladder
     training beat hydrodistension alone at 24 weeks.

 Amitriptyline
   • A TCA and a staple of oral treatment. Properties: (1) central and peripheral anticholinergic action; (2)
     blockade of serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake; (3) sedation — central, but related also to (4)
     antihistamine action, amitriptyline being one of the most potent H1-blocking TCAs. It may also
     stimulate β-adrenergic receptors in the bladder body, reducing detrusor excitability and aiding
     storage.
   • Origin: a “serendipitous” response in a patient treated for depression. The rationale — low-dose
     efficacy in chronic pain plus anticholinergic, β-adrenergic, sedative and H1 actions — led to trials at 25–
     75 mg with promising results but high dropout; sedation the commonest complaint. Uncontrolled
     studies (and desipramine) also reported success.
   • Double-blind intention-to-treat RCT (25–100 mg as tolerated): good or excellent satisfaction far more
     often than placebo; little tachyphylaxis at 19 months, with response across the symptom spectrum.
   • Landmark NIH RCT (Foster et al., 2010): EBMP with vs without amitriptyline 10–75 mg → 55% vs 45%,
     not significant. Subgroup tolerating ≥50 mg: 77% vs 53% — efficacy beyond EBMP.
   • Phenotype reanalysis: benefit confined to widespread pain; no significant effect in localised pelvic
     pain.

   • Efficacy independent of HL status; cystoscopy does not predict outcome. May also help accompanying
     pain disorders (e.g. vulvodynia).
   • Dosing sequence: 10 mg at bedtime → increase by 10 mg weekly → maximum tolerated dose of 50 mg
     or more at bedtime. Median preferred dose 50 mg (range 25–75 mg); slow titration minimises side-
     effects, and long-term benefit may occur below 50 mg.
   • Side-effects: fatigue, constipation, dry mouth, increased appetite and weight gain, loss of libido,
     nausea, dizziness → take at bedtime. Delayed ejaculation, anorgasmia, reduced libido are common to
     all TCAs.
   • Caution: obstructive voiding or incomplete emptying and chronic constipation (both worsened);
     orthostatic hypotension.
   • Contraindications: long QT syndrome; significant conduction disease (bifascicular or trifascicular block);
     myocardial infarction within 6 months; unstable angina; congestive heart failure; frequent premature
     ventricular contractions; history of sustained ventricular arrhythmia. Doses ≥100 mg raise the relative
     risk of sudden cardiac death.
   • Nortriptyline (secondary amine) may cause fewer side-effects than amitriptyline (tertiary amine) and is
     used in other pain syndromes, but no IC/BPS trials.

 Antihistamines
   • Both H1 and H2 receptors are overexpressed in IC/BPS bladders; in animals, mast-cell-dependent
     pelvic pain is mediated by both.
   • Hydroxyzine: rationale — blocks neuronal activation of mast cells. Uncontrolled, unblinded series:
     almost every symptom improved by about 30%; better in patients with documented allergy or
     evidence of bladder mast-cell activation. NIH placebo RCT: no significant response. Still useful for its
     safety and sleep benefit (often with amitriptyline), especially in patients with systemic allergies.
   • Cimetidine: two-thirds improved in uncontrolled series (600 mg daily, divided); effective in a double-
     blind placebo RCT at 400 mg twice daily. Mucosa histologically unchanged → mechanism unexplained;
     H1/H2 expression does not differ between responders and non-responders to cimetidine or
     amitriptyline. Widely used in the UK (over a third of patients).

 Pentosan Polysulfate Sodium
   • Approved 1996 under the FDA orphan products programme — still the only FDA-approved oral drug
     for IC (“relief of bladder pain or discomfort associated with interstitial cystitis”).
   • Synthetic sulfated polysaccharide resembling bladder-surface GAGs. Only 3–6% of an oral dose reaches
     the urine. Proposed actions: repair of GAG defects in a “leaky” urothelium; sequestration of “toxic
     cations”. May take 3–6 months to work.

 Immunoregulating agents
   • Chronic oral steroids are not recommended. Studied and ineffective: suplatast tosilate, mycophenolate
     mofetil, adalimumab. CyA may help refractory HL disease (above).
 Quercetin
   • Over-the-counter bioflavonoid with possible anti-inflammatory action. 500 mg twice daily for 4 weeks:
     almost all of a small uncontrolled group improved on O’Leary–Sant and global scores; placebo-
     controlled trials needed.
 Antibiotics
   • Excluding UTI is integral to diagnosis. No organism has been consistently identified by any technology;
     the urinary microbiome and metabolome may yet inform the question.
   • Double-blind placebo RCT: 18 weeks of rifampicin plus sequential doxycycline, erythromycin,
     metronidazole, clindamycin, amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin (3 weeks each). Improvement was more
     frequent with antibiotics but not significant, confounded by new IC/BPS therapy in both arms. Adverse
     events were significant: 80% vs 40%, mainly nausea/vomiting and diarrhoea. Most correctly guessed
     their arm, and correct guessers were more likely to report improvement; no durable benefit was
     reported.

 Gabapentinoids
   • Gabapentin (anticonvulsant) is effective in neuropathic pain — diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic

     neuropathic pain and FMS. No randomised studies of either in IC/BPS.

   • Appeal: treats the end-organ directly without systemic side-effects; suits patients intolerant of oral

 Dimethyl Sulfoxide
   • 50% DMSO is FDA-approved only as a stand-alone treatment, though often given in a “cocktail” with
     sodium bicarbonate, heparin, steroids and/or lidocaine.
   • By-product of wood pulp, a lignin derivative; exceptional solvent, miscible with water, lipids and
     organic agents. Properties: membrane penetration, enhanced drug absorption, anti-inflammatory and
     analgesic action, collagen dissolution, muscle relaxation, mast-cell histamine release. In-vitro effects
     belie in-vivo benefit, and no histamine release is seen after treatment; it may desensitise lower-
     urinary-tract nociceptive pathways. Human use began in the 1960s (musculoskeletal inflammation,
     scleroderma skin).
   • Two randomised crossover trials: DMSO vs saline (4 instillations of 50 mL at 2-week intervals, 15-min
     dwell) and DMSO vs bacille Calmette–Guérin (BCG, ineffective in IC/BPS; 6 weekly instillations) both
     favoured DMSO.
   • Patients with DO do not respond; HL patients may respond better — pain improves with or without
     HLs, frequency only with HLs. A multicentre double-blind placebo RCT in Japanese patients with
     bladder-centric features (mostly HL, lidocaine responders; 50 mL every 2 weeks for 12 weeks, 15-min
     dwell) improved ICSI; post hoc, no efficacy in non-HL → anti-inflammatory action suits inflammatory
     lesions.
   • Practical: easy, low morbidity. Rat bladder strips: contraction abolished at 40%, compliance reduced at
     30%, negligible effect at ≤25% (human relevance unknown). Rare eosinophilic cystitis after DMSO.
     Warn of severe urgency and frequency with the first instillations. Dwell 15–20 minutes — rapidly
     absorbed; longer holding is unnecessary and may worsen pain. Garlic-like breath (partial pulmonary
     excretion) makes blinded trials impossible.
   • Cocktail: 50 mL Rimso-50 + 80 mg triamcinolone + 44 mEq sodium bicarbonate + 20,000–40,000 units
     heparin ± lidocaine, weekly for 6 weeks; responders may have monthly maintenance for 6 months.
     DMSO enhances absorption of co-administered drugs → risk of systemic lidocaine toxicity. Cohorts
     report about 60% success and propose first-line use; no combination has proved superior to DMSO
     alone (systematic review).

     proliferation, angiogenesis and smooth-muscle proliferation; virtually no systemic absorption, even
     from an inflamed bladder. Regimens: 10,000 U in sterile water alone or with DMSO; 25,000 U twice
     weekly for 3 months (≥50% improvement in International Prostate Symptom Score in most women);
     40,000 U in 20 mL daily, self-administered and held 30–60 min, with “reasonable improvement”
     expected between 6 months and 2 years.

   • Diagnostic and therapeutic; almost immediate effect → useful for flares or maintenance. A positive

   • Lidocaine: sodium-channel blocker, also anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial; short serum half-life; not
     absorbed through urothelium to a significant degree unless alkaline → alkalinise with sodium
     bicarbonate.

     modest 30% response underlines how often pain is extravesical. In the open-label phase about half
     improved at 3 and 10 days. Alkalinised and non-alkalinised lidocaine have never been compared.

 OnabotulinumtoxinA
   • FDA-approved for refractory neurogenic DO and OAB; long used for muscular hypercontraction and
     described for HTPFD and vulvodynia. Not FDA-approved for IC/BPS, but observational studies, a few
     RCTs and several meta-analyses support efficacy and low risk in selected cases. AUA Option. Repeated
     injections, at intervals or on recurrence, remain effective; earlier use may follow shared decision-
     making. Best suited to bladder-centric patients.
   • Mechanisms beyond inhibiting presynaptic acetylcholine release: analgesic (not merely relief of
     spasm); reduces peripheral sensitisation by inhibiting glutamate and substance P release and c-Fos
     expression; reduces sensory feedback from muscle (acetylcholine release from gamma motor neurons
     to spindle fibres); inhibits sensory neurotransmitter release in rat models of acute injury and chronic
     inflammation; reduces chronic inflammation and apoptosis after repeated injection in IC/BPS; blocks
     calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) release in rats; restores afferent responses and minimises DO
     after barrier disruption in animals.

 BOX 28.6 CLINICAL PEARLS OF BTX-A INJECTION
   • Indication: IC/BPS refractory to conservative, oral and intravesical treatment; AUA Option; shared
     decision-making, including that BTX-A is not FDA-approved for IC/BPS; experienced clinicians only.
   • Technique: 100 U diluted in 5 mL preservative-free saline (20 U/mL), injected into the trigone in 1-mL
     aliquots, limiting volume load and minimising impaired emptying.
   • Prerequisite: willing and able to perform clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC). Retention risk is low
     but not zero, and CIC is hard for IC/BPS patients — urethral pain from catheterisation, and frequent
     catheterisation because of low functional capacity and hypersensitivity. Patients already self-
     catheterising for instillations are good candidates. Use with caution when symptoms or signs of
     incomplete emptying are present.

 Neuromodulation
   • International Neuromodulation Society definition: “the alteration of nerve activity through targeted
     delivery of a stimulus, such as electrical stimulation or chemical agents, to specific neurologic sites in
     the body.” Appeal: modulates nociceptive transmission and pain perception; mechanism poorly
     understood (gate-control theory); may reduce pelvic-floor hypertonicity and HTPFD symptoms.

 Surgical Therapy
 Hydrodistention
   • AUA Option: short-duration (1–3 minutes), low-pressure (60–80 cm H₂O) hydrodistension under
     anaesthesia.

 BOX 28.7 CLINICAL PEARLS OF HYDRODISTENTION
   • Sequence: initial cystoscopy (usually unremarkable) → if no HLs, distend 1–3 minutes at 80 cm H₂O by
     gravity, inspecting throughout (mucosal or deeper tears occur rapidly); compress the urethra digitally
     against the scope if leakage occurs → empty and refill → observe for glomerulations → further
     therapeutic distension for several minutes → biopsy rarely needed, and only after distension
     (perforation) → instil anaesthetic at the end (20–30 mL 2% lidocaine, or 1:1 20 mL 1% lidocaine + 20 mL
     0.25% bupivacaine).
   • Counsel that a post-procedure flare is common, often needing a short course of urinary anaesthetics
     or oral analgesics.
   • Outcomes: in a retrospective series, adding a transvaginal trigonal nerve block gave no extra benefit,
     but pain scores fell by about half at 1 month; 2-minute and ≥5-minute distensions were equivalent.
   • About half to 60% respond, but most responses last under 2 months; efficacy declines steeply —
     about half at 1 month, a quarter at 3 months, almost none (0–7%) at 6 months.
   • Repeated hydrodistension does not reduce capacity, with or without HLs.
   • Anaesthetic capacity <600 mL → better response than larger capacities.
   • Favourable results reported combining hydrodistension with BTX-A, with GAG therapy, and in ketamine
     cystitis. Capacity <400 mL marks a distinct bladder-centric phenotype (see Mapping Phenotypes).

 Major Surgery
   • Primum non nocere applies here above all. Cystoscopic triamcinolone/fulguration for HLs earns a
     strong AUA recommendation because of its high success and low complication rates; major
     reconstruction only when all conservative treatment has failed. Worst case: significant complications
     and continuing pain. Conversely, suicides have been attributed to unremitting pain when aggressive
     intervention was withheld.
 Patient selection for major surgery
   • Major surgery (urinary diversion ± cystectomy, augmentation cystoplasty) is the last resort for rare,
     extremely carefully selected patients with bladder-centric symptoms who have failed all other
     therapy.
   • Risks: operative morbidity, mortality, devastating complications and irreversible lifestyle change
     (urostomy). Pain relief is not guaranteed and may persist even after cystectomy; the joy of symptom
     resolution is real, but evidence is small, heterogeneous retrospective series. Deliberate informed
     consent; if expectations are unrealistic, counsel further.
   • Reserve for the motivated, well-informed patient with extremely severe unresponsive disease —
     fewer than 10% of IC/BPS; only experienced surgeons committed to IC/BPS care.
   • Sequence before considering surgery: careful history → body map → pelvic-floor examination for
     levator tenderness/HTPFD → trial of intravesical local anaesthetic (profound relief localises pain to the
     bladder; an equivocal response suggests an extravesical source) → identify and treat other pelvic pain
     generators.

   • Diversion without cystectomy, or trigone-retaining augmentation, is controversial: bladder
     preservation lowers operative morbidity, but persistent bladder or trigonal pain may force later
     cystectomy or revision.
   • Diversion ± cystourethrectomy is the ultimate surgical answer — cutting the “Gordian knot”: it
     eliminates urine–mucosa contact and bladder distension, and with them the pain, frequency and
     urgency. Unlike augmentation, no incomplete emptying or CIC (painful in IC/BPS) and lower metabolic
     risk.
   • Bladder left in situ: lower morbidity, but future persistent pain, pyocystis, haemorrhage, unremitting
     sense of incomplete emptying and spasm. Bladder carcinoma has been reported but is not specifically
     associated with IC/BPS.

 FINAL THOUGHTS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS
   • The chapter incorporates the 2022 AUA Guideline and, beyond it, a phenotypic approach. Analogy:
     cardiologists do not treat all chest pain alike — they distinguish coronary disease, valvular disease,
     aortic aneurysm, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia and pleuritic pain. Urologists should not treat all
     bladder/pelvic pain alike.
   • Bladder-centric, pelvic-floor-centric and widespread-pain subgroups each likely benefit from tailored
     treatment — a step towards precision medicine; trials by phenotype are needed.
   • HL status has such bearing on care that it justifies formally separating the nomenclature: IC (with
     HLs) vs BPS (without).
   • Success also depends on the learned art of the field: medical detective work across many pain
     generators, and a plan built on the whole clinical picture.
