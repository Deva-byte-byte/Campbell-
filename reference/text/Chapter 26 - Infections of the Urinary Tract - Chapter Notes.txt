Chapter 26 — Infections of the Urinary Tract
 • Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are common, vary widely in presentation and sequelae, and are one of the
   commonest reasons for prescribing antibiotics.
 • Most cause morbidity and rarely become life-threatening; in certain populations, undiagnosed and
   untreated UTI carries significant mortality.
 • Infection follows when bacterial virulence rises or host defence falls → inoculation → colonisation →
   infection. Careful diagnosis and treatment resolve most episodes.
 • Urine is not sterile; bacteriuria may even be protective against UTI.
 • With multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms spreading, clinical evaluation plus urinalysis and culture must
   precede antibiotics; guidelines should govern both agent selection and duration; education of
   practitioners and patients is paramount.

DEFINITIONS
 • UTI — an inflammatory response of the urothelium to bacterial invasion, usually with bacteriuria and
   pyuria.
 • Bacteriuria — bacteria in the urine; usually indicates colonisation or infection, but bacteria can sit in the
   urothelium without bacteriuria, and bacteriuria may be contamination at collection.
 • Pyuria — white blood cells (WBCs) in the urine; indicates infection and/or inflammation from bacteria,
   stones, foreign body or other causes.

 • Cystitis — dysuria, frequency, urgency, ± suprapubic pain. The same symptoms occur with urethral or
   vaginal infection and non-infective conditions: interstitial cystitis/painful bladder syndrome (IC/PBS),
   bladder carcinoma, calculi.
 • Acute pyelonephritis — chills, fever, flank pain plus bacteriuria and pyuria; this combination is reasonably
   specific for acute bacterial renal infection.
      ◦ Diagnose on clinical presentation, not on radiological perinephric inflammation alone.
      ◦ Presentation is atypical in spinal cord injury (SCI) — flank symptoms may be absent.
 • Chronic pyelonephritis — atrophic, fibrotic kidney with morphological, radiological or functional renal
   disease; may be post-infective but often not linked to current infection.
      ◦ Infective scar: focal, coarse cortical scar overlying a calyx, almost always with calyceal distortion —
        radiologically identical to scarring from vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) or calyceal stone disease.
      ◦ Less often: generalised cortical thinning (atrophic pyelonephritis) → small kidney resembling post-
        obstructive atrophy.
      ◦ Fig. 26.1 (excretory urogram): focal coarse scarring with blunted calyces, one calyx reaching the
        capsule, in a young adult with febrile infections and VUR in infancy — reinfections later stopped on
        prophylaxis, with normal blood pressure and creatinine.
      ◦ Fig. 26.2: uniform, regular cortical atrophy in spina bifida with neurogenic bladder and childhood
        febrile bacteriuria — reflux of bacteria into virtually all nephrons; this uncommon pattern is
        characteristic of obstruction with superimposed infection.

 • US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) 2018 definition of complicated UTI — pyuria + documented
   pathogen in urine or blood culture + local/systemic features (fever >38 °C, chills, malaise, flank/back pain,
   costovertebral angle pain or tenderness) in the presence of a functional or anatomical abnormality or
   catheterisation.
 • Functional abnormalities: renal disease impairing urinary concentration (tubulointerstitial: post-
   obstructive, sickle cell, lithium, chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis, medullary cystic kidney disease);
   bladder outlet obstruction (BOO), neurogenic bladder (NGB), detrusor underactivity.
 • Anatomical abnormalities: benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH); sites of residual urine (calyceal, urethral
   or bladder diverticula); extrinsic or intrinsic ureteric obstruction.
 • The European Section of Infections in Urology classification combines clinical presentation, severity, host
   and pathogen risk factors — useful for practice and trial design.

 • Antimicrobial suppression — prevents growth of a focus of persistence that cannot be eradicated.
   Contrast: prophylaxis presumes a sterile tract; suppression presumes an ineradicable focus.
 • Domiciliary (outpatient) — acquired outside hospital/institution; nosocomial (health care–associated) —
   acquired while hospitalised or institutionalised.
 • Catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI) — in patients with indwelling drainage catheters; the commonest cause

   female sex, advanced age, diabetes.

 KEY POINTS: DEFINITIONS
   • Bacteriuria and pyuria are not synonymous with UTI.
   • UTIs are classified by presumed site of origin.
   • Uncomplicated = healthy host, normal tract; complicated = factors raising acquisition and lowering
     treatment efficacy.

Concept — Every definition here separates finding from disease: bacteriuria, pyuria and scarring are
observations, whereas "infection", "complicated" and "persistence" are judgements about host, tract and
organism — and it is the judgement, not the finding, that dictates evaluation and treatment.

INCIDENCE AND EPIDEMIOLOGY
 • UTI complaints are among the commonest primary diagnoses in women attending emergency
   departments (USA); millions of office and emergency visits annually.
 • Almost half of all women have a UTI in their lifetime; nearly a third have needed antibiotics for one by
   age 24; about one in ten adult women has one UTI per year.
 • Self-reported cystitis and pyelonephritis admissions are several-fold higher in women than men.
 • Costs are large, and resistant organisms cost more to treat than susceptible ones. A European survey of
   women with recurrent UTI (GESPRIT) documented repeated doctor visits, sick leave and limited-activity
   days each year.
 • Bacteriuria rises with institutionalisation, hospitalisation and comorbidity. Roughly one in ten
   hospitalised urological patients worldwide develops a complicated UTI during admission.
 • CAUTIs are the commonest nosocomial infection, constituting about 80% of nosocomial UTIs; they
   account for a large share of complicated UTIs, most being hospital or health care–acquired.
 • Incidence is also raised in pregnancy, SCI, diabetes mellitus (DM), multiple sclerosis (MS), human
   immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) and organ transplant
   recipients.
 • Probability of recurrence rises with the number of previous infections and falls with the interval since
   the last infection; established historically and confirmed in modern cohorts.
      ◦ Highest in young, sexually active women: about a quarter have a second infection within 6 months of
        a first.
      ◦ Women with ≥2 previous UTIs carry two to five times the 1-year recurrence risk of those with 0–1.
      ◦ Postmenopausal women with prior UTI are similarly at risk; even with daily trimethoprim–
        sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) or lactobacilli prophylaxis most recur within a year.
      ◦ Recurrent bacteriuria segregates into a small subgroup with very high recurrence and a majority with
        low recurrence.
 • About 70% of recurrences are reinfection with a different organism.

 • No association between uncomplicated recurrent UTI and renal scarring, hypertension or progressive
   azotaemia has been established.

 KEY POINTS: INCIDENCE AND EPIDEMIOLOGY
   • UTI is the commonest bacterial infection, with a large health care cost.
   • Bacteriuria rises with institutionalisation/hospitalisation and comorbidities that immunosuppress or
     alter lower tract function (e.g. catheterisation).
   • Probability of recurrent UTI (rUTI) rises with the number of previous infections.

Concept — The best predictor of the next UTI is the last one: recurrence clusters in a susceptible minority
and is mostly reinfection rather than relapse, which is why the chapter later locates the problem in the host's
introital and bladder biology rather than in a hidden focus.

PATHOGENESIS
 • UTI results from uropathogen–host interaction: virulence factors, inoculum size and inadequacy of host
   defence determine infection and the extent of colonisation and damage.
 • Governing asymmetry: strong host → only highly virulent bacteria infect; compromised host → bacteria
   with minimal virulence suffice.

Routes of Infection
Ascending Route
 • Most bacteria enter from the bowel and skin reservoir via the urethra into the bladder; adherence to
   introital and urothelial mucosa is central.
 • Risk factors: faecal soiling of the perineum, spermicide use, intermittent or indwelling catheters.
 • Most pyelonephritis is retrograde ascent bladder → ureter → pelvis → parenchyma.
      ◦ Reflux probably is not required; cystitis-related oedema may distort the vesicoureteric junction
        enough to permit reflux (Fig. 26.3).
      ◦ Ascent is aided by anything that impairs ureteric peristalsis: Gram-negative bacteria and endotoxins,
        pregnancy, ureteric obstruction, high lower-tract pressure (all antiperistaltic).
      ◦ Entry to parenchyma is via collecting ducts at the papillary tips → ascent in collecting tubules;
        hastened by raised intrapelvic pressure (obstruction, VUR), especially with intrarenal reflux.

 • Sequence: periurethral contamination by bowel-colonising uropathogenic E. coli → type 1-fimbriated E.
   coli selected in bladder (high colony-forming units [CFU], low oxygen), invasion and intracellular
   multiplication → ascent → P fimbriae bind renal tubular cells → haemolysin damages epithelium,
   cytokine induction → crosses tubular epithelium → bacteraemia.
Haematogenous Route
 • Uncommon in normal individuals; kidney occasionally seeded by Staphylococcus aureus bacteraemia
   (e.g. oral source) or Candida fungaemia. Obstruction enhances haematogenous infection experimentally.
Lymphatic Route
 • Direct lymphatic extension from adjacent organs (severe bowel infection, retroperitoneal abscess) —
   rare; little role in most UTIs.

Urinary Pathogens
 • Most UTIs are due to facultative anaerobes from bowel flora; Staphylococcus epidermidis and Candida
   albicans come from vaginal or perineal skin flora.
 • Escherichia coli is by far the commonest cause — about 85% of community-acquired and 50% of
   hospital-acquired UTIs.
 • Remaining community infections: other Enterobacteriaceae (Proteus, Klebsiella) and Gram-positive
   Enterococcus faecalis and Staphylococcus saprophyticus.
 • S. saprophyticus causes about 10% of symptomatic lower UTIs in young, sexually active women; rare in
   men and the elderly (age shapes the organism).
 • Nosocomial organisms: E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Citrobacter, Serratia, Pseudomonas aeruginosa,
   Providencia, E. faecalis, S. epidermidis.
 • Less common: Gardnerella vaginalis, Mycoplasma, Ureaplasma urealyticum (some in catheterised
   patients). U. urealyticum and Mycoplasma hominis found in women with chronic symptoms and
   negative standard cultures — targeted treatment improved symptoms.
 • Emerging uropathogens (identified by better culture and microbiome work):

Fastidious Organisms
Anaerobes in the Urinary Tract
 • Symptomatic anaerobic UTI is uncommon, though distal urethra, perineum and vagina are colonised;
   anaerobes are a frequent midstream contaminant but rare in suprapubic aspirates.
 • Suspect them when persistent irritative symptoms, cocci or Gram-negative rods on microscopy of
   centrifuged urine, and routine aerobic culture is sterile.
 • Common in enteric fistulae and suppurative genitourinary (GU) infection — the large majority of scrotal,
   prostatic and perinephric abscesses in men contain anaerobes: Bacteroides (incl. B. fragilis),
   Fusobacterium, anaerobic cocci, Clostridium perfringens.
Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Other Nontuberculous Mycobacteria
 • Urinary tuberculosis (UTB) presents with haematuria (gross or micro) and/or sterile pyuria, storage
   symptoms, dysuria, ± fever and weakness.
 • Diagnosis is delayed by nonspecific symptoms → risk of renal impairment and failure.
 • PCR for M. tuberculosis has replaced acid-fast staining as the ideal diagnostic method.
Chlamydia
 • Not grown in routine aerobic culture; implicated in GU infection.

Bacterial Virulence Factors
 • UPEC resides in bowel flora and infects not by chance but by virulence factors that allow adherence to
   perineum and urethra, migration, and an inflammatory response in the urothelium; the same factors
   appear in rUTI strains.
 • Many determinants lie on about 20 UPEC-specific pathogenicity-associated islands (PAIs), which enlarge
   the genome >20% over commensal strains. Phylogroups are defined by PAIs and their virulence factors
   (adhesins, toxins, surface polysaccharides, flagella, iron acquisition).

Early Events in Uropathogenic Escherichia coli Pathogenesis
 • Sequence: colonisation of periurethral/vaginal tissue and urethra → ascent into bladder → adherence to
   surface urothelium (engaging epithelial defence) → biofilm → invasion and replication as intracellular
   bacterial communities (IBCs), which can form quiescent intracellular reservoirs (QIRs) dormant in
   underlying urothelium → in some, renal colonisation, tissue damage and sepsis.

 • Type 1 pili bind uroplakins Ia and IIIa → signalling (partly unknown) → invasion and apoptosis.
 • Type 1 pili binding α3β1 integrins → internalisation into superficial facet (umbrella) cells → IBCs ("pods").
 • Exfoliation exposes deeper transitional cells → further invasion → QIRs implicated in recurrence. In the
   kidney, haemolysin (HlyA) and secreted autotransporter toxin (Sat) cause tissue damage with neutrophil
   influx.
Bacterial Adherence
 • Adherence to vaginal and urothelial cells is an essential first step; governed by bacterial adhesins,
   epithelial receptivity and the fluid bathing both; it helps determine organism, host and site.
 • Adhesins are fimbrial (pili) or afimbrial. A piliated cell carries 100–400 pili, each 5–10 nm wide, up to 2
   µm long, built from pilin subunits. Pili are defined by haemagglutination of specific erythrocytes.

 • Adherence correlates with severity: strains from acute pyelonephritis are highly adhesive; those from
   asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) or healthy faeces are poorly adhesive.
 • P pili frequency falls stepwise: pyelonephritis (most, about 90%) > cystitis > ASB > faecal isolates (the

Phase Variation of Bacterial Pili in Vivo
 • Phase variation — rapid switching between piliated and non-piliated phases with environment (e.g.
   broth vs solid medium); occurs in vivo (immunofluorescence of fresh urine shows mixed populations;
   eFig. 26.7, online only). Strains from different urogenital sites differ in piliation.
 • Sequence of advantage: type 1 pili help initial bladder adherence → become a liability in suspension
   (enhance apoptosis, phagocytosis and neutrophil killing) → P pili take over in the kidney via glycolipid
   receptors.

Epithelial Cell Receptivity
Vaginal Cells
 • E. coli adheres more avidly to vaginal cells of women with recurrent cystitis — the first demonstrable
   biological difference in UTI-susceptible women (Stamey's work).
 • The increased adherence is also present on buccal cells → receptor differences are not vagina-specific
   and may be genetic.

 • Certain Lewis blood-group phenotypes correlate with rUTI in women.
 • Conclusion: increased epithelial receptivity (introital, urethral, buccal) characterises susceptible women
   and may be inherited.
Bladder Cells
 • Bladder lumen lined by umbrella cells whose apical surface is a quasi-crystalline array of hexagonal
   uroplakin complexes (four integral membrane proteins).
 • FimH binds mannosylated uroplakins; UPIa and UPIb bind type 1-piliated UPEC; pilus tips sit in the
   central cavity of the uroplakin hexamers, and contact triggers local actin rearrangement and membrane
   protrusion around the bacterium (Fig. 26.8). FimH binding is the initial step of the cascade.
 • FimH–UPIb binding → phosphorylation of UPIII → raised intracellular calcium (immediate urothelial
   response).
 • Host elimination mechanisms: (1) urothelial exfoliation, (2) exocytosis from infected cells, (3)
   autophagy.
 • UPEC persistence in the bladder: rapid internalisation into superficial cells (a niche protected from innate
   immunity) → early IBC: rods about 3 µm, loose random clusters → at 6–8 h growth slows (doubling >60
   min), rods shorten to about 0.7 µm, switch to a biofilm-like community.
      ◦ IBC-like cells occur in about a fifth of voided urines from women with E. coli UTI, and such isolates
        recreate IBCs in mice.
 • Biofilms raise antibiotic resistance by (1) poor antibiotic penetration of matrix, (2) slow growth and
   altered physiology, (3) expression of factors that inhibit antibiotic activity; they also exclude
   neutrophils.
 • Fluxing — edge bacteria detach, resume rod form, become motile and escape into the lumen; may
   become filamentous (≥70 µm), by about 24 h; filaments may evade immunity. Flagellar motility is
   common in UPEC and aids adherence and dispersal.
 • Escaped bacteria reinvade → second and further IBC rounds → after a few days, quiescence. Exfoliation
   exposes deeper layers → QIRs in underlying transitional epithelium; urothelial disruption can reactivate
   them → rUTI from an intracellular bladder reservoir.

Natural Defences of the Urinary Tract
Periurethral and Urethral Region
 • Normal flora of introitus, periurethra and urethra — lactobacilli, CoNS, corynebacteria, streptococci —
   forms a barrier to uropathogen colonisation.


                                                    page 8 of 70
Chapter 26 — Infections of the Urinary Tract                                                             PATHOGENESIS
 • Colonisation shifts with oestrogen and pH, but acute change is most often due to antimicrobials and
   spermicides that alter flora and increase epithelial receptivity.
 • Colonisation of periurethra/urethra is a prerequisite for most infections, but overcoming urine and
   bladder defences is pivotal; urethral defence relies on regular urine flow and indigenous flora.
Urine
 • Antibacterial when the inoculum is small. Main inhibitory factors: osmolality, urea, organic acids, pH.
   Growth is deterred by very dilute urine or high osmolality with low pH; clinically these do not separate
   susceptible from resistant patients.
 • Uromodulin (Tamm–Horsfall protein) — the most abundant urinary protein, made in the kidney;
   competes with uroplakins for type 1 pili, blocking adhesion.
Bladder
 • Bacteria probably enter the bladder frequently; whether they establish infection depends partly on the
   bladder's ability to empty. Bacterial recognition up-regulates antimicrobial peptides from urothelium
   and resident immune cells.

Other Conditions That Increase Risk of Urinary Tract Infections
Obstruction
 • Obstruction at any level (anatomical or high storage pressure) is a key host susceptibility factor: stasis
   impairs bladder and renal defences, promotes bacterial growth and adherence.
 • Experimentally the kidney resists haematogenous infection unless the ureter is ligated — then only the
   obstructed kidney is infected. Obstruction converts cystitis or pyelonephritis into a life-threatening
   infection.
Vesicoureteral Reflux
 • Association of VUR + UTI with calyceal blunting and parenchymal scarring first described by Hodson and
   Edwards (1960).
 • Children with high-grade VUR and UTI → progressive scarring, proteinuria, renal failure.
 • Adults — VUR does not reduce renal function unless combined with high storage pressure or stasis and
   concurrent UTI.

Underlying Disease
 • High incidence of renal scarring with causes of chronic interstitial nephritis, almost all of which damage
   the renal papilla first: DM, sickle cell disorders, adult nephrocalcinosis, hyperphosphataemia,
   hypokalaemia, analgesic abuse, sulfonamide nephropathy, gout, heavy-metal poisoning, ageing.
Diabetes Mellitus
 • Increased rUTI from impaired local defence: glycosuria, neutrophil dysfunction, increased uroepithelial
   adherence; plus incomplete emptying from diabetic neuropathy and chronic kidney disease reducing
   anti-infective efficacy.
 • More severe infections: acute pyelonephritis, renal abscess, emphysematous pyelonephritis; atypical
   organisms (yeast); sequelae include papillary necrosis and perinephric abscess.

Renal Papillary Necrosis
 • Role of infection is controversial. Predisposing: DM, analgesic abuse, sickle cell haemoglobinopathy,
   obstruction (Box 26.2).
 • Spectrum: acute fulminating illness ↔ chronic, incidental. Some pass necrotic tissue chronically; some
   never do. Retained papillae may calcify (especially with infection) and act as a nidus for chronic infection;
   opportunistic fungal infection reported.
 • Management: antimicrobials to control infection + early recognition and relief of ureteric obstruction by
   sloughed papillae limit functional decline.

BOX 26.2 CONDITIONS ASSOCIATED WITH RENAL PAPILLARY NECROSIS
 • Diabetes mellitus; pyelonephritis; urinary tract obstruction; analgesic abuse; sickle cell
   haemoglobinopathies; renal transplant rejection; cirrhosis; dehydration, hypoxia and jaundice of
   infants.
 • Miscellaneous: renal vein thrombosis, cryoglobulinaemia, renal candidiasis, contrast injection,
   amyloidosis, calyceal arteritis, necrotising angiitis, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, hypotensive
   shock, acute pancreatitis.
 • Data from Eknoyan et al., 1982. Pattern: nearly all compromise medullary blood flow or oxygenation of
   the papilla. The classic mnemonic group is DM, obstruction/pyelonephritis, analgesics, sickle cell.
Human Immunodeficiency Virus
 • UTIs about fivefold commoner in HIV-positive individuals; more Acinetobacter and Salmonella.
 • AIDS (AIDS-defining illness and/or CD4 <200 cells/mm³) → atypical infection: fungal (Candida,
   Aspergillus, Cryptococcus neoformans, endemic fungi such as Histoplasma), mycobacterial (tuberculosis
   [TB] and non-tuberculous), viral (cytomegalovirus, adenovirus).
 • CD4 <200 → Pneumocystis pneumonia prophylaxis, typically TMP-SMX, which also lowers UTI risk (unless
   sulfa allergy or glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] deficiency).
Renal Transplantation
 • UTIs are among the commonest infections after renal transplant (more than a third of recipients have at
   least one) and may worsen graft outcome.
 • More resistant organisms and recurrent symptomatic episodes (urinary tract colonisation).
 • Risk factors: age, female sex, long pre-transplant dialysis, urinary tract obstruction.
 • Acute pyelonephritis of the graft is an independent risk factor for functional decline.
Menopause
 • UTI frequency rises after menopause (mechanism and treatment later).
Pregnancy
 • See Bacteriuria in Pregnancy.
Spinal Cord Injury With High-Pressure Bladders
 • No bacteriuric group compares in severity and morbidity with SCI. Nearly all need early catheterisation
   (spinal shock, poor contractility); long term → poor compliance, high storage pressure → VUR,
   ureterectasis, hydronephrosis, renal impairment.

 KEY POINTS: PATHOGENESIS
   • Most UTIs in women are ascending, from bowel and skin flora.

Concept — Pathogenesis is a contest between adhesion and clearance: every bacterial virulence factor (pili,

 • Key issues in analysis:
      ◦ Leucocyte esterase — from WBC breakdown → indicates pyuria, not bacteria; common with vaginal
        contamination.
      ◦ Nitrite — bacterial nitrate reductase converts dietary nitrate. All Enterobacteriaceae produce nitrite
        (E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Proteus, Citrobacter, Morganella, Salmonella). Non-producers: all
        Gram-positives and Pseudomonas/Acinetobacter. Low-nitrate diet → false negative.
      ◦ Nitrite: high specificity, low sensitivity. Esterase and nitrite are more reliable at >10⁵ CFU/mL.
      ◦ Cloudy urine = pyuria or phosphate crystals in alkaline/concentrated urine.
      ◦ Analyse promptly; standing urine alkalinises, bacteria multiply, red cells lyse → refrigerate if not
        analysed within a few hours (unless preservative). Hydration dilutes constituents.
 • Urine dipsticks: most useful to rule out UTI; each parameter has false positives and negatives.
      ◦ In strongly suggestive symptoms a positive dipstick supports starting antibiotics; positive nitrite is the
        single variable most likely to rule in UTI (with symptoms).
      ◦ Best combination: positive nitrite, or positive leucocyte esterase plus blood.
      ◦ Borderline sensitivity → cannot replace microscopy in symptomatic patients.
      ◦ Errors: expired or badly stored strips; nitrite pad is air-sensitive (open container → future false
        positives); leucocyte reaction takes up to 5 min (read early → false negative).
 • Urinalysis: automated analysis detects haematuria and bacteria more sensitively than dipstick.
      ◦ Preferred tubes: non-mercuric preservative (chlorhexidine, ethylparaben, sodium propionate) →
        sample stable up to 72 h without refrigeration.
      ◦ Technique: 6–8 mL → centrifuge 1800–2000 rpm for 4 min → microscopy for red blood cells (RBCs),
        WBCs, squamous cells, bacteria, crystals, casts. Squamous cells = quality marker.
      ◦ No single constituent is pathognomonic; interpret together. Pyuria = at least 5–10 leucocytes per
        high-power field (HPF).
      ◦ Culture takes 48–72 h, so urinalysis gives the early answer.
      ◦ Strongest predictors: moderate bacteriuria, moderate pyuria and nitrite. Moderate pyuria (WBC >50)
        with typical symptoms justifies antibiotics.
      ◦ Other causes of pyuria: GU tuberculosis, urolithiasis, urothelial injury (incl. chlamydial urethritis),
        interstitial nephritis.
      ◦ No pyuria → question the diagnosis until culture returns; culture positive without pyuria → consider
        a catheterised sample.
      ◦ False-positive bacteria on microscopy with negative culture — usually vaginal lactobacilli and
        corynebacteria.
      ◦ A negative automated urinalysis reliably rules out UTI (can obviate culture); ruling in is harder —
        abnormal parameters occur in healthy asymptomatic women, so urinalysis alone overdiagnoses.
 • Urine culture: the gold standard for bacteriuria, supporting UTI in a symptomatic patient (technique from
   the 1950s). Collect midstream or catheterised into a vacutainer with freeze-dried preservative (practically
   eliminates false positives) → blood agar and MacConkey plates.

Concept — Urine tests are better at excluding than confirming UTI: a negative urinalysis or dipstick is

LOCALISATION

Ureteral Catheterisation
 • Used in bacterial persistence to locate the source: separates upper vs lower tract, one kidney vs the
   other, and can localise to ectopic ureters or non-refluxing ureteric stumps (saline irrigation) — Stamey's
   technique.
 • Particularly important with fever, flank pain or CVAT, and in renal transplant recipients to decide whether
   native kidneys harbour infection.

Stone Cultures
 • Culture removed stones to identify organisms and sensitivities in their interstices; analyse separately
   from urine — results may be discordant.
 • UTI is the commonest complication after stone procedures; manipulating infected stones can release
   endotoxin → systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) or fatal urosepsis.
 • Stone culture predicts postoperative sepsis and SIRS better than periprocedural urine culture; a
   systematic review supports it for selecting antibiotics to prevent future UTI, though its role in preventing
   postoperative UTI needs further study.
Concept — When bladder urine and the infecting focus can differ, sample the focus: ureteric urine localises
the side, and the stone — not the urine — tells you what will cause postoperative sepsis.

IMAGING
 • Not required in most UTIs; clinical and laboratory findings suffice.
 • Image (to find abnormalities needing changed medical management or percutaneous/surgical
   intervention): most men, compromised hosts, febrile infections, features of obstruction, failure to
   respond, recurrence pattern suggesting persistence (Box 26.5).
 • UTI with possible obstruction must be evaluated urgently (causes in Box 26.5).
 • DM → emphysematous pyelonephritis, papillary necrosis (impacted papilla → acute obstruction).
   Polycystic kidneys on dialysis → prone to perinephric abscess.
 • Image if pyelonephritis persists after several days of appropriate antibiotics (renal/perinephric abscess),
   and with unusual organisms, especially urea-splitters (Proteus) → look for obstructing stones, strictures,
   fungal balls.
 • Second purpose: find a focus of persistence when bacteriuria fails to resolve or recurs rapidly —
   uncommon but often surgically correctable (Box 26.6).

MICROBIOME
 • Urine is not sterile. 16S ribosomal RNA NGS shows many species in the urine of healthy, asymptomatic
   people; standard culture detects only fast-growing, oxygen-tolerant organisms and misses fastidious and
   anaerobic ones.
 • EQUC — larger urine volume, varied atmospheres, longer incubation — grew bacteria from most
   samples that were negative on standard culture, identifying dozens of genera and species (Table 26.1) →
   confirms a female urinary microbiome of mostly non-pathogenic organisms.
 • Implications: bacteria will never be eradicated from urine, nor should that be the goal; many
   microbiome bacteria are protective. Research asks whether dysbiosis underlies urgency incontinence or
   painful bladder syndrome.
 • Commercial NGS (not FDA-approved): no susceptibility testing, costly, risks inappropriate treatment, and
   reports go directly to patients without clinician interpretation → confusion; clinical utility still to be
   established.

PRINCIPLES OF ANTIMICROBIAL THERAPY
 • Agent and duration depend on spectrum against the known or probable pathogen (by source of
   acquisition), uncomplicated vs complicated, adverse effects, cost.
 • Serum levels are irrelevant in uncomplicated UTI (urine levels matter) but critical in bacteraemia and
   febrile parenchymal infection (kidney, prostate).

WHICH DRUG LEVEL MATTERS
 Uncomplicated cystitis                                  Febrile / parenchymal infection

 • Renal insufficiency: adjust dose of renally cleared drugs; the failing kidney may not concentrate the drug
   in urine → eradication difficult. Obstruction also lowers urinary drug levels.
 • Consider the drug's effect on bowel and vaginal flora and the hospital ecology; susceptibility varies by
   exposure and setting.
 • Do not treat a positive culture alone; weigh the likelihood of benefit vs harm. Uncomplicated cystitis
   rarely progresses even untreated — the goal is symptom relief. In placebo arms of trials, a substantial
   minority of women were symptom-free or improved within about 9 days, and progression to
   pyelonephritis was rare.
 • Finucane's framing: ask not whether it is a "real UTI" but whether antibiotics directed at bacteriuria are
   more likely to benefit than harm.
 • Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) alone vs antibiotics (meta-analysis of randomised trials,
   non-pregnant women): antibiotics are more effective (moderate evidence); NSAIDs cure a proportion but
   with higher symptom burden, longer symptoms and more pyelonephritis. Role of analgesics combined
   with antibiotics remains to be defined.

Ecological Impact and Collateral Damage
 • Collateral damage = ecological adverse effects — resistance, selection and MDR colonisation — linked
   especially to broad-spectrum cephalosporins and quinolones.
 • Particularly relevant to uncomplicated cystitis because (1) minimal risk of invasion/sepsis and (2) it is one
   of the commonest antibiotic exposures in healthy people.
 • Resistance rises from microbial traits, antibiotic selection pressure and spread; varies geographically.

Concept — The formulary is organised by a single trade-off: spectrum and tissue penetration buy efficacy in
complicated infection but cost collateral damage — so the narrow, urine-confined, flora-sparing agents
(nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, pivmecillinam) lead in cystitis, and the broad, tissue-penetrating agents
(fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, carbapenems) are held back for infections that invade.

Choice of Antimicrobial Agents
 • Empirical choice: complicated vs uncomplicated, spectrum vs probable pathogen, hypersensitivity,
   renal/hepatic toxicity, ecological impact, cost; use regional antibiograms.
 • C. difficile infection (CDI): increasingly common and resistant; large US burden and mortality.
      ◦ Most likely culprits: cephalosporins, clindamycin, carbapenems, TMP-SMX, fluoroquinolones,
        penicillin combinations. Not associated: macrolides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides.
      ◦ Diagnosis requires both (1) diarrhoea (≥3 unformed stools in 24 h) or radiological ileus/toxic
        megacolon, and (2) stool positive for toxigenic C. difficile or its toxins, or histological/colonoscopic

   • Antimicrobial resistance is increasing.

Uncomplicated Cystitis
 • Acute uncomplicated cystitis is the overwhelming majority of UTIs and is often managed by non-
   urologists.
 • Annually about one in ten women reports a UTI, and most women have at least one in their lifetime;
   incidence is low before puberty and rises sharply in late adolescence and the second to fourth decades;
   about a third of women have had a physician-diagnosed UTI by age 26.
 • E. coli causes 75–90% of acute cystitis in young women; S. saprophyticus (a skin commensal) is the next
   common cause; less often Klebsiella, Proteus, Enterococcus. In men, E. coli and other Enterobacteriaceae.

Clinical Presentation
 • The validated UTI Symptom Assessment questionnaire scores seven symptoms — frequency, urgency,
   painful urination, incomplete emptying, suprapubic pain/pressure, low back pain, haematuria — for
   presence, severity and bother.
 • Cystitis is a superficial mucosal infection → fever and chills are usually absent.
 • Malodorous or turbid urine alone is not a reliable sign of UTI → advise hydration and reassess.
 • More than one irritative symptom raises the likelihood, but dysuria alone is the most reliable single
   indicator and drives the highest severity and bother scores.
 • In baseline voiding dysfunction, establish how symptoms differ from baseline. Distinguish dysuria
   (burning on voiding) from constant vaginal/pelvic burning (painful bladder syndrome, genitourinary
   syndrome of menopause [GSM]).
 • Dysuria + frequency without vaginal discharge or irritation predicts UTI in about 90%; probability rises
   further with haematuria, back pain and CVAT.
 • Symptom meta-analysis: frequency, dysuria, haematuria, nocturia, urgency increase probability; vaginal
   discharge decreases it; haematuria + dipstick nitrite → probability ≥90%.
 • Bottom line: acute-onset dysuria and a change from baseline voiding symptoms best indicate acute
   uncomplicated cystitis.

Bacterial Persistence
 • Failure of an appropriate course for culture-proven acute infection → suspect persistence — rare in rUTI
   but critical to find; requires cystoscopy and imaging.
 • Usually a correctable urological abnormality (Box 26.6) where antibiotics cannot penetrate or eliminate
   the nidus; surgical removal can cure (Stamey).
 • Work-up: cystoscopy, ultrasound, CT; VCUG for VUR or non-refluxing ureteric stump; pelvic MRI =
   standard for urethral diverticulum; retrograde urethrography for suspected stricture in men.
 • Struvite (infection) stones are one of the commonest causes. Urease-producing organisms (P. mirabilis)
   alkalinise urine → precipitation of calcium, magnesium, ammonium and phosphate → branched struvite
   stones. Bacteria shelter within the stone — post-treatment urine may be sterile, misleadingly. Oxalate
   and apatite stones may be secondarily colonised. Risk: chronic catheters, urinary diversion, stasis.
      ◦ AUA (Medical Management of Kidney Stones): surgically remove infection stones in all patients fit
        for intervention. Non-surgical candidates with residual/recurrent struvite → acetohydroxamic acid +
        antibiotic suppression.
 • Non-obstructing stones with rUTI — controversial: half of patients remain infection-free a year after
   complete stone extraction; recurrence more likely with type 2 DM, hypertension, Black race. AUA
   permits observation of asymptomatic non-obstructing stones ≤10 mm. For stones <6 mm with UTI,
   minimise recurrences before considering removal. Whether clearing small stones reduces rUTI is
   unknown.

 • Most women with rUTI have no correctable abnormality. Options in order: behavioural modification →

   Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and other societies' guidelines conflict on prevention →
   confusion.
 • Quality of life (QoL): patients fear the next UTI; anxiety, fragmented care from providers unaware of

 KEY POINTS: RECURRENT URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS
   • Thorough history: prior symptoms, urinalysis/culture results, triggers.
   • Identify and eradicate sources of persistence.
   • Imaging and cystoscopy are not recommended for recurrent uncomplicated UTI.
   • Prevention should focus on non-antibiotic interventions.

Concept — Recurrent UTI in women is a problem of reinfection in a receptive host, not of a hidden focus —
so investigation is reserved for features of persistence or complication, and prevention targets the introital
and bladder environment (flora, pH, adhesion, washout) rather than repeatedly sterilising the urine.

KIDNEY INFECTIONS

Acute Pyelonephritis
 • Defined as inflammation of kidney and renal pelvis, but diagnosed clinically; upper tract infection can be
   proven by ureteric catheterisation or bladder washout, which are impractical and unnecessary in most;
   no non-invasive localisation test is fully reliable.
Clinical Presentation
 • Spectrum: Gram-negative sepsis ↔ cystitis with mild flank pain.
 • Classic: abrupt chills, fever ≥38 °C (100.4 °F), unilateral or bilateral flank/costovertebral angle pain or
   tenderness; lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) often absent.
 • Symptoms correlate poorly with the true site of infection (localisation studies by ureteric catheterisation
   and bladder washout).
 • Deep CVAT on examination. May mimic GI disease (abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea).
 • Asymptomatic progression to chronic pyelonephritis can occur in compromised hosts; acute renal failure
   is rare.

Chapter 26 — Infections of the Urinary Tract                                                                 KIDNEY INFECTIONS
Laboratory Diagnosis
 • Neutrophil leucocytosis; urinalysis — numerous WBCs, often clumped, bacterial rods or chains of cocci.
 • Granular or leucocyte casts suggest pyelonephritis; a cast containing bacteria in its matrix is specific (Fig.
   26.21).
 • Blood: raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine (if renal failure),
   reduced CrCl; blood cultures may be positive.
Bacteriology
 • Urine cultures positive, though a minority have <10⁵ CFU/mL and a negative urine Gram stain.
 • E. coli (a special virulent subgroup) causes most cases. Without VUR, patients of P blood-group
   phenotype are susceptible to recurrent pyelonephritis from P-piliated E. coli binding P antigen receptors.
   Much community pyelonephritis comes from a few resistant clonal groups.
 • Suspect resistant species (Proteus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, Serratia, Enterobacter, Citrobacter) with
   rUTI, hospitalisation, catheters, recent instrumentation.
 • Gram-positives rarely cause pyelonephritis except E. faecalis, S. epidermidis, S. aureus.
 • Blood cultures if systemic toxicity, hospitalisation, or risk factors such as pregnancy.

Perinephric Abscess
 • Beyond the renal capsule but within Gerota's fascia. Sources: rupture of a cortical abscess,
   extravasation of infected urine from obstruction, haematogenous seeding (about a third, usually skin).
   Pyonephrosis with calculus predisposes; about a third are diabetic. Infected perirenal haematoma
   (haematogenous or direct).
 • Rupture through Gerota's fascia → paranephric abscess; paranephric abscess also arises from bowel,
   pancreas or pleura; conversely perinephric/psoas abscess may follow bowel perforation, Crohn disease,
   thoracolumbar osteomyelitis.
 • Organisms: E. coli, Proteus, S. aureus.
Clinical Presentation
 • Insidious; symptoms >5 days in most; resembles pyelonephritis but more than a third are afebrile; flank
   or abdominal mass in about half; CVAT usual. Limp with flexed, externally rotated hip → psoas abscess.
 • Labs: leucocytosis, raised creatinine, pyuria. Urine culture predicts the abscess organism in only about a
   third; polymicrobial blood culture suggests perinephric abscess but identifies all organisms in under half;
   another series found urine the most sensitive → treat culture results with caution.
 • Pyelonephritis responds in 4–5 days; perinephric abscess does not → suspect with UTI plus flank mass
   or fever persisting after 4 days of antibiotics. Often coexists with renal abscess.
 • CT shows the primary abscess, extension to flank or psoas (Fig. 26.33), route of spread and obstruction
   (Fig. 26.34: pararenal abscess involving adrenal) → plans drainage. Ultrasound: anechoic mass displacing
   kidney ↔ echogenic collection blending with perirenal fat; can delineate collections outside Gerota's
   fascia.
 • Mortality fell from 40–50% to about 12% with imaging, yet diagnosis still lags by about 3–4 days and only
   about a third are correctly diagnosed at presentation — delay contributes to nearly all deaths → keep a
   low threshold for imaging.

 • Immediate antibiotics, guided by Gram stain: aminoglycoside + antistaphylococcal penicillin
   (methicillin/oxacillin); penicillin allergy → cephalosporin or vancomycin.
 • Small (<3 cm) abscesses in immunocompetent patients may resolve with antibiotics alone; delay and
   size >3 cm predict failure and death → prompt diagnosis and drainage of collections >3 cm.
 • Larger or non-responding → intervention: classic surgical drainage or nephrectomy (non-functioning/
   severely infected kidney), now largely CT/ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage; the threshold is
   lower than for renal abscess.
 • Then treat the underlying cause — cortical abscess or enteric communication promptly; nephrectomy
   for pyonephrosis at the same time if feasible, otherwise staged. A proportion of large drained abscesses

Xanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis
 • Rare, severe, chronic infection → diffuse renal destruction; mostly unilateral, enlarged, non-functioning
   kidney with obstruction from stones; lipid-laden foamy macrophages; starts in pelvis and calyces, then
   destroys parenchyma and adjacent tissue.
 • Imitates almost every renal inflammatory disease and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) radiologically; on
   frozen section mimics clear cell carcinoma → unnecessary radical nephrectomy.
 • Under 1% of histologically documented chronic pyelonephritis, but up to about a fifth of nephrectomies
   for pyelonephritis.
Pathogenesis
 • Triad: nephrolithiasis, obstruction, infection. Stones in up to about 80%, about half staghorn.
 • Obstruction then E. coli infection reproduces tissue destruction and lipid collection by macrophages
   (xanthoma cells) — sheets around abscesses and calyces with lymphocytes, giant cells, plasma cells.
 • Bacteria of low virulence (spontaneous bacteraemia rare). Other factors: venous occlusion and
   haemorrhage, abnormal lipid metabolism, lymphatic blockage, failed antibiotic therapy, altered
   immunity, ischaemia.
 • No single cause: an inadequate acute inflammatory response in an obstructed, ischaemic or necrotic
   kidney.
Pathology
 • Massively enlarged kidney with normal contour. Diffuse (>80%) or segmental (parenchyma around one
   or more calyces, or one pole of a duplex).
 • Cut surface: stones, peripelvic fibrosis, dilated pus-filled calyces, pelvis not dilated (encased by fibrosis),
   papillary necrosis; advanced — multiple abscesses with viscous pus lined by yellow tissue, thin cortex
   replaced by xanthogranulomatous tissue, thickened capsule, frequent perinephric/paranephric extension
   (Fig. 26.37A).

Malakoplakia
 • "Soft plaque" — unusual inflammatory disease first described in the bladder, also in GU and GI tracts,
   skin, lungs, bones, mesenteric nodes. Described by Michaelis and Gutmann (1902); characterised by von
   Hansemann (1903) as soft yellow-brown plaques with histiocytes containing basophilic lysosomal
   inclusions (Michaelis–Gutmann bodies).
 • Abnormal macrophage function in response to bacterial (mostly E. coli) infection.
Pathogenesis
 • Bacteria or fragments form the nidus for calcium phosphate crystals that laminate Michaelis–Gutmann
   bodies. Leading theory: defective phagocytic/degradative function of histiocytes → chronic
   inflammation, often in immunocompromised hosts.
Pathology
 • Diagnosis by biopsy. Grossly: confluent tumour-like masses and firm plaque-like cortical lesions (Fig.
   26.39). Large histiocytes (von Hansemann cells) and small basophilic intra- or extracytoplasmic
   calculospherules — Michaelis–Gutmann bodies, pathognomonic (Fig. 26.39C: incompletely destroyed
   bacteria with lipoprotein membrane).
 • Electron microscopy: intact coliforms and fragments within phagolysosomes.
 • Michaelis–Gutmann bodies may be absent early and are not required for diagnosis.
 • Macrophages contain abundant α1-antitrypsin, unchanged through early, granulomatous and fibrosing
   phases; lookalike processes lack it (except a few macrophages in TB and XGP) → α1-antitrypsin
   immunostaining aids early diagnosis.

 • Most >50 years; urinary tract female:male 4:1 (not in other organs). Any part of the GU tract, frequently
   bladder.

   transplantation — graft kidney or ureter, GI and skin.
 • Bladder: irritative LUTS, haematuria; cystoscopy — mucosal plaques or nodules → fungating, sessile
   masses causing filling defects; distal ureteric stricture → obstruction or non-function.
 • Renal parenchymal: one or more masses with chronic E. coli infection; may cause renal vein and inferior

 • Multifocal: enlarged kidneys with multiple filling defects; no calcification, lithiasis or hydronephrosis;

BACTERIURIA IN PREGNANCY

Pregnancy
 • Bacteriuria in pregnancy → pyelonephritis → adverse birth outcomes; hence pregnancy is one of the
   few indications to treat ASB (IDSA). American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG):

Anatomical and Physiological Changes During Pregnancy
 • Increase in renal size: length + about 1 cm — vascular and interstitial volume, not true hypertrophy;
   biopsies show no histological change.
 • Smooth muscle atony of collecting system and bladder: reduced ureteric peristalsis; most women have

BACTERIURIA AND URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS IN THE ELDERLY
 • The US population aged ≥65 is projected nearly to double by 2050 → growing public health importance.

Epidemiology
 • Bacteriuria rises with age; the female excess narrows in old age (still commoner in women); most elderly
   bacteriuric patients are asymptomatic.
 • Prevalence rises with age and comorbidity, exceeding 50% in selected groups (figures in Table 26.2);
   functional impairment doubles it.

Screening for Bacteriuria
 • Do not screen community or long-term care elderly. No link between ASB and deteriorating renal
   function; treating ASB does not improve incontinence; the association of bacteriuria with reduced
   survival is not shown to be causal.

Pathogenesis
 • Multifactorial: declining cell-mediated immunity, incomplete emptying, NGB, perineal soiling from
   faecal/urinary incontinence, more catheterisation, oestrogen depletion (women).
 • E. coli remains commonest (50–75%), but more Proteus, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Serratia,
   Pseudomonas and enterococci. Enterococci often resolve untreated (colonisation). Polymicrobial
   bacteriuria commoner.
 • The shift, polymicrobial pattern and resistance are driven by institutionalisation, hospitalisation,
   catheterisation and antibiotic use.

Diagnosis
 • Subtle presentations (abdominal discomfort, delirium); even upper tract infection may lack fever or
   leucocytosis.
 • New localised lower tract symptoms — especially dysuria — remain the key criteria.
 • Bacteriuria + altered mental state ≠ UTI; no data show antibiotics improve confusion in bacteriuric
   elderly.
 • Do not culture for non-specific symptoms (dizziness, confusion), chronic symptoms (incontinence,
   frequency) or asymptomatic patients.
 • Confusion without urinary symptoms → hydrate and reassess; test urine only if altered mental state or
   change in urine persists after fluids, or in dementia unresponsive to other measures.
 • Meta-analysis: delirium is associated with UTI but not with ASB; strongest effect modifiers retention,
   dehydration, catheters; whether treating bacteriuria helps delirium is uncertain.
 • Never culture to document clearance.
 • Thresholds: ≥10⁵ CFU/mL with symptoms; ≥10² CFU/mL in catheterised specimens.
 • Dipstick is better at ruling out than ruling in (moderate sensitivity, lower specificity). Pyuria is common
   and a poor predictor of bacteriuria in the elderly — many women with pyuria have no bacteriuria — and
   pyuria alone is not an indication for treatment; absence of pyuria reliably predicts absence of
   bacteriuria.
 • Catheterised specimen may be the only clean sample in institutionalised or dexterity-limited patients.
 • Urological evaluation (creatinine, CT, ultrasound, urodynamic studies [UDS], cystoscopy) for renal
   dysfunction, calculi, hydronephrosis, retention, NGB; timing by clinical setting.
 • Prompt therapy needed when bacteriuria → UTI with structural abnormality (obstruction/

   damage.
 • Sepsis increasingly common in the elderly: catheters and invasive devices, prostheses, chemotherapy,
   corticosteroids, and longer survival with metabolic, neoplastic and immune disorders.

 • Randomised trials in nursing-home residents: treating ASB gives no benefit, only adverse effects,
   resistant reinfection and cost → do not treat ASB in long-term care.
 • IDSA 2019 recommends against screening for or treating ASB in functionally impaired community-
   dwelling older people and in long-term care residents.
 • McGeer surveillance criteria (Table 26.16) standardise UTI diagnosis in aged-care residents; limited in

 • Mild LUTS → await culture; start empirical antibiotics only if immediate treatment needed.
 • Duration: traditionally 7 days; Cochrane: short (3–6 days) = long (7–14 days) for uncomplicated
   symptomatic UTI; short courses better than single dose for persistence; fever or systemic infection →

MANAGEMENT OF URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS IN PATIENTS WITH SPINAL CORD
INJURY
 • All UTIs in SCI are complicated. No bacteriuric group compares in severity and morbidity. Early
   catheterisation is near-universal (overactivity or flaccidity); many develop ureterectasis, hydronephrosis,
   VUR, calculi. Bacteriological and urodynamic advances have greatly reduced morbidity and mortality.

Epidemiology
 • Before CIC and modern antibiotics, UTI and renal failure caused most SCI deaths; both have fallen since.
 • UTI is among the commonest urological complications — about a third bacteriuric at any time; almost all
   eventually; significant bacteriuria and febrile UTI recur several times per person-year on intermittent or
   condom drainage. UTI is the commonest cause of fever in SCI.
 • Risk factors (National Institute on Disability and Rehabilitation Research Consensus Conference
   [NIDRRCC], 1992): impaired voiding, overdistension, raised intravesical pressure, obstruction, VUR,
   instrumentation, stones; also low fluid intake, poor hygiene, perineal colonisation, pressure sores and
   local trauma, reduced host defence.

Pathogenesis
 • Bladder management method profoundly affects UTI incidence.

 • Most bacteriuric SCI patients are asymptomatic → do not treat ASB; do not screen asymptomatic
   patients on indwelling, condom or intermittent catheters.
 • No standard UTI definition in SCI. Sensory loss → frequency, urgency, dysuria usually absent. Instead:
   flank, back or abdominal discomfort; leakage between catheterisations; increased spasticity; malaise,
   lethargy; cloudy, malodorous urine; fever (commonest cause).

 • Bacteriuria and pyuria; pyuria is not diagnostic (catheter irritation).

 KEY POINTS: MANAGEMENT OF UTIS IN PATIENTS WITH SCI
   • Most bacteriuric SCI patients are asymptomatic; treat only symptomatic patients.
   • Symptoms: fever; flank, back or abdominal discomfort; leakage between catheterisations; spasticity;
     malaise, lethargy; cloudy, malodorous urine.
   • Culture before empirical therapy — diverse, resistant flora.
   • CIC carries the lowest risk of long-term complications.
   • Chronic infection can be carcinogenic.

Concept — In SCI the bladder-management method is the main modifiable determinant of infection: low-
pressure, intermittent emptying (CIC) prevents the stasis, high pressure and reflux that make infection
dangerous, whereas antibiotics can neither prevent colonisation nor safely eradicate it — so they are
reserved for symptoms, which must be recognised through non-classical signs.

 KEY POINTS: ANTIMICROBIAL PROPHYLAXIS FOR COMMON UROLOGIC PROCEDURES
   • AP = antimicrobial before and for a limited time after a procedure to prevent local/systemic infection.
   • Procedure type and host defences determine need.
   • Special considerations: TRUS biopsy, endocarditis risk with bacteriuria, orthopaedic hardware.

Concept — Prophylaxis is justified by the product of wound contamination and host vulnerability: where
neither is raised (diagnostic cystoscopy, SWL with sterile urine, UDS in index patients) the ecological cost
outweighs the benefit; where either is (bowel, infected stones, transrectal biopsy, prostheses,
immunocompromise) a single, well-timed, flora-matched dose is the default, and anything longer is
treatment, not prophylaxis.
