 Chapter 37 — Infection and Inflammation of the
 Paediatric Genitourinary Tract
   • Lifetime childhood incidence: about 1% of boys and 3–5% of girls have at least one urinary tract
     infection (UTI); 30–50% of these recur.
   • Burden (United States): about 1.1 million outpatient visits and 50,000 hospitalisations each year, with
     hospital charges above $520 million.
   • Paradigm shift: UTI has been over-credited for scarring; earlier guidelines (American Academy of
     Pediatrics [AAP] 1999 and others) assumed damage came from UTI alone and overlooked congenital
     dysplasia.

 Concept — The whole chapter is written against the older assumption that every UTI threatens the kidney;
 each later section (imaging, prophylaxis, long-term sequelae) is a retreat from blanket intervention
 towards risk-targeted care.

 EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT OF A CHILD WITH A FEVER
   • Clinically significant fever: rectal temperature ≥38 °C (100.4 °F); in a previously healthy child aged 3–36
     months, ≥38 °C warrants evaluation.
   • Most fevers are viral; 7–13% of children with no clear source have occult bacteraemia or serious
     bacterial infection (bacteraemia, bacterial gastroenteritis, cellulitis, meningitis, osteomyelitis,
     pneumonia, septic arthritis, UTI).
   • Age gradient: serious bacterial infection is commoner below 90 days and especially below 29 days.
   • Goal: do not miss serious infection; start treatment quickly.
   • “Toxic” appearance carries a higher rate of serious infection: cyanosis, decreased activity, hyper- or
     hypoventilation, inability to interact with parents, irritability, lethargy, poor tone, poor perfusion,
     tachycardia, poor eye contact.
   • Predictors of bacteraemia — toxic appearance, age <30 days, rectal temperature ≥39.4 °C — are
     strongly predictive, but none guarantees detection and their absence does not exclude serious
     infection.
   • UTI is the commonest bacterial infection in children: about 7% of febrile infants and 7.8% of febrile
     children older than 24 months.

   KEY POINTS: DIAGNOSIS OF URINARY TRACT INFECTION IN A CHILD
    • Signs and symptoms in young children may be nonspecific.
    • UTI is the commonest bacterial infection in a child presenting with fever.
    • A child who appears “toxic” needs close evaluation: serious infection is likely.


 Definition of a Urinary Tract Infection
   • What counts as a “significant” UTI is controversial; historically bacteria in urine defined infection
     because urine was thought sterile.
   • Urine is not sterile: a urinary microbiome exists from as early as 2 weeks of age, revealed by
     expanded quantitative urine culture (EQUC) and 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing; its role is not
     yet understood.
   • AAP, age 2–24 months: diagnosis must rest on urine from suprapubic aspiration (SPA) or
     catheterisation.
   • Bag specimen pathway: bag urine for urinalysis only → if suggestive (positive leukocyte esterase or
     nitrite, or leucocytes or bacteria on microscopy) → culture must come from SPA or catheter.
   • Reason: a positive culture from a bagged specimen has a false-positive rate of up to 75%.

   • Host, bacterial and immune factors interact and are in constant flux in children — immunity and gut
     colonisation of a newborn differ completely from those of a 6-month-old.

   • Virulence (Latin virulentus, “full of poison”): the ability of an organism to cause disease in a host.
   • Sequence of virulence: attach to urogenital mucosa → trigger signalling and immunological cascades →
     invade the bladder.
   • Uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) is the most studied and by far the commonest pathogen; most
     E. coli UTIs are caused by UPEC rather than commensal strains — evidence that virulence factors
     matter.
   • Four classes of virulence factor: adherence to uroepithelium; nourishment in adverse environments;
     protection from host immunity; toxins enabling invasion.

   • Step 1 signalling is Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-dependent → cytokine secretion and receptor expression.
   • P and type 1 fimbriae use different receptors and different TLR4 pathways: P fimbriae bind Galα1–4Galβ
     on glycosphingolipids (ceramide-anchored); type 1 fimbriae bind α-mannose residues on glycoproteins and
     also signal through TLR4.
   • Intracellular cycle: adherence → signal transduction → uptake into umbrella cells → intracellular
     bacterial communities (IBCs): growth slows until the IBC fills the cytoplasm → bacteria flux out and
     spread → re-enter epithelium → quiescent intracellular reservoir (QIR) in underlying transitional cells.
   • QIR: a handful of non-replicating bacteria, viable for months; reactivated during urothelial turnover,
     they seed the lumen and reinitiate UTI.
   • TLR4-dependent expulsion normally defends the urothelium: LPS on TLR4 → adenylyl cyclase → cyclic
     adenosine monophosphate → exocytic expulsion of UPEC from umbrella cells.
   • Intracellular invasion lets UPEC evade host defences and some antibiotics.

 Concept — The intracellular bacterial community is a biofilm built inside a host cell; that shared
 architecture explains why both catheter infections and recurrent cystitis resist clearance.
 Aerobactin
  • Iron is essential to E. coli (oxygen storage and transport, DNA synthesis, electron transport, peroxide
    metabolism).
   • Host withholds iron during infection: reduced intestinal absorption, more iron-binding proteins, shift
     of plasma iron into intracellular stores.
   • Aerobactin, a siderophore, extracts iron from host binding proteins and delivers it to bacterial iron
     centres.
 Toxins
  • UPEC toxins: α-haemolysin (hlyA), cytotoxic necrotising factor 1 (CNF1), autotransporter toxins —
    they alter host-cell function or shape, arrest the cell cycle or lyse cells.
   • α-Haemolysin is produced by about half of UPEC strains; CNF1 by a minority.
 Capsular Polysaccharide
  • More than 80 capsular types in E. coli: linear polymers coating the cell, hindering antigen detection.
   • UPEC surface = LPS covered by capsular K antigens; both help escape host immunity.
   • Encapsulated K strains are less phagocytosed and anticomplementary → impaired clearance;
     impairment proportional to the amount of polysaccharide.
 Biofilm Formation and Flagella
  • Biofilm scaffold: extracellular DNA, exopolysaccharides, pili, flagella and other adhesins → multicellular
    community protected from immunity and antimicrobials, with a physical barrier to antibiotic entry.
   • Biofilm-like IBCs inside urothelial cells protect UPEC from neutrophils, antibiotics and stress.
   • Flagella also act at several stages of biofilm development.

   KEY POINTS: BACTERIAL FACTORS
    • UTI bacteria are commensal or virulent; both cause UTI, but virulent organisms cause the majority.
    • Virulent bacteria carry adaptations that subvert or hijack host defences and let them live where
      they normally would not.
    • Virulence factors improve adherence, allow nourishment, protect against immunity and supply
      toxins for invasion.

 Concept — Each virulence factor solves one obstacle to infection — attach, feed, hide, damage, persist,
 ascend — and the factors a strain carries predict whether it stays in the bladder or reaches the kidney.

 Host Risk Factors Leading to Paediatric Urinary Tract Infections
   • Host determinants: age, genetics, sex, race, and anatomical, functional and behavioural features.
 Sex and Age
  • Boys exceed girls only below 1 year of age; at every other age, including old age, females
    predominate.
   • Febrile children: UTI incidence 3–5%; girls carry the higher risk after 2 months; at 2 months to 2 years,
     a febrile girl has about 2.3 times the risk of a febrile boy.

  • UTI is commoner in White girls than in girls of other races.

 Circumcision
  • Circumcision reduces UTI in the first 6 months of life by almost tenfold, matching a period of heavy
    uropathogen colonisation of the prepuce that declines and resolves by 5 years.

     1983; UTI proven by catheter or SPA): circumcised 0.11%, uncircumcised 1.12%, girls 0.57%.

 Faecal and Perineal Bacterial Colonisation
  • Faecal–perineal–urethral route: most UTI bacteria colonise gut, perineum and periurethral area, then
    ascend retrogradely.
   • Recurrent UTI in women: most reinfections are the same UPEC strain persisting in faecal flora for ≥12
     months, cycling through faecal and vaginal reservoirs.
   • Loss of protective vaginal lactobacilli → enteric colonisation of vagina and periurethra → more E. coli;
     antibiotics, especially trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), eradicate lactobacilli.
   • Children: mechanism unproven, but antibiotic treatment of schoolgirls for non-urological infections
     (usually otitis media) produced colonisation and bacteriuria with uropathogenic organisms.
   • Urinary microbiome from 2 weeks of age (16S sequencing, EQUC): in girls it changes through
     childhood until the postpubertal adult pattern; in boys it changes less.
   • Presumed role: symbiotic — metabolic and trophic functions and a protective barrier against
     uropathogens; in adult women the microbiome differs in urge urinary incontinence, implying a role in
     common urinary symptoms.
 Anatomic Abnormalities
  • Malformation-associated UTIs usually appear before 5 years; detection matters because many are
    surgically correctable and persistence causes renal damage and recurrence.
   • Abnormalities: hydronephrosis, pelviureteric or vesicoureteric junction obstruction, VUR, infection
     stones, infected non-functioning renal segments, infected necrotic papillae.
   • AAP: renal and bladder ultrasound (RBUS) in every child aged 2–24 months after the first febrile UTI.
 Vesicoureteral Reflux
  • VUR: 1–2% of newborns, 25–40% of children after a first UTI.
   • Most pyelonephritis occurs without VUR (only a minority of scan-proven pyelonephritis has VUR); but
     kidneys with grade III–IV reflux were twice as likely to show pyelonephritic change.
   • Modifiers: reflux grade, distal ureteric diameter, early onset of reflux during filling, age, sex, and
     bladder and bowel dysfunction. Detail in Chapter 41.
 Sexual Activity
  • Sexually active girls have more UTIs; UTI has been proposed as a marker of teenage sexual activity,
    but the relationship remains poorly defined.
 Bladder and Bowel Dysfunction
  • Bladder and bowel dysfunction (BBD) = dysfunctional elimination syndrome: infrequent voiding,
    incomplete emptying, constipation and/or bladder overactivity; contributes to UTI and VUR.

 Neurogenic Bladder
  • UTI is the commonest infection in neurogenic bladder (spina bifida, spinal cord injury) — about 2.5
    UTIs per year; in spina bifida, UTI is the commonest reason for emergency attendance; brings higher
    morbidity, mortality and end-stage renal disease.
   • Cause is not only mechanical (poor emptying, stasis, stones): a dysfunctional bladder immune
     response also contributes.
   • Clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC): chronic bacteriuria and/or pyuria in 40–80%, mostly
     asymptomatic and harmless.
   • Without VUR, asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) on CIC is not a significant risk for renal damage and
     needs no antibiotics.
   • Stopping prophylaxis in spina bifida reduces bacterial resistance; most children can undergo
     urodynamics without prophylactic antibiotics.
   • Daily prophylaxis with CIC may delay bacteriuria briefly but gives no long-term benefit and breeds
     resistance.
   • Catheter type makes no difference: sterile versus non-sterile, single-use versus reusable, lubricated
     versus non-lubricated.
   • In a catheterisation cohort, risk factors are high catheterisation volume in females and low
     catheterisation frequency in males; prophylaxis gave less bacteriuria but more clinical UTIs → timely,
     scheduled catheterisation prevents UTI.

 Iatrogenic Factors
   • Catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI) is the commonest nosocomial infection — more than 1 million cases
     yearly in United States hospitals and nursing homes.
   • Risk rises with catheter duration: bacteriuria about 3–10% per day.
   • Children: nosocomial UTIs make up 6–18% of paediatric nosocomial infections; each adds about one
     hospital day and more than $1000 per case.
   • Prevention works: CAUTI incidence and mortality in catheterised adults fell over 2001–2010 as
     prevention became a target.
   • Paediatric risk factors: duration of catheterisation, contact precautions, prior catheterisation.
   • Prevention: judicious use and removal as soon as no longer necessary; Healthcare Infection Control
     Practices Advisory Committee (HICPAC): catheters for necessity, not convenience; consider external
     (condom) catheters and intermittent catheterisation.
 Immune Status
  • UTI in young healthy women is benign; in young children and the elderly it is more complicated —
    immunity is diminished at both ends of life.
   • First few months of life = greatest risk: serum immunoglobulin G lowest at 1–3 months; serum
     immunoglobulin A low and absent or nearly absent on the urothelium in early months.
   • Obesity: inflammation, altered adipokine signalling, metabolic and epigenetic change → immune
     dysregulation; associated with UTI in febrile children under 2 years and with acute pyelonephritis.

   KEY POINTS: HOST RISK FACTORS
     • A multitude of host factors increase UTI susceptibility.
     • UTI is commoner in boys than girls only below 1 year.
     • Circumcision reduces UTI in the first 6 months almost tenfold.
     • Dysfunctional elimination syndrome is important; treating bladder and bowel reduces recurrence
       and improves VUR resolution.
     • Without VUR, ASB on CIC is not a significant risk for renal damage and needs no antibiotics.
     • CAUTI is the commonest nosocomial infection; most are preventable by judicious catheter use.

 Concept — Every host risk factor either raises inoculum and exposure time (prepuce, faecal colonisation,
 stasis, constipation, catheter) or lowers defence (immature immunity, obesity, receptor-bearing blood
 groups); prevention means reversing whichever applies.

 CLASSIFICATION OF PAEDIATRIC URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS
   • Three axes: complicated versus uncomplicated; upper (pyelonephritis) versus lower (cystitis); first
     versus recurrent.
   • Complicated UTI: anatomical or functional abnormality, or a foreign body (ureteric stent, indwelling
     catheter); neonatal and infant UTIs are presumed complicated because anomalies are common and
     morbidity high.

 Biofilms
   • Biofilm: structured microbial community in a self-made polymeric matrix, adherent to a living or inert
     surface; drives bacterial persistence.
   • Why antibiotics fail: (1) incomplete penetration; (2) slow growth resists agents needing active growth;
     (3) low expression of antimicrobial-binding proteins; (4) activated genes alter envelope, targets and
     susceptibility; (5) survival at 1000–1500 times the usual killing concentration.
   • Renal biofilms: bacteria adhere as thin biofilms to urothelium and papillae before invading
     parenchyma, and are less susceptible to antibiotics there.
   • Foreign bodies: catheters, ureteric stents, calculi.
  Extraluminal catheter colonisation                                   Intraluminal catheter colonisation

   • Stents colonise far more often than urine shows bacteriuria — a sterile urine does not mean a sterile
     stent.

 Asymptomatic Bacteriuria
   • Kass definition: two consecutive cultures ≥105 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL of the same
     uropathogen in a patient free of infective symptoms.
   • E. coli is the commonest organism, as in symptomatic UTI; likely less virulent strains → colonisation
     rather than infection.
   • “Asymptomatic” can mislead: some women later became symptomatic and needed treatment, and
     most schoolgirls with ASB admitted lower urinary tract symptoms and incontinence on careful
     questioning.
   • Screening is not cost-effective: given test performance and prevalence, screening yields many false
     positives, and treatment prevents no long-term harm.

 Bacterial Nephritis
   • Acute bacterial nephritis: infection spreads through the kidney as an increasingly suppurative process
     with heavier leucocytic infiltrate and focal necrosis.
   • Generalised form = acute bacterial nephritis; localised form = acute focal bacterial nephritis (AFBN),
     focal pyelonephritis, lobar nephronia; clinical features of septicaemia often present.
   • Computed tomography (CT): global renal enlargement, perirenal fat inflammation, thickened Gerota
     fascia; on contrast, ill-defined, non-homogeneous, wedge-shaped decreased enhancement (Figure
     37.6: CT of acute focal pyelonephritis, lobar nephronia).

 Pyonephrosis
   • Purulent exudate from APN accumulating in a dilated collecting system, typically a hydronephrotic
     kidney with obstructed outflow; CT: enlarged kidney, grossly dilated system with higher-than-usual
     fluid attenuation.

 Acute Renal Abscess
   • Presents like pyelonephritis, but urine culture is negative in up to 20% → diagnosis is radiological.

 DIAGNOSIS OF PAEDIATRIC URINARY TRACT INFECTION
   • No consensus definition: international guidelines — AAP, National Institute for Health and Care
     Excellence (NICE), Italian Society of Pediatric Nephrology (ISPN), Royal Children’s Hospital (RCH)
     Melbourne — differ significantly.
   • UTI = invasion of the urinary tract causing pathological change, by the organism or by the host
     response.
   • Proof requires two things: the organism in urine (culture) and evidence it is pathogenic (symptoms, or
     an immune response in urine or blood).

 Symptoms
   • Classic adult symptoms (dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic or flank pain) become harder to
     identify with decreasing age.
   • Infants: fever, irritability, poor feeding, jaundice, failure to thrive, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal
     distension, foul-smelling urine.
   • After the neonatal period fever is the primary symptom: UTI prevalence about 5% in febrile infants
     without another source; fever ≥38 °C for more than 2 days without source has a positive likelihood
     ratio of 3.6 for occult UTI, rising with duration and height of fever.
   • Fever does not localise: cystitis can be febrile.
   • Older children: dysuria, incontinence, changed voiding, enuresis, flank or abdominal pain — still
     nonspecific (UTI in only a minority of such children); mimics include BBD and vulvovaginitis.
   • High prevalence means UTI must stay on the differential; clinical probability tools combine symptoms,
     signs and laboratory results to decide when to test.
   • Risk factors to elicit: previous UTI (children under 6 years recur at about 12% per year), genitourinary
     anomaly, abnormal prenatal or postnatal ultrasound, family history, prior genitourinary or
     gastrointestinal surgery.
   • Adolescent urethritis: consider sexually transmitted infection — Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia
     trachomatis, Ureaplasma urealyticum — as well as routine uropathogens.
   • DUTY study (Diagnosis of Urinary Tract infection in Young children; United Kingdom primary care,
     children under 5): predictors for collecting urine — pain or crying on voiding, smelly urine, previous
     UTI, absence of severe cough, impression of severe illness; adding dipstick leucocytes, nitrite and/or
     blood predicted need for antibiotics; the derived points score outperformed clinical impression.

 Physical Examination
   • Specific findings are rare in young children (fever, lethargy).
   • Another source does not exclude UTI: an alternative focus (otitis, upper respiratory infection,
     gastroenteritis) only about halves the UTI risk; clinicians wrongly attributed most febrile UTIs to
     another source.

   • Classic history: recurrent “otitis” before the first diagnosed UTI, then DMSA scars — earlier
     pyelonephritis misdiagnosed.
   • Abdomen: palpable bladder (distension) or flank mass (hydronephrosis); suprapubic, abdominal or
     flank tenderness; costovertebral angle tenderness suggests pyelonephritis.
   • Genitalia: trauma, irritation, meatal stenosis, discharge, phimosis, foreign body, anomalies; boys —
     testicular tenderness (epididymo-orchitis); girls — introital discharge, irritation, ectopic ureter,
     prolapsing ureterocele.
   • Back and neurology: prominent fat pad, asymmetric gluteal cleft, sacral dimple (spina bifida occulta)
     plus neurological examination → neurogenic bladder dysfunction.

   KEY POINTS: SYMPTOMS AND LABORATORY ANALYSIS
     • Symptoms in infants and young children are typically nonspecific.
     • Another source of fever does not exclude a concurrent UTI.
     • Bag urine is reliable only when normal because contamination is frequent.
     • Diagnostic criteria for UTI vary between guidelines.
     • LE: high sensitivity, lower specificity; nitrite: high specificity, lower sensitivity.

 Concept — Every step of diagnosis is a sensitivity–specificity trade: collection method trades invasiveness
 for contamination, LE and nitrite trade in opposite directions, and culture thresholds fall as the specimen
 becomes cleaner.

 Radiographic Imaging
 Controversies With Imaging Strategies
  • No evidence that routine imaging prevents scarring, hypertension or renal failure.
   • Traditional work-up = ultrasound plus VCUG; VUR is found in more than 30% of febrile UTIs, but
     prophylaxis is unproven in low-grade VUR → routine VCUG after every first UTI questioned.
   • NICE: ultrasound routinely under 6 months; above 6 months only for recurrent or atypical UTI (atypical
     defined under Table 37.1).
   • AAP 2011 guideline is now defunct.
   • AAP Section on Urology: VCUG remains an accepted option — children with higher-grade VUR benefit
     from early detection and treatment; the Randomized Intervention for Vesicoureteral Reflux (RIVUR)
     trial showed prophylaxis halved febrile or symptomatic UTI over 2 years.


                                                              page 14 of 25
Chapter 37 — Infection and Inflammation of the Paediatric Genitourinary Tract     DIAGNOSIS OF PAEDIATRIC URINARY TRACT INFECTION
   • Yield: NICE and AAP protocols would miss about half or more of grade III–V VUR and most or all renal
     scars; NICE performs worse in boys (negative predictive value).
   • Counter-argument: most children without high-grade VUR are spared the trauma, radiation and cost
     of VCUG and DMSA; high-grade VUR declares itself at the recurrent UTI, and prompt treatment of that
     recurrence keeps renal risk low.
   • Top-down approach (TDA): DMSA after the first febrile UTI, VCUG only if DMSA abnormal → misses
     only 15–30% of dilating VUR, but lower specificity.
   • Late DMSA: NICE — 4–6 months after acute infection for children under 3 with atypical or recurrent
     UTI and over 3 with recurrent UTI; AAP — no routine DMSA.
   • Bottom line: no strategy tests only the children who will benefit; if a result will not alter management,
     the test should not be ordered.

     • Imaging guidelines conflict and all fall short of testing only the children who will benefit.

 MANAGEMENT OF PAEDIATRIC URINARY TRACT INFECTION
 Antibiotic Treatment
   • Goals: eradicate infection, prevent renal scarring, relieve symptoms.


                                                              page 17 of 25
Chapter 37 — Infection and Inflammation of the Paediatric Genitourinary Tract   MANAGEMENT OF PAEDIATRIC URINARY TRACT INFECTION
   • Urine is usually sterile within 24 hours of effective therapy.
   • Each new febrile UTI raises scar incidence (about 2.8% per episode).
   • Early treatment limits renal involvement and scarring: starting antibiotics at day 3 rather than day 2
     sharply increased acute scintigraphic lesions, and scar formation rose steeply as the start slipped
     towards day 6.
   • Anti-inflammatory steroids (dexamethasone, methylprednisolone) to limit scarring: results vary —
     investigational.
   • Empirical start is routine (nonspecific symptoms; culture takes 2–3 days) — ideally based on UA and
     adjusted to culture; in practice UA and culture are often omitted → overtreatment of non-UTIs or
     undertreatment of resistant UTIs; indiscriminate broad-spectrum use → side effects and resistance.
 Inpatient Versus Outpatient Management
   • Decision factors: likely uropathogen, clinical status, family reliability; fewer than 1% of outpatients
     evaluated for UTI need admission.
   • Outpatient: infants older than 2 months and non-toxic children with suspected pyelonephritis, if oral
     antibiotics are tolerated and compliance assured.
   • Oral equals intravenous for time to improvement and scar prevention in randomised trials — urinary
     antibiotic levels far exceed serum.
   • Newborns and young infants → admit and give parenteral antibiotics: more urosepsis, positive blood
     cultures in about 20%, hyponatraemia and hyperkalaemia.
   • Admission indications: age under 1 month (some say under 2 or even 6 months), toxic appearance or
     dehydration, oral intolerance, doubtful compliance.
   • Neonatal pathway: admit → full septic evaluation → parenteral antibiotics → culture confirms and
     symptoms improve → switch to oral; defervescence takes at least 24 hours; ≤2 days intravenous then
     oral is safe in young infants.
   • Most children (90%) are afebrile within 48 hours; not improving at 48 hours → RBUS and, if culture
     not back, broaden cover.

   • Aim: prevent recurrence. Repeat culture is unnecessary after treatment chosen on susceptibilities.

 Prophylactic Antibiotics
   • Efficacy questioned by small randomised series including low-grade VUR.
   • Neither AAP nor NICE recommends routine prophylaxis after a first UTI.
   • Benefit is demonstrable in high-risk groups, e.g. girls with dilating VUR (grade ≥III) (Swedish Reflux
     Trial and other randomised trials).
   • Low-risk features (benefit hard to show): circumcised boy, no BBD, no recent UTI, normal ultrasound
     or DMSA, no anatomical abnormality, no dilating VUR.
   • Resistance: prior exposure makes later UTIs more likely to be resistant to the same agent (faecal flora
     become resistant) — roughly threefold; so the prophylactic agent should differ from the treatment
     agent.
   • Ideal prophylactic: covers most uropathogens, easy and well tolerated, high urinary and low serum
     levels, minimal effect on flora and resistance.
   • Dose: about one-quarter of the therapeutic dose, given at bedtime in toilet-trained children to
     prolong bladder exposure.
   • Unanswered: which patients benefit, ideal dose and schedule, alternating agents; non-compliance is
     common.

 Bladder and Bowel Dysfunction
   • Assess for BBD in every child with UTI; bladder dysfunction predisposes to recurrent UTI and renal
     injury.
   • Incomplete emptying (dysfunctional voiding, underactive bladder) raises colonisation and UTI risk.
   • BBD and VUR: voiding against a closed sphincter raises bladder pressure and may cause and
     perpetuate VUR; treating overactive bladder improves spontaneous VUR resolution — suggesting a
     causal role.
   • Bowel and bladder are linked: constipation accompanies 30–88% of bladder dysfunction → BBD /
     dysfunctional elimination syndrome (DES).

   • VUR plus BBD = highest risk of recurrent pyelonephritis: recurrence about 45% versus 15% without
     BBD.
   • Treating constipation significantly reduces recurrent UTI and improves bladder function. Detail in
     Chapter 47.
   • Treatment ladder: timed voiding and constipation treatment → if failing, directed therapy:
     pharmacotherapy, biofeedback, electrical stimulation, surgery, CIC, or combinations.
   • Neurogenic bladder on CIC: increasing catheterisation frequency reduces recurrent UTI.

 Management of Vesicoureteral Reflux
   • VUR is a risk factor for recurrent pyelonephritis and scarring; risk rises with grade and becomes
     significant with dilating VUR (grade ≥III).
   • The dogma that every child with febrile UTI and VUR benefits from diagnosis and treatment
     (continuous prophylaxis or surgery) has been challenged; management is increasingly complex —
     detail in Chapter 41.
 Concept — The most effective non-antibiotic prevention is correcting the host factor — emptying,
 constipation, the foreskin — because those, rather than the organism, determine who recurs.

 SEQUELAE OF PAEDIATRIC URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS
 Renal Scarring
   • Pyelonephritic scars favour the renal poles, where compound papillae permit intrarenal reflux.
   • Risk rises with VUR grade.
   • Imaging: parenchymal thinning over the calyces; calyces blunted and deformed.
   • Scar versus dysplasia is hard or impossible to distinguish; a small kidney with diffusely decreased
     uptake and low differential function is usually considered dysplasia.
   • Prompt treatment and preventing further pyelonephritis reduce permanent damage.
   • Delay matters: odds of new scarring rise with every hour of delay and double when antibiotics start
     after 72 hours rather than within 48 hours.
   • Severity gradient: scar risk rises from APN → AFBN → renal abscess.
   • Steroids: no consistent benefit → not recommended routinely; vitamin A may reduce scars in girls
     with APN — investigational.

 Xanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis
   • Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis (XGP): rare chronic inflammatory renal disease in children,
     usually with obstruction and Proteus or E. coli.
   • Presentation: vague — fever, abdominal or flank pain or mass, weight loss, failure to thrive; laboratory
     — leucocytosis, anaemia, pyuria.
   • Imaging: mass lesion, focal or diffuse, with perinephric extension → mimics malignancy; CT — poorly
     enhancing low-attenuation areas and dilated calyces; calculi in 38–70%.
   • Treatment: diffuse → nephrectomy; focal → partial nephrectomy or medical therapy.
   • Surgery is difficult: the process encases psoas and even the great vessels, destroying planes.

 Long-Term Sequelae
   • Hypertension, pre-eclampsia and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are hard to predict after childhood UTI.
   • Management was built on preventing recurrence and finding VUR; recent work questions the UTI/
     VUR → scar → hypertension → end-stage disease chain and implicates congenital dysplasia.

   • Registry and literature review: recurrent childhood UTI accounts for less than 1% of CKD; a child with
     normal kidneys is not at significant CKD risk from recurrent UTI.
   • Weak causal link:; risk of end-stage kidney disease after a childhood UTI about 1 in 10,000.
   • Most children with febrile UTI have no long-term sequelae (systematic review), especially with normal
     kidneys at birth; decline in function is rare — consistent with the International Reflux Study in
     Children and long adult follow-up.
   • Bilateral scars lower glomerular filtration rate (GFR) over decades; unilateral scars do not; mean
     ambulatory blood pressure was not different in that cohort.
   • Hypertension is more prevalent in scarred patients on long-term follow-up and rises with scar
     severity; hypertension in first pregnancies is commoner with severe scarring.
   • Follow-up: children with significant bilateral scars or reduced function need long-term monitoring of
     blood pressure, renal function and proteinuria; proteinuria may hasten CKD; renin–angiotensin
     blockade may slow progression.
   • Large historical cohort: adolescents with a history of congenital kidney and urinary tract anomalies,
     pyelonephritis or glomerular disease but normal adolescent function and no proteinuria still carried
     about a fourfold hazard of later end-stage renal disease.

   KEY POINTS: MANAGEMENT AND SEQUELAE
     • Delay in treating febrile UTI increases parenchymal involvement and scarring.
     • Hospitalise newborns and young infants — urosepsis is commoner.
     • Febrile UTI 7–14 days; afebrile cystitis 2–4 days.
     • Empirical choice from local antibiograms.
     • Nitrofurantoin must not be used for febrile UTI or pyelonephritis.
     • 10–30% have at least one recurrence.
     • Prophylaxis reduces recurrence in high-risk groups such as girls with dilating reflux.
     • BBD raises recurrence risk and should be evaluated and treated in every child with UTI.
     • Long-term risk is low, but bilateral scars or reduced function warrant long-term follow-up of blood
       pressure, renal function and proteinuria.

 Concept — Long-term harm comes from the kidney the child was born with and from delayed treatment,
 not from UTI as such; so the priorities are prompt treatment and follow-up of the child with bilateral scars
 or dysplasia.

 UNCOMMON PAEDIATRIC URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS
 Viral Cystitis
   • Acute haemorrhagic cystitis is occasionally due to adenovirus type 11 (also type 21); E. coli is found in
     some, but most have no identifiable cause.
   • Common after bone marrow transplantation and in other immunosuppressed children; untreated
     severe viral haemorrhagic cystitis carries 50–80% mortality in these children.
   • BK virus (DNA polyomavirus): frequent in urine of immunosuppressed and bone marrow transplant
     patients; 90% of adults seropositive after asymptomatic childhood infection; latent in the kidney,
     reactivates with immune impairment → viraemia.
   • Treatment: intravenous and intravesical cidofovir.

 Funguria
   • Increasing; linked to recent antibiotics and indwelling catheters; a tenfold rise over a decade in one
     neonatal intensive care unit.
   • Predisposing factors: antibiotics, prematurity, intravenous and umbilical artery catheters, parenteral
     nutrition, immunocompromise.
   • Urinary tract is both portal and site of dissemination; in disseminated candidiasis the kidney is the
     organ most often involved.
   • Candida is the commonest cause: C. albicans first, then C. glabrata (formerly Torulopsis glabrata) —
     often fluconazole-resistant.
   • Fungal bezoars in the renal pelvis can obstruct → ultrasound if funguria persists.
   • Fungus ball management: urinary alkalinisation + oral antifungal (may dissolve) → if obstructed,
     percutaneous or surgical removal; percutaneous drainage also allows local antifungal (amphotericin B
     local or systemic, and/or oral fluconazole) → if persistent, endoscopic or open removal.
   • Catheter-related asymptomatic funguria: progression to candidaemia is rare; timing of treatment
     debated; treat when repeated cultures exceed 10,000–15,000 CFU/mL.
   • Adjuncts: stop antibiotics, change or remove catheters, alkalinise urine — helpful but not always
     curative; bladder irrigation with amphotericin B and oral fluconazole both clear funguria.
   • Fluconazole has been used successfully in children, but the chapter lists it as contraindicated under 6
     months.
