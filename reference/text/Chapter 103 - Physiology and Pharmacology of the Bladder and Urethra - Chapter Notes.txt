Chapter 103 — Physiology and Pharmacology of the
Bladder and Urethra
INTRODUCTION
 • Two defining functions of the lower urinary tract (LUT: bladder + urethra): urinary storage (continence)
   and emptying (micturition); a third, immunosurveillance against ascending pathogens, depends on the
   urothelium, the immune system and the urinary microbiome.
 • Both functions are integrated across the central nervous system (CNS), peripheral nerves, detrusor
   smooth muscle (DSM), stroma, suburothelial and intradetrusor interstitial cells, urothelium, urethral
   smooth muscle, pelvic floor and the external urethral sphincter (EUS).
 • Physiology underpins management of lower urinary tract dysfunction (LUTD): urinary incontinence (UI),
   lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), overactive bladder (OAB), bladder pain syndrome/interstitial cystitis
   (BPS/IC), neurogenic bladder and underactive bladder (UAB).
 • Most data are animal-derived; limits to translation: rodent circadian rhythm is opposite to that of
   humans; territorial marking by voiding; animals cannot report urgency or bother, so surrogates
   (frequency, urodynamics) are used; anaesthesia alters reflexes. Translational (bench ↔ bedside) work
   remains essential.
 • Classic model: brain and spinal circuits act as switches alternating storage and elimination. But LUTD may
   arise outside nerve and muscle: endocrine (metabolic syndrome, obesity), inflammation, fibrosis, pelvic
   ischaemia.
 • Bladder and urethra must act oppositely, so single-organ therapy may fail (e.g. boosting contractility in
   detrusor underactivity fails unless the urethra also relaxes). Sensory modulation serves both: reduce
   afferent signalling for overactivity, augment it for underactivity.
 • Management is symptom-based; biomarkers, targeted therapy and prevention are the goal.
Concept — The bladder and urethra are a coupled, reciprocal pair: storage and emptying each need one to
do the opposite of the other, which is why afferent (sensory) control, acting on both at once, is the most
promising therapeutic lever.

LOWER URINARY TRACT ANATOMY
 • Bladder body lies above the ureteric orifices; base = trigone + bladder neck. The two regions differ from
   each other but each is homogeneous in neuromorphology and neuropharmacology.
 • Base is laminar: superficial longitudinal layer beneath the trigone; deeper layer continuous with detrusor,
   with small, predominantly circular bundles.
 • Male bladder neck: complete, competent smooth muscle ring, with dense sympathetic noradrenergic
   supply that closes it during ejaculation → antegrade ejaculation. Female: no such collar and sparser
   adrenergic innervation.
 • Bladder outlet = urethra + pelvic floor. Urethra contains smooth muscle and striated muscle
   (rhabdosphincter, EUS); periurethral striated muscle belongs to the pelvic floor complex.
 • Fig. 103.1: outlet anatomy per Gosling and Dixon vs Elbadawi — body/base detrusor, superficial and
   deep trigone, bladder neck, urethral smooth muscle (lissosphincter), intramural rhabdosphincter vs
   periurethral striated muscle.

BLADDER COMPARTMENTS

Urothelium
 • Multilayered: basal, intermediate, apical; about 7 layers; the apical umbrella cells are the largest,
   sometimes multinucleated, hexagonal with microvilli on scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Figs
   103.2B, 103.3).
 • Uroplakins (unique to umbrella cells) assemble into plaques — outer leaflet thicker than inner, hence
   asymmetrical unit membrane (AUM); the symmetrical areas between plaques are hinge regions.
 • AUM contains hexagonal, chainmail-like uroplakin particles (Fig. 103.4) → barrier function. Escherichia
   coli type 1 fimbriae bind uroplakins, initiating the host–pathogen cascade.

Lamina Propria and Vasculature
 • Lamina propria = proposed "functional centre" coordinating urothelium and DSM: diffuse plexus of
   unmyelinated nerves (contacting urothelium, vessels, DSM), TRPV1 and P2X3 receptors on fibres,
   interstitial cells (myofibroblasts) and microvasculature.
 • Corrosion casts (resin perfusion → digest all other tissue → SEM of vascular tree) show a mucosal plexus
   in the lamina propria and a subepithelial capillary plexus directly under the basal urothelium — the
   vessels seen at cystoscopy (Fig. 103.5; adventitial plexus linked by perpendicular vessels traversing the
   muscularis). Subepithelial flow may be partly urothelium-regulated.
 • Vessels must lengthen with filling without raising resistance. Distension reduces blood flow; intramural
   tension is the principal determinant. Flow adapts during normal filling until pressure rises; in low
   compliance, pressure rises more and flow falls more.
 • Ischaemia rapidly reduces detrusor contractility. Ischaemia–reperfusion may damage intramural
   neurons → patchy denervation and altered DSM seen in detrusor overactivity (DO). Animal pelvic
   ischaemia (iliac intimal balloon injury + high-cholesterol diet) → DO and structural change.

Stroma
 • Collagen and elastin in a proteoglycan matrix; fibroblasts are the main cells. Passive mechanics =
   viscoelasticity of stroma + relaxed detrusor.
 • No longer seen as inert: stroma–muscle interaction likely drives hypertrophy (in arteries, elastin
   disruption triggers smooth muscle proliferation; not yet shown in bladder).

Bladder Wall Collagen
 • Mostly outside the muscle bundles; types I, III and IV predominate.
 • Poorly compliant bladders (augmentation cohort): ↑ connective tissue : smooth muscle ratio and ↑
   type III : type I collagen, even with normal capacity → poor storage is secondary to connective tissue
   change, especially ↑ type III collagen.
 • Obstruction or denervation (rat) → DSM hypertrophy → ↓ collagen concentration (dilution). Ageing →
   ↓ smooth muscle relative to collagen (both sexes), perhaps via ↓ packing density of submucosal
   collagen.
 • In noncompliant bladders, collagen fibres lose the ability to change tortuosity → ↓ capacity.

Bladder Wall Elastin and Matrix
 • Elastic fibre = amorphous elastin (about 90%) + peripheral glycoprotein microfibrils; sparse vs collagen
   but in all layers.
 • Spinal cord injury (SCI) rat: elastin : collagen ↑ over the first 6 weeks (compliance ↑, overdistension) →
   then ↓ as compliance falls with DO emergence by about 10 weeks — elastin modulates compliance.

 • Spina bifida: compliance associates with urinary MMP-2 and TIMP-2 (matrix metalloproteinase 2 and its
   tissue inhibitor).
 • Non-fibrillar matrix = proteoglycan–water gel (glycosaminoglycans, GAGs, on protein cores) — viscous
   under deformation.

Smooth Muscle
 • Bladder body: fascicles in random directions, cells coupled into a functional syncytium — unlike the
   discrete circular/longitudinal layers of ureter and gut.
 • Single central nucleus, no striations, spindle-shaped, several hundred µm long, 5–6 µm diameter.
   Caveolae (flask-shaped invaginations) associate with sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR); their location and
   density may set contractile heterogeneity.
 • Motor supply: postganglionic parasympathetic fibres (intramural ganglia exist). Varicosities release
   acetylcholine (ACh) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) onto muscarinic and purinergic receptors (Fig.
   103.6); not every cell is contacted — gap junctions spread excitation. DSM also has afferent innervation.
Concept — Structure sets compliance: the ratio of contractile protein to collagen (and collagen type and
tortuosity) decides whether the bladder stores at low pressure, and ischaemia, obstruction and ageing all act
by shifting that ratio.

OVERVIEW OF THE URETHRA

Male Urethra
 • Four segments: preprostatic (bladder neck) → prostatic → membranous (through pelvic floor incl. EUS)
   → bulbous and penile (Fig. 103.7, retrograde urethrogram).
 • Male EUS shape: crescent on ventral prostate proximal to verumontanum → horseshoe distal to it →
   crescent at bulbar urethra.

Female Urethra
 • Runs in the distal third of the anterior vaginal wall. Continence aided by tissues beyond the sphincter
   (Fig. 103.8): subepithelial vascular plexus → "urethral seal". Female EUS: horseshoe over the ventral
   urethra, under voluntary control.
 • Continence during raised abdominal pressure:
    ◦ Passive pressure transmission to the proximal urethra.
     ◦ Guarding reflex: active EUS contraction. Transmission alone cannot explain the full rise — urethral
       pressure rises before cough transmission → active neural mechanism.
     ◦ DeLancey "hammock hypothesis": transmitted pressure compresses the anterior against a posterior
       wall kept rigid by pelvic muscle/connective tissue support; pubourethral attachments and vaginal–
       fascial connections compress the urethra against the pubis and move the bladder neck with voiding.
 • Continence = active muscle tone + passive anatomic coaptation.

Anatomy Common to Both Sexes
 • Fetal dissection: sphincteric complex develops alike in both sexes, from detrusor, trigone and urethral
   muscle of different embryonic origins. Levator ani does not surround the ventral urethra in either sex —
   its continence role is questioned.

 • Striated muscle contraction, like smooth, is Ca²⁺-governed — via troponin.
 • Rhabdosphincter (EUS) is intramural, separate from periurethral pelvic floor muscle; cells 15–20 µm
   (smaller than ordinary skeletal muscle).
 • Periurethral pelvic floor = mixed fast + slow; intramural distal sphincter = predominantly slow-twitch,
   providing about 50% of static urethral resistance.
 • Rhabdosphincter composition: male 35% fast / 65% slow; female 13% fast / 87% slow.
 • Most fast and about a quarter of slow fibres in the membranous sphincter stain for nitric oxide synthase
   (NOS) in the sarcolemma.
 • Pelvic floor exercise or electrostimulation may cure stress urinary incontinence (SUI) by converting fast- to
   slow-twitch fibres (speculative).
 • EUS may contain noradrenergically innervated smooth muscle: hypogastric stimulation evokes EUS
   potentials that persist after α-blockade → probably striated in origin.
Concept — Tone vs reserve: slow-twitch oxidative fibres hold resting closure (hence the female sphincter's
slow-fibre dominance), fast-twitch fibres supply the brief extra squeeze on coughing — and continence always
combines active tone with passive anatomical support.

UROTHELIAL PHYSIOLOGY
 • Primary role: relatively impermeable barrier; also sentinel defence against uropathogens, afferent
   signalling and modulation of DSM contractility.

Barrier Function
 • Permeability depends on passive diffusion, osmotically driven diffusion, active transport and membrane
   inertness.
 • Finite passage has long been known (Kaupp, 1856: 12-hourly vs hourly voiding altered urine
   composition). Passive permeability to most solutes; in vivo rat urothelium passes urea, Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, so
   the bladder may modify final urine, depending on hydration and dietary protein.
 • Water crosses via aquaporins; human water permeability about 6.5 × 10⁻⁵ cm/s (tritiated water
   absorption); no direct human diffusive permeability measure yet — one could phenotype LUTD with a
   leaky urothelium.
 • Loss of umbrella cells (cystitis models) → ↑ water and urea permeability; urinary solute leak into the
   lamina propria likely causes cystitis symptoms, worsened by distension (intermediate/basal cells are no
   real barrier). Similar in infective and radiation cystitis.

 • Osmotic gradient maintained: plasma about 300 mOsm/kg vs urine 100–1500 mOsm/kg — unnoticed
   normally, symptomatic once inflamed (BPS/IC).
 • SCI and myelodysplasia → chronic bacteriuric cystitis. Hyperosmolar instillation (rat) → stronger
   contractions, BP rise, neurogenic inflammation (Evans blue extravasation) blocked by capsaicin →
   hyperosmolality excites C-fibre afferents; enhanced after dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) pretreatment
   (simulated cystitis).
 • Tight junction (TJ) proteins: zona occludens-1 (ZO-1), occludin, claudin-4, -8, -12 — block paracellular
   flow (Fig. 103.9: TJs, adherens junctions, desmosomes, gap junctions). With stretch, small-molecule
   permeability is unchanged but transepithelial resistance (TER) falls tenfold → ↑ ionic paracellular
   transport via claudin pathways.
 • GAG layer — controversial:
    ◦ GAG is 100% polysaccharide, made enzymatically (not template-coded) → heterogeneous.
     ◦ Theory arose from acid-injured rabbit bladder: ↑ bacterial adherence, normalising within 24 h →
       "secretory" antiadherence factor, attributed to GAG.
     ◦ Later shown to be a glycoprotein (mucin MUC-1); MUC-1-transfected cells showed no change in
       water/urea permeability. Chondroitin sulfate is the main human GAG.
     ◦ Verdict: GAG may matter for bacterial antiadherence and blocking macromolecules, but is not proven
       to be the primary impermeability barrier.
 • Trigone and detrusor urothelium are alike; the proximal urethra changes to stratified/columnar
   epithelium without urothelial differentiation markers.
 • Urethral chemosensory "brush" cells (paraneurons): luminal, with microvilli, like gut enterochromaffin or
   tracheal brush cells; detect noxious stimuli or bacteria by the taste-transduction pathway, release ACh
   and serotonin onto nearby sensory nerves → alter bladder function. Intraurethral serotonin → ↑
   visceromotor response (VMR) and afferent firing; sacral afferents respond to urethral flow.

Urethral Tone
 • Smooth muscle, striated muscle and lamina propria all contribute to the urethral pressure profile.
 • Nicotinic (striated) blockade reduces tone rarely by >40% → smooth muscle matters; α-blockade lowers
   urethral pressure by about one third; little cholinergic contribution.
 • Despite a posteriorly open horseshoe, EUS pressure during filling rises uniformly like an iris;
   noradrenaline or hypogastric stimulation augments it → adrenergic role in EUS.
 • Urethral stroma: longitudinal collagen and elastin. Vascular filling of the lamina propria aids continence
   (magnitude unknown); oestrogen ↑ urethral blood flow.
 • Impaired arterial supply ↓ urethral pressure: early fall = ↓ vascular filling; later = hypoxic effect on
   smooth muscle.
 • Periurethral collagen in SUI — divergent: premenopausal SUI: more collagen and thicker fibrils;
   postmenopausal: no difference; others report ↓ collagen and ↓ collagen I : III.

Ionic Transport
 • Apical membrane: high resistance; basolateral about tenfold lower. Active Na⁺ transport occurs.
 • Apical Na⁺ channels (surface and subapical vesicles) are amiloride-sensitive and aldosterone-responsive;
   amiloride-insensitive cation channels are degradation products, cleaved by urinary serine proteases
   (kallikrein, urokinase, plasmin).
 • Basolateral Na⁺/K⁺ exchanger extrudes Na⁺ (cell interior negative); K⁺ and Cl⁻ channels, Na⁺/H⁺ and Cl⁻/
   HCO₃⁻ exchangers restore cell volume when serosal osmolality rises.

 • Role of the apical Na⁺ channel unknown: channel degradation with filling may signal fullness, or regulate
   apical membrane insertion/removal during expansion.

Sensor-Transducer Function of the Urothelium
 • Urothelial cells behave like sensory neurons (nociceptors, mechanoreceptors). "Sensor molecules"
   expressed: receptors for bradykinin, neurotrophins (TrkA, p75), purines (P2X, P2Y), noradrenaline (α, β),
   ACh (nicotinic, muscarinic), protease-activated receptors; ENaC (amiloride-sensitive mechanosensitive
   Na⁺ channel); TRPV1, TRPV2, TRPV4, TRPM8 (transient receptor potential channels).
 • Activated by mechanical or chemical stimuli, they release NO, ATP, ACh and substance P (SP), acting on
   nearby afferents directly or via suburothelial myofibroblasts (gap-junction linked) → chemical coupling to
   sensory nerves. (Carbachol causes dose-dependent ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺.)
 • Nitric oxide (NO): released especially in inflammation (by calcium ionophore, noradrenaline, capsaicin,
   SP); urothelium removal cuts adrenergic NO release by about 85%; cultured urothelium of several
   species (incl. human) expresses TRPV1; afferent NOS ↑ in chronic inflammation. NO barely affects
   detrusor but inhibits afferent Ca²⁺ channels → net inhibition of afferent activity (NO scavenging or NOS
   block ↑ bladder activity).
 • ATP from stretched urothelium → suburothelial P2X3 afferents → fullness and pain; P2X3-null mice
   show hyporeflexia. Analogy: airway epithelium modulating submucosal nerves. ATP also acts autocrine
   (P2X, P2Y) to trigger exocytosis/membrane trafficking. Human urothelium releases ATP to muscarinic/
   purinergic stimulation, causing mucosal contraction; ageing ↑ ATP release.
 • Prostaglandins (PGs): urothelium is the main PG source; roles = detrusor regulation and cytoprotection
   (PGs treat haemorrhagic cystitis). Human urothelium: 6-keto-PGF1α > PGE2 > PGF2α > thromboxane B2,
   plus prostacyclin (PGI2); production ↑ greatly in inflammation. Ureteric PGs may regulate peristalsis and
   limit luminal clot.
 • Muscarinic receptors on urothelium at high density; basal ACh release ↑ by stretch and ageing →
   modulates afferents and DSM.
 • Urothelium-derived inhibitory factor: ↓ muscarinic contraction; identity unknown — not NO, PG,
   prostacyclin, adenosine, catecholamine, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) or apamin-sensitive SK-channel
   action; attenuated in fetal bladder outlet obstruction (BOO).

Suburothelial Interstitial Cells
 • Myofibroblasts beneath the basal urothelium: vimentin+ and α-smooth muscle actin+, desmin−; linked
   by connexin 43 (Cx43) gap junctions; close to C-fibre endings → a functional network (Fig. 103.10:
   stretch-released ATP/ACh act via P2Y, M2/M3, TRPV1 on interstitial cells → afferents).
 • ATP → inward current + ↑ Ca²⁺; express P2Y6 and M3. In human idiopathic DO (IDO), M2/M3
   expression on these cells correlates with urgency score.
 • NO donor sodium nitroprusside (SNP) damps their ATP responses → cyclic guanosine monophosphate
   (cGMP)-dependent inhibition.

 KEY POINTS: UROTHELIUM
   • Uroplakins and TJ proteins underpin barrier function.
   • Uroplakins are the attachment site of type 1-piliated uropathogenic E. coli.
   • GAG layer may aid antiadherence; no definite evidence of impermeability function.
   • Urothelial cells release and respond to neurotransmitters.

SMOOTH MUSCLE PHYSIOLOGY
 • DSM must contract for efficient emptying; understanding its quiescence informs OAB, neurogenic
   bladder and underactive detrusor.
 • Differs from striated muscle: autonomic (not somatic) motor supply, modulation by hormones, paracrine
   NO and urothelial factors, and spontaneous rhythmic activity without neural input; efficient voiding still
   needs neural control.

 • Myofibrils run obliquely in a lattice (no sarcomeres); thin and intermediate filaments anchor to dense
   bodies in cytoplasm and dense bands between caveolae; junctional complexes link neighbours →
   syncytial contraction (Fig. 103.11).
 • Myosin II = two intertwined myosin heavy chains (MHCs): rod/coil tail, hinge, globular heads (Fig.
   103.12). Splicing: 5′ end → SM-A / SM-B; 3′ end → SM-1 / SM-2. Bladder is almost 100% SM-B — higher
   ATPase, faster actin movement than SM-A.
 • Each head carries two myosin light chains: MLC20 (regulatory — its phosphorylation drives cycling via
   myosin ATPase) and MLC17 (two splice variants of one gene).
 • Actin is dynamic: actin polymerisation at the cell periphery is a seminal event, recruiting proteins linking
   actin to integrins at adhesion junctions, stiffening the membrane for force transmission — regulated
   separately from cross-bridge cycling (Fig. 103.13).
 • CaD and TM gate myosin access to actin (Fig. 103.14). CaD (isolated 1981 from chicken gizzard): h-CaD in
   all smooth muscle, l-CaD in non-muscle cells; inhibits actomyosin ATPase and motility, enhanced by TM;
   modulated by calmodulin (CaM) — the protein that binds Ca²⁺ to start contraction.

Actinomyosin Cross-Bridge Cycling
 • Sequence (Box 103.1): action potential (AP) → ↑ [Ca²⁺]i → Ca²⁺ binds CaM → CaM activates myosin light
   chain kinase (MLCK) → MLCK phosphorylates MLC20 (ATP → ADP) → phospho-myosin binds actin →
   force; myosin light chain phosphatase (MLCP) dephosphorylates MLC20 → detachment → relaxation, as
   Ca²⁺ falls.
 • Tone = Ca²⁺ balance + MLCK : MLCP balance. Inhibiting MLCP raises force without any rise in Ca²⁺ =
   calcium sensitisation.
 • RhoA (small GTPase): inactive with GDP, active with GTP. Regulators: GEFs (GDP → GTP, activate), GAPs
   (GTP → GDP, inactivate), GDIs (bind RhoA-GDP, block GEFs and membrane translocation).
 • RhoA-GTP activates Rho kinase (ROK) at the membrane → ROK phosphorylates and inactivates MLCP;
   ROK (and protein kinase C, PKC) also phosphorylate CPI-17, which then binds and inhibits MLCP →
   contraction (Fig. 103.15).

 • Membrane potential follows the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation (weighting each ion's gradient by its
   permeability). At rest the membrane is K⁺-permeable only, so resting potential approaches the K⁺
   reversal potential (about −90 mV); reversal potentials: Na⁺ about +65 mV, Cl⁻ about −85 mV.
 • Human detrusor resting potential −50 to −60 mV (cultured cells more depolarised, about −28 mV).
 • Detrusor AP (Fig. 103.16): phase 0 slow depolarisation → phase 1 fast upstroke (Ca²⁺ inward current,
   ICa) → phase 2 repolarisation → phase 3 hyperpolarisation (both K⁺ outward current, IK); continuous
   current gives AP trains.
 • IK channels: SK and BK (small- and large-conductance Ca²⁺-activated K⁺) and Kv (voltage-gated). Blocking
   any → ↑ contractility and spontaneous activity. BK is the most critical: sets membrane potential →
   governs L-type voltage-dependent Ca²⁺ channel (VDCC) opening → [Ca²⁺]i → contraction.

Excitation-Contraction Coupling
 • ACh (postganglionic parasympathetic) → M3 → ↑ [Ca²⁺]i by (1) extracellular entry through L-type VDCCs
   and (2) SR release via Gq → inositol trisphosphate (IP3).
 • Gq cascade (Fig. 103.17): M3 → Gαq → phospholipase C (PLC) → PIP2 cleaved → IP3 (cytosolic; SR IP3
   receptor → Ca²⁺ release) + diacylglycerol (DAG, membrane; activates PKC). Second-messenger families:
   IP3/PKC and cAMP/PKA (cyclic adenosine monophosphate/protein kinase A).

   not tightly coupled to VDCCs in detrusor (SR farther from membrane) unlike cardiac muscle —
   resembles arterial muscle.
 • Ca²⁺ must return to submicromolar levels by P-type ATPases (autophosphorylated aspartate) and other
   routes (Fig. 103.18): (1) Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange, (2) plasma membrane Ca²⁺-ATPase, (3) SERCA into SR, (4)
   Ca²⁺-binding proteins, (5) mitochondrial uptake.
 • Store-operated Ca²⁺ entry (SOCE; capacitive entry): SR depletion sensed by stromal interaction molecule
   (STIM) → STIM clusters tether ORAI tetramers → calcium release-activated channel (CRAC; current
   ICRAC). Not yet measured in detrusor, but likely.

Propagation of Electrical Responses
 • Cx43 gap junction: monomer with four transmembrane domains (both termini intracellular) → six form a
   hemichannel with a central pore → two hemichannels dock → ionic coupling (Fig. 103.19); shown in
   cultured human myocytes by double whole-cell patch and Western blot. Cx43 and Cx45 expressed in
   human detrusor.
 • Yet detrusor is poorly coupled: no fused tetanic contractions, high tissue impedance — probably
   protective, preventing synchronous activation during filling; coupling within bundles does exist
   (measurable length constant).
 • Coupling falls postnatally: neonatal large, low-frequency coordinated activity → adult small, high-
   frequency irregular activity. Altered coupling may generate uninhibited contractions in overactive and
   ageing bladders.

Detrusor Interstitial Cells
 • Normal bladder may be spontaneously active; exaggerated activity may cause DO (in partial outlet
   obstruction, local contractions are more frequent and coordinated). Source unresolved: myocytes
   themselves or interstitial cells as pacemakers.

   • Muscarinic receptors contract detrusor in response to parasympathetic ACh, via Ca²⁺ entry through

BLADDER MECHANICS

Urinary Storage (Filling)
 • Wall composition: besides muscle, about 50% collagen, 2% elastin; collagen ↑ after injury, obstruction
   or denervation. Contractile protein > collagen → compliant; ↑ collagen → ↓ compliance.
 • Filling geometry: lamina propria thins faster than muscle; bundles shift from top-to-bottom to side-to-
   side; coiled type III collagen straightens, becoming taut and parallel to the lumen → storage at low
   pressure.
 • Compliance C = ΔV/ΔP. Slow physiological filling keeps pressure <10 cmH2O; compliance is fill-rate
   dependent (pressure drops when urodynamic filling slows/stops). In women with SUI (50 mL/min),
   detrusor pressure rose only a few cmH2O to capacity.
 • Pressure–volume curve depends on (1) passive collagen, elastin and muscle, (2) active muscle, (3)
   geometry. Low compliance (steep curve) may reflect: fast filling; more collagen/less elastin; smooth
   muscle hyperactivity; or combinations.
 • Acute spinal transection does not alter compliance; pelvic nerve transection reduces it. Compliance
   involves an active afferent-driven CNS loop inhibiting spontaneous contractility during filling.
 • Broad length–tension relationship; viscoelasticity shapes wall tension. Spontaneous activity common in
   small-mammal strips and seen in human, but fused tetanic contractions (as in gut or uterus) almost
   never occur normally.
 • "Dynamic elasticity": compliance is continuously modulated during filling; ex vivo pulsatile compression
   ↑ capacity (pig) — a possible way to reduce urgency from micromotions.

Voiding Mechanics
 • Pdet = Pves − Pabd (detrusor = intravesical − abdominal). Micturition = neurally driven detrusor
   contraction raising Pdet without Pabd change.
 • Pdet alone does not measure contraction strength: muscle spends energy on force (→ Pdet) or
   shortening velocity (→ flow, Q); trade-off ≈ constant power W = Pdet × Q.
 • During voiding Pdet reflects outlet resistance. With a wide-open urethra, little pressure is needed: stress-
   incontinent women void at Pdet at Qmax about 19 cmH2O; healthy men about 60–70 cmH2O.
 • Low voiding pressure in a woman ≠ impaired contractility; male obstruction nomograms must not be
   applied to women without validation.

Motor Sensory Network in Detrusor Muscle
 • Sensory fibres lie beside myocytes and intradetrusor interstitial cells (Fig. 103.21, with intramural and
   external ganglia); spontaneous micromotions trigger afferent signals → ↑ activity may be perceived as
   urgency.
 • Explains how antimuscarinics (tolterodine) and the β3 agonist mirabegron ↓ non-voiding contractions
   (partial obstruction model) and so ↓ sensory input (afferent output not measured). Micromotions seen
   in women with sensory urgency.
 • Efferent and afferent functions are inseparable.
Concept — Pressure is not strength: detrusor power is shared between pressure and flow, so Pdet during
voiding reports outlet resistance, and during filling low pressure depends on wall structure plus an active
afferent–CNS brake on spontaneous contraction.

Afferent Pathways
Overview: properties of afferent neurons
 • Most time is spent storing: reflexes suppress DSM and contract the sphincter; micturition reverses this.
   Sensory signals drive the switch and generate fullness and pain. Dysfunction → OAB, BPS/IC (urgency,
   pain, UI).
 • Current drugs mainly reduce contraction, yet symptoms reflect dysregulated storage sensation rather
   than exaggerated contraction → targeting afferents is rational.
Pathways to the spinal cord
 • Afferents travel in pelvic, hypogastric (lumbar splanchnic) and pudendal nerves. Cell bodies: pelvic and
   pudendal → sacral dorsal root ganglia (DRG); hypogastric → rostral lumbar DRG. Second-order spinal
   neurons mediate spinal reflexes and ascending pathways.
 • Pelvic afferents (volume and contraction) = myelinated Aδ + unmyelinated C. In SCI (and probably
   inflammation) C fibres are recruited into a new pathway → urgency urinary incontinence (UUI) and
   possibly pain (Figs 103.24, 103.25).
 • Sensation: awareness and desire to void map to bladder distension and contraction; sensation of
   imminent micturition may arise from the urethra. Filling produces episodic sensory bursts of rising
   intensity, the last perceived as urgency. Afferent firing during contraction reinforces the central drive.
 • Receptor types: "in-series tension receptors" (respond to distension and contraction; cat, rat) vs volume
   receptors (distension only, possibly urothelial stretch; rat); many rat C afferents are stretch receptors
   unresponsive to contraction.
Functional properties of bladder afferents
 • Human: only unmyelinated nerves in urothelium/suburothelium; small myelinated fibres with muscle.
   Lamina propria plexus densest at bladder neck and proximal urethra, absent at the dome; muscle
   innervation uniform.
 • Most afferents are mechanosensitive with thresholds from physiological to noxious; low threshold =
   small myelinated; high threshold = generally unmyelinated. Others are chemoreceptors (hypertonic
   saline, capsaicin, ATP) or "silent" afferents sensitised by inflammation → pain.

Modulators of afferent sensitivity
 • Stimulus–response function is shaped by the ending's environment: contraction distorts endings;
   connective tissue transmits or dissipates energy (rapid vs slow adaptation); urothelial and lamina propria
   mediators alter firing.
 • Mediators (neurotrophins, purines, proteases, prostanoids, cytokines — from mast cells, fibroblasts,
   neurons) act by three processes: (1) directly open ion channels; (2) sensitise without stimulating
   (hyperexcitability); (3) change phenotype long-term (neurotrophins alter channel/receptor/mediator
   expression).
 • Sensitisation = leftward shift of stimulus–response curve (greater barrage per stimulus). Peripheral
   sensitisation is rapid and short-lived; with maintained injury, gene expression changes → central
   sensitisation.
 • Reduced afferent sensitivity → impaired emptying: diabetic cystopathy (↓ sensation, ↑ residual;
   ischaemia, glucose metabolism, free radicals); aged rats ↓ volume sensitivity and neuropeptides; aged
   humans ↓ brain response to filling.
Nitric oxide
 • Major inhibitory transmitter relaxing urethral smooth muscle during micturition; also inhibits bladder
   afferents.
 • NOS inhibitors do not alter normal micturition, but spinal NOS inhibition ameliorates irritation-induced
   DO. L-NAME ↑ afferent filling response by about 50%, reversed by L-arginine; L-arginine also reverses
   acrolein (cyclophosphamide metabolite) hypersensitivity (BPS/IC model).
 • Signal via cGMP (Fig. 103.27): soluble guanylyl cyclase ← NO; particulate guanylyl cyclase ← C-type
   natriuretic peptide; membrane adenylyl cyclases ← Gsα and forskolin; soluble adenylyl cyclase ← HCO₃⁻
   and Ca²⁺. Phosphodiesterases (PDEs) hydrolyse cAMP/cGMP; PDE5 terminates NO action → PDE5
   inhibition ↓ afferent activity after SCI → target for BPS/IC and OAB. Injury/inflammation ↑ inducible
   NOS.

Efferent Pathways to the Bladder
 • Three pathways (Fig. 103.30): pelvic parasympathetic excites bladder; hypogastric sympathetic inhibits
   bladder, excites neck and urethra; pudendal somatic supplies sphincter and pelvic floor striated muscle.
   Parasympathetic ACh contracts detrusor for voiding; sympathetic noradrenaline (NA) relaxes via β
   receptors during storage.
Terminal nerve fibres
 • Fascicles → bundles with dense innervation along the fascicle axis from connective-tissue trunks →
   widespread coordinated contraction.
 • Most detrusor nerves express acetylcholinesterase and vesicular ACh transporter (VAChT) —
   parasympathetic. EFS responses need muscarinic + purinergic blockade → ACh and ATP carry excitation;
   both released in muscle, persist after mucosal removal.
 • Co-contents: vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP), NOS, galanin; suburothelial cholinergic nerves
   mostly also contain neuropeptide Y (NPY), tyrosine hydroxylase, some NOS. Trigone: VIP and NPY axons;
   noradrenergic fibres sparse in trigone, rare in detrusor, absent in urothelium.
 • Parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP): released by stretched DSM → autocrine/paracrine
   relaxant; suppresses spontaneous phasic contractions but barely affects carbachol/neural contractions
   → stabilises filling without impairing voiding. PTH1R mainly in muscle; augmentation specimens are
   PTH1R-negative; detrusor tenfold more sensitive than muscularis mucosae. Loss may cause OAB or
   reduced capacity; mechanism (possibly cAMP–PKA) unestablished.
Spinal ascending and descending influences: transmitters

Storage phase of the bladder
 • Low, constant filling pressure below the voiding threshold (Fig. 103.33) depends on intrinsic muscle/
   stroma properties, quiescent parasympathetic outflow, urothelial apical membrane changes and
   mediators, and the bladder sympathetic reflex (negative feedback: closes outlet, inhibits contraction).
Reflex circuitry controlling continence and micturition
 • Central pathways = on–off switching circuits (Fig. 103.32) with a reciprocal bladder–outlet relationship;
   low afferent activity → sphincter activation; high afferent activity → switch flips → bladder efferents fire,
   sphincter inhibited.
 • Reflexes can chain: bladder → sphincter guarding reflex → sphincter afferents → inhibition of bladder
   (bladder–sphincter–bladder loop). Sacral neuromodulation probably works by activating these primitive
   reflexes.

Supraspinal Pathways
Pontine micturition centre and brainstem modulatory mechanisms
 • Barrington's cat lesions: micturition abolished by lesions at the inferior colliculus; facilitated by lesions
   anterior to it (removal of inhibition). Animal pontine findings translate to humans (imaging; pontine
   lesions cause continence or retention problems).
 • Dorsal pontine tegmentum = PMC = Barrington nucleus = "M region" (medial). Discrete lesions →
   retention (cat, rat); human stroke or MS in the same region → retention.
 • Stimulation of Barrington nucleus → bladder contraction + urethral sphincter relaxation. Pontine units:
   pre-contraction only; pre-contraction and sustained (found in Barrington nucleus); inhibited during
   contraction (scattered).
 • Barrington nucleus does not project to Onuf's nucleus. L-region (ventrolateral; pontine continence/
   storage centre, cat) projects to Onuf's nucleus — but no Barrington–L-region connections. Instead
   Barrington nucleus inhibits Onuf's nucleus indirectly via excitatory projections to GABA premotor
   interneurons in the dorsal grey commissure and sacral intermediolateral inhibitory interneurons
   (glycine, GABA) → Barrington nucleus = command centre for emptying.
Central circuitry by transneuronal tracing
 • Pseudorabies virus (PRV) retrograde tracing from bladder or urethra labels overlapping neurons with the
   same time course → close central coordination.
 • First labelled (most direct to spinal efferents): ventral medullary raphe, parapyramidal reticular
   formation, A5, Barrington nucleus. Later: periaqueductal grey (PAG), hypothalamus, medial preoptic
   nucleus (main inputs to Barrington), with locus coeruleus (LC), cortex, red nucleus. Distal colon labels
   similarly (shared pelvic control).
 • PRV shows hierarchy, not connectivity or function. Human voluntary control needs frontal cortex ↔
   septal–preoptic hypothalamus and paracentral lobule ↔ brainstem; cortical lesions ↑ bladder activity
   (lost inhibition).
 • Fig. 103.38: interneurons relay to PMC and PAG; PMC receives PAG, lateral hypothalamus, medial
   preoptic input and projects to LC and lumbosacral parasympathetic neurons, and to dorsal grey
   commissure premotor neurons → Onuf's nucleus; pontine continence centre (PCC) = L-region.
Neurotransmitters and modulators within brainstem networks
 • Glutamate is the principal transmitter of Barrington neurons to preganglionic parasympathetic neurons
   (NMDA and non-NMDA). Fig. 103.31: glutamate acts on NMDA and AMPA receptors in ascending and
   descending limbs and sphincter pathway; modulators GABA, enkephalins, ACh, dopamine (ACh and
   dopamine both excitatory and inhibitory).
 • Corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) in Barrington neurons is inhibitory at the spinal preganglionic level
   (CRF antagonist enhances evoked contraction).
 • Serotonin: inhibitory to detrusor, excitatory to urethral sphincter, at dorsal horn and motoneuron levels;
   presynaptic 5-HT1A on afferent terminals ↓ transmitter release.
 • Fig. 103.31 (cat): normal micturition = supraspinal PMC reflex triggered by Aδ tension receptors;
   cortical–diencephalic modulation; after cord transection micturition is first blocked, then a C-fibre spinal

Human brain imaging studies
 • Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), positron emission tomography (PET), functional

Cerebral control of voiding
 • Voiding = long-loop spinobulbospinal reflex: filling afferents → sacral cord → PAG (± PMC); above trigger
   → PMC efferents → indirect inhibition of Onuf's nucleus (sphincter relaxation) + excitation of detrusor.
   A switch — "off" (storage) or "on" (voiding); without higher control, involuntary emptying at a critical
   volume.
 • White-matter damage causing permanent incontinence interrupts the medial frontal → brainstem
   (direct or via thalamus) pathway that tonically inhibits voiding during storage.
 • Storage/withholding activates right inferior frontal or dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (right-sided
   predominance); little medial frontal activation — imaging shows grey matter; lesions cut white-matter
   links.
 • MS with DSD: anterior thalamic radiation (ATR) involvement; transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) of
   these areas ↓ voiding symptoms.
Additional regions
 • PET during voiding: dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate gyrus (ACG), hypothalamus/
   preoptic area, pons, PAG; also inferior frontal gyrus, postcentral and superior frontal gyri, thalamus,
   insula, globus pallidus, cerebellar vermis.
 • ACG responds to filling/withholding; dorsal ACG and SMA over-respond in urge incontinence (urgency
   without contraction). Urgency = compelling, hard-to-defer desire to void with fear of leakage — a
   homeostatic and social emotion. Rats activate cingulate cortex on filling (homology and "rat urgency"
   unknown).
 • Human PMC: posterior fossa tumours, discrete pontine lesions, childhood brainstem gliomas, vascular
   lesions → retention; MRI localises to dorsolateral pons (pontine reticular nucleus/formation, near medial
   parabrachial nucleus and LC); bladder symptoms overlooked amid disturbed consciousness and
   respiration.
 • Also parietal/frontoparietal, precuneus, posterior cingulate, hippocampus, amygdala, cerebellum, basal
   ganglia (striatum, putamen). Dopamine tonically inhibits the PMC; lost in Parkinson disease (→
   overactivity).
Model of brain–bladder control and normal continence mechanism
 • Circuits answer: Is the bladder full enough? Is voiding safe and socially appropriate? → limbic (emotion,
   safety) and cortical (propriety, decision) control of the brainstem switch = PAG + PMC; PMC is the final
   efferent brain nucleus; PAG receives medial and orbital prefrontal input.
 • Storage sequence: filling afferents → PAG (switch not triggered) → thalamus → insula (desire to void) →
   lateral and medial prefrontal cortex (decision, social appraisal) → if no void planned, medial frontal →
   brainstem pathway (direct or ATR) tonically suppresses the reflex = normal continence mechanism;
   medial prefrontal cortex–PAG (mPFC-PAG) white-matter damage → incontinence. Usually no conscious
   bladder awareness.

PHARMACOLOGY

Muscarinic Mechanisms
 • Human detrusor strips contract to muscarinic agonists and cholinergic nerve stimulation — abolished by
   atropine. Five subtypes (M1–M5) by cloning; four pharmacologically defined.
 • Human bladder: M1, M2, M3 by binding; all M1–M5 mRNA present. M2 predominates numerically, M3
   mediates contraction.
 • M3 → PLC → IP3 → Ca²⁺ release; L-type entry also important (nifedipine strongly blocks carbachol
   contraction; PLC or SOCE inhibitors little effect) — but PLC inhibitors do suppress in other rat studies, and
   Ca²⁺ rises without depolarisation in human cells. Guinea pig: both entry and store release.
 • M2 enhances M3 contraction by: (1) inhibiting adenylyl cyclase (opposing sympathetic β relaxation); (2)
   inactivating K⁺ channels; (3) activating non-specific cation channels.
 • ROK inhibitor Y-27632 suppresses carbachol contraction (rat, human) → muscarinic activation recruits
   Rho kinase → calcium sensitisation; mainly M3, possibly M2 (Y-27632 ↑ darifenacin affinity; darifenacin
   ≈ 30-fold M3 > M2).
 • Shift from M3 to M2 in obstructed or denervated hypertrophied rat bladders and human neurogenic
   bladder.
 • M3 knockout: salivation, pupil constriction and detrusor contraction depend on M3 (gut/reproductive
   redundant); male knockouts: distended bladder, larger capacity (sex difference) → model for DO.
 • Oxybutynin: non-selective antimuscarinic + direct smooth muscle relaxation — relevant only
   intravesically. Botulinum toxin A in paediatric neurogenic DO (NDO) ↓ muscular muscarinic receptor
   expression.
 • Metabolites matter: desethyloxybutynin causes more dry mouth than oxybutynin → controlled-release
   oxybutynin keeps efficacy with fewer side effects. Tolterodine (cat) and solifenacin (rat) act less on
   salivary than bladder receptors. Site and speed of metabolism shape efficacy and side effects.

Adrenergic Mechanisms
β-Adrenergic Receptors
 • β2 and β3 in human detrusor → direct relaxation via adenylyl cyclase → ↑ cAMP. Relaxation not
   blocked by β1/β2 antagonists but blocked by β3 antagonists → β3 mediates it; β3 is the most highly
   expressed adrenoceptor mRNA in human bladder.
 • Mirabegron (β3 agonist) is approved for OAB with UUI, including for antimuscarinic contraindication/
   intolerance; mirabegron + solifenacin combination is effective. Acts on afferents too: in rodents ↑
   compliance, ↓ non-micturition contractions and afferent activation (shortened intervoid interval also
   reported).
 • PDE inhibitors also ↑ cAMP/cGMP; PDEs differ in substrate, species, tissue; search for bladder- vs penis-
   specific isoforms. PDE4 degrades cAMP in guinea pig phasic cells; PDE4 inhibitors ↓ DO in rat BOO. PDE1
   and PDE5 inhibitors used clinically for storage/voiding disorders.

α-Adrenergic Receptors
 • Not prominent in normal bladder, but in BOO-associated DO, α-density (especially α1D) may rise
   enough to convert NA response from relaxation to contraction.
 • Rat obstruction: α1D share rises from 25% to 70%; frequency suppressed by tamsulosin (α1D + α1A) but
   not by 5-methyl-urapidil (α1A only); α1D knockouts: larger capacity and voided volume.
 • Human: α1D already predominates in normal bladder, total α mRNA is low vs β3 and not ↑ by
   obstruction → human role unproven.
 • Urethra is where α matters: α1 and α2 present; α1A is the main urethral smooth muscle subtype; α-
   agonists contract isolated urethra; hypogastric stimulation raises urethral pressure, blocked by α1
   antagonists → rationale for α-agonists to ↑ outlet resistance.
 • α-antagonists aid emptying in functional outlet resistance (BPH). Selective α1A blockers (e.g. RS-17053)
   relax prostate and ↑ flow but do not improve LUTS scores; antagonists with α1D activity improve
   storage symptoms → α1D (bladder or spinal) drives storage symptoms of BOO.
 • α2: antagonists ↑ urethral NA release (presynaptic) without altering contractility; human urethra lacks
   postjunctional α2; prejunctional α2 gives feedback inhibition. Hypogastric nerves inhibit vesical
   ganglionic cholinergic transmission via α2; β-agonists facilitate it (cat).

CLINICAL RELEVANCE
 • Ideal treatment reverses the cause (neural injury, obstruction, infection); idiopathic LUTD (OAB, IC/PBS,
   UAB) is treated empirically for symptom relief.
 • Many idiopathic conditions reflect augmented sensory input → "afferent neurourology". Idiopathic
   NOUR (underactive detrusor) may be efferent failure, worsened by ↓ motor-sensory feedback.
 • Closed-loop optogenetics (mouse): cyclophosphamide DO detected by pressure sensors → light activates
   archaerhodopsin 3.0 in afferents (herpes simplex vector) → ↓ afferent activity → ↓ DO.
 • Overarching paradigm: C-fibre activation via neurotrophins (NGF) made by DSM, temporally linked to
   DO (Fig. 103.42: SCI, obstruction, inflammation, denervation, spontaneously hypertensive rat → ↑
   neurotrophic factors → hyperexcitable C fibres → spinal positive-feedback loops; hypertensive rat also ↑
   α1 excitation). Local anaesthetics, intravesical neurotoxins and bladder neck/prostate afferent
   destruction ↓ urgency, frequency, UUI.
 • Ice-water (spinal C-fibre) reflex appears in neurogenic bladder and BOO → shared plasticity; BP–LUTS
   association in BPH links sympathetic tone to symptoms.

Mechanisms of Idiopathic Detrusor Overactivity and Overactive Bladder
 • Classic model = urodynamic DO, yet DO found in only 11% of an OAB cohort.
 • Non-injury DO models: spontaneously hypertensive rat; serotonin reuptake transporter knockout; BK
   channel knockout; urothelial NGF knock-in; urothelial β1-integrin knockout. Other knockouts (M1–M5,
   P2X2, P2X3, TRPV1) study LUT function but not OAB. Theme: growth-factor-driven neural and muscle
   plasticity.
 • NGF as biomarker: ↑ by DSM after BOO and diuresis; urinary NGF studied for OAB (source uncertain —
   unlikely stromal, as it must cross lamina propria and urothelium). Urothelial NGF overexpression →
   frequency, pelvic pain, lamina propria neuronal hypertrophy. Paradox: no ↑ urothelial NGF in DO; less
   in frequency → role unresolved.
 • Cultured OAB urothelium: ↑ polyamine signalling → blocks urothelial BK channels; ↑ TRPV1 signalling.
 • Urethral afferent function ↓ in OAB/UUI (current perception threshold testing) and unchanged after
   successful SNM — counterintuitive.
 • DSM: BK knockout → OAB phenotype; DO myocytes (IDO, NDO) show ↑ resting [Ca²⁺]i and spontaneous
   rises via L- and T-type channels, greater in NDO.
 • Suburothelial myofibroblasts in IDO/NDO: ↑ Cx43, not reversed by onabotulinumtoxinA (OBTX).
   Cytokines (IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, TGF-β1) ↑ Cx43 in myofibroblasts and myocytes; NGF ↑ Cx43 coupling
   in neuronal cells → ↑ connectivity in OAB and IC/PBS.

Stress Urinary Incontinence
 • Integral theory (Petros and Ulmsten, 1993) → mid-urethral sling (MUS), the surgical standard. But
   DeLancey: intrinsic sphincter function matters more than anatomical support.
 • Childbirth injury models (vaginal distension, pudendal injury): stem cell homing chemokines ↑ (CCL7,
   CXCR4, CXCL12, CD195, CD193; monocyte chemotactic protein-3, MCP-3) → maximise post-partum
   urethral healing to prevent SUI.
 • Biomarker: higher urinary N-telopeptide (NTx; bone turnover) → higher MUS failure. MUS failure at 1
   year about 23% (definition-dependent).
 • Duloxetine (SNRI): ↑ Onuf's nucleus output → ↑ striated sphincter activity → ↓ SUI; beats placebo in
   mild–moderate SUI; approved in Europe, not USA; small benefit in post-prostatectomy SUI.

Nocturia
 • Especially isolated nocturia has distinct causes: global polyuria, nocturnal polyuria, reduced capacity,
   sleep disorders, heart failure, circadian clock disorders.
 • Mouse bladder Cx43 is circadian: inactive (light) phase ↓ Cx43 → ↑ capacity; active (dark) phase ↑
   Cx43 → ↓ capacity. Cry clock-gene knockout abolishes both oscillations → clock failure may cause
   nocturia and nocturnal enuresis.

Bladder Outlet Obstruction
 • Symptoms of urethral obstruction are mostly bladder-generated: detrusor hypertrophy and DO.
 • Rat partial obstruction: bladder about 15× heavier, same shape; surface sixfold, muscle threefold
   thicker; no mitoses — massive cell hypertrophy.
 • Muscle mechanisms: denervation supersensitivity to cholinergic agonists; contractile protein change —
   SM-A expression induced, ↑ force but ↓ cross-bridge cycling rate, altered SM1 : SM2.
 • Myogenic theory (Brading and Turner): all DO shares a DSM change predisposing to unstable
   contraction; pig obstruction DO occurs without a micturition reflex. IDO detrusor shows ↑ electrical
   coupling, patchy denervation, K⁺ supersensitivity.
 • Partial denervation (↓ ACh release and nerve fibres 3–6 months after ligation) → muscarinic
   supersensitivity, ↑ purinergic (P2X1) responses, ↑ Cx43 (also in NDO and urgency) → coordinated
   whole-bladder myogenic contraction. Interstitial cells ↑ in BOO; c-KIT inhibitors ↓ spontaneous
   contractions.
 • Neural plasticity: enhanced spinal reflex (rat); ice-water reflex in obstructed humans; spinal GAP43 ↑
   (axonal sprouting) in sacral parasympathetic nucleus; afferent and efferent neuron hypertrophy. New
   circuits may not reverse after relief (persistent frequency). Muscle and neural changes co-exist.
 • NGF: ↑ in obstructed bladders (animals, humans), precedes neuronal enlargement and frequency; NGF
   autoantibodies prevent plasticity and frequency; stays ↑ with persistent frequency after relief → cause
   and effect. fMRI: supraspinal changes in men failing symptom relief after BOO reduction.
 • Urinary NGF: controls 0.5 pg/mL, BOO without OAB 1, BOO + OAB 41, BOO + DO 50. NGF ↑ after MUS —
   consistent with MUS ↑ Pdet at Qmax (a urodynamic obstruction).

 KEY POINTS: BLADDER OUTLET OBSTRUCTION
   • Obstruction-induced DO attributed to denervation supersensitivity; also contractile protein changes.
   • ↑ Receptor-mediated contractility and cell–cell interaction.
   • BOO enhances a spinal reflex (rat).
   • Bladder tissue and urinary NGF ↑ in obstruction.

Concept — Obstruction remodels both ends of the reflex: NGF from the strained detrusor enlarges afferents
and builds new spinal circuits while the partly denervated, better-coupled muscle becomes supersensitive —
which is why frequency can persist after the obstruction is relieved.

Underactive Bladder
 • UAB = LUTS suggesting ↓ contractility (weak stream, hesitancy, interruption, straining), often with
   frequency, urgency, nocturia; may be secondary to BOO (prior sling, BPH) → non-specific, not a
   diagnosis of cause.
 • Detrusor underactivity = urodynamic ↓ contractility; poorly correlated with LUTS; afferent loss may
   cause it (↓ sensory input → ↓ contraction), not just efferent failure.
 • Post-void residual (PVR): expert panel — ≥300 mL before further evaluation; also evaluate if LUTS
   moderately–severely impair quality of life or risk factors (hydronephrosis, renal insufficiency, culture-
   proven UTI). PVR <300 mL does not prove normal contractility.

Bladder Pain Syndrome and Interstitial Cystitis
 • Pain attributed to the bladder + frequency and urgency; no defined cause.

   • Histology (often): oedema, vasodilation, nerve proliferation, mast cell infiltration.
   • NGF links inflammation to altered pain signalling; made by urothelium, muscle and mast cells;
     activates mast cells.
   • NGF sequestration ↓ cyclophosphamide cystitis overactivity.
   • Chronic inflammation alters C-fibre function and chemosensitive receptors (TRPV1).

Concept — BPS/IC is modelled as afferent hyperexcitability: inflammation, stress or neighbouring-organ
irritation raise NGF, ATP and TRPV1 activity and lower C-fibre thresholds — yet single-target trials (anti-NGF,
RTX) have failed, pointing to heterogeneous patients rather than a wrong mechanism.

Aging
 • LUTS common in the elderly: frequency, urgency, UUI, poor emptying; described changes include ↓
   capacity, ↑ sensation, DO.
 • Community-dwelling women: detrusor contractility, bladder sensation and urethral pressure decline
   with age; capacity does not fall with age but is smaller with DO → ageing = hypofunction of bladder and
   urethra. Pelvic ischaemia (vasculopathy) → LUTS, DO.
 • Ageing is the largest risk factor: cellular senescence, oxidative stress → ↓ perfusion, visceral
   hyposensitivity, abnormal urodynamics. Targeting Aging with Metformin (TAME) trial targets ageing

 KEY POINTS: AGING
   • Sensation and urethral pressure decline with age; reduced capacity reflects DO, not age → ageing =
     hypofunction.
   • Detrusor responses to cholinergic/electrical stimulation unchanged with age.
   • α-contraction ↑ and β-relaxation ↓ in old rats.

Concept — Ageing lowers sensation and drive but sensitises the circuits: nerves, afferents and β-relaxation
decline while α-contraction and central excitability rise — reproducing DHIC (urgency with poor emptying)
and explaining why anticholinergic burden is particularly hazardous in this group.

Neuromodulation
Hypothesis of Mechanism of Action of Sacral Neuromodulation
 • Sacral, pudendal and posterior tibial neuromodulation treat refractory OAB and NOUR (pelvic floor
   dysfunction) — the apparent paradox: one therapy both inhibits OAB and restores emptying.
 • Target = afferent axons in the roots (benefit at intensities below striated muscle activation) → somatic
   afferent inhibition of spinal sensory processing: retention resolves by inhibiting guarding reflexes; DO
   suppressed by inhibiting preganglionic neurons and interneuronal transmission in the afferent limb.

Rationale for Neuromodulation to Facilitate Voiding
 • Brain pathways must switch off guarding reflexes to void; SCI → bladder–sphincter dyssynergia (Fig.
   103.43); subtler lesions (after prostatitis or UTI) may cause idiopathic retention.
 • Before brain control matures (animals), perineal pudendal afferents trigger efficient voiding (bladder
   efferents on, outlet excitation off); perineal touch inhibits the sympathetic guarding component. SNM
   may turn off outlet excitation, and ↓ sphincter afferent input disinhibits the bladder.

Rationale for Neuromodulation to Inhibit the Overactive Bladder
 • Inhibitory afferents from sphincters, distal colon, rectum, anal canal, vagina, cervix, perineal skin (Fig.
   103.44: SNS inhibits bladder–bladder and bladder–urethra reflexes).

Pudendal Nerve Stimulation
 • Pudendal nerve carries afferents of all three roots S2–S4; more peripheral → avoids sciatic/sural
   activation (thigh, calf, foot discomfort of S3 stimulation).
 • Course: sacral plexus → on piriformis → greater sciatic foramen (below piriformis, with sciatic, inferior
   gluteal bundle, nerve to quadratus femoris) → around ischial spine, superficial to sacrospinous ligament
   → lesser sciatic notch → ischioanal fossa → inferior rectal, perineal, dorsal nerve of penis/clitoris.
 • Afferent pudendal stimulation inhibits micturition reflex, abolishes uninhibited contractions, ↑
   capacity. Randomised crossover (tined leads): pudendal 63% vs sacral 46% symptom improvement; UUI
   reduction not significant (small numbers).

Inhibitory and Excitatory Stimulation Frequencies of the Pudendal-Bladder Reflexes
 • Mechanism and programming parameters unstudied clinically. SCI cats: 3 Hz pudendal stimulation
   inhibits bladder; intermittent 20 Hz improves emptying. Continuous stimulation may fatigue the
   sphincter and accommodate the nerve; continuous vs intermittent outcomes unexplored.

Activation of Somatic Afferents in the Foot
 • Transcutaneous footpad stimulation inhibits reflex bladder activity (cat) → FootStim: wearable home
   device, 3 h daily, ↓ UUI and urgency/frequency in refractory OAB (women).

OnabotulinumtoxinA Neuromodulation
 • Blocks presynaptic ACh release (striated and smooth muscle). About 150 kDa single chain → cleaved to
   heavy chain (about 100 kDa) + light chain (about 50 kDa, zinc-bound) linked by a disulfide bond.
 • Four steps: heavy chain binds nerve-terminal receptor → internalisation → light chain translocates to
   cytosol → blocks transmitter release. OBTX cleaves SNAP-25 (a SNARE, soluble N-ethylmaleimide-
   sensitive factor attachment protein receptor) → no vesicle fusion.
 • Also afferent: ↓ urgency in NDO/IDO with ↓ TRPV1 and P2X3 in C fibres; ↓ SP, CGRP from sensory
   terminals; ↓ urothelial ATP; lipotoxin (liposomal OBTX) shows the urothelium is a target. Uses extended
   to pelvic floor spasticity, non-neurogenic OAB, BPS.

FUTURE RESEARCH
 • Established newer therapies: OBTX, β3 agonists, SNM, PTNS. Next: "omics" (genomics, proteomics,
   transcriptomics, lipidomics, metabolomics) for deeper, biology-based phenotyping beyond symptoms.
 • Pharmacogenomics → individualised therapy; regenerative medicine and stem cells (e.g. mesenchymal)
   for storage, emptying and urethral closure; neuromodulation of roots, tibial, foot and saphenous nerves,
   and brain stimulation (TMS) (already used in depression, schizophrenia, addiction, tremor).
 • Urinary microbiota characterised in normals, OAB/UUI and IC/PBS; understanding needs host responses
   (urothelium, lamina propria, afferent/efferent pathways).
 • Questions: prevention of LUTD; genotype-stratified ("personalised") pharmacotherapy; biomarkers for
   phenotyping, prognosis and new targets; susceptibility genes; afferent modulation; urothelium as target;
   a treatment for underactive detrusor that makes self-catheterisation obsolete; clinical value of
   regenerative medicine; host response to microbiota.
