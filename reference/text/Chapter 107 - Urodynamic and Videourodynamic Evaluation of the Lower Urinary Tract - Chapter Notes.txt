Evaluation of the Lower Urinary Tract
  • Lower urinary tract (LUT) dysfunction produces lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) that impair quality
    of life, but it can also be "silent" — asymptomatic yet placing the upper tracts at risk.
  • Urodynamics (UDS) is the dynamic study of the transport, storage and evacuation of urine: a set of tests,
    used singly or together, to diagnose suspected dysfunction and assess the effect of known dysfunction.
  • The LUT has two essential functions: low-pressure storage (protects the kidneys, ensures continence)
    and voluntary evacuation (socially acceptable voiding without leakage or overdistension).
  • Disruption produces either bothersome LUTS (frequency, urgency, urgency urinary incontinence (UUI),
    slow or interrupted stream) or harmful sequelae — urinary tract infection (UTI) and renal failure.
  • International Continence Society (ICS) usage distinguishes UDS studies (the tests performed) from UDS
    observations (what is seen during testing).
  • What has changed: not the principles or technique, but the abandonment of absolute reliance on UDS.
    Studies are now tailored to specific populations within a disease state, and used when available data are
    insufficient to choose treatment — to inform, reassure or uncover other pathology.
  • The American Urological Association (AUA) now favours disease-specific guidelines — overactive bladder
    (OAB), neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD), stress urinary incontinence (SUI) — over the
    older UDS-specific guideline designed to help select tests after symptom characterisation.
  • Children: principles and equipment are identical, but in children UDS establishes a baseline (anatomical
    and neurological abnormalities make function variable and unpredictable) so that change with treatment
    and growth can be tracked, and informs caregivers and patient about current and future function. Testing
    must still be relevant, reliable and reproducible. Adult conditions only are covered here.

ROLE OF URODYNAMICS IN CLINICAL PRACTICE
  • Level 1 evidence for specific "indications" is limited, for structural reasons:
      ◦ randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are hard to justify where lower-level evidence strongly supports
        utility and empirical treatment is potentially harmful or life-threatening (e.g. NLUTD);
      ◦ symptoms have many causes, so homogeneous populations are hard to study;
      ◦ high-level data on UDS in counselling and shared decision-making are absent;
      ◦ cost-effectiveness questions (e.g. who benefits from early surgery for bladder outlet obstruction
        (BOO) from benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)) need complex modelling or expensive long-term
        studies.
  • UDS is most useful when history, examination and simple tests are insufficient — two general scenarios:
      ◦ Diagnosis of the condition causing symptoms (LUTS, incontinence).
      ◦ Determining the impact of a disease capable of serious, irreversible upper- and lower-tract damage
        — spinal cord injury (SCI), multiple sclerosis (MS), radiation cystitis. Profound abnormalities may exist
        with few symptoms.
  • Rather than a list of indications, think of the broader clinical roles of UDS:
      ◦ demonstrate the pathophysiology behind presenting signs or symptoms;
      ◦ identify contributing factors and their relative importance;
      ◦ obtain information on other aspects of LUT function, symptomatic or not;
      ◦ predict upper-tract consequences (Box 107.1);

      ◦ predict treatment outcome and side effects, aiding pre-operative counselling;
      ◦ confirm the effect or mode of action of an intervention, especially a new one;
      ◦ explain failure of previous treatment.
  • Governing principles of interpretation:
      ◦ the goal of a symptom-driven study is to reproduce the symptom and identify its cause;
      ◦ customise the study — decide the questions first, then design the study to answer them;
      ◦ UDS is an unnatural setting: an abnormality recorded without reproduction of symptoms is not
        necessarily diagnostic;
      ◦ failure to record an abnormality does not exclude it (e.g. no detrusor overactivity (DO) seen in a
        patient with UUI);
      ◦ not every observation is clinically significant — interpret with history, comorbidity, postvoid residual
        (PVR) and frequency–volume charts.
  • Technical care in data collection remains the cornerstone of a good study, despite advances in recording
    and processing.

 KEY POINTS
   • UDS is the dynamic study of the transport, storage and evacuation of urine, made up of tests used
     singly or together.
   • Guidelines assist selection of tests after evaluation and symptom characterisation.

Concept — UDS earns its place in two situations only: when the diagnosis cannot be made without it, and
when the disease can silently destroy the upper tracts. Every clinical role follows from answering a pre-
defined question, never from the mere presence of symptoms.

Functional Classification of Voiding Dysfunction
  • The single most important concept for setting up, conducting and interpreting UDS — the Wein
    functional classification (1981).
  • Functional division:
      ◦ storage dysfunction — failure to store;
      ◦ emptying dysfunction — failure to empty;

        ◦ combined — failure of both.
  • Anatomical subclassification — the examinable pairing of site against direction:
 Site                                   Overactive                          Underactive
 Bladder                                Failure to store                    Failure to empty
 Bladder outlet                         Failure to empty                    Failure to store

  • Plus combined bladder and outlet dysfunction.
  • Why it matters: it generates the questions before the study, anticipates findings, and organises
    treatment options. It prevents the clinician becoming lost in neurophysiology instead of thinking
    practically: is the problem bladder, outlet or both — and storage, emptying or both?
Concept — The classification is a two-by-two of site against direction: overactivity of the bladder and
underactivity of the outlet both cause storage failure, and the reverse pair causes emptying failure. Each
study is designed to locate the patient in that grid.

Preparing for Urodynamic Studies: Clinician, Patient, and Facility
Defining the Clinical Question
  • Three rules before any invasive study: decide the questions → design the study to answer them →
    customise as necessary.
  • Symptoms alone are not an indication — the information required must be defined in advance, informed
    by expert opinion, history, medication and examination.
  • Medication status must be decided and documented in advance (antimuscarinics, β₃-adrenergic
    agonists):
        ◦ to assess a drug's therapeutic effect → test on a regular dosing schedule;
        ◦ to uncover the aetiology of symptoms treated empirically → consider stopping the drug first (highest
          diagnostic yield).
  • Pre-invasive work-up recommended by the ICS: validated symptom and bother score, 3-day frequency–
    volume chart, and non-invasive uroflowmetry and PVR. The International Urogynecological Association
    (IUGA) proforma begins with an exhaustive pre-study checklist.
  • Why: a normal free flow and low PVR beforehand allow an abnormal flow during invasive testing to be
    recognised as probably artefactual.

Patient Preparation
  • Explain why the test is done, how it may change treatment and what to expect.
  • UDS is generally well tolerated — anxiety, discomfort and embarrassment are minimal to moderate, and
    most patients find it the same as or better than expected; most would repeat it. Commonest complaint
    afterwards: pain on micturition.
  • Younger patients report more pain and anxiety → need more reassurance.
  • Simple environmental measures — dimmed lighting and music (trend only), and lavender aromatherapy
    (reduced anxiety in an RCT) — may help.
  • Explanatory pamphlets have been disappointing; well-trained, attentive, supportive staff matter most.
  • Urinalysis before every invasive study (level 4 evidence).
  • Symptomatic UTI → postpone until treatment is complete.
  • "Index" patients (no UTI risk factors): the AUA Best Practice Policy (BPP) Statement on antimicrobial
    prophylaxis — routine prophylaxis not indicated.

Facility Setup
  • Dedicated, quiet, private room without interruptions — recreating a natural environment is already
    difficult.
  • Size: enough to lie, stand and sit on a commode; room for wheelchairs, walkers, scooters and hoists
    (many patients have neurological mobility limits). Videourodynamics (VUDS) needs more space for
    fluoroscopy, and local radiation regulations apply.
  • Equipment ready beforehand: pessary kits, Scopette swabs and speculums for pelvic organ prolapse
    (POP) reduction; a step stool (male urethral voiding images, or standing female stress views when the C-
    arm has limited minimum height); large catheters to drain a bladder when pre-invasive PVR is high.

Additional Factors
  • An unanswered question → repeat in the same session or later; a second filling and/or voiding cycle is
    widely advocated.
  • Even with best practice UDS does not answer every question — defining the information needed
    beforehand keeps unanswered questions to a minimum.
  • Proper performance with careful attention to technical detail is the prerequisite for accurate
    interpretation (ICS Good Urodynamic Practices).
  • ICS terminology should be used by all performing and interpreting UDS, for consistent reporting.
    "Urodynamic observations" = observations made and measured during the test itself. A list of common
    terms is given in Box 107.2; the ICS maintains an online glossary drawn from 19 publications, including
    condition-specific terminology for female pelvic floor dysfunction and adult neurogenic patients.
Concept — Preparation exists so that the invasive study answers a question decided in advance: the
medication status, the pre-invasive flow and PVR, the antibiotic decision and the room itself are all chosen to
make the result interpretable.

COMPONENTS OF THE URODYNAMIC STUDY
  • Components can be used individually or combined; all are parts of multichannel UDS or VUDS.
Postvoid Residual.
  • Excellent assessment of emptying; by ultrasound (bladder scan) or catheterisation — method must be
    specified.
  • Elevated PVR shows that emptying is impaired, not why.
  • Confirm a raised PVR with a second measurement, especially if the patient had no true desire to void.
Uroflowmetry.
  • Rate of urine flow over time; also an assessment of emptying. Reportable parameters:
      ◦ voided volume (VV), mL; flow rate (Q), mL/s;
      ◦ maximum flow rate (Qmax) and average flow rate (Qave), mL/s;
      ◦ voiding time (total micturition time) vs flow time (time during which flow occurred);
      ◦ time to maximum flow (onset to Qmax);
      ◦ voiding efficiency = VV / (VV + PVR) × 100.
  • Curve shape matters: normal = bell-shaped (Fig. 107.1). Interpretation is partly subjective.
  • Pattern → suggestion, not diagnosis:
      ◦ interrupted or straining → detrusor underactivity (or obstruction, or abdominal voiding);
      ◦ flattened (plateau) → fixed obstruction.

Chapter 107 — Urodynamic and Videourodynamic Evaluation                                      Urodynamic Equipment
  • Without detrusor pressure, the cause cannot be identified.
  • Standalone test: preferred position (sitting or standing); ask whether the flow was representative;
    compare VV with the frequency–volume chart; record voiding position.
Cystometry (Filling Cystometrogram).
  • Pressure–volume relationship of the bladder during filling. Filling runs from the start of infusion to
    "permission to void", which marks maximum cystometric capacity (MCC).
  • A single bladder line records vesical pressure (Pves) only, and cannot distinguish a detrusor rise from an
    abdominal pressure (Pabd) rise.
  • Recommended: measure both Pves and Pabd (rectal or vaginal catheter) and derive the detrusor
    pressure (Pdet):
      ◦ Pdet = Pves − Pabd (Fig. 107.2).
Pelvic Muscle Electromyography.
  • Electromyography (EMG) records potentials from depolarising muscle membranes — here the striated
    perineal sphincter muscles — during filling and emptying.
  • ICS standard: two skin-surface perineal electrodes with an appropriate reference. Alternatives: vaginal
    probe, anal probe, perineal needle.
Urethral Pressure Profilometry.
  • Urethral pressure profilometry (UPP): intraluminal pressure along the urethra, recorded by withdrawing
    a pressure sensor.
  • Urethral pressure = fluid pressure needed just to open a closed urethra.
Pressure–Flow Studies.
  • Pressure–flow studies (PFS): relationship between Pdet and flow rate during voiding, flow measured
    simultaneously by uroflowmeter.
  • Voiding phase: from permission to void (or onset of uncontrollable voiding) until the patient considers
    voiding finished.

Concept — Non-invasive tests (PVR, uroflow) tell you that emptying is abnormal; only subtracted detrusor
pressure tells you why. The whole architecture of multichannel UDS exists to separate the detrusor's
contribution from the abdomen's.

THE URODYNAMIC STUDY: ANALYSIS AND INTERPRETATION
  • Separate the study into filling/storage and voiding phases — mirrors the functional classification.
        ◦ Filling/storage: CMG, provocative testing (abdominal leak point pressure (ALPP)), UPP.
        ◦ Voiding: contractility, outlet resistance and sphincter coordination — by PFS, EMG and, in some
          cases, fluoroscopy.

Filling and Storage Phase
Normal Filling and Storage
  • Normal bladder stores at low pressure and does not contract involuntarily; at capacity or when voiding
    is desired, pressure rises with a voluntary detrusor contraction — preceded by external sphincter
    relaxation.



                                                          page 8 of 26
Chapter 107 — Urodynamic and Videourodynamic Evaluation                                             Analysis and Interpretation
  • Pdet stays near zero throughout filling — constant low baseline, no involuntary contractions (Fig. 107.3).
    ICS term: normal detrusor function (formerly "stable").
  • Pabd by rectal, vaginal or stomal catheter — data on vaginal and stomal lines are limited, so use a rectal
    catheter where possible.
  • Subtraction distinguishes a true detrusor rise (contraction or loss of compliance) from abdominal
    straining — critical when detrusor rises are small or coincide with abdominal changes.

Abnormalities of Bladder Filling: Detrusor Overactivity and Impaired Compliance
  • CMG measures: filling pressure, sensation, involuntary detrusor contractions (IDCs), compliance and
    capacity.
  • Sensation is the only truly subjective parameter — requires an alert patient and attentive clinician. ICS
    sensation categories (e.g. "early filling sensation", "urgency") are now considered more clinically
    important than previously; some treatments act specifically on sensation.
  • End of filling: marked by "permission to void" on the trace or by stopping the pump; should be a strong
    but not uncomfortable desire to void. Consider the bladder diary and prior PVR when setting the target.
Detrusor overactivity. DO = urodynamic observation of IDCs during filling cystometry, spontaneous or
provoked (Fig. 107.4); often with urgency or UUI.

  • Interpretation in context:
      ◦ ideally DO reproduces urgency or UUI — but it can be significant without symptoms, particularly

        ambulatory UDS;
      ◦ absence of DO does not exclude it — up to 50% of women with UUI show no DO;

  • Position: DO is more frequent in the vertical position or on moving from supine to vertical → test in the
    position that provokes the symptoms.
  • NDO and upper-tract risk: higher maximum detrusor pressure (Pdetmax) is associated with worse renal
    function, and longer IDC duration with upper-tract deterioration.
  • Recognise test–retest variation — "usual LUT behaviour" is not always reproduced.
Compliance. Viscoelasticity (smooth muscle, collagen, elastin) makes the normal bladder highly compliant —
little pressure rise with filling.
  • Compliance = ΔV / ΔPdet, in mL/cm H₂O. ICS standard points: Pdet at the start of filling (usually zero)
    and Pdet at cystometric capacity, or before any contraction causing significant leakage — both excluding
    detrusor contractions.
  • No agreed normal value: healthy means span a wide range, with great individual variation, because
    compliance depends on capacity.
  • Impaired compliance: definitions of 10–20 mL/cm H₂O have been used; < 20 mL/cm H₂O has been
    proposed as indicating a poorly accommodating bladder — but a small-capacity bladder can produce a
    low number without danger (Fig. 107.6).
  • Absolute pressure is more useful than the compliance number: storage pressure > 40 cm H₂O is
    associated with upper-tract damage (McGuire, 1981; Fig. 107.5). Prolonged high-pressure storage
    causes upper-tract deterioration.
  • Conventional cystometry can overstate pressure: some neuropathic bladders with severely impaired
    compliance on CMG are normal on ambulatory monitoring with natural filling.
  • Causes of impaired compliance:
       ◦ neurological — SCI, spina bifida: from increased outlet resistance (e.g. DESD) or decentralisation in
         lower motor neuron (LMN) lesions;
       ◦ long-term BOO (benign prostatic obstruction (BPO));
       ◦ structural — radiation cystitis, tuberculosis.
  • Impaired compliance with prolonged high storage pressure is a urodynamic risk factor and usually
    needs treatment to prevent renal damage (Box 107.1).

Leak Point Pressure
  • Leak point pressure (LPP): pressure (spontaneous or provoked) that expels fluid at the moment it is
    visible outside the urethra (also extraurethral loss or stoma). Record provocation and recording site.

Chapter 107 — Urodynamic and Videourodynamic Evaluation                                          Analysis and Interpretation
  • Practically there are two distinct, independent types — ALPP and detrusor leak point pressure (DLPP) —
    measuring completely different things; both occur only in incontinent patients.
Abdominal leak point pressure. Sphincteric "strength" — ability to resist abdominal pressure. Definition:
intravesical pressure at which leakage occurs from raised abdominal pressure in the absence of a detrusor
contraction.
  • Only demonstrable in SUI; no normal value — continent patients do not leak at any physiological
    abdominal pressure. Lower ALPP = weaker sphincter.
  • Measure total pressure, not the change — standing, include the baseline (typically 20–40 cm H₂O). Read
    classically from Pves, provided there is no IDC (Fig. 107.7).
  • Volume: originally 150 mL; ALPP may fall at higher volumes, so record the volume. Practical sequence:
    test at 150 mL → repeat every 50 mL until SUI appears → no SUI at capacity: remove the urethral
    catheter and read ALPP from the rectal line (provided no Pdet rise from DO or poor compliance).
  • ALPP and intrinsic sphincter deficiency (ISD) — McGuire's 1993 series of women with SUI:

Detrusor leak point pressure. A measure of Pdet in a poorly compliant bladder: the lowest Pdet at which

  • The higher the urethral resistance, the higher the DLPP. Low outlet resistance → leakage at a low, "safe"

    cystitis).

Stress-Induced Detrusor Overactivity
  • Stress-induced DO (SIDO): a rise in abdominal pressure triggers an IDC — the symptom looks like SUI but

  • Note whether there is also urodynamic SUI and/or DO independent of SIDO.
  • ICS equivalent: cough-associated DO — DO (with or without leakage) immediately after the cough peak.

Occult Stress Incontinence
  • SUI on prolapse reduction (occult or latent SUI): SUI shown in a clinically continent woman with POP
    only when the prolapse is reduced — by pessary, packing, Scopette, forceps or manually. Shown on UDS
    = urodynamic occult SUI.

Urethral Pressure Profilometry
  • Brown and Wickham (1969) method: small catheter with lateral side holes, continuously perfused,
    slowly withdrawn; bladder and urethral pressures recorded simultaneously. The transducer registers the
    pressure needed to lift the urethral wall off the side holes. Accurate only where the urethra is
    distensible and seals round the catheter.
  • Clinical relevance is controversial; many urologists do not perform it routinely. Parameters (Fig. 107.10):
      ◦ urethral closure pressure (UCP) profile = urethral pressure − intravesical pressure;
      ◦ maximum urethral pressure (MUP) = highest pressure along the profile;
      ◦ maximum urethral closure pressure (MUCP) = maximum difference between urethral and intravesical
        pressure;
      ◦ functional profile length = length over which urethral pressure exceeds intravesical pressure
        (women).
  • Continent women: functional length ~3 cm, MUCP 40–60 cm H₂O — wide normal variation.
  • MUCP and ISD: McGuire (1981) found MUCP ≤ 20 cm H₂O predicted failure of SUI surgery — the fixed,
    open urethra ("type III" SUI), renamed ISD in 1992. MUCP ≤ 20 shares the problems of ALPP-based ISD
    definitions.

Voiding and Emptying Phase
Normal Voiding and Emptying
  • The voiding phase assesses detrusor contractility and outlet resistance: the bladder must contract
    enough to overcome the outlet.
  • Abnormal emptying = outlet overactivity (too much resistance), detrusor underactivity (weak force,
    short duration, slow velocity), or both. PFS is the most accurate way to assess both (Fig. 107.11).
  • Normal voiding function: flow and pressure rise within normal limits, voiding starts promptly after
    permission and ends with an empty bladder. Male terminology: normal detrusor contractile function —
    an adequate continuous contraction emptying the bladder within a normal time.
  • Women may void normally with little Pdet rise → the female definition differs.
  • Commonest reason normal voiding is not seen: increased outlet resistance → bladder outflow
    obstruction (BOO), a pressure–flow-based cut-off of clinically relevant resistance.
  • Micturition reflex, in sequence (Fig. 107.12): striated sphincter relaxes → detrusor contracts → bladder
    neck and urethra open → flow begins. Coordinated by the pontine and sacral micturition centres with
    suprapontine voluntary control.

  • For pure SUI — no urgency, normal emptying, SUI demonstrated on examination — UDS adds little.

    UDS (ALPP, DO) did not help predict efficacy or postoperative voiding dysfunction.
  • NICE (2006): UDS before SUI surgery only if — suspected DO, previous SUI or anterior-compartment
    prolapse surgery, or symptoms of voiding dysfunction.

Evaluation of Males and Females With Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms
  • LUTS is multifactorial — at least four conditions: BOO, detrusor underactivity, DO and "sensory
    urgency". Storage and voiding symptoms coexist; UUI may come from DO with or without BOO.
  • LUTS are common in men ≥ 50; coexistence of BOO and DO rises with age and with BOO severity.

  • PVR (AUA, Clinical Principle): may be done as a safety measure to exclude significant retention, initially
    and at follow-up — cannot separate obstruction from underactivity, and no agreed "elevated" threshold.
  • Uroflow (AUA, Recommendation; C): may be used in the initial and ongoing evaluation of men with
    voiding/emptying LUTS — likewise cannot distinguish obstruction from underactivity, but guides therapy
    and prompts testing.
  • How much diagnosis before treatment?: no UDS needed to start an α-blocker in a man emptying
    normally; as treatment becomes more invasive and morbid, a precise diagnosis becomes more valuable.
  • After TURP, storage symptoms persist in about one-third. When DO accompanies BOO, single terminal
    IDC → storage symptoms more likely to resolve than continuous or sporadic IDCs — valuable for
    counselling (a man bothered mainly by emptying may choose surgery; one bothered mainly by OAB may
    not).
  • Evidence for UDS before BPH surgery is mixed — some support, some find it unnecessary or useful only
    in inconclusive cases, some find modest predictive value. 6th International Consultation on New
    Developments in Prostate Cancer and Diseases: evidence is level 3 but its quantity supports a grade B
    recommendation.
  • "No obstruction on PFS → less favourable surgical results." No critical BOOI level for preventing
    decompensation has been defined, and no evidence-based trigger for surgery to prevent bladder
    decompensation exists — though BOO causes progressive, possibly irreversible structural bladder
    change, prompting some to favour early relief.
  • Significantly impaired compliance is the only "absolute urodynamic indication" for treating BOO.

Evaluation of Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction
  • Presentations: incontinence of bladder origin (DO, impaired compliance) or sphincter origin; poor
    emptying of bladder origin (neurogenic detrusor underactivity, neurogenic acontractile detrusor) or
    sphincter origin (dyssynergia); or silent upper-tract decompensation — hydroureteronephrosis and renal
    insufficiency without bothersome symptoms.
  • Goals: prevent upper-tract decompensation and relieve symptoms; achieve physiological filling (and if
    possible voiding), control symptoms, and a management situation acceptable to the patient. UDS has its
    most important role here.
  • Evidence is mostly level 3 or lower — RCTs are considered dangerous and unethical given the
    consequences of untreated neurogenic dysfunction.
  • Primary aim: convert to a low-pressure bladder during filling, even at the cost of incomplete emptying
    and catheterisation. Therapy depends on whether the detrusor is overactive or poorly compliant — only
    UDS answers this unequivocally. UDS is also essential for monitoring response and sequelae.
Ice water test (IWT) — Bors and Blinn (1957); no agreed protocol.
  • Mechanism: cold instillation elicits a C-fibre-mediated spinal reflex detrusor contraction, normally
    inhibited supraspinally.
  • UMN lesion → inhibition lost → contraction (positive); LMN lesion → no contraction (negative).
  • Helps distinguish reflexic from areflexic bladders; ICS: optional, interpreted with other data, and should
    not precede standard UDS.

 KEY POINTS
   • UDS is not essential before surgery for stress-predominant MUI in women when SUI is clinically
     demonstrated.
   • AUA: PFS to determine obstruction in men with LUTS is important before invasive, morbid or
     irreversible treatment.
   • Women with poor emptying or voiding symptoms benefit from PFS to separate obstruction from
     impaired contractility, especially when the cause is not obvious.
   • UDS has its most important role in NLUTD.

Concept — In NLUTD the kidneys, not the symptoms, set the agenda: because damage can be silent and trials
are unethical, UDS is the only arbiter of whether the bladder stores safely, and low filling pressure outranks
complete emptying.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS
  • UDS remains a key research tool; future value lies in shaping decisions, patient understanding and
    phenotyping, and predicting long-term management.
  • Non-invasive tests for male BOO (systematic review): detrusor wall thickness, near-infrared
    spectroscopy (NIRS), penile cuff test, uroflowmetry with Qmax < 10 mL/s, intravesical prostatic
    protrusion > 10 mm — promising but limited by heterogeneous definitions and thresholds, small
    samples and weak methodology; combinations may be needed.
  • Penile cuff test (UroCuff): cuff pressure needed to interrupt flow estimates bladder pressure; no urethral
    catheter, naturally filled bladder; plotted on a modified ICS nomogram. A meta-analysis found it
    performs sufficiently for diagnosing and managing BOO, but obstruction criteria vary.

  • Storage-phase surrogates for DO: ultrasound bladder/detrusor wall thickness and bladder weight; NIRS
    (oxy-/deoxyhaemoglobin changes during IDCs in real time — limited by motion artefact); biomarkers
    such as nerve growth factor (NGF) (correlate with DO/OAB but lack specificity). None is reliable for
    routine practice or quantifies storage beyond DO → filling cystometry remains the gold standard for
    storage disorders.
  • Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) with concurrent UDS: maps brain network activation
    through the micturition cycle (controls and OAB) — research only.
  • Implantable wireless bladder pressure monitors: longer, out-of-office monitoring; mostly animal data,
    recent human work shows promising reliability, safety and tolerability, but a single intravesical channel
    needs validation.
Concept — Each new technology tries to remove the catheter or the laboratory from urodynamics; none yet
replaces the subtracted-pressure principle, which is why filling cystometry and pressure–flow remain the
reference standards against which they are all judged.
