Chapter 75 — Surgery for Erectile Dysfunction
Matthew J. Mellon and John J. Mulcahy
 • Modern penile implants were first marketed for erectile dysfunction (ED) over 50 years ago: the
   three-piece inflatable implant of Scott (1972) and the semirigid rod prosthesis of Small and Carrion
   (1978). Before them, rudimentary penile stiffeners gave variable, inconsistently reliable support.
 • At introduction they were the only effective treatment for ED; later came oral medication,
   intracorporal injections, intraurethral pellets and vacuum erection devices (VEDs), with varying
   success and acceptance.
 • Improvements in design, surgical placement and repair technique have increased device longevity and
   functionality.
 • Patient and partner satisfaction is high (80–90%); repairs are needed in about 15% of devices at 5
   years and 30% at 10 years (long-term series).
 • Despite this, implants remain a secondary treatment of ED — chosen by strongly motivated men who
   have failed, are contraindicated to, or find unacceptable the less invasive options.
 • Lue's goal-directed approach: outline all treatments → offer a trial of each as the patient wishes →
   settle on the modality that is effective and appealing.
 • Extensive testing for the exact cause of ED is costly and does not change therapeutic outcome; it is
   reserved for rare and unusual cases.
Concept — The implant is the most effective but last-line ED treatment: the goal-directed model selects by
patient acceptance of successive options, not by aetiological work-up.

TYPES OF IMPLANTS AVAILABLE
Historical and Market Background
 • Early 1970s: two types — the Scott inflatable and the Small–Carrion sponge-filled semirigid rod.
   Design improvements and competing vendors (at one point five companies) fuelled growth.
 • Sales grew almost exponentially through the 1980s and early 1990s, peaking in 1996 at about 33,000
   units.
 • 1996: preliminary sildenafil citrate (Viagra) data presented → sales fell to a nadir of about 12,000
   units the following year.
 • Phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (PDE5Is) are not universally effective → implant sales gradually
   recovered.
 • Three United States vendors today: Boston Scientific (Marlborough, MA), Coloplast (Minneapolis,
   MN) and Rigicon (Ronkonkoma, NY); others exited through falling sales or acquisition. All three sell
   internationally.
 • About 60,000 implants per year worldwide, about 30,000 in the United States. South Korea: about
   8000 per year — almost twice the United States rate per capita. All other countries, including China
   and India: fewer than 1000 per year.
 • Larger foreign vendors: Zephyr Surgical Instruments (Geneva) and Promedon (Córdoba, Argentina);
   some foreign vendors do not distribute in the United States.

 INFORMED CONSENT
   • A thorough explanation of procedure and expected result is paramount before implantation.
   • The greatest postoperative disappointment is an erection shorter than the patient remembers —
     caused by pseudocapsule scar forming around every part of the device and limiting cylinder
     expansion.
   • Apparent size reduction is greater in Peyronie disease, where tunical scar already limits expansion.
     Stress this potential.
   • Devices are mechanically reliable but not indestructible — future repair may be needed.

   • Tissue problems — pump migration, erosion and extrusion of parts, infection — may all require
     reoperation. Sensation and climax can be affected.
   • Alternative ED treatments must be explained and offered, although usually already tried.
   • Consent tends to be more extensive with more experienced implanters.
   • Implant candidates are often highly focused on their genitalia; disappointed patients can be litigious.
   • Unrealistic expectations are common: confirm that the planned outcome is a firm penis usable for
     penetrative intercourse, not necessarily with the feelings, desires and abilities known previously.

   KEY POINTS: INFORMED CONSENT FOR PENILE IMPLANT PLACEMENT
    • The erect penis may be shorter and thinner than preoperatively.
    • The goal is a firm penis suitable for intercourse.
    • Penile sensation and ejaculation may both be diminished.
    • Varying degrees of postoperative pain.
    • Reoperation for mechanical problems may be necessary.
    • Tissue problems such as infection may necessitate further surgery.
    • Other ED treatments exist: oral medications, penile inserts and injections, VEDs.
    • A variety of implants exist, including three-piece inflatable and malleable semirigid rods.

 Concept — Consent is expectation management: the device restores rigidity, not the former erection, and
 length loss is the complaint to pre-empt.

 PREOPERATIVE PREPARATION
   • Examine genital and pelvic skin: phimosis, sebaceous lesions, fungal infection must be treated and
     eradicated first.
   • Phimosis: correct first, implant about 6 weeks later once healed.
   • Redundant foreskin with a wish for circumcision: perform simultaneously — implant first, close all
     incisions, then circumcise.
   • Strong-soap washing at home is reinforced.
   • Shave in the preoperative area or theatre, so skin nicks cannot become colonised before surgical
     preparation.
   • Diabetes mellitus: glycaemic targets in the table below.
   • Urine must be sterile. Stop anticoagulants, including low-dose aspirin.
   • Ascertain allergies, especially to antibiotics — used systemically, in irrigating or dipping solutions, and
     as implant coatings.
   • Obtain insurer approval (costly; some policies exclude sexual dysfunction); discounted cash
     arrangements may be available.

 Closure, Pump and Connection
   • Close corporotomies over the cylinders with the stay sutures, or with interrupted/running sutures,
     taking extreme care not to puncture the device.
   • Protect the cylinder with a small malleable or proximal pushing instrument; longitudinal traction via
     the glans suture minimises protrusion and puncture.
   • Keep input tubing at the most proximal point of the corporotomy.
   • Test inflation 2 after closure.
   • Pump: subdartos pouch in the most dependent scrotum, right or left (most patients manage either),
     anterior and lateral to the testis, by finger, ring clamp or nasal speculum dissection.
   • Shodded occlusive clamps; trim all redundant tubing; make a straight pump-to-reservoir connection.
   • Test inflation 3: malfunction, displacement, fluid leak, adequate sizing.
   • Irrigate thoroughly; close with two subcutaneous layers of 3-0 Vicryl and a 4-0 Monocryl skin layer.
 Girth Selection for Malleable Rods
   • Rods have fixed girths — choose width before opening packages: place two Hegar dilators parallel in
     both corpora, then try to touch thumb to index finger between them (technique applicable to
     Ambicor, Tactra and Genesis).

 Space of Retzius
   • Original 1970s route: inferior linea alba, between the recti, into the prevesical space of Retzius — still
     simple and rapid.
   • Via penoscrotal incision, through the inguinal canal to the side of the bladder:
 Drain bladder → Trendelenburg (bowel off pelvis) → sharp instrument pointing medially pierces
 transversalis fascia just above pubic tubercle → index finger confirms space of Retzius → long nasal

     internal ring and becomes fixed by adhesions; inguinal Retzius placement has then compressed bowel

 INTRAOPERATIVE TROUBLESHOOTING

 Fibrotic Corpora
   • Dilation is usually straightforward.
   • Pelvic radiation (common in prostate cancer, frequent implant recipients) does not increase
     placement difficulty or postoperative complications.
   • True scar: after acute ischaemic priapism or after explantation for infection or erosion — creating
     cavities is difficult.
   • Midshaft corporotomy lets dilators travel an equal distance each way.

     cylinder difficult to place, corporal measurements markedly different.
   • Recognise and correct intraoperatively, not when the patient reports an odd-looking erection.

   • Unusual; more common at secondary insertion, where scar forces aggressive dilation.

   • Glans hypermobility and short cylinders are distinct problems: short cylinders → longer cylinders or
     rear tip extenders, not glans fixation.

 Curved Erection After Cylinder Placement
   • Usually straight — the tunica expands uniformly.
   • Tunical scar from Peyronie disease, a prolonged pharmacological injection programme, or pelvic
     trauma → curvature toward the scar.
   • Semirigid rods can usually be bent to overcome it; extensive thick scar needs tunical manoeuvres.
   • Cylinder choice (table below): one of the three high-rigidity cylinders alone straightens most cases.
   • Residual curvature under 20 degrees is acceptable for penetration.
 Modelling
 Shod-clamp pump tubing (protects pump from back pressure) → bend inflated cylinders forcefully to a
 right angle away from the curve → hold 90 seconds → repeat if needed
   • Check for corporotomy disruption and urethral injury after modelling.
   • Works best for untreated Peyronie plaques; less successful for scarring after explantation.
   • Keep the penis partially erect for 3 months so the pseudocapsule forms straight; deflated cylinders
     allow the curve to return.
 Recalcitrant Curvature
   • Nesbit procedure, tunical plication, or plaque incision and grafting.
   • Nesbit: excise small tunical ellipses from the convex side and close with absorbable suture; or
     plication stitches limiting convex expansion.
   • Incision through scar on the concave side lets the cylinder force straighten the erection; graft the
     defect if necessary — defects under 1 cm need no graft.
   • Cautery over Titan (Bioflex) cylinders: below 35 watts; Boston Scientific silicone is unaffected by
     cautery.

 POSTOPERATIVE COMPLICATIONS

 Implant Infection
   • Incidence low (1–3%), but infection still occurs; precautions span before, during and after surgery.
 Antibiotic Prophylaxis
   • Systemic prophylaxis recommended by American Urological Association (AUA) guidelines.
   • Vancomycin or a third-generation cephalosporin, plus gentamicin, intravenously 1 hour before
     incision, continued up to 24 hours or until the wound is sealed.
   • No antibiotic is needed beyond that, yet most urologists give 5–14 days of oral antibacterial;
     explantation for infection is the same with or without postoperative antibiotics, which may even be
     harmful.
   • Organisms from infected implants (multi-institutional series): most, not all, sensitive to vancomycin–
     gentamicin; vancomycin + an antifungal (amphotericin or fluconazole) + piperacillin–tazobactam
     would cover all → prudent prophylactically in high-risk patients and in documented infection before
     cultures return.
   • Caution: nephrotoxicity with combined vancomycin–piperacillin.
   • Vancomycin–gentamicin alone is associated with higher infection risk than non-standard regimens;
     future guidelines will clarify.
 Irrigation, Coatings and Technique
   • Copious irrigation throughout with anti-infective solution or saline; no particular antiseptic is
     currently recommended; chlorhexidine gluconate (Irrisept) is gaining popularity.

 Biofilm and the Revision Washout
   • Repair of a clinically uninfected malfunctioning implant carries about 10% infection.

   • Mechanism: organisms introduced at the original operation form a mucopolysaccharide biofilm
     shielding them from host defence; repair disturbs it → bacteria activate → symptomatic infection.

     new parts are placed → infection reduced to about 3%. Recommended for all secondary procedures
     in which old parts are removed.
 Concept — Infection is a biofilm on a foreign body introduced through the wound: prevention targets the
 operative field, and cure demands removal of every component — salvage merely shortens the interval
 before a new device.

   • Chronic tip wear → extrusion beyond the tunica through skin or into the urethra.

 Penile Necrosis After Implant Placement
   • The penis is a digit: nutrient flow runs proximal to distal; ischaemia → dry gangrene; the glans, most
     distal, is most susceptible.
   • Longitudinal incisions (foreskin and tunica, parallel to shaft) interrupt distal supply less than
     transverse incisions.
   • At-risk patients: severe cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, chronic smoking, previous
     prosthetic implants, prior pelvic radiation — especially two or more.
   • Distal foreskin nourishes the glans; circumcising incisions may contribute to glans necrosis.
   • Tunical incisions to straighten or lengthen → caution in high-risk patients.
   • Mummy wrap without excessive pressure; always leave the glans exposed to monitor perfusion.
   • Necrosis detected → remove cylinders promptly to relieve pressure; conservative debridement aids
     later reconstruction.

 SPECIAL SITUATIONS

 Immunosuppressed Patients
   • Successful implantation after renal, cardiac, hepatic and renal–pancreas transplantation — no
     increase in complications, including infection.
   • Transplant specialist manages immunosuppression; prophylactic antibiotics coordinated with the
     urologist.
   • Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-positive men on antiviral therapy with appropriate cluster of
     differentiation 4 (CD4) counts: no increased infection.

 Penile Implants in Ischemic Priapism
   • Acute ischaemic priapism lasting more than 36 hours → completely scarred corpora; months later an
     implant is the only option — cavities difficult, erection significantly shorter.

   • Implant placed after the graft has matured; single cylinder, usually Titan or AMS 700 CX, proximal end

   • Crura containing proximal cylinder ends or rear tip extenders abut the anterior horn of a standard
     saddle.

     associated with urethral stricture.
   • Solution: a saddle with buttock supports only (no nose) — also used by riders with an artificial urinary

 POSTOPERATIVE CARE
   • Most patients go home the same day.
   • Leave inflatable cylinders partially erect → dilated corpora heal with scar around inflated cylinders,
     preventing a contracted pseudocapsule that would limit expansion.
   • Compressive mummy wrap reduces bruising and oedema.
   • Short-term scrotal drains: prevent haematoma, not proven to raise infection risk; remove within 24
     hours or when bleeding stops.
   • Compressive underwear for 4–6 weeks, penis pointed up toward the umbilicus → avoids permanent
     downward angulation.
   • Indirect ice to the scrotum as needed.
   • Oral opioid analgesia for about a week, then nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.
   • Dorsal neurovascular bundle infiltration with lidocaine and bupivacaine reduces pain and analgesic
     need; long-acting liposomal bupivacaine (Exparel) cut analgesic use threefold versus standard bundle
     block.
   • Infrapubic approach: patient pulls the pump into a dependent scrotal position daily before activation;
     penoscrotal approach: pump secured in the dartos pouch — unnecessary.
   • Activation visit at 4–6 weeks once pain has resolved; partner ideally attends to learn pump operation.
   • Daily cycling passively dilates cylinders and corpora and builds familiarity; sexual use at 4–6 weeks.

 Mechanical Reliability: Repair for Mechanical Defect
   • Mechanical malfunction remains the most common complication of penile prosthesis surgery.
   • Semirigid implants rarely fail mechanically; long-term data are mostly for three-piece inflatables.
   • Failure modes: cylinder aneurysm, pump malfunction, autoinflation, tube kinking — reduced by
     vendor modifications.
   • Long-term survival: the Mentor Alpha-1 (after its 1992 enhancement) and the AMS 700CX/CXM both
     showed high freedom from mechanical failure at 5–10 years.
   • Fluid loss is the commonest problem, usually late; replace the leaking component if identified.
   • Implants in place more than 3 years → remove and replace the entire device (wear on all parts).
   • If parts are left behind, confirm their hydraulic integrity — a second defect may coexist: hydraulic
     distension, or more accurately an ohmmeter test of electrical continuity.
 Ohmmeter Leak Test
