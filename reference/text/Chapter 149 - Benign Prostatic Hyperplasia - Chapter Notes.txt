Chapter 149 — Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia

Chapter 149 — Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia: Aetiology,
Pathophysiology, Epidemiology and Natural History
 • Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a histopathological process, and it is one cause, but not the only
   cause, of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in ageing individuals, also termed male LUTS.
 • Cause and effect remain unestablished after five decades of research. Androgens, for example, are
   necessary but not sufficient to drive BPH.
 • The old term "prostatism" is erroneous. Male LUTS are not simply the result of a mass-related rise in
   urethral resistance.
 • A significant share of male LUTS is not prostatic. Causes include age-related detrusor dysfunction,
   polyuria, sleep disorders and systemic disease unrelated to the prostate–bladder unit.
 • Voiding symptoms correlate poorly with the underlying pathophysiology.
    ◦ The same symptoms arise from any outlet obstruction, for example a urethral stricture.
     ◦ They also arise from impaired detrusor contractility, with no obstruction at all.
     ◦ LUTS are therefore often, but not invariably, due to bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) from benign
       prostatic obstruction (BPO). BPO in turn often accompanies benign prostatic enlargement (BPE)
       resulting from histological BPH.
 • Women have voiding symptoms too. Age-matched community-dwelling men and women report similar
   symptom frequency and severity.
 • Failure to empty results from outlet obstruction, detrusor underactivity, or both.
 • Post-micturition symptoms (for example post-void dribbling) occur in both sexes. They are commonest in
   men, highly troublesome, and interfere significantly with quality of life (QoL).
 • Storage symptoms fall largely under overactive bladder (OAB) syndrome.
     ◦ OAB comprises urgency, frequency, nocturia and urgency incontinence, and is thought to reflect
       detrusor overactivity.
     ◦ Storage symptoms are more bothersome than voiding symptoms, especially when accompanied by
       incontinence.
     ◦ In both sexes they are commonly associated with urinary infection. Rarer associations are bladder
       stones, carcinoma and carcinoma in situ.
 • Four partially overlapping populations (Figure 149.1): histological BPH, LUTS with bother, BPE
   (enlargement) and BOO (obstruction). The circles in the figure are not to scale.
     ◦ Many men over 40 develop histological BPH, but not all have bothersome LUTS. Of those who do,
       some develop measurable enlargement (BPE) and some do not.
     ◦ BPE without LUTS, and LUTS without BPE, are both common.
     ◦ BOO may exist with or without LUTS and with or without BPE, and may occur in men without BPH,
       for example from urethral stricture.
 • Linking cell type–specific alterations to dysfunction is the route to new strategies to treat, or prevent, the
   effect of BPH on lower urinary tract (LUT) function.
Concept — BPH, BPE, BOO and LUTS are four different things (a histological process, an enlargement, an
obstruction and a symptom complex). They overlap only partially, and nearly every argument later in the
chapter depends on keeping them apart.

AETIOLOGY OF BENIGN PROSTATIC HYPERPLASIA
 • The histology is hyperplasia, not hypertrophy. BPH is an increased number of epithelial and stromal cells
   in the periurethral prostate. "Hypertrophy" in the older literature is incorrect.
 • New gland formation otherwise occurs only in fetal development. This is the basis of the concept of
   embryonic reawakening of the inductive potential of the stroma.
 • Increased cell number may come from two sources: proliferation, or impaired programmed cell death
   with cellular accumulation.
 • Candidate contributors, acting singly or in combination: androgens, oestrogens, stromal–epithelial
   interactions, growth factors and neurotransmitters.

Hyperplasia
 • Organ volume reflects the balance between cell proliferation and cell death. An organ enlarges if
   proliferation rises or if cell death falls.
 • Active proliferation is doubtful in established human BPH. Early BPH may involve rapid proliferation, but
   established disease is maintained with an equal or reduced replication rate. Increased expression of anti-
   apoptotic genes (for example bcl-2) supports this.
 • Androgens have two actions: they are required for normal proliferation and differentiation, and they
   actively inhibit cell death.
 • In the dog, androgen plus oestradiol produces experimental BPH. Gland size rises markedly while DNA
   synthesis actually falls, showing that androgens and oestrogens both inhibit cell death.
 • Neural signalling may also balance death against proliferation, especially α-adrenergic pathways.
 • Remodelling of architecture occurs in two stages.
    ◦ Early: epithelial budding from pre-existing ducts, and mesenchymal nodules.
     ◦ Established disease: a highly variable tissue phenotype. This heterogeneity has confounded a cell
       type–specific molecular understanding.
Cellular identity and the normal prostate atlas.
 • BPH is highly pleomorphic. Bulk tissue molecular analyses therefore track the patient-specific phenotype.
   Single-cell RNA sequencing atlases of normal human and mouse prostate now provide the baseline.
 • Architecture: a branching tree of 13–15 ducts on each side of the prostatic urethra.
 • The prostatic urethral urothelium contains club cells (SCGB1A1+) and hillock cells (KRT13+), with
   interdigitating neuroendocrine cells and ionocytes. Club and hillock cells extend into the proximal ducts,
   then give way to NKX3.1+ prostate luminal cells. The same urethral-to-prostatic transition is seen in the
   mouse proximal ducts (Figure 149.2: keratin 5 marks basal epithelium, keratin 13 urethral hillock
   epithelium, NKX3-1 secretory luminal epithelium).
 • Glandular acini are the functional units: secretory luminal and basal epithelium, with rare ionocytes and
   neuroendocrine, club and hillock cells.
 • Fibroblasts come in two populations.
     ◦ Periepithelial fibroblasts surround each acinus and also line the prostatic urethral urothelium.
     ◦ Interstitial fibroblasts form a dense collar around the prostatic urethra from apex to base, and are
       sparse between peripheral zone glands.
 • Peripheral zone stroma is mostly smooth muscle. It contracts the acini at ejaculation, driving fluid
   through the proximal ducts into the verumontanum.

 • Interstitial fibroblasts are increased in BPH, in both stromal and glandular phenotypes. This effectively
   expands the transition zone and may induce new branching morphogenesis.
     ◦ Their trigger is unknown.
     ◦ They are not targeted by any current medical therapy.
BPH as a stem cell disease; regeneration after castration.
 • BPH recapitulates developmental branching morphogenesis (budding → branching → branch
   elongation). This prompts the view of BPH as a stem cell disease, but the cells that form new glands are
   unidentified.
 • Mouse development: multipotent basal progenitors are replaced by lineage-committed unipotent basal
   and luminal progenitors. The proximal (urethral) compartment derives from Nkx3.1-negative urethral
   epithelium marked by Krt4 and Runx1.
 • Both basal and luminal lineages can regrow the adult mouse prostate after stress: inflammation, obesity,
   luminal anoikis or castration.
 • Castration shrinks the distal prostate through loss of androgen-dependent secretory luminal cells.
   Androgen replacement regrows it only to its original size, implying a growth-restricting mechanism.
 • Two older hypotheses located the regenerating cell:
    ◦ An androgen-independent progenitor in the distal prostate, based on where DNA synthesis is
      concentrated.
     ◦ Regenerative capacity in the urethra and proximal ducts, based on explant assays.
 • Modern lineage tracing resolves this.
    ◦ Urethral luminal cells (Sca-1+/Psca+/Trop2+/Krt4+) survive castration but do not regenerate the
      prostate.
     ◦ Most regeneration comes from surviving distal Nkx3.1+ luminal cells. These survive low androgen by
       lineage plasticity, coming to resemble urethral luminal cells.
     ◦ A rare pre-existing distal progenitor population contributes only a very small fraction.
     ◦ Conclusion: any surviving luminal cell can regenerate the gland; there is no dedicated progenitor
       niche.
 • In men treated with a 5α-reductase inhibitor (5ARI) or with androgen deprivation therapy, prostate
   luminal cells undergo lineage plasticity to resemble urethral club cells.
 • In humans the origin of regrowth after androgen withdrawal is unresolved. Three candidates:
     ◦ Pre-existing 5ARI-indifferent SCGB3A1+ club cells in the proximal ducts.
     ◦ 5ARI-induced club-like cells arising from luminal plasticity.
     ◦ 5ARI-resistant NKX3.1+ cells.
Concept — BPH is an accumulation disease. Cells gather because death is suppressed at least as much as
division is driven, and androgens contribute mainly by keeping cells alive. That is why the gland shrinks when
androgen is withdrawn.

The Role of Androgens
 • Androgens are permissive, not causal. BPH needs testicular androgens during development, puberty and
   ageing.
    ◦ Men castrated before puberty, or with genetic defects of androgen production or action, never
      develop BPH.
     ◦ Androgen withdrawal causes partial involution of established BPH.

 • Intraprostatic dihydrotestosterone (DHT) and androgen receptor (AR) levels stay high with age, even
   though circulating testosterone falls.
 • Circulating androgens do not predict prostate size within the normal range.
     ◦ Olmsted County cohort: bioavailable testosterone falls with age, and the oestradiol:bioavailable
       testosterone ratio rises. Both associations with volume weaken markedly after age adjustment.
     ◦ A large BPH medical-therapy study confirmed no relationship among serum testosterone, prostate-
       specific antigen (PSA) and volume (Table 149.1).
     ◦ Exception: higher baseline serum DHT was associated with a higher risk of BPH over 20 years, and a

Mechanism of androgen action.
 • In brain, skeletal muscle and seminiferous epithelium, testosterone acts directly.
 • In the prostate, nuclear membrane–bound 5α-reductase converts testosterone to DHT, the principal
   prostatic androgen.
     ◦ About 90% of prostatic androgen is DHT, derived mainly from testicular androgen.
     ◦ About 10% is adrenal, and its role in BPH is negligible.
 • Testosterone and DHT bind the same high-affinity AR. DHT is more potent because of higher AR affinity,
   and the DHT–receptor complex may be more stable.
 • Pathway: hormone–receptor complex → DNA binding sites → transcription of androgen-dependent genes
   → protein synthesis.
 • Androgen withdrawal does three things: switches off androgen-dependent genes (for example PSA),
   activates programmed cell death genes, and activates inflammatory genes.
 • Neither testosterone nor DHT is a direct mitogen.
    ◦ Neither is mitogenic to cultured prostatic epithelium.
     ◦ Expression studies in the rat ventral prostate show no direct activation of mitogenic pathways.
     ◦ Androgens act indirectly, through autocrine and paracrine growth factors and cytokines.
 • Figure 149.3 — the paracrine model.
     ◦ Testosterone enters stromal and epithelial cells.
     ◦ In the stromal cell most is converted to DHT, which acts autocrinely or diffuses to adjacent epithelium
       (paracrine).
     ◦ DHT made peripherally (skin, liver) reaches the prostate in a true endocrine fashion.
     ◦ Basal cells may also produce DHT.

Androgen Receptors
 • The prostate keeps its androgen responsiveness for life; the penis does not.
    ◦ Penile AR falls to negligible levels at the end of puberty, so the adult penis stops growing despite high
      androgens.
     ◦ Prostatic AR remains high throughout ageing. Nuclear AR may even be higher in hyperplastic tissue.
 • Age-related rises in oestrogen may up-regulate AR, sustaining growth or reducing cell death despite
   falling serum androgen and "normal" intraprostatic DHT.
 • AR gene polymorphism is at most a minor factor. Short CAG (cytosine–adenine–guanine) repeats were
   linked to larger glands and more surgery in one population, but findings are inconsistent across
   populations. Vitamin D receptor polymorphism findings are likewise inconsistent.

Dihydrotestosterone and Steroid 5α-Reductase
 • Intraprostatic DHT is maintained, not elevated, in BPH.
     ◦ Early reports of raised DHT used cadaveric (accident victim) controls, in whom DHT falls after death.
     ◦ Using surgical specimens from men without BPH as controls, DHT is the same in hyperplastic and
       normal glands.
     ◦ The ageing prostate keeps high DHT and high AR, so the machinery for androgen-dependent growth
       persists. Androgens are at least permissive.

 • Type 1–derived DHT is unlikely to matter in BPH. Finasteride (type 2 only) shrinks the gland as much as
   dual inhibitors do, and roughly as much as castration.
 • Any extra benefit of dual inhibition would come from blocking peripherally produced DHT.
 • The interstitial fibroblast is the androgenic amplification step. Type 2 5α-reductase within fibroblasts
   converts testosterone to DHT, which is the core of the paracrine model.
 • Enzyme expression varies within and between prostates, so single biopsies map its distribution poorly.
Resistance to 5α-reductase inhibitors.
 • 5ARIs are the only medical therapy that reduces prostate volume, which makes resistance an important
   question.
 • Clinical resistance is not failure to lower intraprostatic DHT. More likely causes are:
     ◦ absence of luminal epithelial atrophy focally within glandular nodules, leaving persistent anatomical
       obstruction, or
     ◦ widespread stromal hyperplasia.
 • Proposed luminal survival mechanisms, all linked to inflammation:
    ◦ A club cell transition in areas of 5ARI-induced atrophy, with high nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) activation.

     ◦ NF-κB is raised in BPH and can induce androgen-independent AR variants.
     ◦ Epigenetic silencing of SRD5A2 in a subset of patients, switching survival to oestrogenic pathways.
 • Inflammation correlates with 5ARI resistance in clinical trials. All these pathways remain correlative.
 • A proper definition of resistance should have three components: volumetric (volume unchanged),
   histological (no glandular atrophy) and biochemical (DHT decreased).
SRD5A2 genetics.
 • SRD5A2 lies on chromosome 2p23. It carries A49T and V89L substitutions and a thymine–adenine (TA)
   dinucleotide repeat.
     ◦ The 89L allele lowers enzyme activity; the 49T allele raises it.
     ◦ Longer TA repeats make the messenger RNA unstable and lower activity.
 • Links to BPH are inconsistent. In the Olmsted County population there was no consistent genotype
   association, only a weak V89L–volume correlation.
 • Promoter methylation of SRD5A2 occurs in up to 30% of BPH specimens, is thought to switch regulation
   to oestrogenic pathways, and does not correlate with volume.
Vascular effects.
 • Castration causes acute, drastic prostatic vasoconstriction in the rat. This is not mediated by vascular
   endothelial growth factor (VEGF).
 • Vascular disease elsewhere (for example diabetes) may be a risk factor for BPH.
Concept — Androgen levels do not rise in BPH. What persists is the machinery: high intraprostatic DHT, high
AR, and type 2 5α-reductase in fibroblasts. That is why DHT suppression shrinks the gland even though serum
testosterone does not predict its size.

The Role of Oestrogens
 • Oestrogen's role is clear in animals and uncertain in humans.
 • In the dog, oestrogen synergises with androgen. It induces AR and "sensitises" the ageing gland to
   androgen. The canine prostate is rich in high-affinity oestrogen receptor (ER).
 • Canine stromal effect: oestrogen stimulates the stroma and increases total collagen.
 • The two receptors sit in different compartments: ERα in stroma, ERβ in epithelium. The prostate's
   response depends on which receptor is present.
 • Knockout mice suggest oestrogens exert a "constraining influence" on the prostate.
 • In vitro, stromal ERα up-regulation raises fibroblast growth factor (FGF)-2, FGF-7 and other growth
   factors. Adding androgen down-regulates both ERα and these factors.
 • ERβ agonism failed clinically. A randomised placebo-controlled trial in LUTS showed no effect on
   symptoms, PSA, volume or urodynamics.
 • Serum oestrogen rises with age, absolutely or relative to testosterone.
    ◦ Intraprostatic oestrogen may be raised in BPH.
     ◦ Larger glands go with higher serum oestradiol.
     ◦ Olmsted County: in men with above-median bioavailable testosterone, oestradiol correlated positively
       with volume even after age adjustment.
     ◦ Data linking obesity, testosterone, oestradiol and volume conflict.
 • Classic high-affinity ER is sparse in human BPH, but possibly sufficient for activity.
 • Aromatase inhibition reduces drug-induced stromal hyperplasia in animals.
 • Species differences and uncertain cause and effect keep the human role unproven.

Regulation of Programmed Cell Death
 • Apoptosis maintains glandular homeostasis.
    ◦ Sequence: cellular condensation → fragmentation → phagocytosis by neighbouring cells → lysosomal
      degradation.
     ◦ It occurs without immune activation but requires RNA and protein synthesis.
 • In the rat, cell death normally occurs in the proximal ductal segment despite normal testosterone.
   Androgens suppress death elsewhere in the gland.
 • After castration, death rises in the luminal epithelium and in the distal region of each duct.
 • Androgen action is regionally controlled through local growth regulatory factors. The transforming
   growth factor-β (TGF-β) family is the likely mediator.
 • At least 25 genes are induced in the rat prostate after castration.
 • Homeostasis balances growth inhibitors against mitogens. A local growth factor or receptor abnormality
   could produce BPH through more proliferation or less programmed death.
Concept — Androgens control prostate size largely by controlling death rather than division. Withdrawal
therefore triggers an active, gene-dependent apoptotic programme rather than passive shrinkage.

Stromal–Epithelial Interaction
 • Stroma and epithelium communicate in a paracrine fashion.
 • Culture experiment in canine epithelium:
    ◦ Grown on plastic, the cells lose secretion, grow rapidly and change cytoskeletal staining.
     ◦ Grown on prostatic collagen, they keep normal secretion and phenotype and grow slowly.

     ◦ So the extracellular matrix (ECM) partly regulates epithelial differentiation.
 • Hypothesis: BPH may reflect a stromal defect that removes a normal "brake" on proliferation. The same
   defect could act autocrinely to expand the stroma.
 • Embryonic mesenchyme dictates differentiation of urogenital sinus epithelium. New gland formation in
   BPH suggests reawakening of this stromal inductive process.
 • Growth factors and the ECM, which has growth factor–like properties, mediate much of this. The model
   fits 5α-reductase localisation in the periurethral interstitial fibroblast collar.
 • CXC motif chemokines (CXCL1, CXCL5, CXCL6, CXCL12):
    ◦ They are made by the microenvironment and stimulate both stroma and epithelium.
     ◦ With interleukin (IL)-8, they convert fibroblasts to myofibroblasts, which cause collagen deposition
       and fibrosis. Fibrosis may contribute to LUT dysfunction.
 • A putative stem cell niche of poorly defined stromal and epithelial cells is maintained by Hedgehog,
   Notch, WNT and TGF-β signalling. These are potential targets for inducing regression.
Concept — In the prostate the stroma instructs the epithelium, as embryonic mesenchyme does. BPH can
therefore be read as the stroma resuming its fetal inductive role.

Growth Factors
 • Growth factors are small peptides that stimulate or inhibit division and differentiation through surface
   receptors. Their interaction with steroid hormones may tip proliferation against death (Figure 149.4).
 • The first prostatic growth factor was basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, FGF-2), identified by
   sequencing an extract of BPH that stimulated growth.
 • Factors implicated: FGF-1 (acidic), FGF-2 (basic), FGF-3 (Int-2), FGF-7 (keratinocyte growth factor, KGF),
   FGF-10, FGF-17, TGF-β, epidermal growth factor (EGF), VEGF and insulin-like growth factors (IGFs).
 • Figure 149.4 message: androgens are necessary but permissive; growth factors are the likelier site of the
   primary defect.
 • The overall role is still uncertain. Growth factors probably matter, but proliferation is not obvious in BPH,
   and it is unclear which factors are key mediators and which are bystanders.

 • TGF-β has opposite actions by compartment: it restrains epithelium and drives stroma. Loss of epithelial
   restraint plus stromal stimulation fits a stroma-dominant BPH.
 • TGF-β also regulates smooth muscle contractile proteins, and causes versican accumulation in the ECM by
   inhibiting the ADAMTS metalloproteases that degrade it.
 • FGF-7 is the paracrine answer to how an androgen signal in the stroma reaches the epithelium: stromal
   source, epithelial receptor.
 • Hypoxia hypothesis:
    ◦ Local hypoxia (for example from atherosclerosis) → hypoxia-inducible factor 1 → stromal secretion of
      FGF-2 and FGF-7 → nodule growth → further impaired blood flow → more hypoxia.
     ◦ TGF-β, itself activated by hypoxia, also regulates FGF-2.
 • Trap: TGF-β is not simply "growth inhibitory". It inhibits epithelium but is mitogenic for fibroblasts.
Concept — Androgen supplies the permission and growth factors supply the signal. The factors are
compartment-specific: FGF-7 carries a stromal message to the epithelium, and TGF-β brakes the epithelium
while driving the stroma.

Other Signalling Pathways
 • Sympathetic signalling matters in LUTS. α-Adrenergic blockers are highly effective for LUTS.
 • Sympathetic pathways may also drive growth. α-Blockade can induce apoptosis in some models, and α-
   adrenergic pathways modulate the smooth muscle phenotype.
 • The renin–angiotensin system (RAS): all its components are present in the prostate and may be activated
   in BPH. Local RAS may contribute to proliferation and smooth muscle contraction, with or without
   sympathetic modulation.
 • Early growth response gene 1 (EGR-1) is active in a BPH cell line.
 • α2-Macroglobulin binds PSA and many growth factors, is highly expressed in the prostate, and is up-
   regulated in BPH. Trapping inhibitory molecules could promote growth.

The Potential Role of Inflammatory Pathways and Cytokines in Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia
 • Inflammatory infiltrates are an additional source of growth factors.
     ◦ BPH tissue shows extensive infiltration by activated T cells.
     ◦ T cells secrete VEGF (a potent epithelial mitogen), heparin-binding EGF and FGF-2, and so could drive
       stromal and glandular hyperplasia.
 • Cytokines in BPH tissue:
    ◦ IL-2, IL-4, IL-7, IL-17 and interferon-γ and their receptors are present. IL-2, IL-7 and interferon-γ
      stimulate stromal proliferation in vitro.
     ◦ Senescent epithelium secretes IL-8, which promotes proliferation of non-senescent epithelial and
       stromal cells.
     ◦ Macrophage inhibitory cytokine 1 is present in normal prostate and down-regulated in BPH.
     ◦ Chronic inflammation is associated with focal epithelial cyclo-oxygenase-2 (COX-2) up-regulation.

 • Mouse models support a link.
    ◦ The POET (prostate ovalbumin-expressing transgenic) autoimmune model shows lymphocyte,
      macrophage and neutrophil infiltration with epithelial and stromal thickening and epithelial stem cell
      expansion.
     ◦ Bacterial prostatitis drives epithelial turnover.
     ◦ In the human prostate, epithelial progenitors expand near inflammation.
     ◦ Even so, no firm cause and effect has been established.
 • The metabolic syndrome may raise the risk of prostatic inflammation, and so of LUTS and BPH.
 • Androgens are anti-inflammatory.
    ◦ AR activation by testosterone (rabbit) and DHT is protective.
     ◦ Conditional AR knockout in mouse prostate epithelium → more proinflammatory cytokines and
       immune infiltration.
     ◦ Human hypogonadism is associated with inflammation.
     ◦ If confirmed, this links androgen status to inflammation in male LUTS and BPH.
 • BPH as an autoimmune or immune-inflammatory disease (Figure 149.5):
    ◦ T cell–derived proinflammatory cytokines drive immune inflammation and stromal growth.
     ◦ Smooth muscle cells help maintain and propagate the infiltrate.
     ◦ Antigens may be prostatic (PSA, prostatic acid phosphatase) or infectious (bacteria, viruses, protists,
       fungi).
     ◦ Inflammation has been proposed as a therapeutic target.
 • Single-cell sequencing: fibroblasts are the central communicators with the immune infiltrate. The influx
   of T cells, B cells and dendritic cells resembles a mucosal inflammatory disorder.
 • Autoimmune link in a large database:
    ◦ BPH prevalence was higher in patients with autoimmune disease.
     ◦ Tumour necrosis factor (TNF) antagonist therapy was associated with lower BPH incidence.
Concept — Androgens and inflammation pull in opposite directions: androgen is anti-inflammatory and
hypogonadism is pro-inflammatory. Inflammation, not androgen excess, is the more plausible accelerant of
the ageing gland, but causality remains unproven.

Genetic and Familial Factors
 • BPH has a heritable component.
 • Johns Hopkins case–control study:
     ◦ Cases were men undergoing surgery with resected weight in the top quartile (>37 g) and age at
       surgery in the bottom quartile.
     ◦ First-degree male relatives of cases had about fourfold the risk of surgically treated BPH, not explained
       by health-seeking behaviour (Table 149.3).
     ◦ Segregation analysis favoured autosomal dominant inheritance.
     ◦ About 50% of men operated on before 60 may have heritable disease, against about 9% of those
       operated on after 60.
 • Twin data: monozygotic concordance exceeds dizygotic.
 • Community cohort: a family history of enlarged prostate or BPH raises the risk of moderate-to-severe
   symptoms.
 • Finasteride trial families (three or more affected members):
     ◦ Familial BPH is characterised by substantially larger glands than sporadic BPH.

Other Aetiological Factors
 • Only dogs and humans develop BPH, although all mammalian prostates have testosterone, DHT, AR and
   the usual growth factor pathways. The seminal vesicle, also androgen-responsive for life, never becomes
   hyperplastic. Species-specific cofactors must exist.
 • Testicular non-androgenic factors:
     ◦ They may reach the prostate through the vas deferens or deferential vessels.
     ◦ Testis-intact rats and dogs given androgens grow more prostate than castrated ones given the same
       androgen, and intact dogs show more histological BPH.
     ◦ Human spermatocele fluid is mitogenic to prostatic epithelium and stroma.
 • Prolactin: transgenic overexpression enlarges the mouse prostate. Prolactin receptors are present in man,
   but the human role is unclear.
 • No mouse model of human BPH exists. Mouse and human anatomy differ, and the molecular drivers are
   poorly understood. Models of specific aspects are needed.
Concept — The androgen and growth factor machinery is universal across mammals, yet only men and dogs
develop BPH. Something species-specific, possibly testicular, must be added to that universal machinery.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
 • The pathophysiology is a chain, not a single lesion (Figure 149.6): prostatic hyperplasia → raised urethral
   resistance → compensatory change in bladder function.
 • The price of compensation is storage function. The higher detrusor pressure needed to maintain flow
   against resistance is achieved at the expense of normal storage.
 • Obstruction-induced detrusor change, compounded by ageing of bladder and nervous system, produces
   frequency, urgency and nocturia, the most bothersome BPH-related complaints.
 • Figure 149.6 — the interacting inputs to LUTS: BOO (from hyperplasia or from non-BPH causes), the
   detrusor response to obstruction, detrusor ageing effects, neurogenic disease, primary bladder disease
   and polyuria.
 • Understanding BPH therefore requires understanding obstruction-induced bladder dysfunction.

Pathology
Anatomic Features
 • BPH begins in the periurethral transition zone (McNeal's zonal anatomy, Figure 149.7).
 • The transition zone is two separate glands lying immediately outside the preprostatic sphincter. Its main
   ducts arise on the lateral aspects of the urethral wall at the point of urethral angulation near the
   verumontanum.
 • The periurethral zone lies proximal to the transition zone duct origins, confined within the preprostatic
   sphincter, with glands running parallel to the urethral axis.
 • Every BPH nodule arises in either the transition zone or the periurethral region.
    ◦ Early transition zone nodules lie within or immediately beside the preprostatic sphincter.
     ◦ As nodules multiply they appear almost anywhere in the transition or periurethral zone.
     ◦ The transition zone also enlarges with age independently of nodule formation.
 • Figure 149.7 shows the zones in sagittal and coronal section: peripheral, transition and central zones,
   verumontanum, proximal urethral segment, preprostatic sphincter and ejaculatory duct.
The prostatic capsule.
 • The capsule is unique to the human prostate and is central to LUTS.
    ◦ The dog develops natural BPH but rarely develops obstruction or urinary symptoms, because its
      prostate lacks a capsule.
     ◦ The capsule presumably transmits the pressure of tissue expansion to the urethra, raising urethral
       resistance.
     ◦ Clinical proof: transurethral incision of the prostate (TUIP) incises the capsule and relieves
       obstruction significantly although prostate volume is unchanged.
 • Prostate size does not correlate with the degree of obstruction. Dynamic urethral resistance, the capsule
   and anatomical pleomorphism matter more than absolute gland size.
Lobes (Figure 149.8).
 • Hyperplastic tissue obstructs the urethra in recognisable patterns: isolated middle lobe; isolated lateral
   lobes; lateral plus middle lobes; and posterior commissural hyperplasia (median bar).
 • The middle lobe must be periurethral in origin, because there is no transition zone tissue at the bladder
   neck. It results from predominant growth of periurethral nodules at the bladder neck.
 • Whether middle lobe growth is random or genetically determined is unknown.

Stroma and androgen withdrawal.
 • Stroma is usually assumed to resist androgen withdrawal, and short-term ablation affects mainly the
   epithelium.
 • Stromal turnover is much slower than epithelial turnover. If ablation acts by raising cell death, a fall in
   stromal cell number may not show until a year or more of therapy. True stromal resistance is therefore
   unproven.
 • Hormonal therapy may alter stromal function without reducing stromal volume. Contractile proteins,
   neuroreceptors and ECM proteins are hormone- and growth factor–regulated in other smooth muscle
   systems, and androgens modulate α-agonist effects on prostatic smooth muscle in vitro.
Phenotype of BPH smooth muscle.
 • BPH smooth muscle is dedifferentiated. Smooth muscle myosin heavy chain is down-regulated and non-
   muscle myosin heavy chain up-regulated. This indicates proliferation or loss of normal modulation.
 • α-Blockade in BPH further down-regulates normal contractile protein gene expression, specifically
   smooth muscle myosin heavy chain. Adrenergic signalling therefore regulates the muscle cell, not merely
   its contraction.
     ◦ Parallels: adrenergic transmitters regulate contractile gene expression and hypertrophy in cardiac
       muscle.
     ◦ Testosterone may regulate adrenoceptor expression, at least in the kidney.
Other contractile regulators.
 • α1-Adrenoceptor nomenclature has been standardised to reconcile pharmacological and molecular
   classifications.
 • Endothelin and its receptors are present in the prostate; their physiological role is undefined.
 • The kallikrein–kinin system (for example bradykinin) may regulate smooth muscle proliferation and
   contraction.
 • Phosphodiesterase (PDE) type 4 and type 5 isoenzymes are present in the prostate and the detrusor.
   Placebo-controlled trials confirm that erectile dysfunction (ED) drugs benefit men with LUTS and BPH.
Autonomic overactivity.
 • Autonomic nervous system activity correlates positively with symptom score and other BPH measures,
   assessed by physiological tests and plasma and urinary catecholamines.
 • The rise in serum noradrenaline after tilt predicted transition zone size.
Concept — Obstruction in BPH depends on geometry and tone, not on mass. The capsule, lobe pattern and
active and passive smooth muscle tone determine urethral resistance, which is why gland size and obstruction
do not correlate.

Trabeculation and extracellular matrix.

The detrusor smooth muscle cell.
 • The first response to obstruction in animals is smooth muscle hypertrophy. It is adaptive, maintaining
   flow against raised pressure, but brings intracellular and extracellular changes that lead to instability and,
   in some, impaired contractility.
 • Obstruction also causes altered contractile protein expression, mitochondrial dysfunction (impaired
   energy production), calcium signalling abnormalities and impaired cell-to-cell communication.

 KEY POINTS — AETIOLOGY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
 - Androgens are **necessary for maintenance but not growth** of the prostate, and are **anti-
 inflammatory**. - **Inflammation correlates with prostatic enlargement**; its causal role is unclear. -
 **Anatomical pleomorphism within and between patients** confounds cell type–specific understanding
 of BPH and may contribute to **therapeutic resistance**. - **Animal models do not fully recapitulate
 human BPH**, because of anatomical differences and poor mechanistic understanding. - **A heritable
 form of BPH exists**; the precise mutations are unidentified. - **Understanding the secondary effects of
 obstruction on bladder contractile and neuromodulatory function** could improve clinical outcomes in
 BPH and LUTS.

Descriptive Epidemiologic Studies
Histologic or Autopsy Prevalence
 • Definition: stromoglandular hyperplasia in a surgical specimen, or in whole prostates removed at autopsy
   from men who did not die of prostatic disease.
 • Landmark pooled autopsy study (Berry, 1984): no BPH under 30; prevalence rises with each age group,
   peaking at 88% in men in their 80s.
 • Figure 149.11 — pooled autopsy series (United States, England, Austria, Norway, Denmark, China, Japan,
   India): prevalence rises rapidly from the fourth decade and approaches 100% in the ninth decade.
 • Age-specific autopsy prevalence is strikingly similar across all ethnic and geographic populations.

Cross-Sectional Studies of Clinical Prevalence
 • Descriptive epidemiology needs a single accepted disease definition, and BPH has none.
 • "Prostatism" is obsolete and incorrect. It wrongly made the prostate the sole source of LUTS. Paul
   Abrams coined "LUTS" to replace it.
 • Clinical classification uses three independent axes:
     ◦ Symptoms: mild, moderate or severe on a standardised questionnaire.
     ◦ Enlargement: by digital rectal examination (DRE), transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS) or magnetic
       resonance imaging (MRI).
     ◦ Obstruction: by flow rate or invasive pressure–flow study.
 • BPH is only one cause of LUTS. Others are bladder, urethral, neurogenic and unknown causes.
 • Obstruction may exist without BPH: urethral stricture, prostate cancer, primary bladder neck sclerosis
   (Figure 149.1).
 • Bother, interference with daily activities and impact on QoL distinguish patients beyond symptom
   counts.
 • "Clinical BPH" is an imprecise term for this constellation of LUTS, bother, interference and QoL impact,
   with or without enlargement or obstruction.
 • Few clear cut-offs separate disease states. A volume over 30 mL is one proposed definition of clinical
   BPH, but higher and lower cut-offs are argued, and the same applies to symptom and obstruction
   thresholds.
 • What is described is therefore the age-stratified distribution of disease attributes, not true disease
   prevalence.
 • Figure 149.12 — prevalence varies with the definition used: autopsy, clinical diagnosis, low maximum
   flow rate, DRE enlargement and community-based composite definitions give different estimates. A
   common composite was IPSS >7, Qmax <15 mL/s and prostate volume >20 mL.

 • Jewish religion is associated with a higher prostatectomy rate (about 2–2.5-fold) in several studies, but

   perceived improvement: higher-income patients need a larger fall in score to perceive the same

Alcohol and Liver Cirrhosis
 • Alcohol lowers plasma testosterone (reduced production, increased clearance), which would predict less
   BPH.
 • Observed associations are inverse: heavy drinking was associated with lower rates of surgery for BPH.
    ◦ Caveat: poorer health in heavy drinkers may bias physicians against surgery. One study found no
      association with diagnosis or surgery.
 • Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial (PCPT): an inverse relationship between alcohol intake and BPH
   diagnosis, that is, a modest protective effect.
 • Meta-analysis: alcohol reduces the likelihood of BPH but not of LUTS. Higher intake (≥36 g/day) against
   none was associated with a lower likelihood of BPH (odds ratio 0.65).
 • Cirrhosis: most autopsy studies found less BPH in cirrhosis, but because most cirrhosis is alcohol-induced,
   the two effects cannot be separated.
 • Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), part of the metabolic syndrome, is associated with BPH in men
   and OAB in women.

Sexual Activity and Vasectomy
 • The hypothesis that sexual activity increases fibromuscular stroma is unsupported.
    ◦ An apparent protective association of widowhood against prostatectomy was not replicated.
       ◦ Olmsted County: ejaculatory frequency has no effect on LUTS, Qmax or prostate volume; the
         apparent protective effect is confounded by age.
       ◦ The coincident decline of sexual activity and rise of BPH with age may reflect a reverse relationship:
         BPH impairing sexual function.
 • LUTS severity correlates strongly with erectile and ejaculatory dysfunction (Figure 149.16).
    ◦ Erectile function falls and ejaculatory disturbance rises with age, and within every decade also with
      worsening LUTS.
       ◦ Correlation does not prove causation. A shared mechanism, most obviously ischaemia of genital and
         lower urinary tract organs, is plausible.

Hypertension
 • Sympathetic α-adrenergic pathways are common to hypertension and LUTS, but both rise with age, so
   causality is hard to prove.
 • Most large studies found no association; a small, flawed clinic study suggested one.
 • Autonomic hyperactivity is implicated in both LUTS and ED, without conclusive clinical data.
 • EpiLUTS: heart disease and hypertension were associated with more severe symptom constellations.

Depression
 • Depression is strongly associated with LUTS. In NHANES, men with moderate depression had the highest
   age-adjusted odds of clinical LUTS. The association is confirmed by others, and in women.
 • Proposed link: bladder inflammation and central neuroinflammation.
 • Post-finasteride syndrome: depression, other mood disorders and even suicidal tendencies have been
   linked to finasteride use for LUTS/BPH.

Smoking
 • Nicotine raises testosterone and oestrogen, which would predict more BPH; surgery is a poor end point
   because physicians avoid operating on heavy smokers.
 • Findings are inconsistent:
     ◦ No correlation with prostatectomy or diagnosis in some studies; an inverse association with
       prostatectomy and smaller glands in smokers in others.
     ◦ Olmsted County: a biphasic relation (light-to-moderate smokers less likely to have moderate-to-severe
       LUTS; heavy smokers at least the same risk as never-smokers). Later: smoking associated with lower
       flow and more symptoms, not with prostate volume or PSA, and not with urinary retention.

     ◦ EpiLUTS: ex-smokers had higher, never-smokers lower, risk of severe symptom clusters; current
       smoking was not a factor.
     ◦ BACH, a large meta-analysis and the REDUCE trial (Reduction by Dutasteride of Prostate Cancer
       Events) found no relationship.
 • Bottom line: any association is weak and of limited clinical significance.

Physical Activity, Diet, Obesity, Body Mass Index, and the Metabolic Syndrome
 • Diet: no single dietary factor has a convincing major role. Isolated associations have been reported with
   beef, milk and low vegetable intake. A higher dietary inflammatory index was associated with higher
   odds of clinical LUTS in NHANES.
 • Metabolic syndrome: obesity, glucose intolerance, dyslipidaemia and hypertension, raising cardiovascular
   risk and arising mainly from physical inactivity and westernised diet.
 • Biological plausibility: adipose tissue is the main site of aromatisation of testosterone to oestrogen, and
   leaner men have higher testosterone.
 • Findings on body mass index (BMI) conflict.
     ◦ BMI was inversely associated with surgically treated or diagnosed BPH in two large cohorts.
     ◦ Prostate weight and adenoma size rose with obesity (with rising oestradiol) in others, but symptom
       severity did not.
     ◦ Two biases: DRE under-detects enlargement in the very obese, and obese men may be steered away
       from surgery.
 • Abdominal obesity is the relevant measure. In the Health Professionals Follow-up Study, waist
   circumference predicted prostatectomy and frequent urinary symptoms independently of BMI; BMI, hip
   circumference and waist:hip ratio did not.
 • Hyperinsulinaemia hypothesis: non-insulin-dependent diabetes, hypertension, height, obesity, high
   insulin and low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol were risk factors for BPH in a LUTS cohort. The
   proposed mechanism is insulin-driven sympathetic overactivity.
 • Physical activity is associated with a lower risk of BPH and LUTS in pooled analysis of observational
   studies, at light, moderate and vigorous levels alike, and in EpiLUTS.
 • But a Cochrane review of randomised trials found very low-quality evidence; whether exercise actually
   improves LUTS scores remains uncertain.
 • Current position: inactivity, obesity, BMI and other metabolic syndrome measures are positively
   associated with LUTS and BPH (including volume). The benefit of increasing activity is less clear.

Medications
 • Evidence is very limited.
 • Cold remedies containing α-sympathomimetics worsen LUTS by stimulating bladder outlet smooth

 • Olmsted County: daily antidepressants, antihistamines or bronchodilators add 2–3 IPSS points; daily

Correlations Between Parameters
 • All parameters worsen with age: symptoms, bother, interference, HRQoL, Qmax and volume.
 • Correlations between them, and with pressure–flow findings, are generally weak.
 • The exception is the subjective measures: symptom score, bother, disease-specific HRQoL and
   interference correlate strongly with one another (Figure 149.17, which shows worse age-adjusted bother,
   interference, general health and sexual satisfaction with higher IPSS in the United States, Scotland, France
   and Japan).
Why correlations are weak.
 • Weak numerical correlations can arise artefactually, whether or not a physiological relationship exists.
 • Variability: within-individual, day-to-day, measurement error and the natural history itself.
 • Restriction of range: correlations emerge most easily across the full spectrum of values. Entry thresholds
   in BPH trials truncate the range and suppress correlation.
     ◦ Figure 149.18: in unselected volunteers, symptom score and Qmax correlate clearly. Restricted to
       typical trial entrants (score >10, Qmax 5–15 mL/s), the correlation virtually disappears and the
       regression line is flat.
 • Weak numerical correlation does not preclude a strong clinical relationship.

Natural History of Untreated Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia
 • Natural history is the prognosis of a disease over time: the change in parameters of interest and the
   incidence of significant outcomes.
 • It is the baseline against which every treatment is judged. The benefits and risks of intervention must be
   balanced against the risks of simply watching.
 • How much natural history must be known depends on the seriousness of the disease and the risk of
   treatment. The analogy is abdominal aortic aneurysm, where prognosis by size governs the decision to
   accept operative risk.

Regression to the mean.
 • Mechanism: entry thresholds select men with higher scores and lower flows. The same thresholds are not
   applied at follow-up, so values drift back toward the mean. This falsely suggests improvement.
 • Demonstration: untreated volunteers completed the IPSS and a flow test twice within a month.
    ◦ Unselected: individual values moved in both directions, but the means did not change.
     ◦ Selected by trial-type thresholds (IPSS above 7, 10 or 15; Qmax below 15, 12 or 10 mL/s): the second
       test showed significant "improvement" in both score and flow.

Concept — Untreated BPH progresses slowly and unevenly: symptoms fluctuate and often remit, while

Complications of Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia
 • Most complications are rare, and knowledge comes mainly from case series of men presenting with
   them rather than from cohorts observed for their development.

Mortality
 • BPH mortality has collapsed. In 1950–1954, most reporting countries had rates >10 per 100,000; by
   1985–1989 only one of 61 did. Applying 1950 rates to 1990 implies many thousands of deaths avoided in
   the United States alone.
 • LUTS type predicts mortality in a long-term cohort: daytime frequency and nocturia (especially when
   bothersome) were associated with increased mortality; voiding LUTS with decreased mortality.

Bladder Stones
 • Autopsy: bladder stones are about eight times commoner with histological BPH, with no increase in
   ureteric or renal stones.
 • Clinically the risk is small: one stone in the VA watchful waiting arm over 3 years.
 • Screen only when clinically indicated, for example haematuria or stuttering of the stream.

Bladder Decompensation
 • The classic sequence: normal mucosa → trabeculation → cellules → diverticula → detrusor failure.
 • When it starts, whether it is due to obstruction, and when intervention prevents it are all unknown.
 • Trabeculated, obstructed bladders show dense connective tissue, as in experimental obstruction; but
   bladder fibrosis also occurs with age in both sexes.
 • Is there a window for cure? There is no direct evidence that delay causes irreversible loss. The indirect
   evidence is the lesser benefit of delayed TURP in the VA trial.
 • High-quality data are lacking; randomised comparisons of early versus deferred intervention are needed.

Urinary Incontinence
 • Incontinence is among the most feared complications of BPH surgery.
 • BPH causes it in two ways:
    ◦ Overflow incontinence from bladder overdistension.
     ◦ Urge incontinence from detrusor instability, which affects up to half or more of obstructed men.
 • It is also age-related, and commoner in women than men over 50 in the community.
 • Rates in BPH studies are low: equal in both arms of the VA trial; 0.3 per 100 patient-years (socially
   unacceptable incontinence) in MTOPS.

Urinary Tract Infections
 • UTI was the main surgical indication in about one-eighth of older surgical series.
 • The link between residual urine and UTI is intuitive but unproven.
 • Best data: MTOPS placebo arm, 0.1 UTI per 100 patient-years.

Upper Urinary Tract Deterioration and Azotemia
 • Older series: a mean of about one in seven men presenting for TURP had renal failure (range very wide),
   and about one in thirteen of those imaged had hydronephrosis, one-third of whom had renal
   insufficiency.
 • Renal failure raises TURP risk: higher complication rate and mortality up to six times higher.
 • "Silent prostatism" (silent obstruction): asymptomatic men who develop renal failure from BOO. Rare
   but important.
 • Modern cohorts:
    ◦ VA trial: azotaemia (creatinine doubling) in very few men in either arm.
     ◦ MTOPS: not one case of renal insufficiency due to BPH in more than 3000 men over 4 years.
     ◦ Caveat: trial screening may have excluded higher-risk men.
 • No cohort or population study has reported renal failure clearly attributable to BPH, although self-
   reported episodes occur in cross-sectional surveys.

Hematuria
 • Gross haematuria and clots can arise from BPH alone.
 • Men prone to it have higher prostatic microvessel density.
 • Finasteride is a reasonable first-line therapy, apparently by modulating VEGF expression.
 • No precise incidence data exist; management depends on circumstances.

Acute Urinary Retention
 • AUR is one of the most significant outcomes of BPH.
    ◦ It was historically an immediate indication for surgery (about a quarter to a third of TURP indications
      in older series), and most men who fail catheter removal still have surgery.
     ◦ It is painful, costly and time-consuming: inability to void → emergency attendance → catheter →
       follow-up → trial of removal → recovery or surgery.
     ◦ Older literature: recurrence in 56–64% within a week of a first episode, and 76–83% with diagnosed
       BPH.
 • Aetiology is poorly understood: obstructive, myogenic and neurogenic mechanisms.
    ◦ Proposed precipitants: prostatic infection, bladder overdistension, excessive fluid, alcohol, sexual
      activity, debility and bed rest.

Concept — The serious complications of BPH (renal failure, stones, infection, death) have become rare. What
remains common is AUR and surgery, and both are predicted by the same factors: age, symptoms, low flow
and, above all, a large gland as reflected by PSA. AUR is the hard end point; surgery is soft and varies with
practice.

 KEY POINTS — EPIDEMIOLOGY AND NATURAL HISTORY
 - **Autopsy prevalence of BPH rises linearly from age 40** and is **remarkably similar across cultures
 and ethnicities**. - **LUTS severity, frequency, bother and QoL impairment rise with age**,
 fundamentally alike in all societies studied. - **Analytical studies have failed to identify significant risk
 factors** for BPH and LUTS. - **Natural history varies widely between individuals**, measured chiefly by
 **prostate volume growth**. - **Variability also affects** flow, residual urine, complications, **retention
 and the need for surgery**. - **Certain baseline factors allow assessment of risk** of volume growth,
 progression and complications.
