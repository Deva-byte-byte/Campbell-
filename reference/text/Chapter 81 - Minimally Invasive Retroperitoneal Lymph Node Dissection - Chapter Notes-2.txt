Chapter 81 — Minimally Invasive Retroperitoneal Lymph
Node Dissection
 • Retroperitoneal lymph node dissection (RPLND) is a central component of treatment for testicular germ
   cell tumours (TGCTs).
 • Treatment goals in early-stage TGCT (stage I and low-volume stage II): maintain excellent oncological
   outcomes, minimise dual therapy (surgery plus chemotherapy), and minimise long-term treatment-
   related harm.
 • Guideline-directed indications, chemotherapy-naive: serum tumour marker-negative patients with stage
   I nonseminomatous germ cell tumour (NSGCT), and small-volume stage II seminoma and NSGCT.
 • RPLND is both diagnostic and therapeutic in these settings:
     ◦ Accurate staging and cure of occult metastases in clinical stage I disease.
     ◦ Cure of modest-volume retroperitoneal metastases in clinical stage II disease.
     ◦ Avoidance of systemic therapy in the 20–30% of patients with enlarged retroperitoneal nodes who
       prove pN0 at RPLND.
 • Postchemotherapy indications (after appropriate risk-directed chemotherapy): NSGCT with residual
   masses >1 cm, and select seminomatous germ cell tumours (GCTs).
 • Why toxicity reduction drives the field: once platinum-based chemotherapy made metastatic GCT
   curable, awareness of its long-term toxicities grew, prompting efforts to reduce treatment burden and
   protect quality of life:
     ◦ Fewer chemotherapy cycles, or omission of bleomycin, in International Germ Cell Cancer
       Collaborative Group (IGCCCG) good-risk disease.
     ◦ Elimination of routine suprahilar dissection from RPLND.
     ◦ Nerve-sparing technique to preserve ejaculatory function.
     ◦ Templates to reduce the morbidity of RPLND.
     ◦ Minimally invasive RPLND (MI-RPLND) — the newest of these endeavours.
 • Purely laparoscopic RPLND is technically demanding — reserved for centres with high-volume
   laparoscopic surgeons and high GCT volume. Robotic surgery reduced some of the technical demand,
   and robotic experience is growing rapidly.

 KEY POINTS
   • MI-RPLND aims to minimise treatment-related toxicity and its impact on quality of life in GCT.
   • Purely laparoscopic RPLND is a technically demanding operation.
   • Dissemination of robotic platforms has renewed interest in MI-RPLND.

Concept — Because TGCT patients are young and almost always cured, the field is judged on cure preserved
at the lowest lifetime toxicity; MI-RPLND is one more de-escalation measure and must be weighed against
that standard, not against convenience.

RETROPERITONEAL LYMPH NODE DISSECTION: AN ENDURING OPERATION WITH AN
EVOLVING APPROACH
Open era
 • Classic access: midline xiphoid-to-pubis incision; thoracoabdominal incision for suprahilar or retrocrural
   disease. Excellent exposure, but prolonged convalescence.
 • Historical perioperative care compounded morbidity: full bowel preparation and prolonged fasting →
   excessive intravenous fluids and non-contemporary anaesthetics → routine nasogastric tubes, opioid-only
   analgesia, limited mobilisation and neglected chest physiotherapy.
 • Morbidity was accepted as the price of excellent long-term outcomes in young survivors; adequate
   exposure remains critical.
Approach-agnostic advances
 • Prehabilitation, optimised anaesthetic protocols, extended-release non-opioid analgesia and Enhanced
   Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols improved convalescence after RPLND, as after radical
   cystectomy, prostatectomy and radical nephrectomy with inferior vena cava (IVC) thrombectomy.
 • Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) was attractive for its reported lower blood loss, better convalescence,
   better cosmesis and faster return to normal activity.
Laparoscopic era
 • The multiquadrant, technically demanding nature of RPLND delayed laparoscopic adoption; expert
   laparoscopic GCT centres pursued it nonetheless.
 • First laparoscopic RPLND: 1992 — an extended modified template case report, with minimal blood loss
   and a disease-free state at early follow-up.
 • Subsequent series versus open RPLND: shorter hospital stay and less blood loss, but longer operative
   time; acceptable node yield, short- and intermediate-term oncological outcomes and complication
   profiles.
 • Why the evidence stayed weak:
     ◦ Rarity of the indication and few centres willing to operate laparoscopically.
     ◦ Early series were mostly stage I patients, many of whom may have been cured by orchidectomy
       alone.
     ◦ Steep learning curve limited adoption.
     ◦ Adjuvant chemotherapy after limited nodal involvement confounded judgement of oncological
       efficacy.
Robotic era
 • Robotic platforms spread rapidly through urology and training.

CONTROVERSY SURROUNDING MINIMALLY INVASIVE RETROPERITONEAL LYMPH
NODE DISSECTION
 • For radical and partial nephrectomy, radical cystectomy and radical prostatectomy, high-quality evidence
   shows MIS is noninferior to open surgery with better convalescence. No such evidence exists for MI-
   RPLND, owing to technical difficulty, disease rarity, an uncommon indication, and concerns raised by high-
   volume centres of excellence.
 • MI-RPLND is therefore the most controversial operation in urological oncology.

 • What the table is for: the MI-RPLND evidence base — entirely case series, no comparator arm.
 • Pattern: short stay and modest blood loss throughout; node yields rise in the recent robotic series
   toward the open benchmark (≈30); adjuvant chemotherapy falls from the laparoscopic to the recent
   robotic era; follow-up is short, and shorter in robotic series.
 • The trap: low recurrence rates look reassuring, but are confounded by high pN0 rates and adjuvant
   chemotherapy — they do not prove MI-RPLND is oncologically equivalent to open RPLND.
Concept — Every criticism of MI-RPLND reduces to one fear: that the approach changes the operation — a
lesser dissection, the wrong patient, or an unsafe setting — and so trades the single-modality cure that
justifies RPLND for a smaller scar.

RATIONALE FOR MINIMALLY INVASIVE RETROPERITONEAL LYMPH NODE
DISSECTION
 • Reported benefits: less blood loss, better convalescence, better cosmesis, quicker return to normal
   activity, and consistently shorter hospital stay than open RPLND.
 • Theoretical benefit: fewer incisional hernias, extrapolated from other open-versus-MIS comparisons.
 • Counter-argument: the benefits may be only incremental. Without level 1 evidence including patient-
   reported outcomes, it is unknown whether MI-RPLND confers no benefit, incremental benefit, major
   benefit or harm.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE
Unchanging principles
 • Indication decided by a multidisciplinary team.
 • Contemporary staging: axial imaging within ≈4 weeks of surgery; serum tumour markers within 1–2
   weeks.
 • Surgeon expert in retroperitoneal anatomy and common vascular and visceral variants.
Core operative steps — identical whatever the approach
 1. Safe abdominal entry → retroperitoneal exposure
 2. → complete removal of the appropriate nodes by split-and-roll
 3. → prospective identification and sparing of the ejaculatory nerves
 4. → division of lumbar vessels → spermatic cord removal → closure.
 • None of these steps may be compromised: MI-RPLND must be a complete duplication of open RPLND.
Extent of dissection
 • Completeness, template and nerve-sparing are not altered by approach; tumour laterality and
   intraoperative findings dictate extent.

Boundaries and completeness
 • Boundaries: renal hila superiorly, ureters laterally, crossing of the ureter over the common iliac vessels
   inferiorly, plus the spermatic cord.
 • Intentional division of lumbar arteries and veins is required to clear all retroaortic and retrocaval tissue.
 • Poor-quality RPLND or mass excision gives worse oncological outcomes and survival than complete
   RPLND.
 • GETUG T93BP: good-risk metastatic NSGCT randomised to bleomycin, etoposide and cisplatin (BEP) ×3
   versus etoposide and cisplatin (EP) ×4. Relapses (70%) and deaths (50%) were dominated by patients
   with no surgery or incomplete RPLND. This supports complete RPLND, not split-and-roll or lumbar
   division specifically.
 • Meticulous lymphostasis prevents chylous ascites.
Nerve-sparing
 • Performed when feasible without compromising oncological efficacy.
 • Spares the sympathetic chains and postganglionic sympathetic fibres from L1–L4, identified from both
   chains and preserved to their coalescence in the hypogastric plexus beneath the inferior mesenteric
   artery (IMA).
 • Typically three efferent fibres from each chain contribute to the hypogastric plexus.
Centre and surgeon
 • High-volume GCT centres get the indication, timing and sequence of chemotherapy and surgery right;
   surgical experience improves outcome.

 • Anatomical variation and postchemotherapy desmoplasia demand retroperitoneal and vascular skill. The
   MI-RPLND surgeon must be competent in GCT management, open RPLND and MI-RPLND.

 KEY POINTS
   • Completeness, template and nerve-sparing are not affected by approach (robotic versus open).
   • Tumour laterality and intraoperative findings dictate extent.
   • MI-RPLND should completely duplicate open RPLND.

Concept — Approach is a means of access, never a determinant of the operation; the oncological operation
— boundaries, lumbar division, retrovascular clearance, nerve-sparing — is fixed, and anything less is a
staging biopsy.

PERIOPERATIVE PREPARATION
 • Surgical–anaesthetic plan for major complications agreed in advance.
 • Prior bleomycin → review fluid requirements and oxygenation and ventilation.
 • Group and save with crossmatch; large-bore intravenous access.
 • Open instrument sets in the theatre and available for conversion.
 • Vascular sutures prepared: 4-0 or 5-0 Prolene on an RB needle with a LAPRA-TY ≈4 inches from the
   needle.
 • HandPort systems may allow rapid abdominal access and pressure.
 • Experienced bedside assistant in complex MIS.
 • Continuous insufflation system helps if prolonged suction is needed for visibility.
 • Second time-out to confirm all emergency systems are in place.

PATIENT AND TUMOUR FACTORS
 • No absolute contraindications to MI-RPLND.
 • Relative contraindications (as for any MIS): extensive previous abdominal surgery, morbid obesity.

PATIENT COUNSELLING AND PREPARATION
 • Counsel explicitly on: bleeding, infection, anejaculation, visceral injury, simple or chylous ascites,
   hernia, and the laparoscopy- and pneumoperitoneum-specific risks.
 • Conversion is not a default complication — it may be done electively for safety or oncological reasons.
   However, there is a theoretical excess risk of major bleeding if rapid conversion and control fail.
 • High-fat diet the day before surgery may help identify lymphatics.
 • Full bowel preparation is generally unnecessary; moderate preparation may limit gaseous distension.
 • ERAS pathway: preoperative carbohydrate loading, hydration, non-narcotic analgesia.

 TIPS, TRICKS AND TOOLS
   • Robotic fourth arm: lifts kidney and Gerota’s fascia for paracaval and paraaortic dissection, then
     retracts bowel medially for interaortocaval dissection.
   • Lumbar vessels: Weck clips on the great-vessel side, vessel sealer on the specimen side.
   • Robotic clip appliers place clips parallel to the great vessels.


 KEY POINTS
   • Discuss the common and serious adverse events of RPLND.
   • Bleeding is rare but potentially catastrophic and may require conversion.
   • Surgical and anaesthetic teams agree plans for major complications.

APPROACH TO RIGHT-SIDED TEMPLATE DISSECTION
Set-up
 • Right side up (left modified lateral decubitus, or supine with left tilt). 30° lens in the second-most cranial
   port; scissors in the cranial-most port; bipolar in the left hand; inferior-most port retracts bowel
   medially or kidney laterally.
 • Liver retractor helpful. Retraction: laparoscopic paddle; robotic fourth arm with an atraumatic retractor
   (Small Graptor, ProGrasp or Cadiere forceps).
Sequence
 1. Incise the white line of Toldt from caecum around the hepatic flexure to the foramen of Winslow,
    dividing the root of the small-bowel mesentery → Kocherise the duodenum.
 2. Separate ureter from spermatic cord; formal ureterolysis may be needed postchemotherapy. Fourth
    arm lifts ureter, kidney and Gerota’s fascia for paracaval work, then retracts bowel medially for
    interaortocaval and paraaortic work.


                                                          page 8 of 12
Chapter 81 — Minimally Invasive RPLND                                            Approach to Left-Sided Template Dissection
 3. Gonadal vein clipped and divided at its ostium, traced to the internal ring; the distal cord stump is
    usually removed at the end, after moving ports one slot inferiorly.
 4. Split over the right common iliac artery at the ureteric crossing, caudal → cranial to the IVC (Fig. 81.5) →
    continue up the IVC to the renal hilum. Clip retropancreatic and retroduodenal lymphatics at the left
    renal vein.
 5. Roll the paracaval packet laterally → sympathetic chain found lateral to the lumbar vessels; preserve
    L1–L4 postganglionic fibres → divide lumbar vessels (Weck clips on the great-vessel side) → free the
    packet from the psoas fascia up to the right renal hilum.
 6. Know right renal artery–vein relationships from imaging; preserve accessory lower-pole arteries
    anterior to the IVC.
 7. Extend the split to the left renal vein → roll medially off IVC and aorta (interaortocaval packet) → divide
    medial lumbar vessels → lift the packet off the anterior spinous ligament from the bifurcation to the
    renal hilum. Do not ligate the right renal artery in the interaortocaval space, especially when it lies at the
    lower edge of the left renal vein.
 8. Paraaortic (above the IMA): roll laterally → preserve sympathetic fibres → retract ureter laterally →
    ligate lumbar vessels → free from psoas to the left renal vessels.
 9. Closure: haemostatic and lymphostatic agents → complete cord dissection → reduce
    pneumoperitoneum to check haemostasis and lymphostasis → return colon → remove ports under
    vision → close fascia at the extraction site and any port ≥10 mm → no routine drain.
 • Tumour-spill precautions: bag each packet (Endo Catch) as soon as it is freed; handle suspicious nodes
   delicately to avoid rupture or aerosolisation of tumour cells.

APPROACH TO LEFT-SIDED TEMPLATE DISSECTION
 • Left side up (right modified lateral decubitus, or supine with right tilt); no liver retractor usually.
 1. Medialise the descending colon, and also the spleen and tail of pancreas, for medial rotation of the
    mesocolon.
 2. Separate ureter from gonadal vein; divide the gonadal vein at the left renal vein; trace and remove it to
    the internal ring.
 3. Start as for a renal operation: dissect the hilum as the superior limit; control the lumbar vein draining
    into the left renal vein; clip the major lymphatics around the left renal vein, which is rich in lymphatics
    from the superior mesenteric artery trunk.
 4. Split from the ureteric crossing of the left common iliac artery cranially along the aorta to the left renal
    vein → roll laterally (paraaortic and common iliac packets) → divide lateral lumbar vessels → preserve
    sympathetic fibres → free from psoas fascia and diaphragmatic crus.
 5. Rotate the packet medially into the interaortocaval space → divide medial lumbar vessels (for a
    template, only above the IMA); the IMA may be ligated to aid mesocolic rotation → roll off the anterior
    spinous ligament.
 6. If the template extends, handle precaval, paracaval and retrocaval nodes as on the right.

BILATERAL TEMPLATES AND STEEP TRENDELENBURG APPROACH
 • Flank or tilt positions: complete the ipsilateral side → tilt the other way or reposition for the
   contralateral side.
 • Steep Trendelenburg is newer: unlike lateral approaches (extensions of laparoscopy), its novel positioning
   facilitates bilateral dissection.
 1. Supine, arms padded and tucked → steep Trendelenburg as for deep pelvic surgery, so bowel falls
    cranially.
 2. Pneumoperitoneum → camera 4–6 cm below the umbilicus → two robotic arms on the patient’s left,
    one on the right; scissors right hand, bipolar left, atraumatic bowel grasper in the fourth arm; assist port
    right or left by laterality.
 3. 0° lens first, then 30° down. Docking: da Vinci Si over the left shoulder; Xi from the side.
 4. Incise the posterior peritoneum from the ligament of Treitz around the caecum.
 5. Peritoneal hammock: cut peritoneal edges fixed to the anterior abdominal wall (straight needles or
    running self-locking sutures), holding up small bowel, duodenum and both colons.
 6. Right, left or bilateral template without repositioning.
Concept — Positioning is a trade between familiarity and reach: flank positions borrow renal-surgery habits
but confine the surgeon to one side, whereas steep Trendelenburg with a peritoneal hammock opens the
whole retroperitoneum at the cost of an unfamiliar view.

ADVERSE EVENT MITIGATION AND MANAGEMENT
 • Most feared: major vascular injury. Prevention: surgeon proficient in GCT management, open RPLND and
   MIS; vascular sutures, blood products and open sets confirmed; qualified bedside assistant.
Major bleeding — sequence
 1. Temporise with pressure (robotic instrument or assist port) plus increased pneumoperitoneum.
 2. → Prepare for conversion; notify anaesthesia; blood products; consultant urology or vascular help as
    needed.
 3. → If not controllable by MIS: keep holding pressure, undock, convert to open.
If managed minimally invasively
  • Blood on the camera → single-eye setting on the robot, or move the camera to another port.
 • Extra trocar for exposure, pressure and suction plus a second assist instrument; laparoscopic sponges and
   laparotomy pads.
 • Identify, understand and isolate the injury (avulsed lumbar, cavotomy, aortotomy). No blind suturing or
   clipping. Vascular sutures with LAPRA-TY speed repair.

Lymphostasis
 • Lower pneumoperitoneum at the end to reveal leaks → suture or clip. This may still miss leaks, because
   the lymphatic system is low-pressure.
 • Liberal clips around lymphatic-dense sites: left renal vein and the inferior mesenteric vein crossing
   anterior to it.
 • Bipolar and electrosealing devices may help (extrapolated from pelvic lymphadenectomy); sealants
   (Tisseel, Vista Seal) at completion.
Concept — Crisis management follows temporise → prepare → decide: pressure buys time, preparation
makes conversion controlled rather than desperate, and no manoeuvre is performed on an injury not yet
seen.

POSTOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT
 • Extubate and remove the gastric tube; in shorter cases remove the catheter before recovery.
 • Early ambulation, early diet, multimodal non-narcotic analgesia, ERAS.
 • Discharge typically within 1–2 days.

DATA AND OUTCOMES FOR MINIMALLY INVASIVE RETROPERITONEAL LYMPH NODE
DISSECTION
 • Evidence is limited to single- and multi-institutional case series (Table 81.1).

Safety
 • Complication profile broadly consistent with open surgery.
 • Brisk bleeding is the complication unique to MI-RPLND → conversion; conversion ≈ 5%.
 • Antegrade ejaculation preserved in ≥90% after primary MI-RPLND.
 • Chylous ascites rates commensurate with open RPLND.
 • Postchemotherapy safety data: small series only — no meaningful conclusions.

Efficacy
 • Chemotherapy-naive RPLND must be diagnostic and therapeutic.
 • Benchmark: recurrence-free survival ≈85% for limited pathological node-positive disease without
   adjuvant chemotherapy. Large contemporary high-volume series show ≈80% regardless of pN1, pN2 or
   pN3, which questions reflex adjuvant chemotherapy for pN2–pN3.
 • Recurring criticisms: (1) unindicated surgery in stage IA (arguably IB); (2) diagnostic-only intent with low
   node yields; (3) very high adjuvant chemotherapy use.
 • Consistent themes across series: follow-up <5 years; node yields variable but approaching the open
   benchmark of ≈30; node-positive rates lower than expected for stage IB–II; adjuvant chemotherapy too
   frequent; recurrence acceptable, probably because of high pN0 rates and adjuvant chemotherapy.
 • Postchemotherapy efficacy data: small series only — no meaningful conclusions.

 KEY POINTS
   • No randomised data compare perioperative outcomes or long-term efficacy of open versus robotic
     RPLND.
   • Node yields in recent series approach the open benchmark, though historical MI series were lower.

Concept — MI-RPLND’s outcome data are reassuring but uninterpretable: acceptable recurrence is bought

FUTURE DIRECTIONS
 • RPLND remains a mainstay in both chemotherapy-naive and postchemotherapy settings, and indications
   are expanding as patients and clinicians seek to avoid systemic chemotherapy and abdominal
   radiotherapy and their long-term harms.
 • Stage II seminoma: renewed interest in RPLND, with excellent complication profiles and acceptable
   oncological outcomes.
 • Circulating microRNA miR-371a-3p — a sensitive and specific biomarker that may:
     ◦ identify occult metastases in stage I patients who would benefit from RPLND;
     ◦ identify clinically small-volume nodes that are pN0, sparing RPLND;
     ◦ select patients for adjuvant chemotherapy after RPLND.
 • A randomised open-versus-robotic trial is unlikely (recruitment, community support) → rely on well-
   conducted prospective registries.
 • Three conditions for MI-RPLND: (1) multidisciplinary decision; (2) MIS duplicates open surgery in extent,
   nerve-sparing and indication; (3) surgeon expert in GCT management, open RPLND and MIS.
Concept — The future of RPLND lies in better selection rather than better access: biomarkers such as
miR-371a-3p promise to operate only on those who harbour disease, which is precisely the weakness MI-
RPLND series have exposed.
