 Chapter 99 — Prevention of Urinary Stone Disease

 INTRODUCTION
 Stone disease as a chronic condition
  • From the prevention perspective, urinary stone disease (USD) is best conceptualised as a chronic
    metabolic condition punctuated by periodic stone attacks, rather than as a series of isolated colic
    episodes.
   • It is characterised by high recurrence, association with predisposing chronic conditions (diabetes,
     obesity, gout) and identifiable metabolic disturbances such as hypocitraturia.
   • Preventive practice follows the American Urological Association (AUA) and European Association of
     Urology (EAU) guidelines.
 Recurrence and its prediction
  • Recurrence risk is up to 50% within 5 years, but varies greatly between patients.
   • The Recurrence of Kidney Stone (ROKS) nomogram predicts the risk of a second stone from
     demographic and clinical factors; 10-year risk ranges from about 12% in the lowest to 56% in the
     highest risk stratum.
   • ROKS 2 predicts multiple recurrent symptomatic episodes (highest-risk group: four or more). Risk
     factors: younger age, male sex, higher body mass index (BMI), family history, pregnancy, an
     asymptomatic or suspected stone before the first episode, a history of brushite, struvite or uric acid
     stone, and radiographic factors.
   • Where urine chemistry was available, low urine volume and low citrate predicted recurrence;
     predicted 5-year recurrence spans almost the full range (about 1% to 94%), allowing personalised
     counselling.
 Link with the metabolic syndrome
   • National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data show strong associations between
     obesity, diabetes, gout and lifetime stone risk.
   • Gout lowers urine pH, favouring uric acid stones; diabetes raises urinary calcium and also lowers urine
     pH.
 Prevalence, incidence and sex distribution
  • Prevalence is rising rapidly: about 1 in 11 people in the United States form a stone in their lifetime,
    nearly double the figure of 15 years earlier; recent 12-month incidence exceeds historical estimates.
   • Historically male-predominant, the disease is now close to equal between the sexes (male:female
     about 1.2–1.45:1), because of a disproportionate rise in female (especially younger female) stone
     formation, not a fall in male stone formation.

 MORBIDITY
   • The burden comprises severe colic, emergency care, lost work and life activity, and for many, surgery;
     about one-third of employees treated for stones miss work.
   • First-time stone formers carry about a 50% risk of recurrence within the subsequent 10 years.

 QUALITY-OF-LIFE IMPACT
   • Recurrent stone formers are especially affected, through chronic or episodic pain, repeated clinic visits
     and interventions, and side effects of preventive drugs or diets.
   • On the Short Form Health Survey (SF-36), health-related quality of life (QoL) is below the population
     mean, and worse in females than males.
   • The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS), supported by the
     National Institutes of Health, shows worse pain intensity, pain interference and physical function than
     the general population across phases of a stone episode.
   • A validated disease-specific QoL instrument has shown worse QoL in cystine than non-cystine formers,
     and in patients with asymptomatic stones than in those with no stones.

   KEY POINTS
     • The prevalence and incidence of USD are increasing.
     • Prevalence is increasing rapidly in females and non-White populations.
     • USD is a chronic metabolic condition, with recurrent events in up to 50% of affected individuals
       within 5 to 10 years.
     • USD negatively affects functioning and QoL.

 Concept — stone disease is a rising, recurrent, systemic metabolic disorder allied to the metabolic
 syndrome; the colic is the symptom, recurrence is the disease, and prevention is therefore the core long-
 term task.

 SCREENING EVALUATION FOR NEW STONE FORMERS (BOX 99.1)
   • AUA and EAU recommend a screening evaluation for every first-time stone former, to identify those at
     high risk of recurrence from routine history, blood tests, urinalysis and stone analysis.
   • History: comorbidities, medications and family history. The EAU asks for a holistic assessment — the
     likelihood of recurrence and also its consequences for that individual (e.g., chronic kidney disease,
     mineral bone disorder). Eliciting gastrointestinal (GI) disease and sarcoidosis is critical. Diet is
     reviewed: calcium, sodium, fluid, protein, fruit and vegetables, oxalate-rich foods and supplements
     such as vitamin C.
   • Children who form stones are generally high risk and should have metabolic evaluation.
   • Blood tests look for an underlying cause — for example, hypercalcaemia suggesting
     hyperparathyroidism.
   • Stone analysis points to the cause: uric acid suggests a metabolic condition, cystine a genetic one, and
     struvite in recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI) confirms infection as the cause and recurrence risk.
   • Imaging quantifies stone burden, which predicts future symptomatic events.

 GENERAL RECOMMENDATIONS FOR STONE PREVENTION IN NEW STONE
 FORMERS
   • Dietary and lifestyle advice in both guidelines — particularly important for low-risk patients who will
     not undergo metabolic evaluation — covers fluid, protein, fruit and vegetables, sodium, calcium and
     oxalate.
 Fluid
  • Low urine volume is perhaps the most important single risk factor; higher fluid intake prevents
    recurrence of all stone types, probably by lowering urinary supersaturation.
   • Borghi et al. (1996), the landmark randomised controlled trial (RCT) in idiopathic calcium oxalate
     formers: fluid sufficient to keep urine volume above 2 L/day reduced 5-year recurrence (12.1% vs
     27%).
   • The Health Professionals Follow-Up Study (HPFS) and Nurses' Health Study (NHS) I cohorts confirm
     lower stone incidence with fluid intake above 2.5 L/day.
   • Guideline target: fluid sufficient for urine output of at least 2.5 L/day; in practice about 100 oz (about
     3 L) daily. There is no strong evidence for targets tailored to body habitus or sex.
 Adherence
  • Adherence is poor: counselled patients raise urine volume only modestly, and only about half reach 2.5
    L/day at 6 months.
   • Mobile health technology (applications, fitness trackers, social media) supports behaviour change
     through goal setting, self-monitoring and feedback; short-term smart water bottle studies suggest
     increased intake, with longer trials in progress.
 Animal protein
  • Nondairy animal protein (meat, fish, poultry, eggs) raises urinary calcium through bone resorption and
    reduced tubular calcium reabsorption.
   • Its purine and acid load reduces tubular citrate reabsorption, causing hypocitraturia, and raises urinary
     uric acid, causing hyperuricosuria. Its effect on urinary oxalate is conflicting.

   • The harm is greatest when a high animal-protein diet is combined with low calcium intake. Short-term
     protein restriction in hypercalciuric formers improves urinary calcium, uric acid, oxalate and citrate.
   • Clinical-trial support is thin: both guidelines endorse limiting animal protein — especially in uric acid
     formers and calcium oxalate formers with high urinary uric acid — on expert opinion. A 4-year RCT of
     low-animal-protein or high-fibre diets showed no reduction in urinary calcium or recurrence.
   • Borghi et al. (2002), landmark RCT in hypercalciuric formers: a low-protein, low-salt, normal-calcium
     diet halved stone events compared with a low-calcium diet, although the three components cannot be
     separated.
 Sodium, calcium, fruit and vegetables
  • The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet is associated with lower stone risk; low
    sodium is recommended because sodium raises calcium excretion and lowers urinary citrate.
   • Calcium restriction increases stone risk; a diet high in fruit and vegetables improves urine parameters.
   • These recommendations are guideline-endorsed but need stronger randomised evidence.

   KEY POINTS
     • Fluid intake to maintain urine output of 2.5 L/day is the cornerstone of dietary prevention of urinary
       stone recurrence.
     • A diet low in animal protein and high in fruits and vegetables is recommended by current guidelines,
       although randomised trial evidence is limited.
     • Guidelines also support low sodium and moderate calcium intake, along with consideration of a low-
       oxalate diet.

 Concept — dilution is universal and evidence-based; the dietary levers (less animal protein and salt,
 normal calcium, more fruit and vegetables) act by lowering calcium and uric acid and raising citrate —
 mostly on physiological and expert-opinion grounds.

 METABOLIC EVALUATION FOR HIGH-RISK AND RECURRENT STONE FORMERS
   • On expert opinion, metabolic testing is one or two 24-hour urine collections on a random diet,
     analysed at minimum for volume, pH, calcium, oxalate, uric acid, citrate, sodium, potassium and
     creatinine.
   • One versus two collections is unresolved: one retrospective comparison found two random-diet
     collections similar enough that one suffices; a larger series found clinically relevant differences between
     paired collections in most comparisons, favouring two.
   • Fast and calcium-load testing to subclassify hypercalciuria is discouraged, because it does not change
     management.
   • Real-world uptake is low — few eligible patients have a 24-hour urine, and few on drug therapy
     complete the recommended follow-up tests; yet claims data show that targeted drug therapy matched
     to 24-hour urine findings significantly reduces symptomatic stone events.

   KEY POINTS
     • High-risk stone formers include those with GI disease, obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes
       mellitus, primary hyperparathyroidism, renal tubular acidosis, sarcoidosis, gout, and family history
       of nephrolithiasis.

     • High-risk individuals should be offered metabolic evaluation, including at least one properly

 Concept — the 24-hour urine converts non-specific advice into targeted therapy, and targeted therapy
 works — yet it is badly underused in practice.

 PHARMACOLOGIC PREVENTION STRATEGIES

 Hypercalciuria
 Thiazides and Thiazide-Like Diuretics
   • Thiazides are the preferred drug for hypercalciuria and among the best studied in stone prevention,
     effective in both specific and unselected groups of stone formers.
   • The AUA recommends them for hypercalciuria with recurrent calcium stones, and for recurrent
     calcium stones despite a normal metabolic evaluation.
   • First used by Yendt (1966) for undifferentiated hypercalciuria. Indapamide is a thiazide-like diuretic,
     also effective.
   • Mechanism: direct stimulation of distal nephron calcium reabsorption while promoting sodium
     excretion; with long-term use, volume contraction adds proximal tubular reabsorption of sodium and
     calcium.
   • Ettinger et al. (1988), double-blind RCT: chlorthalidone reduced predicted stone formation by about
     90% versus placebo or magnesium hydroxide. A second double-blind RCT with hydrochlorothiazide and
     a Cochrane review both confirm protection against recurrence.
 Potassium and electrolytes
  • Effects on urinary magnesium and zinc are inconsistent.
   • Chain of harm: potassium loss → hypokalaemia with intracellular acidosis → hypocitraturia. Hence
     potassium is supplemented as potassium citrate or potassium chloride; adding potassium citrate
     prevents hypokalaemia and hypochloraemic metabolic acidosis during long-term therapy.
   • Check serum potassium 1–2 weeks after starting or changing the dose; take particular care with
     known potassium deficiency, digitalis therapy or developing hypocitraturia.
 Potassium-sparing combinations
  • Amiloride plus thiazide (Moduretic) may lower calcium excretion more than thiazide alone but does
    not raise citrate; because amiloride spares potassium, potassium supplements should not be given.
   • Triamterene is used with caution because of triamterene stones.
 Bone
  • Thiazides protect against osteopenia: combined with potassium citrate and diet, stone formation fell
    and bone mineral density rose; a Cochrane review found fewer hip fractures on thiazides.
 Side effects
  • Usually mild at low dose and improve with time, yet about one in six stop therapy (Antihypertensive
    and Lipid-Lowering Treatment to Prevent Heart Attack Trial [ALLHAT]).
   • Thirst, polyuria, cramps and GI upset; thiazides may precipitate gout or diabetes by raising serum uric
     acid and glucose; a small percentage develop reduced libido or sexual dysfunction.

 Hyperoxaluria
   • One of the commoner abnormalities on metabolic evaluation. There is no threshold: stone risk rises
     continuously with urinary oxalate, even within the "normal" range.
   • Oxalate comes from diet and metabolism and has no known function in humans; susceptibility to
     dietary oxalate absorption varies, and heritable sensitivity is debated (Chapter 95).

   • Deficiency of Oxalobacter formigenes in the gut flora is increasingly implicated in calcium oxalate
     stones; it may be iatrogenic, as the organism is sensitive to many common antibiotics. Recolonisation is
     under study.

 Enteric Hyperoxaluria
   • Urinary oxalate above 40 mg/day arising from GI malabsorption and diarrhoea; often well over 60–70
     mg/day, with urinary calcium often well under 100 mg/day.
   • Mechanism: malabsorbed fat saponifies dietary calcium, leaving oxalate free for absorption; stone risk
     is added by dehydration, hypokalaemia, bicarbonate loss and hypocitraturia.
   • Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), a malabsorptive procedure, is an increasingly common cause: urinary
     oxalate rises and urinary citrate and volume fall, and stone risk exceeds that of matched obese controls.
     Restrictive procedures (band, sleeve) add less direct risk, mainly through dehydration.

 Primary Hyperoxaluria
   • Primary hyperoxaluria (PH) is an extremely rare inborn error of glyoxylate metabolism with three
     genetic types (see table).
   • Presents in childhood with early stones, oxalosis (tissue deposition) and frequently renal failure from
     nephrocalcinosis; untreated, death may occur before age 20. Urinary oxalate is often above 100 mg/
     day, with high serum oxalate. Subtypes overlap clinically.

 DIETARY MANAGEMENT FOR HYPEROXALURIA
   • Although oxalate cannot be avoided entirely, recognising and avoiding high-oxalate foods is a
     cornerstone of management.
   • Diet contributes a variable share of urinary oxalate — a minority on a low-oxalate diet, rising
     substantially on a high-oxalate diet — and the dietary share rises when calcium intake falls.
   • Avoid large portions of spinach, beets, chocolate, nuts and tea.
   • General advice suits recurrent formers; a strict low-oxalate diet is most useful in enteric hyperoxaluria,
     bowel disease and after gastric bypass.

 MEDICAL THERAPY FOR HYPEROXALURIA
   • For idiopathic hyperoxaluria, oxalate avoidance and correction of underlying disease are the
     cornerstones; drugs are occasionally useful.

 Medical Therapy: Enteric Hyperoxaluria
   • No RCTs exist.
   • Calcium supplementation is the primary measure: up to 1 g/day in divided doses, taken with meals
     so that it binds oxalate in the gut. Calcium citrate also raises urinary citrate and pH through its alkali
     load. Very high fluid intake must accompany it, or calcium may raise overall stone risk.
   • Cholestyramine, a bile acid sequestrant, should reduce colonic irritability and oxalate hyperabsorption
     in principle and works in animals, but human results are disappointing.
   • Magnesium binds oxalate and destabilises calcium oxalate crystals; malabsorption causes
     hypomagnesuria and more free oxalate. Supplements (gluconate, oxide, hydroxide) correct it, but may
     worsen diarrhoea; optimal dosing is unknown, and it may complement potassium citrate.
   • Fluid above 2 L/day at minimum offsets GI losses; potassium citrate corrects hypokalaemia and
     acidosis — in liquid form when transit is rapid, as wax-matrix tablets are poorly absorbed.

 Medical Therapy: Primary Hyperoxaluria
   • Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) acts as a cofactor, increasing the expression, activity and peroxisomal import
     of the PH1 enzyme; with low-oxalate counselling it also lowered urinary oxalate in idiopathic
     hyperoxaluria without significant side effects.
   • Oral O. formigenes showed early promise, but longer studies with modified formulations failed to
     replicate it.
   • Lumasiran, approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for PH1, uses RNA
     interference against glycolate oxidase messenger RNA, reducing hepatic oxalate production.
   • ILLUMINATE-A (phase 3, PH1, 2:1 randomisation, subcutaneous): at 6 months plasma oxalate fell
     substantially versus placebo, and 84% on lumasiran versus 0% on placebo reached 24-hour urinary
     oxalate no more than 1.5 times the upper limit of normal. ILLUMINATE-B (children under 6 years):
     rapid, sustained fall in spot urinary oxalate:creatinine ratio.
 Concept — urinary oxalate risk is continuous; enteric disease is a gut-binding problem (restore luminal
 calcium–oxalate binding with calcium at meals), whereas primary hyperoxaluria is a hepatic
 overproduction problem (supply the cofactor or silence the upstream enzyme).

 HYPOCITRATURIA

 Pharmacologic Intervention for Hypocitraturia
   • Mechanism: oral citrate is metabolised in the liver to bicarbonate; the alkali load inhibits proximal
     tubular citrate reabsorption, raising urinary citrate.
   • Potassium citrate is standard in the United States (wax tablet, liquid, powder), 30–60 mEq/day divided
     or as a single evening dose. Sodium citrate is used more in Europe or when hyperkalaemia is a concern,
     but does not lower urinary calcium, probably because of its sodium load.
   • AUA: offer potassium citrate to recurrent calcium formers with low citrate, and — like thiazides — to
     recurrent calcium formers without any abnormality.
   • Evidence: long-term therapy durably raises urine pH and citrate within about 6 months and lowers
     stone formation. In RCTs, Ettinger et al. (1997) — potassium magnesium citrate, relative risk of a new
     stone about 0.16 at 3 years — and Barcelo et al. (1993) — potassium citrate, markedly fewer new
     stones at 3 years; a Cochrane review confirms a large reduction.
   • Side effects: GI upset is commonest; take with water after meals. The wax tablet shell seen in stool is
     normal and does not mean the drug was not absorbed. More trial dropouts occur for adverse events.
     Monitor potassium in renal insufficiency and avoid with potassium-sparing diuretics.
   • Urine pH: alkalinisation helps calcium oxalate formers (inhibitors work better in alkaline urine), but pH
     of 7 or above raises calcium phosphate stone risk. The calcium phosphate content of stones has risen
     over three decades with rising urine pH, and patients converting to calcium phosphate had received
     more potassium citrate.
   • Citrate in calcium phosphate formers is controversial (no RCTs): if used, repeat the 24-hour urine for
     citrate, pH and supersaturation. Patients whose pH rises without a citrate rise benefit less than those
     whose citrate rises without excessive alkalinity.

 DIETARY INTERVENTION FOR HYPOCITRATURIA
   • Citrus juices, chiefly lemonade and orange juice, supplement water to raise volume and citrate. Citrate
     is a weak organic acid that acts as a conjugate base when paired with sodium or potassium; absorbed
     citrate is almost entirely metabolised to bicarbonate.
   • Citrate inhibits calcium oxalate stones by forming soluble complexes with calcium and impairing
     crystal nucleation and growth.
   • Natural juices are richest in citrate and potassium: grapefruit highest, then lemon and orange (similar);
     Crystal Light is the richest commercial citrus drink.
   • Key distinction: citrate in orange and grapefruit juice is complexed with potassium (an alkali load that
     raises pH), whereas in lemon juice it is citric acid (little alkali). Malate also raises urinary citrate.
     Therefore alkaline citrate matters more than total citrate.
   • Lemonade: several studies showed rises in urinary citrate (one with smaller stone burden), cheaply and
     with good tolerance; at least three found no benefit, one showing potassium citrate succeeded where
     lemonade failed, another that lemonade helped only when combined with potassium citrate.
   • Orange juice: less citrate than lemon but potassium-bound; associated with lower stone risk in three
     large cohorts and gives an alkali load and citrate rise comparable to potassium citrate — but it raises
     urinary oxalate and does not lower calcium, unlike potassium citrate.
   • Other juices (lime, cranberry, black currant, grapefruit, tomato) give variable citrate rises.

 URIC ACID STONES
   • Urine pH is the most important determinant of uric acid solubility; with no known crystallisation
     inhibitors, undissociated uric acid precipitates once urine is supersaturated.
   • Sigmoid solubility curve: pKa about 5.5 (50% soluble); at pH 6.5 over 90% is ionised and soluble. Urine
     pH below 5.5 is the commonest cause of uric acid stones.

   • Low urine pH has two mechanisms: increased net acid excretion and reduced renal ammonium
     excretion.

 Dietary Intervention for Uric Acid Stone Prevention
   • Fluid for urine volume of at least 2.5 L/day.
   • With hyperuricosuria, limit nondairy animal protein — dietary purines account for about 30% of
     urinary uric acid; eat fruit and vegetables for their alkali load.
   • Exercise, weight loss and glucose control help in obesity, metabolic syndrome and diabetes.

 Pharmacologic Intervention for Uric Acid Stone Prevention
   • Goal: urine pH above 5.5, preferably 6.0–6.5.
   • Sodium alkali raises pH but can cause calcium-containing stones; potassium citrate alkalinises without
     that complication and is preferred, at 30–60 mEq/day in two or three doses to keep pH about 6.5.
     Avoid pH above 7.0 (calcium phosphate risk).
   • Allopurinol is not routine first-line, because low urine pH is by far the dominant risk. Exceptions:
     hyperuricaemia with hyperuricosuria (e.g., primary gout); AUA also allows it as an adjunct when
     alkalinisation fails or stones recur despite it.
   • Allopurinol: 300 mg/day; renally cleared, so reduce the dose in renal impairment. Common effects:
     rash, muscle pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea.
   • In 2–8%, reactions range from cutaneous hypersensitivity to allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome
     (AHS), fatal in up to 27%; allopurinol is a common cause of Stevens-Johnson syndrome and
     complicated renal failure. Risk rises with higher dose, renal insufficiency, diuretics and statins, and with
     concurrent thiazide in reduced renal function.
 Concept — uric acid stones are a pH disease more than a uric acid disease: alkalinise to 6.0–6.5 with
 potassium citrate, and reserve allopurinol for true hyperuricosuria or failed alkalinisation.

 CYSTINE STONE PREVENTION

 Dietary Interventions for Cystine Stone Prevention
   • Two goals: lower cystine concentration below 250 mg/L (hydration, diet) and raise cystine solubility
     (alkalinisation, binding drugs).
   • High fluid is absolutely essential: urine output of at least 3 L/day, spread over 24 hours including the
     night, to prevent physiological nocturnal concentration. Intake 3–5 L/day for adults and 3 L/day for
     children, increased with exercise, fever and heat.
   • Sodium restriction reduces cystine excretion; restriction to 1 mmol/kg/day also increases proximal
     tubular cystine reabsorption. Target sodium of 2300 mg/day or less. Alkalinising with sodium rather
     than potassium citrate reduces the efficacy of tiopronin.
   • Protein restriction: animal protein supplies cystine and methionine (metabolised to cystine), and a
     low-protein diet lowers cystine excretion — but not in children and adolescents, as methionine is
     essential for growth and scarce in plant foods; no effect on recurrence has been shown.
   • AUA: counsel limited sodium and protein; encourage fruit and vegetables rich in malate and citrate.

 Pharmacologic Intervention Prevention of Cystine Stones
   • First line (with hydration and diet): alkalinise to pH 7.0–7.5, preferably with potassium citrate 30–60
     mEq/day titrated to pH, since sodium increases cystine excretion.

 PREVENTION OF INFECTION STONE RECURRENCE
   • Infection (struvite) stones are treated by aggressive surgery aiming at complete clearance; the AUA
     strongly recommends endoscopic surgery — percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) first line for
     complex staghorn calculi — because every infected fragment must be removed to prevent recurrence
     (Chapter 98).
   • Medical management aims at preventing recurrence, not dissolution: long-term control of urea-
     splitting infection, better bladder health, adequate drainage and surveillance for reinfection.
   • Control is hard with residual fragments, which harbour organisms and endotoxin; recolonisation is
     likely even after cultures turn negative.

   • Antibiotics follow culture, but urine and stone cultures correlate poorly — so all removals of presumed
     infection stones need broad-spectrum cover suited to local resistance.
 Acetohydroxamic acid
  • AHA is an oral urease inhibitor that reduces struvite saturation, concentrates in urine and penetrates
    bacterial walls; at 250 mg three times daily it prevents new stones and slows growth, as shown in
    placebo-controlled RCTs (including in spinal cord injury), and occasionally dissolves stones.
   • AUA: offer AHA for residual or recurrent struvite stones only after surgical options are exhausted
     (Grade B).
   • Side effects in up to 30%; haemolytic anaemia in 3–15% requires stopping; the most severe is deep
     venous thrombosis, from low-grade intravascular coagulation. Trial withdrawal rates were high.
   • Contraindications: creatinine above 2.5 mg/dL, pregnancy, and childbearing age without
     contraception; renal insufficiency increases toxicity. It is therefore reserved mainly for patients unfit for
     surgery.
 Metabolic evaluation in struvite formers
  • Controversial: mixed struvite–calcium oxalate formers have more metabolic abnormalities than pure
    struvite formers, yet most of both groups have abnormalities — commonly hypercalciuria and
    hypocitraturia. Treating these along with the infection (AHA and/or suppressive antibiotics) prevented
    growth of residual stones in most, so a 24-hour urine is worthwhile.
 Concept — struvite is a surgical disease first: every fragment is an infected nidus, drugs only hold the line,
 and the toxicity of AHA confines it to the patient who cannot have surgery.

 MONITORING PREVENTIVE STRATEGIES
   • AUA: one 24-hour urine within 6 months of starting drug therapy, then at least annual evaluation for
     adherence and response, with blood tests for adverse effects.
   • If stones recur on therapy, repeat stone analysis to detect an evolving metabolic condition.
   • Periodic imaging for silent growth or new stones, balanced against radiation exposure (expert opinion).

   KEY POINTS
     • Dietary prevention measures, including high fluid intake, form the foundation for preventive
       measures, even among individuals with high-risk stone disease.
     • Effective pharmacotherapeutic options exist for hypocitraturia and hypercalciuria.
     • RNA interference represents a recent breakthrough for individuals with PH1.

 Concept — prevention is a loop: test, treat the specific abnormality, re-test within 6 months and then
 yearly, and re-analyse any new stone.
