Genitalia

 • Diagnosis and treatment of genital cutaneous disease is a core element of urological practice, yet is often
   overlooked in formal urology residency training.
 • The subject lies at the interface of several specialties: urology, infectious diseases, rheumatology, allergy–
   immunology and dermatology.

INTRODUCTION TO BASIC DERMATOLOGY
Diagnostic philosophy
 • Diagnosis rests critically on history and physical examination; laboratory testing is peripheral and
   confirmatory.
 • Visual inspection alone often narrows the differential substantially.
 • The skin has a limited repertoire of morphological expression, so many diseases mimic one another. The
   urologist should therefore have a low threshold for skin biopsy or targeted laboratory tests to separate
   clinical mimics.

History
 • Hundreds of dermatoses can involve the genitalia, and each evolves in appearance over time, so a
   methodical, systematic approach is essential.
 • The history covers duration, rate of onset, location, associated symptoms, family history, allergies,
   occupation and previous treatment.
 • Common symptoms are pruritus (itching), burning, stinging and pain.
 • The absence of a symptom (for example, a painless lesion) is diagnostically informative and should be
   recorded.
Examination
 • Examine the distribution of primary and secondary lesions with a full skin survey, not the genital skin
   alone.
     ◦ Example: red, silvery-scaled genital plaques plus similar plaques on the extensor surfaces point to
       psoriasis.
 • The primary lesion is the key to diagnosis. Describe its colour (red, brown, black, yellow, white, blue,
   green) and its morphology.
 • Because genital skin is mucosal in character, papules and macules may present as erosions.
 • Secondary lesions arise as the disease evolves, or from scratching, rubbing or superinfection.

      ◦ Disruption of bowel and vaginal flora
 • Altered flora and susceptibility patterns can persist for protracted periods, which demands truly
   appropriate antibiotic use.
Systemic antifungals
 • The same caveats apply. Superficial dermatophytosis (tinea cruris) responds to diligent topical antifungal
   therapy.
 • Systemic antifungals are indicated only for:
     ◦ Very extensive cutaneous dermatophytosis
      ◦ Endemic mycoses with skin involvement
      ◦ Majocchi granuloma (deep follicular infection)
      ◦ Severe immunocompromise
      ◦ Infection resistant to topical therapy, even in immunocompetent patients
 • Ketoconazole: avoid routine systemic use for superficial infection because of unpredictable life-
   threatening hepatotoxicity and adrenal insufficiency (United States Food and Drug Administration [FDA],
   2013).
Systemic glucocorticosteroids
 • Pharmacokinetics:
    ◦ Absorbed in the jejunum; peak plasma level at 30–90 minutes.
      ◦ Plasma half-life is 1–5 hours, but the duration of effect is 8–48 hours, depending on the agent.
 • Mechanism:
    ◦ They release neutrophils from marrow but inhibit their migration to inflamed tissue.
      ◦ They impair T-cell activation and antigen presentation by dendritic cells.
 • Short-term use (≤3 weeks), for example allergic contact dermatitis: give a single morning dose to
   minimise hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis suppression.
 • Prednisone is the GCS of choice (low cost, intermediate duration of action, variety of dosage forms).
   Methylprednisolone may be substituted to reduce mineralocorticoid effects.
 • Long-term adverse effects: osteoporosis, cataract, hypertension, obesity, hyperglycaemia, avascular
   necrosis of the femoral head, immunosuppression, psychiatric change.
 • Therefore prefer topical steroids whenever clinically feasible.
Topical therapy
 • Topical agents are the mainstay for genital dermatoses; urologists are generally less familiar with them
   than dermatologists.
 • Five classes: emollients, anti-inflammatories, antibiotics, antifungals, chemotherapeutic agents.
 • The vehicle determines the rate of absorption of the active ingredient.
Emollients
 • They restore water and lipids to the epidermis and are used in dry-skin disease.
 • Apply to moist skin (after bathing) for maximal effect.
 • Ointment versus cream: ointments are preferred because creams more often irritate or burn.
 • Urea and lactic acid preparations are particularly potent hydrating agents.
 • Ceramides (fatty acid plus sphingoid base) are the main intercellular lipids of the outermost skin layer.
   They are critical for hydration and barrier function, so ceramide formulations suit xerosis (dryness).

Topical corticosteroids
 • They are potent anti-inflammatory agents available in many preparations and strengths.
 • Adverse effects arise from both systemic absorption and local application:
    ◦ Epidermal atrophy
      ◦ Striae of the upper inner thigh
      ◦ Dermal change (telangiectasia, hypopigmentation)
      ◦ Allergic reactions
      ◦ Altered course of skin infections and infestations
 • Male genital skin is at higher risk of steroid atrophy because of its thin dermis.
    ◦ Atrophy is usually reversible over several months but may be permanent.
 • Under the foreskin, the prepuce acts as an occlusive dressing and enhances penetration, so atrophy is
   especially likely (Fig. 30.2).
Topical JAK inhibitors
 • Ruxolitinib is the example. It blocks the pro-inflammatory JAK signalling pathway without the local
   adverse effects of long-term corticosteroids.

Atopic Dermatitis (Eczema)
Definition, lesion and epidemiology
 • AD is a chronic relapsing dermatitis with a predilection for flexures, intense pruritus and epidermal
   damage.
 • Lesion: erythematous papules and thin plaques with secondary excoriations (Fig. 30.3).
 • Border contrast: the AD border is ill-defined, whereas papulosquamous disorders are sharply bordered.
 • Onset: any age, but 90% present before age 5 years.
 • There is susceptibility to irritants (fragrances, preservatives, proteins) and a propensity to asthma and
   allergic rhinitis.
Genetics
 • Strong familial clustering of atopy; monozygotic twin concordance far exceeds dizygotic.
 • There is no single marker gene; at least 11 loci are associated.
 • The key defect is the inability to synthesise functional filaggrin, producing a "leaky" epithelial barrier
   plus chronic immune activation.
Pruritus and superinfection
 • Intense pruritus is the hallmark, and controlling the urge to scratch is critical to successful treatment.
 • Itch is worse in the evening, aggravated by sweating, occlusive undergarments and wool.
 • Scratching leads to Staphylococcus aureus superinfection; bacterial toxins act as superantigens that
   sustain the inflammatory cascade.
Diagnosis
 • There is no pathognomonic test, biopsy result or single clinical feature.
 • A biopsy showing spongiotic dermatitis is suggestive.
 • A personal or family history of atopy is the critical clue.
 • With genital AD, extragenital involvement is commonplace.
Triggers
 • Chemicals, detergents and house dust mites; remove them on an individualised basis.

Contact Dermatitis
Shared features
 • There are two distinct entities: irritant contact dermatitis (ICD) and allergic contact dermatitis (ACD).
 • Their mechanisms differ but their presentations are similar; the affected area is usually sharply limited
   to the zone of exposure.
 • Primary treatment: identify and reduce exposure to the offending agent.

Psoriasis
Epidemiology and pathogenesis
 • Affects up to 2% of the population. Predisposition is likely polygenic.
 • Triggers of a flare:
     ◦ Trauma
      ◦ Streptococcal infection
      ◦ Psychological stress
      ◦ New medications
 • One-third of patients have a family history.
Lesion and distribution
 • Characteristic lesion: a sharply demarcated erythematous plaque with silvery-white scale, produced by
   rapid epidermal proliferation.
 • Distribution ranges from elbows and knees alone to the entire skin surface.
 • Bimodal onset: 20–30 years and 50–60 years.
 • Quality-of-life burden comes from pruritus, bleeding, and the cosmetic and psychosocial impact.
Genital psoriasis
 • Relatively common, but usually within generalised disease.
 • Patients may present fearing malignancy or a sexually transmitted disease (STD).
 • It impairs self-esteem and sexual self-image, particularly in females.
 • Diagnostic clues elsewhere: elbows, knees, buttocks, nails, scalp, umbilicus (Fig. 30.8).
 • Inverse psoriasis: in the inguinal folds and intergluteal cleft, scale may be absent. For a non-scaling fold
   plaque, exclude tinea or Candida by KOH preparation or fungal culture.
 • Circumcised versus uncircumcised: plaques sit on the glans and corona if circumcised, and are hidden
   under the prepuce if uncircumcised.
 • It may involve the entire penis and scrotum (Fig. 30.9).
 • Histology — "sandwich sign" (Fig. 30.8B): alternating neutrophils and parakeratosis in the stratum
   corneum.
Treatment of genital psoriasis
 • The disease is chronic, relapsing and remitting; a substantial proportion of patients are frustrated by the
   ineffectiveness of current treatment.

Reactive Arthritis (Formerly Reiter Syndrome)
Components and epidemiology
 • The syndrome comprises urethritis, arthritis, ocular findings, oral ulcers and skin lesions; only about
   one-third show all features.
 • It is commoner in males, rarely diagnosed in children, and commoner in human immunodeficiency virus
   (HIV)-positive patients.

Triggers
 • Urethritis: Chlamydia, gonococcus, Ureaplasma.
 • Gastrointestinal infection: Yersinia, Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter.
 • Strong association with human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-B27; whether molecular cross-reactivity
   between bacterial antigens and HLA-B27 causes autoimmunity remains controversial.
Extracutaneous features
 • Ocular: conjunctivitis is commonest; iritis, uveitis, glaucoma and keratitis also occur.
 • Musculoskeletal: polyarthritis and sacroiliitis are commonest; chronic disability in a small minority.
Skin features
 • Balanitis circinata: scaly erythematous psoriasiform lesions of the penis (Fig. 30.11).
 • Keratoderma blennorrhagicum: similar lesions on the soles.
 • These are difficult to distinguish from psoriasis; histology cannot consistently separate them and may
   show psoriasiform dermatitis.
 • Differential diagnosis: pustular psoriasis, gonococcal infection, Behçet syndrome.
Course and treatment
 • Genital disease is usually self-limited, lasting weeks to months; it responds to low-potency topical
   corticosteroids, and systemic therapy is rarely required.
 • Sole lesions are more persistent and respond to potent topical retinoid (tazarotene) or systemic retinoid
   (acitretin).

Lichen Planus
Definition and pathogenesis
 • LP is the prototype lichenoid dermatosis: an idiopathic inflammatory disease of skin and mucous
   membranes.
 • Lichenoid tissue reaction: basal cell damage with massive mononuclear infiltration of the papillary
   dermis.
 • Epidemiology: cutaneous LP in up to 1% of adults; oral lesions in up to 4%.
 • Pathogenesis: an autoimmune reaction against basal keratinocytes expressing altered self-antigens.

Lesion and distribution
 • Primary lesion: a small, polygonal, violaceous, flat-topped papule; lesions are separate or coalesce into
   plaques that may ulcerate, especially on mucosa.
 • Sites: flexor surfaces of the limbs, trunk, lumbosacral area, oral mucosa, glans penis.
 • Male genital patterns (eFig. 30.12):
    ◦ Isolated or grouped papules
       ◦ White reticular pattern
       ◦ Annular arrangement, with or without ulceration
 • Koebner phenomenon: linear lesions along lines of skin trauma (also seen in psoriasis).
 • Female genital pattern: painful erosion of erythematous plaques; in long-standing vulvar LP, white
   hyperhydrated hyperkeratosis surrounds shallow erosions.
 • Concomitant oral LP (buccal mucosa, tongue) is commoner in females.
Differential diagnosis and biopsy
 • Differential diagnosis:
    ◦ Invasive and in situ SCC
       ◦ Zoon balanitis
       ◦ Psoriasis
       ◦ Secondary syphilis
       ◦ Herpes
       ◦ Extramammary Paget disease (EPD)
       ◦ Lupus erythematosus
 • Biopsy is especially needed when lesions are small, multiple and ulcerated.
 • Lichenoid reactions also follow ingested drugs and contact allergens, so search for an offending agent.
Natural history and symptoms
 • Papular LP is benign: spontaneous resolution in up to two-thirds by 1 year.
 • Oral and erosive forms persist longer; isolated SCC has arisen in chronic genital LP, so perform regular
   skin checks throughout the disease.
 • Symptoms: pruritus is commoner in males; pain or burning is commoner in females.
 • Asymptomatic genital lesions need no treatment.
 • Vaginal erosive LP can cause adhesions and permanent damage, so manage it with care.

Lichen Nitidus
 • Lesion: tiny, discrete, flesh-coloured papules in large clusters.
 • Relationship to LP: debated as a variant, but the two are histologically distinct; lichen nitidus (LN) shows
   a dense, well-circumscribed lymphohistiocytic infiltrate closely apposed to the epidermis.
 • Sites: flexor aspects of the upper limbs, genitalia, trunk, dorsal hands; nail involvement is common.
 • Natural history: spontaneous resolution, with the majority clearing within 1 year.
 • Management:
    ◦ Reassure that the lesions are not infectious and will resolve.
      ◦ For pruritus: mid- to low-potency topical corticosteroids plus oral antihistamines.

Lichen Sclerosus
Epidemiology
 • Lichen sclerosus et atrophicus (LS) is a chronic inflammatory disease of unknown cause with a
   predilection for the external genitalia.
 • It is 6–10 times more prevalent in females, around menopause or in the prepubertal years.
 • In males it tends to affect the older man (>60 years), and may cause pain on voiding or erection.
 • There is a strong familial predisposition, suggesting a genetic contribution.
 • 15–20% of patients with genital LS have extragenital disease.
Clinical features
 • LS is a scarring disorder with tissue pallor, loss of architecture from fibrosis, and hyperkeratosis (Fig.
   30.13).
 • Purpura and fissuring may be prominent; purpura can be severe enough to obscure the typical white
   colour.
 • Male distribution: glans and foreskin; perianal involvement is usually absent, in contrast to females.
 • Male sequence: preputial scarring → phimosis → circumcision (usually curative); recurrence in the
   circumcision scar may occur.
 • Late stage = balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO): it can involve the penile urethra and cause troublesome
   urethral strictures.
 • Female sequelae: vulvar adhesions, labial fusion, clitoral phimosis, vaginal obstruction; symptoms
   include itching, burning, pain and dyspareunia.
Contrast with LP and LN, and malignant potential
 • Despite the similar name, LS shares little with LP and LN beyond pruritus and genital predilection.
 • Critical distinction: LS is associated with SCC of the penis and vulva, particularly human papillomavirus
   (HPV)-independent variants; LS may be premalignant.
Histology and biopsy
 • Histological features:
    ◦ Basal cell vacuolation
      ◦ Epidermal atrophy

      ◦ Collagen homogenisation
      ◦ Focal perivascular papillary-dermal infiltrate
      ◦ Plugging of follicular and eccrine ostia
 • Biopsy is worthwhile to confirm the diagnosis and exclude malignant change.
 • Differential diagnosis: erosive LP, vulvar eczema, lichen simplex chronicus, child sexual abuse,

 • Prognostic markers: p53, survivin, telomerase, Ki-67 and cyclin D1 may distinguish indolent LS from LS

 • Long-term follow-up is required because of the SCC association; monitor non-healing fissures, ulcers,
   papules and nodules carefully.
 • Topical steroid sequence (established in females): potent topical steroid (clobetasol propionate 0.05%
   or halobetasol 0.05%) for 3 months → taper → limited twice-weekly maintenance; this improves

 • Refractory disease: topical or systemic retinoids, photodynamic therapy.

Fixed Drug Eruption
Timing and sites
 • Fixed drug eruption (FDE) is one of the commonest cutaneous drug reactions, following oral
   medications, usually 1–2 weeks after first exposure.
 • Sites: lips, face, hands, feet and genitalia, particularly the glans penis (Fig. 30.14).
 • Genital bullous FDE is commoner in males.
 • Re-exposure → recurrence at exactly the same site, usually within 24 hours — the origin of the term
   "fixed".
Causative drugs
 • Commonest: sulfonamides, NSAIDs, barbiturates, tetracyclines, carbamazepine, phenolphthalein, oral
   contraceptives, salicylates.
 • Urological agents reported in isolated cases: finasteride, tadalafil, fluconazole (for vulvovaginal
   candidiasis).
Appearance and outcome
 • On the shaft or glans: an asymptomatic, solitary, violaceous, well-defined patch or plaque with an
   erythematous halo; it may become bullous, pruritic, erosive and painful.
 • Genital differential diagnosis: herpes simplex, insect bite.
 • Outcome: withdrawal of the drug → resolution; post-inflammatory brown pigmentation may remain,
   but there is no lasting functional defect.
 • Histology (Fig. 30.14D): normal stratum corneum; chronic superficial dermal change with an eosinophilic
   infiltrate — necrotic keratinocytes, papillary dermal fibrosis, eosinophils and perivascular melanin.

Seborrhoeic Dermatitis
Lesion and distribution
 • Sharply demarcated pink-yellow to red-brown plaques with adherent flaky scale; SD overlaps with
   eczematous dermatitis and could be grouped with it.
 • Dandruff is mild SD of the scalp.
 • SD favours sebaceous-rich areas, appearing in the first few months of life or after puberty, when
   sebaceous glands are active.
 • Sites: scalp, eyebrows, nasolabial folds, ears, chest; also the anus, glans penis and pubic area.
 • Circumcision is somewhat protective: penile SD risk is higher in the uncircumcised.
Associations
 • Adult SD follows a chronic relapsing course.
 • It is particularly common in Parkinson disease and in acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) — up
   to 83% of patients with AIDS.
 • In the immunosuppressed it may cover a large body surface area.
 • Extensive or severe SD should raise concern for underlying HIV infection or Parkinson disease.
Aetiology
 • A possible autoimmune response to the yeast Malassezia furfur (Pityrosporum ovale) is proposed but
   controversial:
     ◦ Organism counts in SD lesions are only modestly higher than in normal skin.
        ◦ HIV patients with SD carry no more organisms than HIV patients without it.
 • Elevated skin-surface triglycerides and cholesterol are another possible factor.

Aphthous Ulcers and Behçet Disease
Aphthous ulcers
 • Aphthous ulcers are small, painful erosions, commonly of the oral cavity (canker sores) and occasionally
   the genitalia.
 • Coexisting oral and genital aphthous ulcers should prompt serious consideration of Behçet disease (BD).
Nature and pathogenesis
 • BD is a generalised relapsing and remitting ulcerative mucocutaneous disease with a genetic
   predisposition and an autoimmune mode of pathogenesis.
 • Many loci are implicated; the strongest genetic association is HLA-B51.
 • Oxidative stress is implicated: neutrophils overproduce superoxide radicals.
 • Other proposed mechanisms include interleukin-10 (IL-10) gene mutations.
 • Treatment response varies widely, which implies that the inflammatory pathways are not uniform
   across patients.

Pyoderma Gangrenosum
Definition and associations
 • Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare ulcerative neutrophilic dermatosis associated with systemic
   illness:
      ◦ Inflammatory bowel disease
       ◦ Arthritis
       ◦ Collagen vascular disease
       ◦ Chronic active hepatitis
       ◦ HIV infection
       ◦ Myeloproliferative disorders
 • It most commonly affects females in the second to fifth decades; the pathogenesis is likely autoimmune,
   given its association with other autoimmune diseases.
Epidemiology
 • Genital PG is rare; 20–50% of cases are idiopathic; annual incidence in the United States is about 1 per
   100,000.
Morphology
 • Painful cutaneous and mucosal ulceration with extensive tissue loss and a purulent base (Fig. 30.20).
 • Classic appearance: a rapidly developing lower-leg ulcer with an overhanging, undermined violaceous
   border, arising at a site of trauma.
 • Unusual sites: penis, scrotum, vulva, peristomal skin.
Diagnosis
 • As in BD, there is no pathognomonic test or histological feature; a history of underlying systemic disease
   raises suspicion.
 • Biopsy 5–10 mm from the ulcer edge, where a dense neutrophilic infiltrate is found.
 • Differential diagnosis of penile PG, beyond the ulcerative sexually transmitted infections:
    ◦ Calciphylaxis
       ◦ BD
       ◦ Necrotising fasciitis and Fournier gangrene (FG)
       ◦ Cutaneous metastatic Crohn disease
       ◦ Deep fungal infection
       ◦ Pemphigus vegetans
       ◦ Neoplasia
       ◦ Erosive LP
       ◦ Trauma, factitious damage

Balanitis and Balanoposthitis
Definitions and causes
 • Balanitis is inflammation of the glans; balanoposthitis is inflammation of the glans and prepuce, so it

 • Causes by age:

Fournier Gangrene (Necrotising Fasciitis of the Perineum)
Microbiology and triggers
 • Fournier gangrene (FG) is a potentially life-threatening progressive infection of the perineum and
   genitalia.
 • Microbiology: polymicrobial (gram-positive, gram-negative and anaerobic); common pathogens are
   Escherichia coli, Bacteroides spp., S. pyogenes and *S. aureus; fungal FG* has been reported.
 • Inciting events:
     ◦ Skin or mucosal tear
      ◦ Penetrating trauma
      ◦ Recent urogenital or colorectal instrumentation
 • Risk factors:
     ◦ Alcoholism
      ◦ Diabetes
      ◦ Cancer, malnutrition
      ◦ Chronic kidney disease
      ◦ Urethral stricture disease
      ◦ Advanced age
      ◦ Genitourinary infection
      ◦ Peripheral vascular disease
Progression and spread
 • Hallmark sequence: cellulitis (erythema, swelling, pain) → blisters → visible ischaemia → foul-smelling
   necrosis (Fig. 30.25).
 • Spread along fascial planes from the genitalia and perineum to the abdominal wall fascia (Scarpa's); the
   skin shows only a small fraction of the infected and necrotic tissue.
 • FG is a surgical emergency: progression takes hours, and septicaemia is the usual cause of death.
 • Consider FG in every genital soft-tissue infection consultation.
Clinical clues to FG
 • Pain out of proportion to the visible infection.
 • A greyish cast to the skin.
 • A rank odour, uncharacteristic of uncomplicated cellulitis.
Imaging
 • Plain radiographs, computed tomography (CT) and bedside ultrasonography may show soft-tissue gas; CT
   has the highest specificity.
 • Imaging must not delay surgery in obvious cases.
Management sequence
 • Broad-spectrum antibiotics + extensive debridement to healthy bleeding tissue → second-look
   operation at 24–48 hours → repeated debridements as needed → multidisciplinary care (urology,
   general surgery, infectious diseases, critical care).

Folliculitis
Features
 • Inflammation of the hair follicle, producing perifollicular pustules or papules on an erythematous base.
 • Sites: heavily hair-bearing areas (scalp, beard, axilla, groin, buttocks).
 • Aggravated by: occlusion (e.g. truck drivers), shaving, rubbing, clothing irritation.
 • Symptoms: pruritus or pain, or none.
 • Cause: usually bacterial infection of the superficial or deep follicle; also fungal, viral or noninfectious.
 • Diagnosis: cultures are generally negative, so the diagnosis is clinical.
Hot-tub folliculitis
 • Follows contaminated hot tubs and swimming pools; the organism is usually Pseudomonas aeruginosa
   (Fig. 30.26).
 • Onset within 48 hours of exposure.
 • Pool and hot-tub P. aeruginosa isolates are overwhelmingly multidrug-resistant, which matters most for
   the immunosuppressed.
Treatment
 • Hygiene, removal of irritants, and topical or systemic antiviral, antibiotic or antifungal agents.
 • Recurrence is common.
 • Failure of conservative measures → lesion culture with sensitivity testing.

Furunculosis
Features
 • A deeper follicular infection forming a red, tender boil or abscess.
 • By definition a furuncle arises from a hair follicle, whereas abscesses can occur anywhere.
 • Organism: S. aureus.
 • Sites: areas of minor trauma — groin, axilla, buttocks (Fig. 30.27).

Hidradenitis Suppurativa (Acne Inversa)
Definition and epidemiology
 • Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory disease of apocrine gland-bearing skin; the
   primary sites are the intertriginous axillae and anogenital regions.
 • Onset is after puberty, usually in the second or third decade.
 • It is commoner in females and in patients of Black ancestry.
 • Genetic predisposition is likely: up to 41% have a family history.
Pathogenesis
 • Originally considered an apocrine disease, HS is now considered follicle-centred.
 • Sequence: follicular plugging and swelling → rupture of follicular contents (bacteria, keratin) into the
   dermis → marked inflammation → abscesses and sinus tracts.

Clinical features and complications
 • Features: painful inflammatory nodules, skin tunnels and sterile abscesses in the axillae, groin, perianal
   and inframammary areas (Fig. 30.28).
 • Over time: draining sinus tracts and hypertrophic scars.
 • Serious complications:
    ◦ Hypoproteinaemia
      ◦ Secondary amyloidosis
      ◦ Fistulae to the urethra, bladder, peritoneum and rectum
      ◦ Lymphatic obstruction
      ◦ Strictures and contractures
      ◦ SCC in heavily scarred areas
General measures
 • Patient education, smoking cessation, hygiene, weight reduction.
 • Management of metabolic syndrome and hormone-related disorders.
 • Reduce friction and moisture (loose undergarments, absorbent powder).
 • No single intervention is universally effective.

Ecthyma Gangrenosum
Definition and lesion
 • The cutaneous manifestation of gram-negative bacteraemia, most commonly P. aeruginosa, in
   debilitated, immunocompromised patients.
 • Sites: perineum, buttocks, axillae, lower limbs.
 • Lesion sequence: tender grouped erythematous or purpuric macules → bullae or a haemorrhagic
   pustule → rupture → gangrenous ulcer with a thick black eschar (Fig. 30.30).
Diagnosis
 • Biopsy is diagnostic: necrotising vasculitis with gram-negative organisms — necrotic vessels surrounded
   by a "blue haze" of organisms, with little inflammation.



                                                            page 27 of 39
Chapter 30 — Cutaneous Diseases of the External Genitalia                                   Infections and Infestations
 • Differential diagnosis:
    ◦ PG
      ◦ Necrotising vasculitis
      ◦ Cryoglobulinaemia
      ◦ Calciphylaxis
      ◦ Septic emboli from other organisms: Candida, Aspergillus, Citrobacter, E. coli, Aeromonas hydrophila,
        Fusarium
Management and prognosis
 • The prognosis is poor, reflecting the underlying sepsis.
 • Immediate intravenous antipseudomonal antibiotics, with debridement if needed.

Genital Bite Wounds
 • Organism: after deliberate or accidental genital bites, Eikenella corrodens (normal human oral flora) is
   implanted in the skin.
 • Result: rapid, extremely painful necrotic ulceration at the bite site (Fig. 30.31); progression to
   necrotising fasciitis has been described.
 • Distinguishing triad from sexually transmitted and other ulcers:
    ◦ Rapidity of ulceration
      ◦ Extraordinary pain
      ◦ History of traumatic orogenital contact
 • Treatment: high-dose oral amoxicillin–clavulanate 875/125 mg twice daily until healed, plus aggressive
   local wound care.
 • Surgery is often needed: irrigation → debridement → primary closure → occasionally reconstruction.

Candidal Intertrigo
Features
 • Candidal infection of macerated skin folds in warm, moist areas: groin, axillae, beneath the breasts,
   intertriginous zones.
 • Pruritic, red skin with characteristic satellite lesions (Fig. 30.32).
 • Differential diagnosis: tinea cruris, pemphigoid, psoriasis, SD, contact dermatitis.
 • Risk factors: obesity, diabetes, immunocompromise, prolonged heat and moisture.
Diagnosis
 • KOH preparation shows round yeast cells and elongated pseudohyphae; culture is usually unnecessary.
 • Candidal intertrigo may be the presenting sign of diabetes, so test for diabetes.
Treatment
 • Daily topical imidazole for at least 2 weeks.
 • Oral fluconazole 150 mg is occasionally required.
 • Relapse prevention: drying powders, loose clothing.

Dermatophyte Infection
Organisms and pathogenesis
 • Dermatophytes are filamentous fungi of three genera — Trichophyton, Microsporum, Epidermophyton
   — that invade keratinised tissue (skin, hair, nails).

 • Mechanisms:
    ◦ Keratinases digest keratin, and the breakdown products aid invasion.
        ◦ Cell-wall mannans are immunoinhibitory.
Tinea cruris ("jock itch")
 • Commonest in postpubertal males.
 • Favoured by: heat and humidity, concomitant tinea pedis, diabetes, obesity.
 • Distribution: an erythematous rash of the inner thighs and inguinal region; the scrotum and penis are
   usually spared, although isolated penile dermatophytosis occurs.
 • Significant scrotal involvement → suspect cutaneous candidiasis.
 • Lesion: sharply demarcated erythematous plaques with a raised scaly border (Fig. 30.33); intensely
   pruritic, but pruritus may be absent in the immunocompromised.
 • Mimics: SD, psoriasis, contact dermatitis, cellulitis, intertrigo, erythrasma.

 • Skin scraping + KOH preparation (branching hyphae); culture is rarely needed.
 • A negative scraping from an untreated lesion → consider an alternative diagnosis.

 • Prevention: loose clothing, laundering contaminated garments, weight reduction, glycaemic control,
   drying powders.
 • Topical antifungals are the mainstay; powder forms also dry the area.
 • Treat active disease only, not the post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation of chronic recurrent infection.
 • Systemic antifungals are rarely needed; for follicular involvement too deep for topicals, the drug of

Infestation
 • Pediculosis pubis and scabies (Sarcoptes scabiei) are the commonest genital infestations.
Pediculosis pubis
 • Caused by the crab louse (Phthirus pubis); it is pruritic and sexually transmitted.
 • It coexists with other sexually transmitted diseases — affected adolescents carry a markedly higher risk
   of concomitant gonorrhoea or chlamydia, so screen for them.
 • It also involves other hair-bearing sites: eyelashes, beard, axillae.
 • Diagnosis: crab lice attached to hairs, often with perifollicular erythema (Fig. 30.34).
 • Transmission: usually sexual contact; contaminated clothing, bedding and towels are occasionally
   implicated.
 • Standard treatment sequence: 5% permethrin cream overnight to all affected hair-bearing areas →
   remove all visible nits → clean underwear → repeat application at 1 week.
     ◦ The second application is essential, because a single application frequently fails.
 • Refractory disease, or eyelash involvement (phthiriasis palpebrarum): add oral ivermectin.
 • Alternative: malathion 0.5% lotion.
 • Epidemiology: incidence in industrialised countries has fallen sharply with widespread pubic hair
   removal ("Brazilian waxing").
Scabies — epidemiology and transmission
 • Caused by the mite Sarcoptes scabiei; a worldwide problem spread by overcrowding, delayed treatment
   of index cases and poor public awareness.
 • Highest prevalence in the Pacific and Latin American regions.
 • Transmission: direct, prolonged skin-to-skin contact; common among close contacts and family
   members.
 • Mite load:
    ◦ Immunocompetent host: usually fewer than 100 mites.
      ◦ Immunosuppressed host: far greater numbers — crusted (Norwegian) scabies.
 • Incubation: days to months, typically about 6 weeks.
Scabies — clinical features and diagnosis
 • Severe pruritus is the hallmark, worst at night or after bathing.
 • Distribution: genital areas in both sexes; sides and webs of fingers; axillary folds, wrists, elbows.
 • Lesions: small erythematous pruritic papules, and excoriations with secondary bacterial infection (Fig.
   30.35).
 • Thin grey or white serpiginous burrows are pathognomonic.
 • Crusted scabies on genital skin presents as thickly crusted plaques, as elsewhere.
 • Secondary staphylococcal or streptococcal infection occurs.

Squamous Cell Carcinoma in Situ
Nomenclature and epidemiology
 • Squamous cell carcinoma in situ (SCCis) is a full-thickness intraepidermal carcinoma, also called
   intraepithelial neoplasia, and named by organ: penile intraepithelial neoplasia (PeIN) and vulvar
   intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN).
 • Bowen disease: described by Bowen in 1912.



                                                               page 31 of 39
Chapter 30 — Cutaneous Diseases of the External Genitalia                                             Neoplastic Conditions
 • On extragenital sites it is strongly associated with ultraviolet exposure.
 • Demography: usually presents in the seventh decade, with a slight female predominance.
 • Course: indolent; rarely progresses to invasion.
 • Erythroplasia of Queyrat: SCCis on mucosal surfaces of the male genitalia, notably the glans of the
   uncircumcised (Fig. 30.36).
Risk factors and classification
 • HPV coinfection in genital sites: types 8 and 16 predominate, with other types in the remainder.
 • Other risk factors:
    ◦ Ionising radiation
      ◦ Immunosuppression
      ◦ Thermal injury
      ◦ Arsenic
      ◦ Chronic dermatoses (long-standing LP)

 • Mimics: basal cell carcinoma (BCC), eczema, seborrhoea, psoriasis.
 • Site variants:

 • Symptoms: usually asymptomatic; sometimes pruritic or painful.

Verrucous Carcinoma (Buschke–Lowenstein Tumour)
Features
 • A rare, low-grade variant of SCC: slow-growing, locally aggressive, exophytic, with low metastatic
   potential.
 • The Buschke–Lowenstein tumour is verrucous carcinoma of the anogenital mucosa; it may represent up
   to 24% of penile tumours.
 • Sites: uncircumcised males, glans or prepuce; also the vulva, vagina, cervix and anus.
 • HPV: associated with types 6 and 11 — not the oncogenic types 16 and 18.
 • Cofactors: chronic inflammation, tobacco, other carcinogens.
 • Lesion: warty, "cauliflower-like", often large and fungating (Fig. 30.39); deeply destructive locally.
 • Extragenital sites: oral and nasal cavities, plantar feet.
Treatment
 • Local excision is preferred, but recurrence is high.
 • Mohs surgery traces the tumour and minimises tissue loss.
 • Primary radiotherapy is relatively contraindicated: risk of anaplastic transformation and increased
   metastatic potential.

Basal Cell Carcinoma
Epidemiology and subtypes
 • BCC is the commonest cutaneous neoplasm overall, arising on chronically sun-exposed skin (head and
   neck).
 • Genital BCC is very rare, most often on the scrotum in males and the vulva in females.
 • Subtypes: nodular, superficial, micronodular, morphoeaform, infiltrating.
 • Nodular is the commonest genital subtype: a pearly, skin-toned papule or plaque with overlying
   telangiectasia that may ulcerate centrally (Fig. 30.40).
 • Distant metastasis is uncommon but portends a poor prognosis.
Treatment
 • Local excision is primary; Mohs micrographic surgery should be considered to preserve genital form and
   function.
 • Metastatic, locally advanced or unresectable disease: FDA-approved Hedgehog signalling pathway
   inhibitors — vismodegib (2012) and sonidegib (2015).

Kaposi Sarcoma
Nature and epidemiology
 • Kaposi sarcoma (KS) is an angioproliferative disease of endothelial origin that requires human
   herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8).
 • Whether it is neoplastic or hyperplastic is controversial; clonality evidence is mixed.
 • Classic KS: a chronic disease of elderly men of Jewish, Mediterranean or Eastern European descent.
 • HIV-1 increased KS incidence more than 7000-fold:
    ◦ It affects patients with significant immune impairment (CD4+ T cells <500/mm³).
       ◦ It affects about 40% of homosexual men with AIDS, versus <5% of other risk groups.
       ◦ Incidence is falling with antiretroviral therapy (ART).
 • KS also occurs in solid-organ transplant recipients.
Clinical features
 • Lesions: painless, non-pruritic; may be mistaken for purpura or haematoma; may coalesce and cause
   lymphatic or venous obstruction → oedema.
 • Glans lesions can cause urethral obstruction at the meatus or fossa navicularis.
 • Genital KS is rare even with HIV — about 3% of patients with AIDS.

Pseudoepitheliomatous, Keratotic and Micaceous Balanitis
Features
 • Pseudoepitheliomatous, keratotic and micaceous balanitis (PEKMB) is rare: a thick hyperkeratotic
   plaque on the glans of older men, typically men circumcised as adults; often asymptomatic (Fig. 30.42).
 • It was thought purely benign, but concurrent verrucous carcinoma has been reported; premalignant
   status is controversial.
 • Histology is essential to exclude SCC and verrucous carcinoma; it shows a hyperplastic epidermis with
   ridges extending deeply into the dermis.
Treatment
 • Local surgical excision or ablation, with close follow-up.
 • Topical 5-fluorouracil (anecdotal).
 • Refractory disease: glans resurfacing and split-skin graft reconstruction.

Melanoma
Epidemiology and risk factors
 • A malignant skin cancer of melanocytes; the fifth most commonly diagnosed cancer in the United
   States, with rising incidence.
 • Risk factors: family history, genetic markers, fair skin, light eye colour, excessive ultraviolet exposure
   (multiple blistering childhood or adolescent sunburns).
Genital melanoma
 • Male: uncommon — <1% of penile cancers and of melanomas; urethral melanoma is rarer still.
 • Female: commoner — 7–10% of vulvar malignancies, the second commonest after SCC; commoner in
   White females, but the prognosis is worse in Black females.
 • Presentation: a black, blue or brown macule or papule with an irregular border; may be unpigmented or
   ulcerated.
 • Early diagnosis is critical: superficial lesions treated by wide local excision or partial penectomy have
   excellent control; the same applies in females.
 • Metastatic disease was historically universally poor despite surgery and multi-agent chemotherapy;
   targeted and immune therapy have transformed advanced disease.

Extramammary Paget Disease
Epidemiology
 • Extramammary Paget disease (EPD) is uncommon, arising at apocrine gland-bearing sites, in the sixth to
   eighth decades, with a female predominance; the penis and scrotum can be involved.
 • Commonest site: the vulva in females; the perianal region is also affected in males.
Associated malignancy
 • An underlying malignancy is present in at least 10–30%.
 • In males: associated with urethral, prostate, bladder, rectal and apocrine carcinoma.
 • A systematic search for underlying carcinoma is mandatory in every case.
Clinical features and diagnosis
 • Lesion: an erythematous plaque with a sharp border (Fig. 30.43); may be asymptomatic, pruritic or
   burning.
 • Histology: vacuolated Paget cells in the epidermis ("buckshot scatter"), staining for glandular
   cytokeratins, epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA).
Treatment
 • Surgical excision or Mohs micrographic surgery.
 • Alternatives: radiotherapy, photodynamic therapy, topical imiquimod 5% or 5-fluorouracil.

Cutaneous T-Cell Lymphoma
Definition and scope
 • Cutaneous T-cell lymphomas (CTCLs) are a heterogeneous group of T-cell neoplasms presenting in the
   skin without extracutaneous disease at diagnosis.
 • Members: mycosis fungoides, Sézary syndrome, lymphomatoid papulosis, pagetoid reticulosis.
 • Risk is increased with HIV.
 • Genital involvement occurs in both sexes, usually with extragenital disease.
 • CTCL is the majority of primary cutaneous lymphomas; B-cell lymphomas account for only 20–25%.
 • Biopsy histopathology is definitive.
Clinical course
 • Initial pruritic patches must be distinguished from psoriasis, eczema, superficial fungal infection and
   drug reaction.
 • Early lesions favour the suprapubic and buttock skin in both sexes (Fig. 30.44).
 • Progression: patches → plaques → erosions and ulcers → tumours; haematological involvement =
   Sézary syndrome; chronic, over many years.

BENIGN CUTANEOUS DISORDERS SPECIFIC TO THE MALE GENITALIA
Angiokeratoma of Fordyce
 • Benign vascular ectasias of dermal vessels on the penis and scrotum of adults.
 • Lesion: multiple 1–2 mm red or purple papules (Fig. 30.45A), sometimes with generalised scrotal
   redness.
 • Usually asymptomatic, but bleeding can occur.
 • Similar lesions occur in Fabry disease, a lysosomal storage disorder (Fig. 30.45B).
 • Treatment is usually unnecessary; laser photocoagulation (erbium:YAG, neodymium:YAG, potassium
   titanyl phosphate (KTP), argon) has been used in selected cases.

Pearly Penile Papules
 • Lesion: white, dome-shaped or filiform, closely spaced papules on the coronal ring of the glans (Fig.
   30.45C).
 • Prevalence: common — 14–48% of young postpubertal men, especially the uncircumcised.
 • Often misdiagnosed as condyloma, but there is no evidence of an HPV role and no association with
   cervical intraepithelial neoplasia in partners.
 • Histology: angiofibromas, similar to the facial angiofibromas of tuberous sclerosis.
 • Management: reassure — a benign normal variant; for cosmetic concern, CO₂ laser or cryotherapy.

Zoon Balanitis
 • Synonyms: plasma cell balanitis, balanitis plasmacellularis.
 • Demography: middle-aged or elderly uncircumcised men; cause unknown.
 • Lesion: smooth, shiny, moist, erythematous, well-circumscribed papules and plaques on the glans (Fig.
   30.45D); shallow erosions may occur; lesions reach up to 2 cm.
 • Symptoms: asymptomatic, or mild pruritus or tenderness.
 • SCC and EPD must be excluded, typically by biopsy.
 • Treatment:
     ◦ Circumcision prevents the disease and cures most cases.
       ◦ For men averse to circumcision: topical corticosteroids (symptomatic), topical calcineurin inhibitors
         (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus), topical retinoids, laser.

 KEY POINTS
   • Diagnosis rests on history and examination; extragenital findings may be the key, so perform a full
     skin survey.
   • Topical corticosteroid adverse effects are significant, both systemic and local, and worse under the
     foreskin (occlusion); on genital skin, use low-potency agents for short courses in general.
   • Genital dermatoses fall into categories: allergic, papulosquamous, vesiculobullous, ulcerative,
     infectious, neoplastic and miscellaneous.
   • Biopsy separates look-alike diseases and excludes malignancy.
   • Local modalities (laser, photodynamic therapy, ultraviolet, cryotherapy) are alternatives to excision
     in some cases.
