Chapter 7 — Genitourinary Tract Imaging: Basic
Principles of Nuclear Medicine
INTRODUCTION
 • Nuclear medicine uses radionuclide-labelled pharmaceuticals to visualise organ function, treat disease
   and characterise molecular processes within cells; the last is termed molecular imaging.
 • Radiotracers are given in subpharmacological quantities, so they do not perturb the process being
   measured. Their emissions are detected by an external sensor and formatted as an image.
 • Contrast with anatomic imaging: plain radiography, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance
   imaging (MRI) and ultrasonography all need an external energy source; in nuclear medicine the patient
   is the source.
Concept — Nuclear imaging shows what tissue does rather than what it looks like, because the signal
comes from a tracer taken up by a physiological or molecular process.

PRINCIPLES OF SINGLE-PHOTON AND PET IMAGING
Single-photon imaging
 • Gamma photons of 100–300 keV are detected by a gamma camera, in planar or tomographic mode.
 • Sequence: gamma photon → scintillation crystal (usually sodium iodide) absorbs it and emits light →
   photomultiplier tubes collect and amplify the light → circuitry integrates the output → image.
 • Collimator (between patient and crystal): its geometry admits photons travelling only in a certain
   direction, encoding spatial information.
 • Planar mode: detection at a single angle → two-dimensional image.
 • Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT): the camera rotates slowly around the patient
   → multiple angles → three-dimensional image. A non-contrast CT is acquired at the same time for
   anatomic localisation and attenuation correction (signal from deeper tissue is attenuated on its way
   out). SPECT is either routine or a problem-solving adjunct when planar images are inadequate.
 • Radionuclides: technetium-99m (⁹⁹ᵐTc) and indium-111 (¹¹¹In) (Table 7.1).
Positron emission tomography (PET)
 • Sequence: positron emitted → travels a short distance → collides with an electron → annihilation →
   two 511-keV photons 180° apart → coincidence detection → image.
 • Acquired tomographically, usually with non-contrast CT (localisation and attenuation correction), or
   with MRI in PET/MRI scanners at large centres.
 • Radionuclides: carbon-11 (¹¹C), iodine-124 (¹²⁴I), fluorine-18 (¹⁸F), gallium-68 (⁶⁸Ga) (Table 7.2).
 • Conspicuity: PET rejects photons without a coincident partner, minimising noise; a gamma camera
   treats any photon through the collimator as true signal.
 • Quantification: PET measures standardised uptake values (SUVs), corrected for injected dose and body
   mass; clinical single-photon imaging is only semiquantitative.
 • Limitation of PET: high cost, limiting access in much of the world.
 • Current practice: most urological functional imaging uses scintigraphy; cancer imaging mostly uses PET
   with CT or MRI.

 FUNCTIONAL IMAGING OF THE KIDNEYS
   • Applications: renal blood flow, differential renal function, presence and degree of obstruction, and
     renal scarring. Interpretation depends on renal physiology and the properties of each agent.

 Relevant Renal Physiology
   • Kidneys receive 20% of cardiac output: 5 L/min → about 1 L/min renal blood flow. Plasma is 60% of
     blood, so renal plasma flow ≈ 600 mL/min.
   • Plasma clearance: about 20% by passive glomerular filtration, 80% by active tubular secretion. The
     kidneys reclaim free water and electrolytes according to need.
   • Principal tracers: technetium-99m diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (⁹⁹ᵐTc-DTPA), technetium-99m
     mercaptoacetyltriglycine (⁹⁹ᵐTc-MAG3) and technetium-99m dimercaptosuccinic acid (⁹⁹ᵐTc-DMSA).

 ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DTPA
   • DTPA (pentetic acid) is a heavy-metal chelator. Extracted entirely by glomerular filtration, not
     reabsorbed → measures glomerular filtration rate (GFR) directly.
   • Used for differential function and obstruction, but impaired renal function gives high background and
     poor images.

 ⁹⁹ᵐTc-MAG3
   • Protein-bound; cleared by tubular secretion → unaffected by impaired GFR, higher extraction
     efficiency, better images, lower non-target radiation, and cheaper than DTPA.
   • Agent of choice for split function and functional obstruction.

 ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DMSA
   • Cleared mainly by glomerular filtration, then taken up by proximal tubular cells by receptor-mediated
     endocytosis via megalin and cubilin → retained in the cortex.
   • Ideal for cortical processes: acute pyelonephritis and renal scarring.

 Dynamic Renal Imaging With ⁹⁹ᵐTc-MAG3 and ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DTPA
   • Split function and obstruction require dynamic imaging over time, unlike the single static image of
     most anatomic modalities.

 Patient Preparation
   • Good hydration ensures optimal tracer delivery.
   • Record medications, particularly diuretics, and any anatomic abnormalities affecting positioning,
     camera set-up or interpretation.
   • Bladder outlet obstruction or neurogenic bladder → Foley catheter to reduce retrograde pressure on
     the kidneys.
   • Nephrostomy tubes are usually capped so that native ureteric patency can be tested.

 Dosing and Pharmacokinetics
   • 5–10 mCi (185–370 MBq) intravenous push for both MAG3 and DTPA.
   • MAG3 sequence: extraction nearly 60% → peak cortical uptake 3–5 min → collecting system shortly
     after → bladder by 10–15 min. The half-clearance time (peak to 50% clearance) for a non-obstructed
     unit is given as 15–20 min.

 Image Acquisition and Interpretation
   • Perfusion phase: frames every 1–2 seconds for about 1 minute; regions of interest (ROIs) over the
     aorta, each kidney, and just outside each kidney for background subtraction; counts per unit time
     plotted as a time–activity curve (TAC).
       ◦ Renal activity should appear within seconds of the aorta, with a brisk upstroke matching the aortic
         curve.
       ◦ A slow rise to peak suggests poor flow and likely poor function.
   • Functional phase: about 1 frame per minute. Relative function = area under each TAC between 1 and
     3 minutes. A split difference up to 10% is normal (i.e. up to 45:55).
   • Obstruction: a healthy kidney clears tracer within 15 minutes; an obstructed unit retains it, giving a
     progressively rising TAC with no peak.
   • Delayed pelvic clearance without true obstruction (a patulous system) is separated by furosemide: 0.5
     mg/kg intravenously, maximum 40 mg in adults; higher doses in renal impairment.
   • Timing is not agreed: the authors give furosemide 15 minutes after the tracer (F+15); others at the
     start (F0) or 15 minutes before (F−15). Always read the report to learn whether and when a diuretic
     was given.

 Additional Applications of ⁹⁹ᵐTc-MAG3 and ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DTPA Scintigraphy
 Evaluation of Renal Vascular Hypertension
   • Mechanism: autoregulation keeps GFR near-constant via renin from juxtaglomerular cells. Perceived
     low arteriolar flow → renin: angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → angiotensin-converting enzyme
     (lung) → angiotensin II → aldosterone release, efferent arteriolar constriction (maintaining filtration
     pressure) and peripheral vasoconstriction (hypertension).
   • Renal artery stenosis keeps the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system constantly active →
     hypertension and, in time, glomerular sclerosis.
   • Scintigraphy separates renovascular from essential hypertension. DTPA is preferred because it is
     cleared purely by glomerular filtration.
   • Two-day protocol: day 1, oral captopril (an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor) → dynamic
     renogram; stenosis shows slow uptake and low peak → if abnormal, repeat 1–2 days later off
     angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and calcium channel blockers → improvement of 10% or
     more = high probability of renovascular hypertension → likely benefit from angioplasty or surgery.
   • Why captopril unmasks stenosis: the stenotic kidney maintains GFR through angiotensin II–mediated
     efferent constriction; blocking it drops filtration in that kidney.
   • Use has declined with CT angiography, magnetic resonance angiography and Doppler ultrasonography.

 Renal Transplant Evaluation
   • MAG3 or DTPA helps separate acute rejection from delayed graft function (prolonged ischaemia at
     harvest), whose management differs. Renal biopsy is the best differentiator.

   • Caveats: overlapping causes of graft failure are common, and ischaemia can progress to chronic kidney
     disease, making serial imaging misleading — clinical context is essential.
   • Anastomotic (urine) leak: tracer collecting outside the kidney on delayed images — valuable because
     transplant patients often cannot receive iodinated contrast.

 Assessment of Vesicoureteral Reflux
   • Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) is retrograde flow from bladder to upper tracts, common in children,
     causing ascending infection and eventual loss of renal function.
   • Indirect method: MAG3 or DTPA, then void once tracer is in the bladder. Direct method (more
     common): ⁹⁹ᵐTc-sulfur colloid instilled into the bladder.
   • Initial screening must be fluoroscopic voiding cystourethrography (VCUG), not scintigraphy: children
     with urinary tract infection also need assessment for posterior urethral valves (males) and bladder
     diverticula, which require anatomic detail.
   • Nuclear cystography is used for follow-up — more sensitive for subtle VUR, lower radiation dose than
     contrast cystography.

 Renal Cortical Imaging With ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DMSA
   • Preparation as for dynamic imaging. Dose: 50 µCi/kg in children, 5 mCi in adults, intravenously →
     images at 2 hours with a pinhole collimator or SPECT. Uptake depends on filtration, so imaging may
     need delaying in renal failure.
   • Normal: homogeneous, smooth parenchyma. Acute inflammation or infection: photopenic defects.
     Scarring: also photopenic.
   • Serial imaging about 6 months apart separates the two: pyelonephritic defects resolve; scars persist.

   KEY POINTS — FUNCTIONAL IMAGING OF THE KIDNEYS
     • The commonest renal agents are ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DTPA, ⁹⁹ᵐTc-MAG3 and ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DMSA.
     • DTPA and MAG3 measure renal blood flow, differential function and obstruction.
     • DTPA is cleared by glomerular filtration; MAG3 by tubular secretion.
     • MAG3 is preferred at most centres: higher extraction efficiency, less affected by renal function.
     • DMSA is retained by proximal tubular cells and is used for infection and scarring.
     • DTPA and MAG3 also evaluate renovascular hypertension, transplant function and VUR.


 Concept — Each renal tracer images the part of the nephron that handles it: filtration (DTPA) gives GFR,
 secretion (MAG3) gives the best dynamic curves whatever the GFR, and tubular retention (DMSA) gives a
 static map of the cortex.

 INFECTION IMAGING
   • Genitourinary infection is usually identified clinically and by culture; difficulty arises in children,
     neurological disorders and immunocompromise.
   • ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DMSA is the most widely used agent for renal infection, though its role has declined with CT.
     Active pyelonephritis → decreased uptake that resolves; scar → persistent photopenia with cortical
     thinning. Scars also occur in VUR without documented pyelonephritis. Scarring predisposes to
     hypertension and chronic kidney disease.
   • ¹¹¹In-oxine leucocytes: oxine is a lipid-soluble chelator that diffuses passively into leucocytes.
     Sequence: draw ~50 mL venous blood → remove erythrocytes → label leucocytes → reinject. Normal
     uptake in liver, spleen and bone marrow; uptake elsewhere means infection. Blood-pool clearance
     half-life ~7.5 h → whole-body imaging at ~24 h.

   • Intra-abdominal infection without localising symptoms or a convincing collection: ¹¹¹In-oxine

   • ⁶⁷Ga-citrate: binds transferrin → carried to inflammation. Drawbacks: non-specific uptake in
     metabolically active sites, delayed imaging to 48–72 h, high cost.

 MOLECULAR IMAGING OF GENITOURINARY MALIGNANCIES
   • 2-deoxy-2-[¹⁸F]fluoro-D-glucose (¹⁸F-FDG) is the commonest oncological PET tracer. Sequence: glucose
     analogue enters via GLUT transporters → phosphorylated by hexokinase → cannot diffuse out →
     lacking the 2-hydroxyl group it cannot be metabolised further → trapped and imaged.
   • Physiological uptake in brain and kidneys. Malignant uptake reflects the Warburg effect (aerobic
     glycolysis) — a shift from oxidative phosphorylation to lactic acid fermentation.
   • FDG-imageable: urothelial carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma (RCC), penile squamous cell carcinoma,
     testicular germ cell tumours. Urinary excretion obscures the tract, so FDG is reserved for distant
     disease.
   • Prostate cancer rarely uses aerobic glycolysis → poorly seen on FDG.

 Bladder Cancer
   • FDG is the most studied PET tracer for bladder urothelial carcinoma (Fig. 7.4). For nodal staging a
     meta-analysis showed modest sensitivity but high specificity. PET upstages a significant minority
     thought to have organ-confined disease and changes management, including to palliation.
   • Guidelines do not support routine FDG-PET in clinically localised disease, because outcome benefit is
     unproven. American Urological Association (AUA): PET only when conventional imaging is equivocal,
     or to confirm a finding that cannot be biopsied.

   • Upper tract urothelial carcinoma: FDG-PET is most useful for distant disease; guidelines keep
     conventional imaging for staging and follow-up; PET selectively for patients at risk of metastatic
     recurrence who cannot have contrast-enhanced imaging.
   • Prognostication (emerging): preoperative maximum SUV independently predicts recurrence after
     radical cystectomy with extended lymphadenectomy; FDG-PET may predict histological response to
     induction or neoadjuvant chemotherapy; metabolic response after systemic therapy then cystectomy
     is associated with lower progression and cancer-specific death in advanced disease.
   • ¹¹C-choline and ¹¹C-acetate (membrane building blocks, concentrating in rapidly dividing cells):
     potentially more sensitive than FDG, but not approved, and the 20.3-minute half-life of ¹¹C has limited
     adoption.

 Kidney Cancer
   • FDG-PET detects recurrent or metastatic RCC with good sensitivity and specificity, but adds little
     beyond anatomic imaging. It is most sensitive for type II papillary RCC.
   • Guidelines recommend against routine FDG-PET for staging or follow-up of RCC; moreover clear cell
     RCC (ccRCC), the commonest subtype, is less FDG-avid than other subtypes.
   • Carbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX): a cell-surface protein nearly universally expressed by ccRCC but not
     other histologies. Leading agent: monoclonal antibody girentuximab (G250).
   • ¹²⁴I-girentuximab phase III trial: for distinguishing localised ccRCC from other histologies, sensitivity
     86.2% and specificity 85.9%, outperforming contrast-enhanced CT (sensitivity 75.5%, specificity
     46.8%).
   • ZIRCON (confirmatory phase III) uses ⁸⁹Zr-girentuximab (Fig. 7.5), because ⁸⁹Zr is "charge-trapped" in
     the tumour cytoplasm after antibody internalisation → higher intratumoural signal, less blood-pool
     activity. It is also being studied for nodal and distant ccRCC. At the time of writing, not yet approved.
   • ⁹⁹ᵐTc-sestamibi SPECT/CT (available off-label): a lipophilic cation binding cells rich in mitochondria;
     approved for breast and myocardial imaging and widely used for parathyroid adenoma. It accumulates
     in oncocytic tumours (high mitochondrial content, low multidrug-resistance pump expression) →
     oncocytoma and hybrid oncocytic/chromophobe tumours are "hot", ccRCC is "cold" (Fig. 7.6). A 2024
     meta-analysis showed high sensitivity and specificity, but the literature is small and adoption limited.

 Penile Cancer
   • Penile squamous cell carcinoma is highly FDG-avid.
   • Palpable inguinal nodes: FDG-PET reliably confirms metastasis and its extent (very high sensitivity on
     meta-analysis).
   • PET-positive nodal disease carries worse overall survival, and only PET-positive patients benefited
     from neoadjuvant chemotherapy → FDG-PET helps steer PET-positive patients to neoadjuvant
     chemotherapy.
   • Limited role: non-palpable nodes (poor sensitivity for occult metastases) and primary tumour staging;
     data for distant disease are scarce.

 Testis Cancer
   • Seminoma: European Association of Urology (EAU) endorses FDG-PET for a residual post-
     chemotherapy mass >3 cm, to separate fibrosis from viable tumour; meta-analysis showed moderate-
     to-good sensitivity and specificity.
   • Non-seminomatous germ cell tumour (NSGCT): FDG-PET not indicated — no benefit over CT plus
     serum markers. Standard of care is retroperitoneal lymph node dissection for residual masses >1 cm;
     a PET tracer for viable NSGCT is a critical unmet need.

Chapter 7 — Genitourinary Tract Imaging: Basic Principles of Nuclear Medicine   Molecular Imaging of Genitourinary Malignancies

   KEY POINTS — MOLECULAR IMAGING
     • Molecular cancer imaging most commonly uses ¹⁸F-FDG PET.
     • Several genitourinary malignancies are FDG-imageable, with varying utility beyond anatomic
       imaging.
     • FDG is excreted in urine, so it is used mainly for distant disease.
     • FDG has little role in prostate cancer; other tracers were developed for it.
     • PSMA-targeted PET has become the standard of care for prostate cancer imaging in many parts of
       the world.
     • A well-established FDG-PET indication is residual seminoma after chemotherapy.


 Concept — A tracer is only as useful as the biology it exploits: FDG follows glycolysis, so it fails in indolent
 prostate cancer and clear cell RCC and is swamped by urinary excretion, whereas target-specific agents
 (PSMA, CAIX, mitochondrial content) succeed because they image a molecule the tumour actually carries.
