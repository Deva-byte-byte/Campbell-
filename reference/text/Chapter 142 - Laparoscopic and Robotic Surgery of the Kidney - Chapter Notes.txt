Chapter 142 — Laparoscopic and Robotic Surgery of the Kidney                                        Introduction


 Chapter 142 — Laparoscopic and Robotic Surgery of the
 Kidney
   • First minimally invasive renal operation: laparoscopic nephrectomy, Clayman and colleagues,
     Washington University, St Louis, 1991, in an 85-year-old woman.
   • Since then applied to every surgical renal pathology.
   • Against open surgery: equivalent long-term outcomes, faster recovery, no additional cost.
   • Robotic technology drove widespread adoption of minimally invasive renal surgery.
   • Consistent advantages over open surgery: less pain and blood loss, shorter convalescence, better
     cosmesis.

 PATIENT EVALUATION AND PREPARATION
   • Same principles as general minimally invasive surgery (MIS) and the equivalent open operation
     (Chapters 11, 12 and 18).
   • History and examination anticipate intraoperative problems; body habitus dictates positioning and
     access.
   • Prior abdominal or retroperitoneal surgery alters trocar placement and the choice of transperitoneal
     versus extraperitoneal access.
   • With experience, several former absolute contraindications are now relative; careful selection matters
     most during the learning curve.

 Anaesthetic Considerations for Laparoscopic and Robotic Renal Surgery
   • General anaesthesia is mandatory; cardiopulmonary function must tolerate it.
   • Pneumoperitoneum compromises ventilation, venous return and cardiac output.
   • High-risk comorbidities: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), intracranial pathology,
     cardiac disease — each poses haemodynamic problems with insufflation.
   • Severe COPD may be unable to compensate for pneumoperitoneum-induced hypercarbia → options:
     lower insufflation pressure → helium insufflation → conversion to open surgery.
   • AirSeal Intelligent Flow System (valveless trocar): continuous low-pressure, high-flow
     pneumoperitoneum → lower carbon dioxide (CO2) consumption → potentially better tolerance of
     laparoscopy.
   • Robotic surgery:
       ◦ Dedicated robotic theatre to accommodate robot, tower and console.
       ◦ Robot heavy and bulky with a wide external arc of arm movement → protect head, shoulders and
         arms from collision.
       ◦ Docking and undocking delay patient assessment and management in an emergency.
       ◦ Surgeon at the console is less likely to notice the patient slipping → close communication between
         anaesthetist, bedside assistant and surgeon is essential.

 Considerations in Obese Patients
   • Difficulties: access; anatomy hidden or distorted by fat; restricted trocar and instrument range; longer
     instruments; higher pneumoperitoneum pressures.
   • With experience and technical modification, safe in obese patients.

   • Body mass index (BMI) is a poor predictor of difficulty: it separates neither fat from muscle nor
     visceral from subcutaneous fat.
   • Visceral adipose tissue is the better index of operative difficulty, particularly when BMI is normal.

 Considerations in Elderly Patients
   • Benefits of MIS apply irrespective of age.
   • Particularly valuable in the elderly: less pain and opioid use → early mobilisation → shorter stay.
   • Safe and valid in carefully selected elderly patients.
 Concept — What the patient must tolerate is the pneumoperitoneum, not the renal operation;
 cardiopulmonary reserve, not age or BMI, therefore governs fitness for minimally invasive renal surgery.

 SURGICAL APPROACHES AND OBTAINING ACCESS
   • Categories, each laparoscopic or robotic: transperitoneal; retroperitoneal; hand-assisted; single-site;
     natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery (NOTES).
   • Choice depends on resources, surgeon experience, and patient and disease characteristics.
   • Transperitoneal is the most widely used; retroperitoneal remains viable for selected patients and
     pathologies, and demands familiarity with landmarks to create a working space without breaching the
     peritoneum.

 Patient Positioning
  • Sequence: intravenous access → induction → endotracheal intubation → bladder catheter →
    sequential compression stockings → modified flank position.
   • Modified flank: operative side up 30–45°; back supported on rolled blankets or gel rolls.
   • Lower arm on a padded armrest, upper arm flexed at the elbow across the chest (or on pillows); head
     and cervical spine on towels, blankets or foam donuts; pillows beneath the knees.
   • Pad all pressure points; secure with wide cloth or silk tape so the table can be tilted; rotate the bed to
     each side to test stability.
   • Theatre arranged so the whole team can see the procedure (left nephrectomy: two monitors; scrub
     technician placed for instrument exchange).
   • Prepare and drape the entire abdomen and flank in case of conversion.
 Trocar Placement in Transperitoneal Laparoscopic Surgery
  • Pneumoperitoneum by Veress needle or Hasson (open) technique → three to five trocars;
    configuration adapted to surgeon preference.
   • Template:
       ◦ 10-mm camera port at the umbilicus.
       ◦ 5- or 10-mm midline port, about 2 cm below the xiphoid.
       ◦ 12-mm port, anterior axillary line at the level of the umbilicus — sutures, clamps, staplers.
   • Short patient: 12-mm port moved to the midline, halfway between umbilicus and pubis.
   • Obese patient: shift all trocars laterally.
   • Accessory ports: low midline 10/12 mm for colon retraction; 5-mm subxiphoid for liver retraction.

 Transperitoneal Robotic Approach
   • Allows surgeons with limited laparoscopic skills to perform MIS.
   • Advantages: three-dimensional (3D) view, more degrees of instrument motion, motion scaling,
     tremor elimination, ergonomic seated posture, wrist articulation easing suturing.
   • Positioning and port placement mirror laparoscopy.
   • Three-arm configuration: camera periumbilical; two robotic ports in the anterior axillary line — one
     above the iliac crest, one subcostal; arm spacing 8 cm (Si) or 6 cm (Xi); 12-mm assistant port in the
     low midline for sutures, clamps, staplers, suction, retraction.
   • Linear configuration: all 8-mm ports along the ipsilateral lateral rectus sheath; camera just cranial to
     the umbilicus; one port 6–7 cm cranial, two caudal about 6–7 cm apart; assistant port midline between
     cranial and camera ports.
   • Whatever the template, ports form a broad-based triangle with the target anatomy.
   • No stapler anticipated (e.g. pyeloplasty) → 5-mm assistant port suffices; additional 5- or 12-mm
     subxiphoid port for liver retraction.

   • Short patients: risk of arm collision; obese patients: lateral shift.
 Concept — Every port template is the same triangle — camera at the apex, working ports at the base,
 target at the far point; body habitus only moves or compresses the triangle.

 Retroperitoneal Approach
   • Resembles open flank surgery; peritoneal cavity not violated. Indications, advantages, limitations and
     outcomes as in the table above.
 Patient Positioning and Trocar Placement in Retroperitoneal Laparoscopy
  • Full flank; table flexion widens the rib–iliac crest gap; axillary roll protects the lower arm; pad and
    secure for tilting.
   • Surgeon stands behind the patient.
   • Initial incision: transverse, posterior axillary line, halfway between the tip of the 12th rib and the iliac
     crest.
   • Creating the space — options:
       ◦ Visual obturator + 12-mm trocar + laparoscope → enter under vision → insufflate → sweep with the
         scope.
       ◦ Blunt split of flank muscles → lumbodorsal fascia divided → finger dissection between posterior
         abdominal wall and kidney; correct position = smooth psoas and lower pole within Gerota's fascia
         felt.
       ◦ Balloon dilator displaces fat and peritoneum anteromedially; home-made from two fingers of a size
         8–9 glove → removed → blunt-tip trocar with cuff cinched to skin prevents gas leak.
       ◦ 0° lens through a visual obturator; characteristic yellow retroperitoneal fat confirms the correct
         plane → insufflate → develop with scope, working-channel Kittner or suction; peritoneum pushed
         medially.
   • Trap: balloon must lie wholly within the retroperitoneum — inflation within muscle → postoperative
     flank hernia.
   • Plane errors (pair): too anterior → peritoneal breach or colon injury; too posterior → bleeding from
     quadratus lumborum or psoas.
   • Then orientate → 5-mm trocar just off the 12th rib tip; 12-mm trocar posterior and superior to the
     camera, both under vision.
   • Bail-out: convert to transperitoneal by opening the peritoneum under direct vision.
 Figure notes —
  • Fig. 142.7 variant: full lateral, hips flexed, kidney rest elevated; 15-mm incision 2 cm below the 12th rib
    tip, between rib and anterior superior iliac spine; glove-finger balloon tied with silk over a red rubber
    catheter and filled with 600–800 mL saline; balloon-and-collar blunt-tip trocar needs no sutures and
    rotates 360°.
 Patient Positioning and Trocar Placement in Retroperitoneal Robotic Surgery
  • Space developed as for retroperitoneal laparoscopy.
   • 8-mm camera port, midaxillary line, 2 cm above the iliac crest; second and third ports in the posterior
     and anterior axillary lines, 1 cm cephalad; assistant port 4 cm cephalad to the third arm, anterior
     axillary line.
   • Robot docked over the patient's head, not from the side → can dock perpendicular or parallel to the
     bed.

 Hand-Assisted Laparoscopy
   • Hand-assisted laparoscopy (HAL): first HAL nephrectomy in the pig 1996; human 1998.
   • Combines open advantages — tactile feedback, manual retraction, haemostasis — with laparoscopic
     visualisation and small incisions.
   • Uses: adjunct for surgeons with limited laparoscopic training; popular in donor nephrectomy; hand
     port added when a laparoscopic case becomes difficult.
   • Suits the novice where dense perirenal scarring is expected, e.g. xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis
     (XGP); aids intracorporeal suturing and knot tying.
   • Emergency role: extend a port site → hand port → finger pressure on the bleeding vessel while open
     conversion is prepared — keep in the armamentarium.
 Patient Positioning and Trocar Placement in Hand-Assisted Laparoscopic Renal Surgery
  • Positioned as for transperitoneal surgery.
   • Sequence: hand-port incision through skin, fascia and peritoneum → hand port placed →
     pneumoperitoneum → further trocars under vision → lubricated hand inserted; laparotomy sponge
     optional for retraction and haemostasis.
   • Site depends on side, habitus, handedness, preference; incision not too large (gas leak, loss of
     pneumoperitoneum); hand must not obstruct the laparoscope.
   • Forearm pressure 30–100 mmHg → tingling, numbness and pain in hand and forearm.
   • Specimen extracted in an entrapment bag through the hand port; irrigate copiously before closure
     (infection).

 Single-Site Minimally Invasive Renal Surgery
   • Aim: further reduce morbidity by consolidating ports into one skin incision — laparoendoscopic
     single-site surgery (LESS) — or through a natural hollow viscus (NOTES).
   • LESS: entire spectrum of renal procedures performed; pure NOTES: very few.
   • Hybrid NOTES: standard laparoscopy with the vagina as access and extraction route in nephrectomy.
   • NOTES adoption limited by no clear therapeutic benefit, safety concerns, restrictive selection.
   • LESS: longer operation, shorter stay, less analgesia; held back by lack of triangulation, optical
     limitation, technical complexity.

 Single-Port Robotic Surgery
  • Robotic surgery (2000) revived single-site surgery — aims: less trauma, shorter stay, faster recovery.
   • First single-port (SP) robotic urological surgery 2009: standard robot, 12-mm scope + two robotic
     ports through a multichannel port in a 2-cm umbilical incision; successful, but technically difficult.
   • da Vinci SP, 2018: purpose-designed; 27-mm entry guide (see table); on-screen navigator shows each
     instrument's position in real time to prevent collision.
   • Limited availability; feasibility, safety and promising outcomes reported.
   • Assistant port for devices the platform lacks (staplers, suction).
 Patient Positioning and Trocar Placement in Single-Port Robotic Renal Surgeries
  • Flank 60°, flush with the bed edge; bed elevated so the arm clears the floor and the assistant can
    exchange instruments.
   • 10–25 cm between cannula tip and target — room to deploy elbow and wrist joints; avoids external
     collision.
   • Sequence: 3-cm periumbilical incision → entry guide → insufflation at 10–12 mmHg.
   • Assistance: separate AirSeal trocar, or a GelPort housing insufflation and entry guide and giving
     distance to the target; mini-GelPort or SP Access Kit prevents gas leak and accommodates entry guide,
     AirSeal port and flexible suction.
   • Remote-operated suction irrigation (ROSI) device steered by a robotic arm gives constant suction and
     irrigation.
   • If assistance through the GelPort fails → extra 5- or 10-mm lower-quadrant assistant port.
   • Mini-Pfannenstiel alternative: cosmesis, less pain → opioid-free and same-day surgery pathways.
 Single-Port Retroperitoneal Access and Trocar Placement for Renal Surgeries
  • Narrow profile suits the confined retroperitoneum; technique mimics multiport.
   • Sequence: full flank → 2.5–3-cm transverse incision 2 cm anterior and inferior to the 12th rib tip →
     split flank muscles → thoracolumbar fascia → enter retroperitoneum, sweep peritoneum medially →
     balloon dilatation → 25-mm GelPort or Access Kit → entry guide.
   • Instrument clock: camera 12 o'clock; monopolar scissors 3 o'clock; Maryland forceps 9 o'clock.

 SIMPLE NEPHRECTOMY
   • Indications — benign renal disease: chronic pyelonephritis; obstructive or reflux nephropathy; renal
     tuberculosis; multicystic dysplastic kidney; renovascular hypertension; acquired renal cystic disease in
     dialysis patients; nephrosclerosis; symptomatic autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease
     (ADPKD); post-transplant hypertension.
   • Chronic refractory pyelonephritis, including XGP, can be managed minimally invasively, but dense
     perinephric adhesions and lost planes make simple nephrectomy at times harder than radical, with a
     higher conversion rate.
   • Predictors of difficulty: previous pyonephrosis and an indwelling nephrostomy tube.
   • Stone disease carries the highest intraoperative and postoperative complication rates (national
     database).
   • Rescue manoeuvres: hand assistance or subcapsular nephrectomy.

 Procedure
   • Safe retroperitoneally or transperitoneally, laparoscopically or robotically; steps similar to radical
     nephrectomy.

 Reflection of the Colon
  • Left side:
       ◦ Incise peritoneum along the white line of Toldt from below the lower pole to above the upper pole,
         lateral to the spleen (endoshears, bipolar or ultrasonic energy); avoid the diaphragm; extend
         inferiorly as needed.
       ◦ Leave the lateral renal attachments intact at this stage — otherwise the kidney rolls over and
         obscures the hilum.
       ◦ Divide the lienocolic ligament → spleen, pancreas and colon fall medially; divide thin colorenal
         attachments → retract colon medially.
       ◦ Key plane: brighter-yellow mesenteric fat versus pale-yellow retroperitoneal (Gerota's) fat — the
         avascular plane between them avoids colonic injury and incision of mesocolon or Gerota's fascia.
       ◦ Mesocolic defect → repair (internal hernia).
       ◦ Paddle retractor via an extra low midline trocar retracts colon, pancreas and spleen.
   • Right side:
       ◦ Liver retractor via the anterior axillary line or a high midline port to expose the upper pole.
       ◦ Medial traction on the colon reveals colorenal attachments → divide.
       ◦ Cold sharp dissection around the duodenum — avoids thermal injury.
       ◦ Kocher manoeuvre (lateral duodenal mobilisation) → exposes medial kidney, hilar connective tissue
         and inferior vena cava (IVC).
 Dissection of the Ureter
  • Sequence: psoas muscle and tendon below the lower pole → follow medially → gonadal vessels met
    first → ureter just posterior to them → elevate ureter, sweep gonadal vessels medially → follow up to
    the lower pole.
   • Peristalsis distinguishes the ureter from adjacent vessels.
   • Do not divide the ureter yet — it is the handle for lifting the kidney.
   • Sweep tissue behind ureter and lower pole anteriorly to bare the psoas; stay above the psoas fascia →
     protects cutaneous nerves → avoids thigh numbness, pain, paraesthesia.
   • Right side: tearing the gonadal vein at its insertion into the IVC during elevation causes significant
     bleeding.
 Identification of the Renal Hilum
  • Exposure: colon and small bowel medial (gravity or low midline retractor) + anterolateral lift of the
    kidney out of the renal fossa → hilum on stretch.
   • Lateral grasper under ureter and kidney until it abuts the sidewall; never into the parenchyma.
   • Layer-by-layer blunt dissection until the renal vein appears; gonadal, lumbar and accessory veins
     occluded and divided as needed.
   • No clips in the anticipated staple line → stapler misfire.
 Securing the Renal Blood Vessels
  • Renal artery usually lies posterior to the vein; preoperative imaging maps hilar anatomy.
   • Blunt and sharp dissection: irrigator-aspirator tip, hook electrode, scissors, forceps.

   • Sequence: artery ligated and divided first → then vein, with an endoscopic vascular (gastrointestinal
     anastomosis, GIA) stapler; stapler must cover the whole vessel.
   • Left renal vein stapled lateral to the adrenal vein; any clips on gonadal or adrenal vessels excluded
     from the stapler jaws.
   • En bloc hilar stapling is a safe alternative (see Radical Nephrectomy).
   • Bleeding along the staple line → reinforcing clips.
   • Clips and staplers have equivalent safety — device failure, severe haemorrhage, conversion, mortality
     (meta-analysis, donor nephrectomy).
   • Polymer clips safe when properly placed: 2-mm separation between the two clips, a few millimetres
     from the cut end of the artery.
 Dissection of the Upper Pole
  • After hilar division, dissection continues posteriorly and superiorly to the upper pole.
   • Adrenal preservation: incise Gerota's fascia anteriorly just above the hilum → peel perinephric fat
     circumferentially off the upper pole (LigaSure laparoscopically; vessel sealer robotically).
   • Then clip and divide ureter → divide lateral attachments → rotate kidney anteriorly over the liver
     (right) or spleen (left) → divide uppermost attachments under vision.
   • Extreme fibrosis → subcapsular nephrectomy once artery and vein are controlled.
   • Avoid diaphragmatic injury.
 Organ Entrapment and Extraction
  • Specimen in an extraction bag → intact through an extended trocar site or Pfannenstiel incision, or
    piecemeal after morcellation (ring forceps, Kocher clamp) under endoscopic vision to avoid bag
    disruption.
   • Fig. 142.20: camera moved to the lateral port and Endo Catch passed through the umbilical site; for
     Pfannenstiel extraction the Endo Catch enters through a separate 10-mm suprapubic incision.
     Morcellation technique: see Specimen Extraction under Partial Nephrectomy.
 Postoperative Management
  • Diet as tolerated; urethral catheter out once comfortably ambulant; activity as tolerated.
   • No heavy lifting for 4–6 weeks where there is an extraction incision.

 Results
   • Laparoscopic versus open simple nephrectomy: less pain and blood loss, shorter stay, quicker return to
     activity; comparable complications.
   • Retroperitoneal and transperitoneal equivalent in experienced hands — operative time, blood loss,
     stay.
 Concept — Simple nephrectomy for inflammatory disease is paradoxically harder than radical
 nephrectomy for tumour, because inflammation obliterates the tissue planes that tumour usually leaves
 intact.

 SURGERY FOR RENAL CYSTIC DISEASE
   • Renal cysts are common but rarely need surgery.
   • Indications: pain, infection, obstruction, concern for malignancy (Fig. 142.22: peripelvic cysts on
     delayed-phase contrast computed tomography [CT] in left flank pain).
   • Sequence: percutaneous aspiration first — tests whether the cyst causes the symptoms → recurrence
     → decortication, marsupialisation or unroofing.

   • Laparoscopic treatment achieves better symptomatic and radiological success than percutaneous
     aspiration, with similar complications.
   • Multiple and bilateral cysts can be treated in one operation.
   • ADPKD: laparoscopic cyst decortication effective for cyst-related pain; end-stage renal disease with
     enlarged, symptomatic or infected kidneys → bilateral synchronous laparoscopic nephrectomy;
     intractable ADPKD pain → laparoscopic renal denervation (both efferent and afferent sympathetic
     fibres ablated).
   • Cyst suspicious for malignancy → explore, biopsy, and treat by cryoablation, enucleation, or partial or
     radical nephrectomy.

 Procedure
   • Mobilise → incise Gerota's fascia → expose capsule → identify cyst (intraoperative ultrasound helps);
     hilar control usually unnecessary.
   • Sequence: dissect cyst wall free before draining → aspirate (laparoscopic needle) → grasp and excise
     wall along the cyst–parenchyma junction → evacuate fluid by suction → biopsy suspicious areas (5-
     mm biopsy forceps) → fulgurate the base (electrocautery or argon-beam coagulator).
   • Ablating the base risks inadvertent or occult entry into the collecting system; packing with
     haemostatic agents or suturing may be needed.
   • Collecting system entered → close it and leave a drain.
   • Malignancy found → extirpation or cryoablation of the remaining lesion or kidney.
   • Drain technique (Fig. 142.24): hemostat through a side stab incision → drain passed via a trocar to the
     hemostat → colon brought back over the kidney and fixed to the sidewall to "reperitonealise" kidney
     and drain.

 Results
   • Highly effective: symptom improvement in the great majority, high radiological resolution, durable pain
     relief; major complications rare.
 Concept — Aspiration is diagnostic before it is therapeutic: it proves the cyst is the pain generator before
 decortication is committed to.

 RENAL BIOPSY FOR MEDICAL RENAL DISEASE
   • Central to diagnosis and management of medical renal disease; default is percutaneous biopsy under
     CT or ultrasound guidance.
   • Laparoscopic biopsy preferred for: failed percutaneous biopsy; anatomical abnormality (multiple
     cysts, aberrant vessels); bleeding diathesis; morbid obesity; solitary kidney; patient choice or non-
     compliance with percutaneous biopsy.
   • Adequate tissue and excellent haemostasis in almost all cases; more glomeruli than percutaneous
     biopsy (matched-pair analysis).

 Procedure
   • Retroperitoneal is more efficient than transperitoneal; full flank; two-port technique usual (LESS also
     described).
   • 10-mm trocar above the iliac crest, posterior axillary line; 5-mm trocar at the same level, anterior
     axillary line.
   • Ultrasound localises the kidney in abundant retroperitoneal and perinephric fat.

   • 5-mm two-tooth biopsy forceps: two or three cortical samples from the lower pole after opening
     Gerota's fascia; or 16-gauge Tru-Cut needle passed percutaneously under vision for deeper tissue.
   • Haemostasis: manual compression, electrocautery, topical collagen mesh, argon-beam coagulator.

 Results
   • Short operation, negligible blood loss, about one day in hospital, rare complications, diagnostic in all
     cases.
 Concept — Laparoscopic biopsy exists for the patients in whom a blind percutaneous pass is unsafe or has
 failed — those who bleed, those with one kidney, those who cannot be reached.

 NEPHROPEXY
   • Surgical fixation of a mobile (ptotic) kidney.
   • Nephroptosis: descent >5 cm, or two vertebral bodies, on intravenous urography from supine to erect
     (Fig. 142.26, from El-Moula et al., 2008).
   • Usually right-sided; young, slim women; asymptomatic in 20% of healthy women.

     urinary drainage on imaging.

 Procedure
   • Incise posterior peritoneum → expose psoas and quadratus lumborum fascia → strip Gerota's fascia
     to the capsule → complete nephrolysis (free from peritoneum and colon) for full mobility.
   • Suture fixation (commonest): interrupted non-absorbable 2-0 polypropylene (Prolene), lateral and
     posterior capsule to underlying muscle fascia; optional anterior capsule to parietal peritoneum.
   • Avoid the iliohypogastric and genitofemoral nerves during suturing.
   • Sliding-clip tightening: sutures placed loosely, ends joined by a clip, each clip slid down to tighten once
     all are placed.
   • Alternative sling: tension-free tape or polyglactin/polypropylene mesh — needle below lower pole →
     through abdominal wall → tape over lateral margin → through wall → tightened until the kidney is fixed
     to the dorsal wall.

 Results
   • High rates of pain relief, radiological correction and functional improvement on nuclear renography;
     no major complications; high satisfaction.
 Concept — Nephroptosis is common and usually harmless, so the operative trigger is never the descent

   • Lateralise by cystoscopy — chylous urine seen emanating from the ureteric orifice.

 Transperitoneal
 Procedure
  • Positioning and trocars as above; extra trocars or a hand port for large tumours, IVC involvement or

  • Minimally invasive versus open: shorter stay, less blood loss, fewer transfusions, earlier oral intake,

     mortality, recurrence or intraoperative complications.

 Special Considerations
 Large Tumours
  • Minimally invasive surgery is feasible and safe for most large renal masses.
   • Tumours >10 cm (registry): laparoscopic/robotic versus open — shorter stay; similar readmission and
     mortality; conversion higher laparoscopic than robotic; conversion adds about a day in hospital;
     minimally invasive use rising, open falling.
   • Problems: reduced working space; distorted landmarks → disorientation; hilum obscured by perihilar
     collaterals or by the kidney falling over it.
   • Aids: continuous reference to preoperative imaging; intraoperative ultrasound; hand port or extra
     trocars for lateral distraction and distributed retraction.
   • Approach individualised to patient, pathology and surgeon proficiency.

   • Same complications; operative time shortened by about three-quarters of an hour.

 PARTIAL NEPHRECTOMY
   • Partial nephrectomy (PN), where feasible, is now the default for renal masses: preserved function,
     less morbidity, equivalent oncology → paradigm shift from radical nephrectomy (RN).
   • Minimally invasive versus open PN: shorter operation, less blood loss, shorter stay.

 Indications
   • First laparoscopic PN 1993 (Winfield); retroperitoneal approach 1994.
   • Initially small exophytic clinical T1a masses (Fig. 142.33); now large and complex tumours.
   • RENAL nephrometry score: Radius; Exophytic/endophytic; Nearness to the collecting system;
     Anterior/posterior; Location relative to the polar lines.
   • Score predicts: complications, warm ischaemia time, conversion to RN, non-neoplastic parenchymal
     loss, collecting-system repair or injury, operative time, stay, blood loss, recurrence.
 Clinical Stage T1b and Greater Tumours
  • No randomised trials of PN versus RN for large masses; nephron-sparing surgery is feasible.
   • PN: better renal function; lower chronic kidney disease (CKD), recurrence, cancer-specific and all-
     cause mortality.
   • cT1–2 tumours upstaged to pT3a after PN: oncology not compromised, function possibly better.
   • >4 cm (cT1b+), laparoscopic PN versus RN: same margins and survival; two-stage CKD progression only
     after RN; far greater estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline after RN; PN longer and
     bloodier but equal complications and oncology.
   • pT1b: laparoscopic and robotic PN lower positive-margin rates than open; robotic less blood loss,
     shorter ischaemia, fewer intraoperative complications than laparoscopic — in high-volume centres (>50
     PN per year).
   • cT2a: robot-assisted PN (RAPN) versus minimally invasive RN — longer operation, more blood loss, but
     matched analysis: same oncology and morbidity; RN worse eGFR decline.
   • Contrary signal: tumours >5 cm upstaged to pT3a recur more after PN than after RN.
 Concept — For large tumours the case for PN rests on renal function, not oncology; the one oncological
 caveat is the >5 cm tumour upstaged to pT3a.

     intentional pelvicalyceal entry, sometimes needing repair.

 Positive Surgical Margins
   • Negative margins are always the goal; positive margins occur in 0–7% of minimally invasive PN series.
   • Recurrence risk doubled only in high-risk tumours (pT2–3 or nuclear grade 3–4); low-risk tumours
     (pT1, Fuhrman grade I–II) not affected.
   • Other series: disease-free survival unaffected by margin status; advanced clinical stage and
     pathological upstaging, not margin status, predict recurrence.
   • Microscopic positive margin → careful surveillance, not immediate reintervention, particularly in
     advanced stages.

 Tumour Seeding and Port-Site Recurrence
   • Rare — about 0.1% of laparoscopic cancer operations, most of them port-site recurrences.
   • Potential factors: tumour aggressiveness, immune status, local wound factors, surgical technique,
     tumour spillage.
   • Minimally invasive PN does not increase atypical recurrence (propensity-matched).
   • Not contributory: pneumoperitoneum, aerosolisation of tumour cells, insufflation gas, wound closure
     technique.
   • Prevention: sound technique, no spillage, extraction in an impermeable retrieval bag.

 Local Recurrence and Repeat Minimally Invasive Surgery
   • Isolated local recurrence: ipsilateral retroperitoneal nodes, renal fossa or adrenal — rarely recurrent
     venous thrombus — without distant metastasis.
   • Incidence about 2% after RN.
   • Literature mostly open (morbid); small laparoscopic series feasible without conversion; recurrence
     may appear many years after nephrectomy.
   • Surgical resection with curative intent + systemic therapy is superior to either alone, even with
     metastatic disease; large recurrence → consider intraoperative radiotherapy.
   • Salvage robotic surgery after PN or ablation: salvage RAPN (sRAPN) or salvage robot-assisted RN
     (sRARN); after prior PN most need sRARN; complexity may force RN; no major complications or
     conversions; good metastasis-free survival.
   • Recurrence in a prior resection bed is infiltrative, without a pseudocapsule → avascular planes hard
     to find.
   • RAPN for recurrence after ablation or PN: low conversion, transfusion and major complication rates;
     eGFR largely preserved.

 Surgical Salvage After Failed Ablative Therapies
   • Minimally invasive PN and RN feasible after failed cryoablation or high-intensity focused ultrasound

 LAPAROSCOPIC ABLATIVE TECHNIQUES
   • Temperature extremes kill tumour cells; goal: complete destruction with minimal or no morbidity.
   • Versus PN: less blood loss, shorter operation, less dissection, fewer complications.
   • Commonest indications: significant comorbidity, solitary kidney, hereditary RCC.
   • Modalities: cryoablation, radiofrequency ablation (RFA), microwave.

 Cryoablation
   • Transperitoneal or retroperitoneal by tumour location; no hilar clamping.
   • Sequence: mobilise → incise Gerota's → expose tumour (overlying fat may be excised) → biopsy (14-
     or 18-gauge needle) → cryoprobes placed percutaneously under laparoscopic and ultrasound
     guidance → two freeze–thaw cycles → probes removed with a gentle twist.
   • Probes parallel, in a triangular or quadrangular array with overlapping cryolesions; number and
     spacing set by probe ablation geometry and tumour size.
   • Probe tips just beyond the deepest tumour margin.
   • Iceball monitored in real time by ultrasound; extends 0.5–1 cm beyond the tumour edge.
   • Iceball must not touch collecting system, ureter, renal vessels or adjacent organs.
   • Bleeding: pressure, or fibrin glue or Floseal.

 Radiofrequency Ablation
   • Transperitoneal or retroperitoneal; no hilar control.
   • Probe into tumour → tines deployed to cover tumour plus a 1-cm margin of normal tissue.
   • Mechanism: frictional heating from ionic oscillation under monopolar or bipolar high-frequency
     alternating current → 60–100°C throughout the tumour.
   • Avoid >105°C — tissue vaporisation.
   • Ablation-zone size depends on electrode length, diameter, surface area and temperature.
   • No imaging monitors RFA in real time → treatment guided by direct temperature or electrical
     impedance.

 Results
   • Effective for small renal masses ≤3 cm; laparoscopic ablation now reserved for a highly selected group
     because percutaneous techniques have advanced.
   • Laparoscopic cryoablation for T1a: low treatment failure; durable 5- and 10-year disease-free survival.
   • Failure predictors: larger size, endophytic lesion, hilar location; 4–7 cm masses recur more after
     cryoablation than after PN.
   • No randomised trials against PN. Meta-analyses: ablation — better perioperative outcomes but higher
     local progression; metastatic progression higher in one analysis, similar in another.

 Complications
   • Laparoscopic versus percutaneous: similar overall rate; Clavien I commoner percutaneously, Clavien III
     commoner laparoscopically.
   • Laparoscopic cryoablation: modest overall complication rate, low major rate.
   • Risk factors: larger tumour, pre-existing heart disease, female sex.
 Concept — Ablation trades oncological certainty for low morbidity; its place is therefore the small (≤3
 cm), exophytic tumour in a patient who cannot afford an operation — and for that patient the
 percutaneous route has displaced the laparoscopic.

 Single-Port Robotic Renal Surgery
   • Evolved from the 2009 exploration; purpose-built platform, United States Food and Drug
     Administration (FDA) approval 2018; not yet widely available; safe and feasible transperitoneally and

 Concept — Single-site gains are confined to pain, cosmesis and stay; operative and oncological outcomes
 are unchanged, and the price is lost triangulation (LESS) or longer warm ischaemia (SP).

 COMPLICATIONS OF MINIMALLY INVASIVE RENAL SURGERY
   • Complication = deviation from the normal postoperative course; knowledge of each procedure's
     pitfalls prevents and manages them; risk discussion is part of informed consent.
   • Large series: overall complication rate about 15%, mostly postoperative.
   • Commonest intraoperative: bleeding, then bowel, spleen, pneumothorax, liver. Commonest
     postoperative: bleeding, wound infection, pneumonia, prolonged ileus.
   • By procedure: PN > RN > simple nephrectomy.
   • Robotic versus laparoscopic PN: same complication risk, about half the conversion to open.
   • Patient factors: obesity, comorbidity, prior abdominal surgery; advanced age is not a major risk factor.
   • Prior surgery is not a contraindication, but adhesions raise bowel injury risk → Veress and first trocar
     away from scars, open (Hasson) access, or retroperitoneal approach.
   • Disease factors: large tumours, central lesions; conversions cluster in infective disease (chronic
     pyelonephritis).
   • Same-day discharge: no increase in 30-day readmission, reoperation or complications (National
     Surgical Quality Improvement Program [NSQIP]).
   • Hospital volume inversely related to robotic PN morbidity, plateau at 18–20 cases per year (may
     reflect surgeon volume).
   • Learning curve about 20 laparoscopic nephrectomies — most complications in the first 20 cases; for
     laparoscopic PN, complications still falling beyond 750 cases.
   • Early recognition, communication and timely intervention limit consequences; complications are
     shared with open surgery but differ in type and presentation.

 Vascular Complications
   • Vascular injury is the commonest complication of minimally invasive urological surgery.
   • Major vascular injury: <1 in 1000 — but fatal in 8–17%.
   • Most life-threatening injuries occur at access (Veress needle, first trocar) or during hilar dissection.
   • Other causes: excessive IVC traction, Hem-o-lok clip disconnection, Endo-GIA misfire.
   • Hilar injury: structures divided before identification, sharp hilar dissection, stapler not covering the
     vessel or misfiring.

   • Arterial injury → clips, sutures, cautery or stapler as suits location and size; brisk bleeding → hand
     through a lower midline incision to compress → controlled suturing or conversion.
   • Mild-to-moderate venous bleeding → gauze pressure for several minutes, dissection may continue;
     visible hole → control with a grasper → clip or suture.
   • Never blind clipping or suturing — worsens injury.
   • Major injury sequence: locate → temporary control (clamp or compression) → inform anaesthetist
     and theatre (blood, conversion set) → clear the field → raise pneumoperitoneum or add a trocar →
     expose vessel → definitive clip or suture → if not feasible, convert and call vascular surgery.
   • Inadvertent ligation reported: IVC, aorta, contralateral renal vessels, mesenteric vessels. Left RN:
     superior mesenteric artery (SMA) mistaken for the left renal artery → bowel necrosis; risk higher in
     obesity and large medial or hilar tumours; meticulous hilar dissection prevents it; open conversion for
     rapid reconstruction usually needed.
   • Haemostatic device failure: vascular staplers > non-locking titanium clips > locking clips.
   • Stapler malfunction (Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience [MAUDE] database, RN): death,
     conversion, bleeding, reoperation; misfiring and jamming commonest; mostly operator misuse:
       ◦ Firing errors: tissue thicker than rated; stopping the firing cycle early; obstruction in the jaws.
       ◦ Loading errors: improper cartridge loading; wrong-size reload.
   • Preventable: stapling over clips; incomplete transection from malplacement → train surgeon and
     scrub staff.
   • End of case: inspect; lower insufflation pressure to unmask venous bleeding; commonest sites —
     dissection bed, adrenal, mesentery, gonadal vessels, ureteric stump.
   • Postoperative bleeding: hypotension + tachycardia + falling haematocrit → CT; few need exploration;
     renal arterial bleeding → angiography and embolisation.
   • Gross haematuria after PN → renal artery pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula → selective
     arterial embolisation (SAE), successful without loss of renal function; typically presents about 12 days
     after PN; endophytic and posterior tumours at higher risk; pseudoaneurysm the usual angiographic
     finding.
   • Thromboembolism: minimally invasive approach lowers risk versus open; post-discharge
     thromboembolism predicted by significant preoperative weight loss and chronic steroid use; deep
     vein thrombosis (DVT) risk very low → prophylaxis stratified by risk: low risk → sequential
     compression devices (SCDs); high risk → pharmacological (unfractionated or low-molecular-weight
     heparin); optimum controversial.

 Urinary Complications
   • Persistent urine leak: possible after any renal surgery that opens the pelvicalyceal system; falling with
     better renorrhaphy.
   • Incidence after laparoscopic and robotic PN 0.8–10%; also after cyst or tumour ablation.
   • Risk factors: large tumour, hilar location, pelvicalyceal repair, prolonged operation and warm
     ischaemia.
   • Management ladder: percutaneous drainage → if it fails or distal obstruction → ureteric stent with
     bladder decompression, or nephrostomy.

 Visceral and Bowel Complications
   • Unrecognised bowel injury: among the most significant complications; mortality about 3%; incidence
     <1%.

   • Prior abdominal surgery raises risk; 32% occur at access; thermal energy near bowel causes about
     half of unrecognised injuries; injury also occurs out of view during instrument passage.
   • Recognised intraoperatively: superficial thermal injury → imbricate with 3-0 silk; multiple
     enterotomies or devitalised segment → resection and anastomosis.
   • Presentation: persistent, increasing pain at the trocar site nearest the injury, without erythema or
     pus; distension, nausea, diarrhoea, anorexia, low-grade fever, bowel sounds preserved, white cell
     count low or normal → rapid deterioration and death if missed.
   • Thermal injuries present later than mechanical ones.

     unstable, distended or gross spillage → open exploration; controlled fistula → bowel rest and
     parenteral nutrition (months).
   • Mesenteric defects, especially left-sided, must be closed (internal herniation) without compromising
     colonic blood supply.

 Other Complications
   • Diaphragmatic injury during upper-pole dissection on either side; significant injury with pneumothorax
     → sudden rise in peak airway pressure, difficult ventilation — usually recognised at once.
   • Repair sequence: purse-string suture → closed tube (back end of a closed suction drain) into the
     defect under suction → withdraw while tightening the purse-string during a large inspiratory breath
     → chest radiograph; large or unrecognised pneumothorax → chest drain.
   • Unrecognised pneumothorax after laparoscopic nephrectomy about 0.3% → routine postoperative
     chest radiograph not recommended in asymptomatic patients at low suspicion.
   • Neuromuscular injury in 2.7% of laparoscopic urological surgery: abdominal wall neuralgia, motor and
     sensory deficit, rhabdomyolysis, orchialgia — from surgical trauma or positioning.
   • Rhabdomyolysis possibly commoner with laparoscopy → shorter operations; partial rather than full
     flank position; suspect with pain in back, buttocks, thighs or calves → serum creatine kinase,
     myoglobinuria → aggressive hydration, correct metabolic acidosis.
   • Axillary roll in full lateral decubitus → prevents brachial plexus injury.
   • Preserve psoas fascia in posterior dissection → prevents thigh paraesthesia.

 SUMMARY
   • MIS effectively treats surgical renal pathology: less perioperative morbidity than open surgery,
     without sacrificing therapeutic outcome.

   KEY POINTS: LAPAROSCOPIC AND ROBOTIC SURGERY OF THE KIDNEY
    • Less incisional pain, shorter convalescence, better cosmesis than open surgery, with comparable
      efficacy and long-term outcomes.
    • Indications are the same as for open renal surgery.
    • Oncological principles unchanged: complete excision with negative margins, with maximal
      preservation of renal parenchyma where feasible.
    • Familiarity with landmarks, techniques, instruments and each procedure's pitfalls is the key to
      success.
