Chapter 4 — Basic Principles of Urologic Ultrasonography

Chapter 4 — Urinary Tract Imaging: Basic Principles of
Urologic Ultrasonography
 • The “urologist’s stethoscope”: much of the genitourinary tract cannot be examined clinically, and
   ultrasound often gives superior and faster diagnostic information than plain radiography, computed
   tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).
 • Excellent safety profile, low cost and portability make it a natural first-line point-of-care tool in the clinic,
   the ward and the operating room.
 • Its multiple modes and live imaging assess fluid and flow dynamics, which is why it suits urology in
   particular.
 • The same physician performs and interprets the study, fusing technical and anatomical expertise; this is
   its defining advantage over radiology-performed imaging.
 • Full use requires three competencies: the physical principles, the technical skill to image each organ, and
   the expected normal appearances of the major structures.

Concept — Each milestone applied one physical principle — echo timing, Doppler shift or focused energy — to

oldest Doppler trap in urology and still stands.

PHYSICAL PRINCIPLES
 • Every ultrasound image is the product of sound interacting with tissue.
 • Sequence of generation: short bursts of alternating current → crystals in the transducer → piezoelectric
   expansion and contraction → mechanical wave → coupling medium → skin → body → partial reflection →
   transducer “listens” and reconverts mechanical to electrical energy.
 • The waves are longitudinal: particle motion is in the direction of propagation, creating alternating
   compression and rarefaction of tissue.
 • The transducer needs direct, secure contact to transmit and receive.
 • Repeated transmission and reception at short intervals give a real-time image. Urological imaging uses
   3.5–20 MHz.
WAVE NOMENCLATURE

 • Amplitude sets pixel brightness on the grey-scale image: greater amplitude → brighter pixel.

Ultrasound Image Generation
 • The transducer is both sender and receiver; pulses are partly reflected and returned echoes are
   converted to electrical signal. It acts as a receiver for more than 99% of the time.
 • Signal chain: master clock → pulse generator → transducer → echoes analysed for amplitude and transit
   time → time-gain compensation (TGC) → amplifier → scan converter → monitor. The image is a series of
   vertical lines continuously refreshed.

 • The focal zone is operator-adjustable and marked on screen by a caret; the example places it on the
   medial renal cortex. Modern transducers add beam steering, multiple foci and azimuthal-plane focusing.
 • Velocity = frequency × wavelength. Average velocity in soft tissue is 1540 m/s; being constant, frequency
   and wavelength vary inversely.
 • The resolution–penetration trade-off: 7–18 MHz gives excellent resolution for superficial structures
   (testis, paediatric kidney); deeper structures need lower frequency and therefore have poorer axial
   resolution.

Doppler Ultrasound
 • Principle: a wave striking a moving object is shifted in frequency according to the object’s direction and
   velocity and the angle of insonation; mostly used for blood, also for urine flow.
 • Resistive index (RI) = (peak systolic velocity [PSV] − end-diastolic velocity [EDV]) / PSV; reflects
   peripheral vascular resistance; used in renal artery stenosis, ureteral obstruction and penile arterial
   insufficiency.

Harmonic Scanning
 • Principle: waves propagating through tissue undergo non-linear distortion, generating harmonic
   frequencies at multiples of the transmitted (fundamental) frequency; the transducer selectively receives
   the harmonic to build the image.
 • Harmonics are less affected by noise and artifact → clearer image, better contrast resolution; improves
   small structures and reduces tissue clutter. A cyst with a mural nodule shows less spurious internal and
   distal echo.
 • Used in contrast-enhanced ultrasound to improve visualisation of contrast agents.

Spatial Compounding
 • The direction of insonation is altered electronically and a composite image generated → less artifact and
   noise. Urological uses: Peyronie plaques and renal cysts.

Sonoelastography
 • Ultrasound “palpation”: hard lesions are often pathological; elastography maps tissue elasticity
   (compressibility and displacement) in colour. It needs a compressing mechanical wave within the tissue.

 • Principle: the same force gives a larger displacement in softer tissue; measuring displacement estimates
   hardness and separates benign (soft) from malignant (hard).
 • Young’s modulus: E = σ / ε (stress / strain); E is higher in hard tissue.

   generated low-frequency vibration in shear wave elastography [SWE]) → tissue imaged to analyse strain
   → stiffness parameters defined.

 • Spatial resolution encompasses both axial and lateral resolution.
 • Colour coding is set by the manufacturer or user — always read the colour bar, as with colour Doppler.

Three-Dimensional Scanning
 • Widely used in obstetrics and gynaecology, limited in urology. A composite dataset is manipulated to
   generate new views (e.g. testicular flow on power Doppler).
 • Roles: procedural planning, precise volumetry, and recognition of tissue patterns inapparent on 2D.

SOLID ORGAN EVALUATION
Renal Masses
 • Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) enhances the acoustic signal of blood flow within a mass, helping
   characterise solid renal tumours.
 • Sequence: small intravenous bolus → systemic circulation → renal perfusion → real-time imaging of
   enhancing flow in the mass → mass distinguished from normal parenchyma.
 • Less accurate than CT or MRI for initial work-up, but useful for tumour vascular architecture, live
   intraoperative assessment during cryoablation, and solid-tumour surveillance; it detects recurrent RCC
   in chronic renal insufficiency, where nephrotoxic contrast is best avoided.

ASSESSMENT OF COLLECTING SYSTEM AND URINARY SPACE
 • Contrast injected directly into the urinary space shows collecting system anatomy and urinary patency.
 • Intravenous microspheres never opacify the collecting system — they are too large to cross the
   glomerular filtration barrier.
 • Viewed in B-mode or harmonic mode; harmonic mode delineates contrast from debris better but
   sacrifices refresh rate.
 • Applications: vesicoureteral reflux in children (contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography); ureteric
   patency after percutaneous nephrolithotomy; fidelity of retrograde renal access during ultrasound-
   guided stent placement; experimentally, rete testis injection of germ cells for autotransplantation.
Concept — Route determines compartment: intravenous microbubbles stay intravascular and characterise
perfusion; to image urine, the contrast must be placed in urine.

Images
 • Each image carries patient identification, date and time, and clear orientation; measurements identified;
   anatomy and abnormalities labelled; interpretable by any trained sonographer; attached to or accessible

 • Orientation convention: superior to the left of the screen, inferior to the right. For paired organs, right

 • Number of images: as many as needed to document a systematic, complete examination and the

Report and Image Storage
 • Electronic records ease documentation but complicate archiving: images are storage-heavy and contain
   protected health information, so storage must meet regulation; cloud storage brings opportunity and
   security challenges, answered by validated systems for practices of any size.

Renal Ultrasound
 • Comprehensive studies remain standardised radiology examinations, but a focused point-of-care
   examination by the urologist is highly useful.

Technique
 • Best viewed through the flank, supine, prone or flank position.
 • Sequence: probe longitudinal on the midaxillary line just below the costal margin, parallel to the body
   axis, cranial end on screen left → slide or rock cranio-caudally to centre the kidney → rotate parallel to
   the ribs to remove rib shadow → if not found, move medially and cephalad → rotate for transverse views
   of hilum and collecting system.
 • B-mode first: gain set so the collecting system is nearly black and parenchyma grey; depth set to
   maximise kidney size; colour Doppler for hilar and intraparenchymal vessels and RI.
 • Left versus right: technique identical, but the left kidney is slightly more cephalad, has no liver window
   and may be obscured by the splenic flexure → reposition or breath-hold after inspiration.

Indications
 • Renal and perirenal masses; transplant health; hydronephrosis; urolithiasis; haematuria when CT or MRI
   with intravenous contrast is not possible; point-of-care detection of bladder clot as a cause of retention;
   guidance for percutaneous access, biopsy, cyst aspiration or renal mass ablation.

Normal Findings
 • Sagittal view: entire cortex pole to pole; central hyperechoic band = perihilar fat and vessels.
 • Adult kidney: 10–12 cm long, 4–5 cm wide; use nomograms (age, height, weight) in children; volume
   may be appropriate in severe renal impairment.
 • Measure in the midsagittal and midtransverse planes — off-axis planes give spuriously low values.
 • Echogenicity: adult cortex slightly hypoechoic to liver and much more hypoechoic than retroperitoneal
   and perihilar fat; infant cortex relatively hyperechoic with a smaller central band; chronic medical renal
   disease → thinned cortex, iso- or hyperechoic to liver. The capsule may appear as a bright thin line.
 • Pyramids are hypoechoic, prominent in children, and mistaken for calyces; distinguish by position:
   pyramids lie outside the hilar fat, the collecting system inside it.
 • Randall’s plaques: bright spots or lines at the tip of each calyx.

Interventional Ultrasound Applications
Percutaneous renal access.
 • Increasingly the urologist’s modality of choice for percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) access:
   continuous imaging of kidney and needle without radiation to patient or surgeon, and visualisation of
   liver, spleen, colon and lung (invisible on fluoroscopy) → widens the usable access window.
 • Access from longitudinal or transverse views after removing rib shadow: longitudinal — shift the target
   calyx toward the side of needle entry; transverse — centre it.
 • Plan puncture from above, below or beside the probe according to position (prone, flank, modified
   lithotomy) for maximal freedom; the angle must keep the needle hub clear of iliac crest, ribs and table;
   adjust the probe dynamically to hold the target.
 • Longitudinal in-plane sequence: puncture a set distance from the probe end, bisecting probe width, at
   an angle approximating the target → keep the tip aligned with the probe midline → visualise the tip
   throughout its trajectory (either approach).
 • Dilation under fluoroscopy or ultrasound; under ultrasound, keep the wire visible across the parenchyma.
   Coaxial and wrapped wires are echogenic; hydrophilic wires are poorly visible. Polyurethane dilators
   obscure the wire — its disappearance marks dilator depth.
Limitations of ultrasound-guided access.
 • Patient: long skin-to-stone distance, no hydronephrosis, staghorn stones, poor tissue echogenicity.
   Anaesthetic: respiratory rate, tidal volume, depth of relaxation.
 • Learning curve short for access, significantly longer for ultrasound-guided dilation.

Percutaneous Renal Biopsy
 • Office ultrasound-guided biopsy under local anaesthesia has been safe and effective for two decades.
 • Bottom lines: more passes did not raise complications; severe hypertension did; outpatient and
   inpatient complication rates are equivalent; in large series intervention for bleeding is rare, minor

   haematoma and self-limiting macrohaematuria are uncommon, haemodynamically irrelevant
   arteriovenous fistula is not rare on Doppler, and adequate tissue is almost always obtained.
 • Six-hour observation is comparable to 24-hour admission: the chief complication, gross haematuria,

 • Safe in children and the elderly.

Limitations
 • Obesity, bowel gas and deformity impede complete evaluation.
 • Poor sensitivity for renal masses < 2 cm.
 • No tumour-type specificity except angiomyolipoma (AML), which is distinctively highly echoic — but
   some small RCCs are indistinguishable from AML.
Concept — Renal ultrasound is read against two landmarks — the liver for echogenicity and the hilar fat for
anatomy — and its weakness is small or hyperechoic masses, where only cross-sectional imaging can decide.

Transabdominal Pelvic Ultrasound
 • Non-invasive assessment of the lower urinary tract and prostate in men and bladder in women;
   automated bladder scanners for volume or post-void residual (PVR) only.

Technique
 • Patient supine, sonographer on the patient’s right; warm room, draping for privacy, roll beneath hips if
   needed; moderately full bladder.
 • Sagittal and transverse scanning, angling beneath the pubis; prostate size and morphology shown, though
   at lower resolution than transrectal imaging.
 • Transvaginal or transrectal routes for obese or otherwise unsuitable patients.

Indications
 • Bladder volume or PVR; prostate size and morphology; secondary signs of BOO; bladder wall
   configuration and thickness; haematuria of lower-tract origin; ureteroceles; ureteric obstruction;
   perivesical collections; clot retention; catheter position; retained catheter removal; suprapubic tube
   guidance; volume before and after flow-rate study.

Normal Findings
 • Assess lumen, wall configuration and thickness; document stones or tumours; assess distal ureters,
   prostate, uterus and ovaries (uterus lies posterior to the bladder).
 • Ureteric jets are demonstrable, but their clinical value is questioned; up to 10 minutes of continuous
   observation may be needed to confirm absence.
 • Bladder volume = width × height (transverse) × length (midsagittal) × 0.625; accurate to within 10–20%
   for 100–500 mL.
 • Bladder wall thickness varies with volume and site; measured posteriorly near the midline (trabeculation
   visible); it predicts BOO better than free uroflowmetry, PVR or prostate volume.
 • Transabdominal prostate sequence: angle beneath the pubis → fan transverse probe inferiorly to the
   largest transverse diameter → measure width and height → rotate 90° clockwise → fan to the midline,
   identified by the V-shaped bladder-neck indentation (with hyperechoic trigone) → measure length from

   and middle lobe.
 • Intravesical prostatic protrusion (IPP): perpendicular distance from the bladder-base line to the greatest
   intravesical protrusion on sagittal view; correlates well with urodynamic BOO.

Procedural Applications
 • Suprapubic bladder aspiration with or without catheter (neonates, children, adults); treatment of

Limitations
 • Empty bladder limits the study: distal ureteric obstruction, stones and tumours need a full bladder;
   prostate assessment is easier when full. Protuberant abdomen or panniculus impedes.
 • Automated bladder volume measurement uses ultrasound but is not an imaging study → inaccurate
   PVR in obesity, clot retention, ascites, bladder diverticulum, or perivesical collections (urinoma,
   lymphocele).
Concept — The full bladder is the acoustic window: every pelvic finding depends on urine as the medium,
and the automated scanner fails precisely where fluid outside the bladder mimics urine inside it.

Ultrasonography of the Scrotum
 • No area suits ultrasound better: superficial contents allow high-frequency, detailed anatomical and
   physiological imaging correlated with examination findings.

Technique
 • Quiet, warm room; supine, scrotum supported on a towel or thighs; penis draped out of the field.
 • Copious gel — scrotal hair traps air and creates artifact.
 • Complete but gentle contact: pressure moves or compresses the testis, altering echogenicity, obscuring
   detail and distorting volume.
 • Linear array 7–18 MHz; a 4-cm transducer is more manoeuvrable, a 7.5-cm one images the whole testis in
   one sagittal view.
 • Systematic sequence: sagittal from midline → medial → lateral; transverse from mid-testis → upper pole
   → lower pole; then epididymis and all scrotal contents.

Indications
 • Scrotal or testicular mass; pain; trauma; infertility; follow-up after scrotal surgery; empty or abnormal
   scrotum.

Normal Findings
 • Document size (and volume) and echo architecture: homogeneous, finely granular.
 • Always compare both testes side by side: diffuse infiltration (lymphoma, leukaemia) gives a diffusely
   hypoechoic, homogeneous testis, possibly unilateral, visible only against its mate; the bilateral view also
   documents two testes.
 • Paratesticular fluid helps identify the epididymis, appendix testis and appendix epididymis.
 • Intratesticular flow is low velocity: mean PSV < 10 cm/s; supplied by the testicular artery dividing into
   septal branches.

 • Mediastinum testis: hyperechoic linear structure on sagittal view where fibrous septa coalesce —
   normal, not a lesion.
 • Spectral Doppler: intratesticular RI > 0.6 suggests impaired spermatogenesis.
 • Increased, irregular flow with hypoechoic areas of reduced vascularity was seen in necrotising vasculitis.

Procedural Applications
 • Non-palpable lesions localised for needle biopsy or dye injection to guide open biopsy.
 • Guidance for percutaneous testicular and epididymal sperm aspiration; future spermatogonial stem cell
   transfer after gonadotoxic therapy.

Sonoelastography
 • Rationale: malignant lesions are stiffer (higher vessel and cell density).
 • Bottom lines: RTE showed high positive and negative predictive value and accuracy for malignant versus
   benign testicular lesions; non-obstructive azoospermia has different testicular elasticity from obstructive
   azoospermia and normal controls.
 • Not yet sufficient to forgo surgery on its findings.

Limitations
 • Hallmark of torsion: absent intratesticular flow. Paratesticular flow in epididymal collaterals may appear
   within hours and mislead.
 • Compare with the contralateral testis to confirm the study is technically capable of showing
   intratesticular flow.
 • Ultrasound cannot diagnose torsion — only the surgeon (or pathologist) can.
Concept — Scrotal ultrasound is an exercise in comparison: the contralateral testis is the control for
echogenicity, for flow and for the technical adequacy of the study itself.

Ultrasonography of the Penis and Male Urethra
 • Exquisite anatomical detail; can often replace studies using ionising radiation.

Technique
 • Linear array 12–18 MHz; longitudinal and transverse planes; dorsal and ventral surfaces; warm, quiet
   room with draping.
 • Systematic from base to glans; proximal urethra and corpora through the scrotum or perineum.
 • Sonourethrography for stricture: retrograde saline or sterile gel distends the urethra, showing urethral
   and spongiosal anatomy. Versus fluoroscopic retrograde urethrography: no radiation, and can be done in
   lithotomy during urethroplasty without repositioning.

Indications
 • Penile vascular dysfunction; corporal fibrosis; foreign body; urethral stricture; urethral diverticulum;
   penile trauma or pain.

Normal Findings
 • Scan dorsally or ventrally; the ventral approach compresses the urethra and spongiosum even with
   minimal pressure.
 • Transverse: paired corpora cavernosa dorsally, corpus spongiosum and urethra ventrally in the midline.
 • Sagittal: cavernosal artery as a hyperechoic double line within the corpus cavernosum; corpus
   spongiosum iso- to slightly hypoechoic, containing the coapted urethra.

 • The urethra is collapsed except during voiding. Calcifications (between the corpora, or in the cavernosal
   artery wall) cast posterior shadows.

Perineal Ultrasound
 • Transducer on the perineum shows the proximal urethra and corpora: bulbar urethra with the bulbar
   artery, proximal cavernosal bodies with the cavernosal artery.
 • The internal pudendal artery gives the bulbourethral, dorsal and cavernosal arteries; the most proximal
   cavernosal artery is best seen perineally.
 • Bulbospongiosus (bulbocavernosus) muscle (BCM) cross-sectional area: novel marker of androgen
   receptor sensitivity, inversely related to cytosine–adenine–guanine (CAG) repeat number and directly
   related to total and free testosterone and dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) bone density.

Transperineal Ultrasound
 • Transperineal and translabial ultrasound assess the pelvic floor diagnostically and after procedures;
   unlike transvaginal imaging they are non-invasive and do not distort anatomy.
 • Compartments: anterior — bladder and urethra; apical (central) — vagina and uterus; posterior —
   rectum; 2D and 3D.
 • Translabial: 5-MHz curved array between the labia majora; shows urethral diverticula, tumours, foreign
   bodies; real-time bladder–urethra–pelvic floor relationships in stress urinary incontinence and pelvic
   organ prolapse.
 • Synthetic slings and meshes are echogenic → locate a midurethral sling in failure, erosion or de novo
   voiding dysfunction.

Procedural Applications
 • Commonest penile use: erectile dysfunction (ED) and penile curvature. Pharmacostimulation
   (intracavernosal vasoactive injection) quantifies cavernosal artery flow velocity.

Transrectal Ultrasonography of the Prostate
 • Transrectal ultrasonography (TRUS) is the ultrasound study most commonly performed by urologists:
   minimally invasive, exquisite prostatic and periprostatic detail, real-time information.

Technique
 • High-frequency 7.5–10 MHz, biplane or single-plane (end-fire or side-fire).
 • Sequence: digital rectal examination (DRE) before probe insertion — pain, tenderness, rectal stricture,
   mass or bleeding may preclude TRUS → survey scan base to apex, including seminal vesicles and rectal
   wall → seminal vesicles in transverse for symmetry of echogenicity, seminal vesicle height and ampulla
   (vas) diameter → midsagittal and transverse prostate for anteroposterior, height and length → machine
   calculates volume, predicted prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and PSA density (PSAD) → colour Doppler
   as needed.
 • Rectal wall thickness and findings must be documented; rectal cancer, polyps or inflammation require
   evaluation or referral.

Indications
 • Prostate volume for PSAD; abnormal DRE; ultrasound-guided biopsy; cysts; prostatic abscess (evaluation
   and aspiration); suspected congenital anomaly; lower urinary tract symptoms; pelvic pain; prostatitis/
   prostatodynia; haematospermia.
 • Infertility (e.g. azoospermia): low-volume or poorly motile ejaculate, cysts, hypoplastic or dilated seminal
   vesicles, impaired motility, antisperm antibodies.

Normal Findings
 • Assess echogenicity by comparing left and right. The “sonographic capsule” reflects the prostate–fat
   impedance difference; the urethra is prominent because urethral muscle reflects poorly.
 • Base: superior, contiguous with the bladder base. Apex: inferior, continuous with the striated urethral
   sphincter.

Microultrasound
 • ~29 MHz versus 3.5–12 MHz conventionally → much higher resolution, less penetration; detects very
   small tumours.
 • First described in 2003 (animal model, Japan); now widely used for prostate evaluation and biopsy
   guidance.
 • Detection rates and negative predictive value comparable to mpMRI at much lower cost.
 • Example: a tumour invisible at 9 MHz appeared at 29 MHz as a mottled hypoechoic area scored PRI-MUS
   4 (Prostate Risk Identification using Micro-Ultrasound) — Gleason 4+3 cancer.

Special Considerations
Paediatrics.
 • Preferred modality in paediatric urology: safety, low cost, no ionising radiation, and small body size
   allows imaging of deep structures not usually seen in adults.
 • Diagnoses and monitors congenital anomalies, urinary tract infection, obstructive uropathy and
   vesicoureteral reflux.
 • Smaller probes in newborns and infants; examinations take longer owing to size and movement —
   distraction or gentle restraint.
Pregnancy.
 • Ionising radiation is relatively contraindicated → ultrasound is first-line, with minimal fetal risk and safe
   in all trimesters.
 • Hydronephrosis with possible ureteric stone needs a multidisciplinary approach; grey-scale sensitivity for
   ureteric stones is poor.
 • Adjuncts: transvaginal ultrasound for distal ureteric stones, RI and ureteric jets on Doppler; ultrasound-
   guided stent exchange reduces fetal radiation.

PRACTICE ACCREDITATION
 • Why: quality assurance, patient expectations and third-party payer requirements. Few laws regulate
   ultrasound — any licensed physician may buy a machine and perform and interpret studies.
 • Two accrediting agencies: American College of Radiology (ACR) and AIUM. They began developing
   programmes in 1995 and accredited the first practices in 1996; both accredit urology practices.
 • American Urological Association (AUA)–AIUM partnership: accreditation pathway recognised by
   regulators and payers, plus training guidelines and practice parameters for urologists.

 KEY POINTS
   • An ultrasound wave is a mechanical wave producing alternating compression and rarefaction.
   • Axial resolution improves, and penetration falls, with increasing frequency; optimal imaging trades
     resolution against depth.

   • Capture as many images as needed to document a systematic, complete examination and relevant

   • AML is characteristically hyperechoic, but some RCCs are too.
