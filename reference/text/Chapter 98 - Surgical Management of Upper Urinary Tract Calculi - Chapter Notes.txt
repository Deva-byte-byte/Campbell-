 CHOOSING YOUR APPROACH
 American Urological Association Guidelines
   • The 2016 joint American Urological Association (AUA) and Endourological Society Guideline is the
     reference framework for upper tract stones, built around Index Patients.
   • Decision making rests on shared decision making plus a synthesis of the best available evidence, with
     the caveat that endourology innovates faster than guidelines revise.
   • Management is driven by three domains — stone-related, renal anatomic and clinical (patient)
     factors.

   • The trap it sets: obesity, coagulopathy and pregnancy are listed as patient factors, not
     contraindications — each excludes a different modality, not surgery itself.

 Asymptomatic Renal Stones
   • Truly asymptomatic means no symptoms and no sequelae, including no recurrent urinary tract
     infection (UTI). Prevalence in screened populations is approximately one in ten.
   • The natural history is not benign — roughly half sustain a stone-related event within five years.
   • Predictors of an event across cohorts: number of stones, previous stone history, stone size, renal
     pelvis location, male sex and younger age.
   • Lower pole stones are the commonest site observed; over medium-term follow-up about a third
     enlarge, a fifth pass spontaneously and a tenth come to intervention.
   • Stone size is not a reliable predictor of symptom development, but it does predict intervention — risk
     rises above 5 mm and again above 10 mm. Size forecasts treatment, not symptoms.
   • Residual fragments >4 mm after ureteroscopy (URS) should be treated — higher rates of growth,
     complications and reintervention.
   • Sorensen et al. (2022), landmark randomised trial: removing small asymptomatic contralateral stones
     at the time of surgery for the symptomatic side cut relapse to 16% versus 63% over a mean 4.2 years.

   • Large asymptomatic stones may cause focal calyceal obstruction without symptoms — counsel
     regarding gradual focal renal deterioration and infection risk.
   • Non-obstructing asymptomatic stones with recurrent UTI are a special case: the association is clear
     and removal is recommended irrespective of composition. Send the stone for culture — it confirms
     the nidus and directs tailored antibiotic therapy.
   • Where treatment is declined, the alternative is observation with a shift to medical prevention plus
     serial imaging.

   KEY POINTS: ASYMPTOMATIC RENAL STONES
     • Approximately half of patients will have a stone-related event over five years.
     • Number of stones, previous stone history, size and location all matter in counselling.
     • Counsel about treating contralateral stones, even if asymptomatic.
     • Residual stones >4 mm should be treated after ureteroscopic treatment.
     • With recurrent UTIs, treating the stone may eradicate the infections.

 Preoperative Evaluation
   • Intravenous pyelography is largely obsolete, displaced by renal ultrasound, plain radiography of the
     kidneys, ureters and bladder (KUB), and increasingly computed tomography (CT), which is sensitive and
     specific even at low dose.
   • AUA position: clinicians should obtain non-contrast CT before percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL),
     and may obtain it when choosing between shock wave lithotripsy (SWL) and URS.
   • Two SWL-specific CT variables: stone-free rate (SFR) is highest when stone density is <900 Hounsfield
     units (HU) and skin-to-stone distance is <10 cm — neither obtainable without cross-sectional imaging.
   • Functional imaging where loss of ipsilateral function is suspected; contrast-enhanced CT for complex
     anatomy.
   • All patients require preoperative urinary testing for infection; add full blood count and serum
     electrolytes for relevant comorbidity or high bleeding risk. No randomised data on timing — urinalysis
     and culture within the month before surgery is the convention.

 Renal Stones: Ureteroscopy versus Shock Wave Lithotripsy versus Percutaneous
 Nephrolithotomy
   • Governing principle: more invasive procedures clear more stone, and that benefit must be set against
     operative risk, patient goals and risk tolerance.
   • Staged URS is the alternative to PCNL for large burdens — it avoids PCNL bleeding risk at the cost of
     longer cumulative operating time. PCNL bleeding risk has itself fallen with tract miniaturisation, from a
     historically quoted 7% to transfusion rates nearer 1%.

 SHOCK WAVE LITHOTRIPSY FOR RENAL STONES
 History
   • The tissue effect of shock waves was found by accident at Dornier, during work on raindrop and
     micrometeorite damage to aircraft, when an engineer touched the shock wave apparatus.
   • First human SWL: 7 February 1980, Dornier HM1. First HM3 installations: Stuttgart 1983 and
     Indianapolis 1984. The HM3 was large, fixed and expensive but outstandingly effective with an
     excellent safety profile — later generations traded efficacy for lower cost, smaller footprint and
     versatility.

 Mechanisms of Stone Fracturing
   • A shock wave is a short-duration acoustic pressure wave: peak positive 30–100 MPa followed by a
     negative tensile tail of about −10 MPa.
   • Waves are generated at F1 (in the therapy head), focused, and transmitted to the stone at F2.
     Machines differ chiefly in peak positive pressure and the size and shape of the focal zone at F2.
   • A focal zone larger than the stone improves breakage — greater stress within the stone and less
     penalty from respiratory movement of the target.

 Stone Comminution
  • Applied shock waves generate stresses in the stone; beyond a critical value cracks form, then grow and
    coalesce under repetitive stress. Five mechanisms are proposed.

   • 1. Tear and shear forces — elastic waves generated inside the stone reflect, superimpose and focus;
     cracks form where shear and tension are highest. Once a fragment is smaller than the pulse duration,
     internal wave propagation, and hence this mechanism, falls away.
   • 2. Spallation — the pulse inverts on reflecting at the soft tissue or fluid interface distal to the hard
     stone; incident and reflected waves overlap to create maximal tension that breaks the distal end of
     the stone.
   • 3. Quasistatic squeezing — when the wave is wider than the stone it travels faster in stone than in
     surrounding fluid, leaving the stone in a circumferential hoop of squeezing pressure.
   • 4. Cavitation — the negative phase generates bubbles at the stone; their collapse erodes the stone
     surface, and this matters more as fragmentation proceeds. The paired caveat: bubbles decay slowly, so
     excess cavitation shields the stone from subsequent shocks.
   • 5. Dynamic squeezing — with a focal area wider than the stone, mechanisms 1 to 3 combine: intense
     shear waves at the stone edges are reinforced by surrounding waves and focus at the distal end,
     coinciding with the spallation waves. This is the most powerful force in initial comminution.

 Factors to Improve Success and Safety
 Patient Factors
  • Efficacy is governed by stone density and composition, skin-to-stone distance, stone volume, location
    within the collecting system, and the Triple D score.
   • Two groups to divert to a more efficient modality: infection stones, and stones in multiple locations
     likely to need several sessions.
   • Absolute contraindications in the body text add severe untreated hypertension and aortic aneurysm
     >4 cm to the boxed list, and specify current antiplatelet, antithrombotic or anticoagulant use
     alongside uncorrected coagulopathy.

 Treatment Factors
  • Coupling quality is the key technical determinant. Apply ultrasound gel directly from the jug onto the
    therapy head as a large mound — never spread or layered.
   • Air pockets in the coupling medium are the classic avoidable error, degrading fragmentation by up to
     two-fifths. Contemporary machines such as the Dornier Delta III mount a camera in the therapy head
     to inspect coupling.
   • Manufacturers: Dornier, Siemens, Storz, Wolf and EDAP-TMS; head-to-head comparisons are few, and
     most modern multifunctional lithotripters use an electromagnetic source.

   • Shock rate: more than a dozen randomised trials give more than twofold better outcomes at 60–70 or
     80–90 shocks per minute than at 100–120. Fast rates accumulate a cavitation bubble cloud, causing
     more tissue injury and more shielding of the stone.
   • Ramping: stepwise voltage escalation improves fracturing and reduces renal injury. Slow ramping over
     at least four minutes, with or without a brief pause, produces detectable renal vasoconstriction early
     in the session. Low-energy pretreatment markedly reduces lesion size in animals and substantially
     lowers the risk of renal haematoma in humans.
   • Cavitation is essential to comminution but is likely the greatest contributor to adverse tissue effects —
     rupture of a small vessel allows blood to collect in tissue, which in turn increases further cavitation and
     injury.

 Preparation for Shock Wave Lithotripsy
   • Sequence: history and examination → review medications and conditions for contraindications and
     bleeding risk → imaging to confirm the stone is visible on the targeting modality, KUB or ultrasound
     → urinalysis with reflex culture → treat bacteriuria or UTI before the procedure.
   • AUA and EAU agree: antibiotic prophylaxis is unnecessary with a negative urinalysis and no risk
     factors.

 Complications
   • SWL is non-invasive but has been associated with injury to kidney, liver, skeletal muscle, colon, spleen
     and pancreas — exceptionally rare and usually not clinically significant.
   • Cardiac arrhythmia is common during treatment and usually resolves with a treatment pause;
     occasionally the machine must be gated to the refractory phase of the cardiac cycle.
   • Renal haematoma occurs in a minority, mostly asymptomatic or mild; rarely significant enough to need
     selective embolisation. Risk factors: increasing age, hypertension (especially poorly controlled),
     obesity, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, thrombocytopenia, narrow-focus machines with
     high peak positive pressures, higher energy settings, and greater total shock number.
   • Steinstrasse affects a small minority overall but rises several-fold with a stone burden above 2.0 cm².
   • Do not stent at the time of SWL — no improvement in passage rates, only added stent morbidity.
   • Medical expulsive therapy with alpha-blockers improves fragment clearance, reduces pain and
     shortens time to clearance. Mechanical percussion, diuresis and inversion therapy also improve
     clearance, in weekly ten-minute sessions over several weeks.
   • Refuted associations — worth knowing as negatives: diabetes mellitus, renovascular hypertension,
     long-term renal function decline and reduced fertility.

 Future Directions: Burst Wave Lithotripsy and Ultrasonic Propulsion
   • Two investigational technologies on one ultrasound platform: burst wave lithotripsy (BWL) breaks
     stones; ultrasonic propulsion repositions fragments. Both are usable awake, in clinic or the
     emergency department, with real-time ultrasound targeting of breakage, repositioning and cavitation;
     the probe is handheld or support-assisted.

   • Mechanism resembles SWL, but multiple reflections of many cycles superimpose internal waves and
     amplify peak negative pressure more than fivefold, so intrastone tensile stress matches SWL despite
     lower pressure through tissue.
   • Coupling remains essential, with gel; the safeguard is elegant — if coupling is lost, the image is lost,
     which itself signals the user. The beam must be wider than the stone, as with SWL. Excessive
     cavitation appears as a dynamic white cloud on the ultrasound image.
   • Propulsion repositions by momentum transfer when the focused wave reflects off the stone; force is
     directed away from the transducer, so the probe must be manipulated to steer fragments. Uses:
     accessing stones intraoperatively, and facilitating passage of ureteral stones and residual renal
     fragments.

   KEY POINTS: SWL, BWL AND ULTRASONIC PROPULSION
     • Modern lithotripters use an electromagnetic shock wave source.
     • Several mechanisms contribute to stone fracturing with SWL.
     • Stone density and composition, skin-to-stone distance, stone volume, location and the Triple D
       score influence SWL efficacy.
     • Absolute contraindications include pregnancy, uncorrected coagulopathy or antiplatelet/
       antithrombotic/anticoagulant use, severe untreated hypertension, aortic aneurysm >4 cm, and
       untreated UTI.
     • Gel application, minimising air bubbles, and 60–70 or 80–90 shocks per minute increase
       effectiveness.
     • Renal haematoma, steinstrasse and cardiac rhythm abnormalities are the known complications.


 URETEROSCOPY FOR RENAL STONES
 History
   • 1806 — Bozzini's Lichtleiter ("light conductor"), the first clinically used endoscope: a 35 cm tube of
     sheet metal, paper and leather housing a candle-holder and mirror system. Results were mixed,
     Bozzini died of typhoid in 1809, and the device was never clinically popularised — it survives in the
     William P. Didusch Museum of the AUA.

   • 1826 — Ségalas' "speculum urethrocystique", the first endoscope used for urological purposes;
     likewise never popular.
   • 1962 — Marshall visualised a ureteral stone with a fibreoptic endoscope, the advance that enabled
     miniaturisation and flexible urological endoscopes.
   • Charge-coupled device (CCD) technology, first described in the 1970s, was refined and made cost-
     effective from 2005; the first commercially available endoscope with a distal digital sensor appeared
     in 2010. The latest iteration is the single-use digital ureteroscope.
   • The universal limitation of flexible ureteroscopes is diameter — often exceeding the narrowest
     ureteral segments, especially the orifice — because the design must accommodate both a working
     channel large enough for irrigation and instruments and the CCD or fibreoptic bundle.

 Preoperative Considerations
   • General anaesthesia is typical, by endotracheal intubation or laryngeal mask airway. The trade-off as a
     pair: laryngeal mask is often preferable overall, but intubation allows precise control of the
     respiratory cycle, reducing renal excursion and improving lasering efficiency. URS under local
     anaesthesia alone is feasible.
   • Both AUA and EAU strongly recommend urinary testing for infection before elective URS.
   • Single-dose perioperative antibiotics are required, by institutional antibiogram. Asymptomatic
     bacteriuria or very high infection risk may warrant additional pretreatment; optimal duration is not
     agreed.
   • Antiplatelet and anticoagulant agents. The AUA minimum position: for patients who must remain on
     these agents, recommend URS rather than SWL or PCNL. Over-the-counter use without clear
     indication should be held; recent cardiac or vascular surgery makes interruption unacceptable.
   • The evidence, in order: higher bleeding rates on anticoagulation without change in SFR or
     complications in early work; meta-analysis does show increased bleeding-related complications;
     quality-collaborative data show URS on anticoagulation is safe whereas antiplatelet use increases
     postoperative hospitalisation.

 Principles of Retrograde Renal Access
 Wires
  • Wire design balances rigidity, hydrophilicity and cost; choice is largely surgeon preference.
   • Both AUA and EAU recommend a safety wire for most endoscopic procedures, though the EAU
     concedes the recommendation is controversial.
   • The counter-evidence, as a pair: insertion and retraction forces are higher when passing a
     ureteroscope alongside a safety wire, and a multicentre study found no outcome difference with or
     without one.
   • Wires can harm: with severely impacted stones the wire may not pass, and forceful advancement
     causes submucosal passage.
 Ureteral Access Sheaths
  • Takayasu, 1974 — the first ureteral access sheath (UAS), a "guide tube for pyeloureteroscopy" devised
    to negotiate the intramural ureter, where scopes habitually met obstruction.
   • Intrarenal pressure: any sheath lowers intrarenal pressure versus unsheathed URS, in cadaveric and in
     vivo animal models, and larger sheaths lower it more — at presumed cost of greater injury risk.
   • The examinable disconnect: UAS demonstrably reduces intrarenal pressure, yet meta-analyses have
     not shown a clear reduction in postoperative infection.

   • Dusting versus basketing is surgeon preference; the sheath's practical advantage is simplified repeated
     passes of the scope, favouring basketing and extraction of larger stone volumes. Evidence is split —
     one single series showed improved SFR with a sheath, the large CROES prospective study found no
     difference, and a multicentre dusting-versus-basketing comparison found equivalent SFR with
     basketing procedures substantially longer.
   • Injury: in a consecutive series, UAS placement caused ureteral injury in nearly half of patients, severe
     (involving smooth muscle layers) in about one in eight. Pre-stented patients were at lower risk,
     presumably from prior passive dilatation. Against this, videorecorded series with 12/14 Fr sheaths and
     long follow-up found almost no de novo strictures.
   • Why the true stricture rate is unknown: around two-fifths of patients have no imaging at all in the
     year after URS, and many of the remainder have KUB only, which cannot assess hydronephrosis.
 Baskets
  • Variety in size, material and shape, with limited comparative data. Trade-off as a pair: smaller baskets
    are more fragile but permit greater irrigation through the working channel. Some designs extract
    faster, particularly for novices. Selection remains surgeon preference.
 Irrigation Devices and Pressure Management
   • CCD miniaturisation has shrunk working channels to typically 3.6–4.6 Fr, so pressurised irrigation is
     usually needed to overcome resistance and maintain vision.
   • Hand-action pump use was associated with higher postoperative fever and emergency department
     presentation compared with gravity bags.
   • Ranked by force generated: gravity least, then pressurised systems and foot or hand pumps, with
     considerable variability.
   • Pressure sensors embedded in a wire or in the ureteroscope allow real-time intrarenal pressure
     monitoring — promising, but clinical trials are needed before the data can be acted on.
 Anti-Retropulsion
  • Dretler, 2001 — the "Stone Cone": nitinol and stainless steel wires as an expandable tapered cone
    placed cephalad to the stone during laser lithotripsy; it effectively prevented proximal migration, with
    very few reinterventions for retropulsed fragments over subsequent use.
   • Similar results with the NTrap device (also improved SFR) and the BackStop thermosensitive polymer;
     modified patient positioning also reduces retropulsion.
   • Use varies widely by country, probably on cost grounds — in a large global study, only about one in
     seven cases used such a device.
 Stenting
  • AUA: consider omitting a stent after uncomplicated URS when all four conditions hold — normal
    anatomy, normal renal function, no concern for ureteral injury, and no planned second-stage
    procedure.
   • Rationale: multiple meta-analyses show no clear benefit of routine post-URS stenting, against well-
     documented stent-related urinary symptoms and significant quality-of-life reduction.
   • Practice lags the evidence — many community and academic urologists still stent routine cases.

 Success Rate
   • "Success" is not a settled definition — completely stone-free versus free of clinically significant
     fragments, at what interval, and on how sensitive an imaging modality.

   • Lingeman, 1986 — "clinically significant fragments" defined as >5 mm. The problem: smaller
     fragments frequently become symptomatic, requiring further procedures in up to a fifth of patients
     within roughly 18 months.
   • Proposed remedy: report both zero-fragment rate and adjunct residual fragment rates (>2 mm, >4
     mm).
   • SFR is dominated by imaging modality — CT-assessed SFR spans a very wide range, with the highest
     "success" in studies permitting larger residual fragments. Non–lower pole location, smaller stone size,
     and postoperative ultrasound or radiography rather than CT all inflate reported SFR.

   KEY POINTS: URETEROSCOPY
     • For patients who must stay on anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy, ureteroscopy is the modality
       of choice.
     • Ureteral access sheath use decreases intrarenal pressure and facilitates basketing, but may
       increase the risk of ureteral injury.
     • A postoperative stent may be omitted when there is normal anatomy, normal renal function, no
       concern for ureteral injury, and no planned second-stage surgery.
     • The size of clinically relevant residual fragments, and the optimal timing and modality of
       postoperative imaging, are all matters of ongoing debate.
     • The commonest complications are stent-related symptoms, sepsis, bleeding and ureteral injury.

 PERCUTANEOUS NEPHROLITHOTOMY FOR RENAL STONES
 History
   • 1976 — Fernström and Johansson: first percutaneous stone removal under fluoroscopic guidance.
     Through the late 1970s and early 1980s, Arthur Smith and Ralph Clayman with Kurt Amplatz and Paul
     Lange (University of Minnesota) drove the early evolution.
   • 1983 — Lawson: first retrograde-assisted access. 1984 — Wickham: first tubeless PCNL. 1987 —
     Valdivia-Uría: first supine PCNL. 1992 — pneumatic lithotripsy introduced (Swiss LithoClast).
   • Position today: PCNL gives higher SFRs and needs fewer procedures than SWL or URS for 1–2 cm
     renal stones, and is the treatment of choice for staghorn calculi and most stones >2 cm, with shorter
     operative times above 2 cm.

 Preoperative Considerations
   • History focused on fitness for surgery, coagulopathy, willingness to be transfused, and reversible
     cardiopulmonary comorbidity. Identifying uncorrected coagulopathy and untreated UTI is critical.
   • Short-term preoperative drainage — nephrostomy or stent — where there have been repeated
     admissions for complicated UTI, sepsis or pain.
   • Stop anticoagulants in consultation with the prescriber; PCNL has been shown safe on low-dose
     aspirin; coordinate bridging for high thrombotic risk.
   • Examination targets positioning: general fitness, frailty, respiratory status, body habitus, spinal
     curvature, and knee and hip flexibility.
   • Baseline laboratory work: renal function, full blood count and urine culture; group and screen
     optional. Stress testing or pulmonary function testing for marginal functional status.
   • Non-contrast CT of abdomen and pelvis is the gold standard preoperative study. Read axial, coronal
     and sagittal series plus three-dimensional (3D) reconstructions for stone location and branching, renal
     anatomy, infundibular lengths and angles, skin-to-stone distance, stone density in HU, and the
     relationships of skin, ribs and stone to diaphragm, spleen, liver and colon.
   • A pre-existing urogram helps select access points, but a retrograde or antegrade pyelogram during the
     procedure serves the same purpose and spares intravenous contrast.
   • 3D reformatting, immersive virtual reality and 3D printing show early promise for planning
     challenging anatomy; against this, retrospective data found no added benefit of 3D reformatting over
     orthogonal measurements for predicting SFR.

 Perioperative Considerations
   • General anaesthesia is preferred; epidural or local techniques are described. Adding local or regional
     anaesthesia reduces analgesic requirement and 24-hour pain scores.
   • Single-dose antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended, culture-specific and/or tailored to the local
     genitourinary antibiogram. Evidence is emerging for an extended course to reduce infective
     complications.
   • Tranexamic acid (TXA) — Cochrane review of ten randomised trials in adults: no increase in
     thromboembolic events; probable reduction in transfusion, major and minor complications and
     length of stay, with improved stone-free rates, at the cost of drug adverse effects.
   • Documented TXA adverse reactions: seizures, headache, backache, abdominal pain, diarrhoea,
     fatigue, pulmonary embolism, deep vein thrombosis, anaphylaxis, impaired colour vision and other
     visual disturbance. Given low baseline transfusion rates in modern series, the decision is individualised.

 Access Techniques
 Choice of Access
  • Access is arguably the most important step of PCNL. PCNL's share of stone cases has stayed stable at
    about 4.5%, while urologist-obtained de novo access has risen to roughly a third — and urologist-
    obtained access is associated with shorter stay and lower readmission, transfusion and secondary
    procedure rates than radiologist-obtained access.
   • Access is tailored to gross anatomy, collecting system configuration and stone location, guided by CT
     plus a pyelogram taken after positioning.
   • Use ultrasound once positioned but before prepping and draping, even when fluoroscopy is the
     primary modality — it delineates renal anatomy through respiration, the stones, adjacent organs,
     diaphragm level and overlying ribs.
   • Posterior calices with a short, wide infundibulum are ideal. Upper pole posterior access gives the
     most access to the rest of the collecting system in most kidneys, but chest complications rise
     significantly above the 12th rib.
   • Lower pole access is safe from thoracic injury but slightly increases colonic injury risk, especially on
     the left. Two further caveats as a pair: a too-anterior lower pole is hard to reach, and a steep lower
     pole-to-renal pelvis angle makes the ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) hard to reach with rigid
     instruments and antegrade treatment of upper ureteral stones difficult even with flexible instruments.
   • Renal distraction by traction on a through-and-through wire in the lower pole, thoracoscopic or
     laparoscopic direct visualisation, and preoperative CT-guided access are options where safety
     windows are tight.
 Fluoroscopy-Guided Access
  • ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) governs. Dose-limiting measures: visual and tactile rather
    than fluoroscopic cues; fixed low mAs and kVp; a laser-guided C-arm; single pulse-per-second
    fluoroscopy; a urologist-controlled footpedal or designated technician; a saved pyelogram as a
    roadmap; last-image hold; spot films for alignment, reserving live fluoroscopy for needle
    advancement.
   • Orientate first: spine across the top of the image with the collecting system aligned to it, to avoid
     confusion over needle movement.

 Ultrasound-Guided Access
  • Principal advantages: no ionising radiation, plus identification of bowel, spleen, liver and pleura.
   • Curved array 2–5 MHz transducer, depth and gain optimised per patient. Stones are identified by
     posterior shadowing and twinkle artefact; posterior calices are easily seen.
   • If images are to be saved and interpreted, use radiology-standard alignment; otherwise orientating
     screen left–right to probe left–right movement may be easier. Free-hand and needle-guide techniques
     are both described.
 Combined Retrograde- and Antegrade-Guided Access
  • Lineage: Lawson (1983) retrograde needle placement through a ureteroscope → Hunter (1985) series
    → first simultaneous retrograde and antegrade renal stone treatment (1988).
   • Technique elements: place a UAS at the start and pass a ureteroscope through it into the collecting
     system, improving drainage and flushing fragments down the ureter; the ureteroscope lets the
     surgeon watch and correct the percutaneous needle in the desired calyx; a basket can grasp the
     percutaneous guidewire for through-and-through access; threading a 0.035 inch wire retrograde
     through the ureteroscope and out through the 18-gauge needle saves re-advancing the scope; tract
     dilatation can be watched in real time.
   • Double bulls-eye technique, as a sequence: first bulls-eye lines the needle up from skin to stone →
     with the needle on the stone in the target calyx, place the retrograde scope at the ostium → second
     bulls-eye lined up from the stone or calyx to the ostium, using the scope tip as the target → through-
     and-through access obtained → dilate to the stone.
   • Further uses: the scope itself becomes the fluoroscopic or ultrasound target when the collecting
     system is poorly opacified or extravasated; it reaches areas percutaneous instruments cannot,
     fragmenting or relocating stones and fulgurating small tumours; and retrograde basketing and laser
     lithotripsy at the end of the case clears calices inaccessible from the original tract.
   • Outcomes: ureteroscopic-assisted access may lower transfusion rates, permits wire placement
     beyond obstruction under direct vision, and reduces fluoroscopy time and the need for multiple
     tracts. Meta-analysis advantages: a single position for the whole case, fewer tracts, less bleeding,
     fewer ancillary procedures and higher stone clearance.

 Conversion of Access and Tract Dilatation
   • Warn the anaesthetist before dilating — the step is stimulating and complications begin to manifest
     there.
   • A through-and-through wire obviates a safety wire; otherwise place an antegrade safety wire, ideally
     beyond the level of obstruction.
   • Alken metal serial dilators — dilate even dense scar effectively and are reusable. Amplatz serial
     dilators — progressively larger firm polyurethane dilators over an 8 Fr polytetrafluoroethylene
     catheter that rides a 0.035 inch wire.
   • Balloon dilators — the advantage is one-pass dilatation. Sequence: pass the balloon over the dilating
     wire → position the tip fluoroscopically, under ultrasound, or under direct vision (endoscopic
     combined intrarenal surgery, ECIRS) → insufflate with contrast using a LeVeen pressure syringe to
     20–30 atm → confirm full insufflation with no waist → advance the preloaded access sheath over the
     balloon with controlled continuous back-and-forth screwing pressure, not overshooting the balloon
     → fluid return confirms position.
   • Multiple tracts may be needed for suboptimal angles, especially spindly and bifid collecting systems
     — but independently carry more bleeding and transfusion, and versus matched single-tract cases
     reduce renal function, especially in chronic kidney disease. The combined retrograde and antegrade
     approach reduces the need for them.

 The Role of Flexible Nephroscopy
   • Once the bulk of stone is cleared through the access point, antegrade and/or retrograde flexible
     nephroscopy and ureteroscopy is recommended — it improves SFR by clearing isolated inaccessible
     calices and ureteral fragments, and inspects for collecting system injury. A randomised trial showed
     markedly higher stone-free rates, fewer access sites and less bleeding.
   • Technical points: use occasional fluoroscopy to orientate and target elusive densities; use the
     roadmap nephrostogram taken at the start of the case; when using a flexible cystoscope as a
     nephroscope, beware the UPJ — the ureter usually will not accommodate that diameter; use a
     flexible ureteroscope to clear the ureter at the end.
   • In combined cases, one surgeon lasers and irrigates from below while the other suctions through the
     percutaneous tract. Large stones basketed from difficult calices can be brought into view for the
     percutaneous operator and grasped with a larger percutaneous grasper, or the retrograde
     ureteroscope driven out through the percutaneous sheath with the stone.

 Exit Strategies and Drainage
   • Goals: good drainage with low intrarenal pressure, and haemostasis.
   • Sequence at the end of the case: confirm the entire collecting system and the ureter are clear by rigid
     and flexible endoscopy → back the access sheath out under direct vision, leaving the safety wire as
     the last hardware removed — at minimum to the lower ureter, ideally into the bladder and out
     through the urethra → if the safety wire has fallen out during the case, re-establish it endoscopically
     before sheath removal → observe the access site and apply direct pressure until bleeding stops.
   • In thin patients, the rib serves as a backstop against which to compress the superficial tract. Most
     bleeding is controlled conservatively. Fibrin glue injection into the tract has not reduced bleeding in
     several randomised trials.
   • Escalation for profuse, uncontrollable tract bleeding: re-insert and insufflate the dilatation balloon,
     or place a Kaye tamponade catheter over the safety wire under fluoroscopy → alert the anaesthetist
     → arrange selective angioembolisation.
   • Drainage options: percutaneous nephrostomy catheters, a double-J ureteral stent, or totally
     tubeless. A Foley catheter is typically left overnight either way. Small-calibre nephrostomy tubes
     (≤10 Fr) cause less pain with no increase in blood loss.

   • Historically PCNL carried significantly more complications than retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS).
   • Cochrane review of 42 trials (Söderberg et al., 2023) — the position-changing evidence: PCNL
     showed no effect on major complications compared with RIRS for renal stones, while reducing the
     need for secondary procedures. This is credited to the evolution of PCNL tools and technique.
   • Distinctive complications of renal access — uncommon or rare, but the list to know: acute
     haemorrhage; delayed haemorrhage; infundibular stenosis; collecting system injury; small and large
     bowel injury; splenic and hepatic injury; hydrothorax and pneumothorax; postoperative systemic
     inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and sepsis; metabolic and physiological complications related

 Horseshoe Kidney
   • Stones occur in roughly one in five horseshoe kidneys. URS, SWL and PCNL are all feasible with safety
     and efficacy broadly comparable to normal kidneys.
   • Concomitant ureteropelvic junction obstruction is common, and the course of the proximal ureter
     predisposes to obstruction by crossing vessels.
   • CROES PCNL study group (Osther et al., 2011): median operative time was longer and percutaneous
     access failed more often — 5% versus 1.7% in orthotopic kidneys.
   • Flexible antegrade nephroscopy reaches the otherwise inaccessible lower medial calices.

 Calyceal Diverticulum and Infundibular Stenosis
   • Stones form within calyceal diverticula or behind a stenotic infundibulum; diagnosis is by intravenous
     urography or CT urography.

   • Treatment trigger, as the pair: asymptomatic patients need no treatment; treat those presenting
     with flank pain, recurrent UTI or haematuria.
   • Direct percutaneous puncture onto the stone is ideal but difficult — the cavity is small and the
     parenchyma overlying a focally obstructing stone is often thin.
   • Retrograde-first strategy, as a sequence: ureteroscopy identifies whether an ostium to the obstructed
     calyx exists → pass a wire → if angles are favourable, laser-incise the ostium or dilate it with a small-
     calibre ureteral balloon → then treat the stone retrograde, or obtain percutaneous access targeting
     the tip of the scope or contrast passed through the dilated ostium.
   • Fulguration of the diverticular urothelium after stone clearance is controversial — where performed,
     by resectoscope with a roller ball electrode, or by laser.
   • Mini-PCNL versus RIRS in a large global match-paired study: after propensity matching, both were
     effective with similar stone-free and reintervention rates — RIRS gave the shorter hospital stay.

 Urinary Diversions
   • Modality is dictated by stone volume, location, number and type; the type of diversion dictates the
     approach and the positioning.
   • In patients whose native bladder forms part of the diversion, establish whether the bladder neck has
     been closed — a surgical planning point, not a detail.
   • From established wire access, the surgeon may proceed antegrade, retrograde or combined,
     depending on the location and volume of stone.

 Renal Transplants
   • General consensus is to remove upper tract stones in transplant kidneys, for two reasons kept as a
     pair: risk of renal failure with obstruction, and non-traditional presentation because the graft is
     denervated.
   • Presenting signs may be only: vague graft site discomfort, fever, oliguria, haematuria and anasarca —
     classic pain is absent.

 Patients with Spinal Deformity or Limb Contractures
   • Assess flexibility preoperatively — it determines the whole approach.
   • Fragment passage after SWL is hampered by lack of ambulation and aberrant renal and ureteral
     anatomy; older literature shows only modest stone-free results with frequent need for multiple
     sessions.
   • The operator requirement: be comfortable with supine, flank and prone positioning, and be
     hypervigilant about padding pressure points wherever they fall — standard positioning protocols do
     not apply to this population.

 Training Considerations
   • Training has concentrated on the technically demanding skill sets: upper tract ureteroscopic access
     and manoeuvres; three-dimensional visualisation aids using virtual and augmented reality and 3D
     printing; and models and curricula teaching fluoroscopic and ultrasound-guided percutaneous
     access, dilatation and stone treatment.
   • Several physical and virtual reality training platforms carry validity evidence for training, catalogued
     in a systematic review.

   KEY POINTS: PCNL OUTCOMES, SPECIAL ANATOMY AND TRAINING
     • Flexible nephroscopy and ureteroscopy ensure a stone-free state at the end of PCNL.
     • Serious complications of PCNL are rare but may carry significant morbidity and even mortality.
     • Ureteroscopy and/or PCNL are good approaches to stones in calyceal diverticula and behind
       infundibular stenosis.
     • Ureteral stents and small percutaneous nephrostomy tubes are both acceptable forms of drainage
       after PCNL.
     • Positioning, access, modality, approach and exit strategies should all be individualised to
       maximise good outcomes.
     • Many simulation-based curricula have validity evidence for training the knowledge and skills to
       treat upper tract calculi.
