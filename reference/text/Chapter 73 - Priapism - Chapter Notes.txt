Chapter 73 — Priapism
 • Priapism is a penile erection that is either unrelated to sexual stimulation or persists ≥4 hours after its
   conclusion; it results from dysregulation of the normal processes governing rigidity and detumescence,
   and it is a medical emergency.
 • The first step is to characterise penile haemodynamics and oxygenation; appropriate treatment follows
   from that characterisation.
 • Guidelines to be familiar with: American Urological Association (AUA)/Sexual Medicine Society of North
   America (SMSNA) (acute ischaemic priapism 2021; recurrent ischaemic, sickle cell and nonischaemic
   priapism 2022), European Association of Urology (EAU) (2014), and British Association of Urological
   Surgeons (BAUS).
 • Time is of the essence because of the high potential for adverse sequelae: pain, infection, fibrosis, penile
   shortening and permanent erectile dysfunction (ED).
 • Many aspects of the disease process still require investigation.

PRIAPISM: DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATIONS

Priapism Definition
 • Full or partial erection ≥4 hours after sexual stimulation and orgasm, or unrelated to sexual stimulation.
 • Three subtypes: ischaemic (low flow, veno-occlusive), nonischaemic (high flow, arterial) and stuttering
   (intermittent, recurrent ischaemic).

Priapism Classifications/Subtypes
Ischaemic Priapism (Low Flow, Veno-Occlusive)
 • Painful erection with low inflow of oxygenated arterial blood; with time, tissue develops hypoxia,
   hypercarbia and acidosis.
 • Penile pain develops within 6–8 hours of onset and usually prompts emergency presentation.
 • Examination: fully erect, rigid, tender, sometimes oedematous.
 • Progressive; likened to compartment syndrome: high intracavernosal pressure, sinusoidal thrombosis,
   diminished arterial inflow and tissue ischaemia.
 • Untreated, it takes days to weeks to resolve and commonly ends in penile fibrosis and permanent loss of
   erectile function.

Stuttering Priapism (Intermittent, Recurrent Ischaemic Priapism)
 • First described in sickle cell disease (SCD).
 • Painful, recurrent, unwanted erections lasting minutes to hours; often nocturnal, waking the patient.
 • Onset usually in childhood; over time episodes become more frequent and longer.
 • Episodes usually self-resolve, but can progress to a full ischaemic episode requiring treatment.

Nonischaemic Priapism (High Flow, Arterial)
 • Dysregulated arterial inflow; corpora erect but usually lacking full rigidity.
 • Arterial blood with normal oxygen tension → no pain, no hypoxia, hypercarbia or acidosis → fibrosis and
   ED usually do not follow.
 • Once confirmed, no acute intervention is needed; expectant management may be pursued.

 KEY POINTS: DEFINITION OF PRIAPISM
   • Priapism is a penile erection that is either not related to or continues for ≥4 hours after the conclusion
     of sexual stimulation.
   • Priapism is typically classified as ischaemic (low flow, veno-occlusive), stuttering (intermittent,
     recurrent ischaemic) or nonischaemic (high flow, arterial).


Concept — Priapism is classified by oxygenation, not by duration: failed venous outflow produces ischaemia
and a time-dependent emergency; excess arterial inflow produces a well-oxygenated, painless erection
that can wait.

PRIAPISM HISTORICAL CONSIDERATIONS
 • Priapus: Greek god depicted with an erect penis and strong sexual desire; son of Zeus and Aphrodite;
   cursed by Hera with abnormally large genitals; rejected by Aphrodite and raised by shepherds; wherever
   he went flowers bloomed and animals mated — "god of fertility".
 • Nineteenth-century reports established conservative therapy as the norm; the turn towards surgery
   began with Morris.
 • Nonischaemic priapism: literature is small series and case reports, focusing on observation as first-line,
   then embolisation for refractory cases.
 • Embolisation agent and ED: historically temporary agents were thought to carry lower long-term ED;
   more recent series report lower long-term ED with permanent agents.

THE EPIDEMIOLOGY OF PRIAPISM
 • Population incidence 0.34–5.3 cases per 100,000 person-years across registries and national data sets.
 • Reported rates rose sharply with intracavernosal injection (ICI) therapy for ED — the Finnish registry
   doubled in its final years, coinciding with ICI introduction.
 • SCD is a major risk factor for ischaemic and stuttering priapism: 29–42% of males with SCD develop
   priapism in their lifetime. Population SCD prevalence drives population priapism incidence.
 • Higher incidence in older men (aged 40 and over) in Dutch general-practice data; United States (US)
   inpatient data show a peak at 40–44 years, mean age early thirties, predominantly Black patients.
 • Inpatient data sets undercount: only a small minority of emergency department visits are admitted, so
   emergency-department-based data give the highest incidence; quoting inpatient data as true incidence is
   a trap.
 • Seasonality: incidence higher in summer than winter.
 • Predictors of admission: Charlson comorbidity index ≥3, Medicaid (vs private) insurance, very high
   emergency department volume, SCD and drug abuse (increase); rural hospital and very high ZIP-code
   income (decrease).
 • SCD share is highest in children and in admitted patients.
 • Readmission: about a quarter readmitted within a year, mostly within 60 days; most only once. Risk
   factors: SCD, drug abuse, psychiatric disease, prior ED, non-commercial insurance. Inpatient admission at
   first presentation was protective. Identifying these factors allows intervention to prevent readmission.

 KEY POINTS: EPIDEMIOLOGY OF PRIAPISM
   • Population-based estimates of priapism incidence range from 0.34 to 5.3 cases per 100,000 person-
     years.

THE AETIOLOGY OF PRIAPISM

Aetiology of Ischaemic Priapism (Low Flow, Veno-Occlusive)
 • 95% of all priapism; causes numerous and wide-ranging.
 • Erection starts normally; beyond 4 hours → ↓O₂, ↑CO₂, acidosis → cavernosal thrombosis and smooth
   muscle necrosis.
 • Natural course untreated: painful erection lasting days to weeks → fibrosis and ED.
Intracavernosal injection therapy
 • ICI agents: papaverine, phentolamine, prostaglandin E1 (PGE1); prescribed after an in-office test dose to
   set a safe starting dose.
 • Low priapism risk at clinic initiation of PGE1.
 • At home: incorrect dosing, deliberate escalation, or recreational use.
 • Recreational ICI (without prescription) accounted for about half of ICI-related ischaemic priapism in a Los
   Angeles series; recreational users were younger, presented later, and a high proportion were HIV-
   positive — a public-health issue (drug "from a friend": no dosing, no counselling, possible needle sharing).

Toxins
 • Tityus serrulatus (South American scorpion) stings and Phoneutria nigriventer (Brazilian banana spider)
   bites.
 • Phoneutria neurotoxin → pain, vascular congestion, priapism, loss of muscle control, paralysis, death; its
   peptide eretina interferes with the nitric oxide pathway and induces erection in mice even after
   cavernous nerve denervation.
Haematological and prothrombotic states
 • Haematological: SCD, thalassaemia, granulocytic, myeloid and lymphocytic leukaemia, multiple myeloma.
 • Prothrombotic: asplenia, erythropoietin, cessation of warfarin.
 • Haemodialysis with heparin: proposed heparin-dependent antiplatelet antibodies → platelet
   aggregation → cavernosal flow disruption; high haematocrit and hypovolaemia increase risk on dialysis.
Post-shunt thrombosis and perioperative anticoagulation
 • Recurrence after a shunt may be thromboembolic, like thrombus in a vascular graft.
 • Cavernosal blood normally does not clot (endothelial fibrinolytic and anticoagulant factors).
 • Mechanism: shunt injures tunica and endothelium → blood exposed to collagen-rich tunica → tissue
   factor binds factor VIIa → coagulation cascade; exposed collagen releases inorganic phosphate →
   thrombin activation → platelets and fibrin → clot seals the tunica and occludes the shunt.
 • Regimen: preoperative aspirin 325 mg orally + heparin 5000 units subcutaneously → postoperative
   aspirin 81 mg daily for 2 weeks, to keep the cavernosum–spongiosum shunt patent.
Leukaemia
 • 1–5% of males with leukaemia; associated with high white blood cell (WBC) counts.
 • Four mechanisms: (1) venous congestion from splenomegaly compressing abdominal veins; (2)
   leukaemic cell sludging in corpora and dorsal veins; (3) sacral nerve infiltration; (4) central nervous
   system infiltration.
 • Treat both the penis and the malignancy: cytotoxic therapy and leukapheresis.
Solid tumour metastasis
 • Testicular, renal and lung primaries can metastasise to the penis.
 • Usually obstruct venous outflow → ischaemic; less commonly destroy sinusoidal architecture → high
   flow.
 • Individualised: expectant management, partial penectomy, radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy; too few
   cases for best-practice guidance.
Sickle cell disease
 • Point mutation in the β-globin chain producing haemoglobin S (HbS): valine substituted for glutamic acid.
 • Sickled cells obstruct penile venous outflow → stagnation in corpora.
 • SCD causes 23% of adult and 63% of paediatric ischaemic priapism.
 • Multicentre study, United Kingdom and Nigeria: mean onset about 15 years; most have had an episode
   by age 20; distinguished acute severe prolonged (>24 hours, needing medical attention) from stuttering
   (short, self-limited), which was the more common form — typically a few episodes a month lasting
   around an hour.

 • Precipitants: sexual arousal, intercourse, fever, sleep, cold weather, dehydration. Self-measures:
   analgesics, oral water, exercise.
 • Laboratory picture: ↓haemoglobin, ↑reticulocytes, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), bilirubin and aspartate
   aminotransferase (AST); haemolysis and reduced nitric oxide are central to pathogenesis.
 • Sickle cell trait: generally benign, low risk; rare case reports.

Aetiology of Stuttering Priapism (Recurrent Ischaemic Priapism)
 • Most commonly in males with SCD. About 300,000 infants born annually with SCD, mostly in sub-
   Saharan Africa, India, the Mediterranean and the Middle East; about 100,000 affected in the US.
 • Pathophysiology: deoxygenation in capillary beds → HbS polymerisation → sickling → ↑adhesion
   molecules → abnormal endothelial binding → rapid haemolysis → inflammation, endothelial dysfunction,
   reticulocytosis → vaso-occlusion → acute and chronic organ damage (stroke, pulmonary hypertension, leg
   ulcers).
 • In the penis: vaso-occlusion → impaired cavernosal outflow → venous congestion → impaired arterial
   inflow → priapism.
 • Supportive measures (oral fluids, warm showers, walking) help some episodes.
 • Episodes lasting 4–6 hours or longer are full ischaemic priapism → seek care → same first-line treatment
   as ischaemic priapism.
 • Intravenous (IV) fluids and analgesia may run concurrently, but only after first-line priapism treatment is
   prioritised.

Aetiology of Nonischaemic Priapism (High Flow, Arterial)
 • Persistent erection from unregulated arterial inflow, usually after blunt or penetrating penile/perineal
   trauma → laceration of the cavernosal artery or a branch.
 • Mechanisms: straddle injury, coital trauma, pelvic fracture, iatrogenic needle injury, neoplastic vascular
   erosion.
 • Delayed onset (≥1 day after injury) is common: the clot occluding the arterial injury disperses, typically
   during a nocturnal erection, unmasking the fistula.
 • Iatrogenic after treatment of ischaemic priapism by needle aspiration, irrigation or phenylephrine; colour
   duplex Doppler ultrasonography (DUS) shows the fistula at the needle site.
 • Erection partially rigid, not painful.
 • Mimic: postischaemic hyperaemia — common, transient after treatment of ischaemic priapism; colour
   DUS distinguishes the two.

 KEY POINTS: AETIOLOGY OF PRIAPISM
   • Ischaemic priapism aetiologies include exposure to certain medications and venomous toxins, as well
     as leukocytosis, malignancy and blood dyscrasias.
   • SCD is the most common cause of stuttering priapism.
   • Sickling of red blood cells can cause penile vasculature occlusion and predispose to recurrent episodes
     of ischaemic priapism.
   • Nonischaemic priapism can be caused by trauma, with formation of a cavernosal artery–corpus
     cavernosum fistula.


Concept — Every ischaemic cause converges on outflow obstruction or failed smooth-muscle contraction;
every nonischaemic cause converges on a single mechanical lesion — an arterial–sinusoidal fistula.

MOLECULAR MECHANISMS OF ISCHAEMIC AND INTERMITTENT PRIAPISM
 • Interplay of corporal smooth muscle, endothelium and signalling through nitric oxide (NO), PDE5, cyclic
   guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β.
 • Core defect: imbalance between vasoconstriction and vasorelaxation → hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis.
 • Prolonged hypoxia → smooth muscle apoptosis → poor contractility and fibrosis, mediated by reactive
   oxygen species (reperfusion injury) and TGF-β upregulation.

Nitric Oxide Dysregulation
 • Normal: NO from endothelium and cavernous nerves → guanylyl cyclase → ↑cGMP → smooth muscle
   relaxation.
 • Paradox: priapism is a low-NO state from dysregulated endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS).
 • eNOS-knockout mice show phasic priapic activity; eNOS gene transfer partially restores erectile
   function.

PDE5/Cyclic GMP Dysregulation
 • eNOS dysregulation acts downstream through PDE5: eNOS-deficient mice have lower PDE5 and cGMP;
   adenoviral eNOS restores cGMP and abolishes priapic activity.
 • Rat cavernous cells: ischaemia downregulates PDE5; sildenafil upregulates it. PDE5 is central in sickle cell
   priapism.
 • Mechanism: chronic low NO → altered cGMP-dependent feedback → PDE5 downregulated → neuronal
   NO surge → cGMP surge insufficiently degraded → unopposed smooth muscle relaxation → priapism.
 • This explains the paradoxical role of PDE5 inhibitors in preventing recurrent ischaemic priapism.

Other Pathways: Rho, Adenosine Dysregulation
 • Rho-kinase mediates contractility that maintains flaccidity; in SCD mice nerve stimulation produced a
   decline in Rho-kinase activity → unopposed relaxation.
 • Adenosine, a pro-erectile vasodilator, is upregulated in sickle cell priapism in a hypoxia-inducible factor
   (HIF)-1α-dependent manner.
 • Net effect: NO imbalance, PDE5 dysregulation, adenosine excess and reduced Rho-kinase → excessive
   relaxation with impaired vasoconstriction.
 • TGF-β-mediated corporal fibrosis is the common downstream pathway: ↑TGF-β with histological change
   after prolonged pharmacological erection (canine); recombinant TGF-β injection reduced smooth muscle
   (rabbit); TGF-β receptor gene polymorphisms predispose men with SCD to priapism.

 KEY POINTS: MOLECULAR MECHANISMS OF ISCHAEMIC AND INTERMITTENT PRIAPISM
   • Ischaemic priapism is characterised by dysregulated homeostasis and an imbalance between penile
     smooth muscle constriction and relaxation.
   • Ischaemic priapism commonly results in acidosis, hypoxia and hypercapnia within the corpus
     cavernosum.
   • Dysregulation of NO, eNOS, PDE5, cyclic GMP, Rho-kinase and adenosine has been implicated in the
     pathogenesis of priapism.


Concept — Priapism is a disorder of failed detumescence, not excess erection; low basal NO lowers PDE5, so
the next erection cannot be switched off — which is why a PDE5 inhibitor can prevent it.

PRIAPISM: DIAGNOSIS
 • Built on history, physical examination, laboratory testing and adjunct imaging.

History
 • Comorbidities: haematological dyscrasias, genitourinary or systemic infection, metabolic conditions,
   malignancy (haematological, metastatic, primary), neurological disease.
 • Complete medication history including hormonally active agents and recreational drugs (Table 73.1).
 • Inciting events: penile/perineal trauma (e.g. straddle) → nonischaemic; penile/perineal surgery or pelvic
   vascular procedures; recent oral or intracavernosal erectogenic agent → ischaemic highly likely.
 • Prior episodes and treatments; duration, pain, continuous vs intermittent rigidity.
 • Pain → ischaemic; painless → nonischaemic; intermittent pain and rigidity → stuttering.
 • Baseline erectile function informs counselling, particularly on early prosthesis; validated questionnaires
   are impractical acutely, but baseline function and prior therapies should be documented.

Physical Examination
 • Vital signs: hypertension, tachycardia or bradycardia — baseline before sympathomimetic administration.
 • General appearance and distress; expedited abdominal, pelvic and perineal examination for trauma,
   malignancy or instrumentation.
 • Penis: needle marks, trauma, anomalies; fully rigid and tender (ischaemic) vs partially rigid and
   nontender (nonischaemic).
 • Glans and corpus spongiosum are uninvolved in both; rigidity or abnormality there suggests alternative
   pathology.

Laboratory Testing
Corporal Aspiration and Blood Gas
 • Cornerstone and definitive test for ischaemic vs nonischaemic; obtain in most cases. Exception: known
   recent ICI of vasoactive medication.
 • Even after recent ischaemic priapism, repeat gas to assess acidosis or conversion to nonischaemic.
 • Aspirate colour is the first indicator: dark red (ischaemic) vs bright red (nonischaemic).

Adjunctive Testing
 • Establishes aetiology; performed in parallel, never delaying emergent treatment, and unlikely to change

 KEY POINTS: DIAGNOSIS OF PRIAPISM
   • The primary goal of diagnosis is to distinguish ischaemic versus nonischaemic priapism.
   • The type of priapism can be determined through a combination of history, physical examination and
     corpus cavernosal blood gas assessment.
   • Imaging is not essential for the routine diagnosis of priapism, but PDUS may be useful as an adjunctive
     diagnostic test in specific circumstances.


Concept — Diagnosis is one question — is the corpus cavernosum ischaemic? — and corporal blood gas
answers it; history and examination predict the answer, and PDUS rescues an equivocal result.

MEDICAL THERAPIES

Ischaemic Priapism
 • Prompt, expeditious treatment; delay risks permanent ED.
 • Cold compresses, cold baths, exercise, induced ejaculation, cold-water enemas or oral medication must
   never replace or delay first-line treatment.

Conservative Therapies
 • Limited role; must not delay definitive therapy.
 • Supportive care (cold, ice, exercise) after oral or ICI ED drugs achieved detumescence in only a minority
   (retrospective series, incomplete follow-up).
 • Ateyah et al. (2005) randomised controlled trial (RCT), ICI-induced priapism after failed cooling: aspiration
   plus irrigation at 10, 15, 20 or 37 °C — cold 10 °C saline: 96% detumescence vs 60% at body temperature
   → recommended cold (10 °C) saline irrigation.
 • SCD series: IV hydration alone resolved some paediatric cases, others needed exchange transfusion or
   shunting; in Nigeria, conservative care (IV fluids, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, adrenaline,
   stilboestrol) worked in a minority over days, the majority needing a glanular–cavernous shunt.
 • Earlier Nigerian paediatric series: nearly all had SCD; most responded conservatively, surgical outcomes
   "poor". Later series: Al-Ghorab shunt gave immediate detumescence in almost all → timely diagnosis and
   surgery advocated.
 • AUA: IV hydration and other conservative measures are adjunctive only.

Aspiration/Irrigation of Corpora Cavernosa
 • Aspiration ↓intracavernosal pressure, removes hypoxic, acidotic, hypercarbic blood and relieves pain.
 • Technique: single large-bore 19-gauge needle on a 10-mL syringe → enter the corpus cavernosum at the
   penoscrotal junction at 3 or 9 o'clock (avoids the dorsal neurovascular bundle) → aspirate while
   compressing the shaft between thumb and index finger until soft → adjust needle for return → leave
   needle in, let the shaft refill → recompress and re-aspirate → repeat serially → for irrigation, exchange
   the syringe for normal saline through the same needle.

Intracavernosal Injection (ICI) of Vasoactive Medication
 • AUA guideline: first-line = intracavernosal phenylephrine + corporal aspiration, ± irrigation.
 • Sympathomimetics: phenylephrine, etilefrine, ephedrine, adrenaline (epinephrine), noradrenaline
   (norepinephrine), metaraminol → cavernosal smooth muscle contraction.
 • Toxicity: α → hypertension; β → inotropy/chronotropy; most importantly reflex bradycardia. Adverse
   effects: headache, hypertension, arrhythmia.
 • Blood pressure and cardiac monitoring for all, especially with cardiovascular disease, hypertension,
   stroke or monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs).
 • α-agonists contract cavernous smooth muscle so trapped sinusoidal blood drains; β-agonists relax
   smooth muscle and dilate arteries, admitting oxygenated blood to wash out the sinusoids.
 • Case-report efficacy of these agents is variable.

Stuttering Priapism
 • Most agents are prophylactic, for frequent and troublesome episodes; evidence is largely anecdotal.

Oral Alpha-Adrenergic Agents
Pseudoephedrine
 • Sympathomimetic of the phenethylamine and amphetamine classes; decongestant, also used as
   stimulant.
 • Often used as off-label first-line for stuttering priapism; α-mediated corporal contraction.
 • Dose poorly defined; small numbers of SCD patients respond.
Terbutaline
 • β2-agonist with minor β1 effects; postulated to alter cavernosal permeability and ↑sinusoidal plasma
   flow.
Etilefrine
 • Selective α1-agonist (possible β1 chronotropic/inotropic effect); ↓venous pooling, ↑blood pressure;
   cavernosal contraction → detumescence.
 • Reduced episodes in most SCD patients without altering baseline erectile function, but a Cochrane
   systematic review found insufficient evidence.

Hormonal Therapies
GnRH agonists
 • Goserelin, leuprolide: initial luteinising hormone (LH) and testosterone flare → receptor desensitisation
   → castrate LH and testosterone within weeks.
 • Adverse effects: hot flushes, gynaecomastia, loss of sexually induced erections, ↓spermatogenesis,
   asthenia, loss of libido → quality-of-life cost.
 • Case: non-SCD man on adrenaline self-injection + monthly GnRH analogue → no recurrence, erectile
   function preserved; cessation usually brings recurrence.

Antiandrogens
 • Flutamide, bicalutamide, chlormadinone, cyproterone acetate; relieve stuttering priapism without the
   libido loss typical of GnRH agonists and oestrogens.
 • Bicalutamide effective in SCD and spinal cord injury (SCI), with dosing frequency later reduced;

 • Cyproterone: ↓LH, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and testosterone; high complete response but

 • Evidence is small, non-randomised and marred by poor compliance, hence no consistent improvement in
   severity or frequency.
5α-Reductase inhibitors
 • Block testosterone → dihydrotestosterone; licensed for alopecia and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH);
   off-label for idiopathic and stuttering priapism.
 • SCD series: finasteride 5 mg (days 1–40) → 3 mg (days 41–80) → 1 mg (days 81–120) → marked fall in
   monthly episodes.
 • 3 and 5 mg optimal; episodes rose on 1 mg; gynaecomastia; fertility effect in young men unassessed; RCT
   needed.

Other Agents
Digoxin
 • Inhibits smooth muscle Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase → ↑intracellular Ca²⁺ → cavernosal contraction; also impairs NO-
   mediated relaxation.
 • May ↓libido, excitement and rigidity, more at 0.25–0.5 mg/day; a placebo-controlled trial showed no fall
   in testosterone, oestrogen or LH.
 • Idiopathic stuttering: maintenance therapy → fewer hospital visits, better quality of life.
Baclofen
 • Centrally acting γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) derivative for spasticity; inhibits ejaculation; prevents
   reflexogenic erections in neurological disease and spinal cord lesions.
 • Intrathecal more effective than oral: first intrathecal pump for recurrent priapism in SCI, refractory to oral
   75 mg/day but responsive to a 25 µg intrathecal test dose.
 • Oral 40 mg nightly abolished idiopathic nocturnal recurrent priapism with normal erectile function.
 • Adverse effects: drowsiness, nausea, fatigue, ED; optimal prevention strategy unknown.
Phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (PDE5Is)
 • NO/cGMP dysregulation is the primary molecular mechanism of recurrent ischaemic priapism; PDE5
   downregulation → cGMP accumulation → exaggerated erection.
 • Sildenafil, vardenafil, tadalafil, udenafil, avanafil: a novel prophylactic option.
 • Burnett et al. (2014) RCT, SCD: sildenafil 25 → 50 mg daily vs placebo; primary endpoint (≥50%
   reduction) not met on intention-to-treat or per-protocol analysis; in the open-label extension most
   reported ≥50% reduction.
 • Tadalafil long-term: alleviated recurrences and improved erectile function in idiopathic (post-Winter
   shunt) and SCD cases.

 KEY POINTS: MEDICAL THERAPIES
   • Oral medications are not recommended for the primary management of either acute ischaemic or
     nonischaemic priapism.
   • Corpus cavernosal aspiration and intracavernosal injection of phenylephrine, with or without irrigation,
     are first-line treatments for ischaemic priapism.
   • Oral PDE5 inhibitors are among the most promising therapies for preventing stuttering priapism
     episodes, though supporting evidence currently is limited.


Concept — Prophylaxis attacks either the trigger (androgen-driven nocturnal erections) or the molecular
imbalance (α-tone, PDE5); PDE5 inhibitors are the paradox that follows directly from the low-PDE5 model.

SURGICAL THERAPIES
 • Indicated when first-line measures fail. Goals: restore ingress and egress of blood, restore homeostasis,
   prevent ischaemia and fibrosis.
 • Most procedures create a corpus cavernosum–corpus spongiosum fistula; historical fistulae to the dorsal
   penile or saphenous vein have limited supporting data.

Distal Percutaneous Corporoglanular Shunt Procedures
 • After failed phenylephrine and aspiration ± irrigation → consider a distal corporoglanular shunt (AUA).
 • Optimal distal shunt undefined: no head-to-head comparisons, and no comparison of shunt alone vs
   shunt with tunnelling.

Winter Shunt Procedure
 • Tru-Cut biopsy needle through the glans into the corpus cavernosum → removes a small core of tunica
   between glans and cavernosum → fistula → several passes bilaterally.
 • Lower success than scalpel shunts (Ebbehoj, Al-Ghorab, T-shunt); AUA still recognises it as valid.

Ebbehoj and T-Shunt Procedures
 • Ebbehoj: scalpel number 11, 12 or 15 through the glans into the cavernosum → cavernosum–spongiosum
   fistula → drainage into spongiosal venous system → glans skin sutured.
 • T-shunt (Brant et al., 2009): scalpel number 10, 11 or 12 inserted vertically through the glans, lateral to
   the meatus at a safe distance → advanced into cavernosum → rotated 90° laterally → T-shaped tunical
   window → unilateral first; contralateral T-shunt if erection persists or returns.

Distal Open Corporoglanular Shunt Procedures
Al-Ghorab Shunt Procedure
 • Conscious sedation, spinal or general anaesthesia → transverse incision 1 cm distal to the dorsal coronal
   ridge → sharp dissection to the distal corpora → excise the tips of the corpora → large cavernosum–
   spongiosum fistula → milk out deoxygenated blood → close skin.
 • Most outcome data aggregate multiple distal shunts; Al-Ghorab-specific series show durable
   detumescence in almost all, including late presenters.

Al-Ghorab Shunt Procedure With Tunnelling (Corporal Snake) Maneuver
 • Burnett and Pierorazio (2009): general anaesthesia → urethral catheter → 2-cm transverse glans incision
   → dissect to corporal tips → 2-0 stay sutures → incise corpora → milk out blood → 7/8 Hegar dilator
   through the corporotomy to the proximal phallus (breaks thrombus, drains stagnant blood) → further
   milking → close glans → gentle compressive dressing.

Proximal Shunt Procedures
Open Corporospongiosal Shunt Procedures (Quackels Shunt, Sacher Shunt)
 • Both need general anaesthesia and a transscrotal or transperineal approach; bilateral shunts staggered
   ≥1 cm to enhance drainage and reduce urethral stricture.
 • Longitudinal scrotal or perineal incision → dissect to the bulb of the spongiosum and urethra → excise a
   1-cm ellipse of tunica from cavernosum and spongiosum → evacuate stagnant blood → anastomose with
   5-0 polydioxanone (PDS).
 • Few outcome data; unilateral vs bilateral not compared. AUA: low utilisation; proximal shunt only after
   failure of more established measures, including distal shunting with tunnelling.

Saphenous Vein Anastomotic Shunt Procedure (Grayhack Shunt)
 • Supine frog-leg → incision at the saphenofemoral junction, 3–4 cm below the inguinal ligament →
   mobilise saphenous vein about 10 cm → second incision at the lateral penile base to the tunica → ligate
   distal saphenous → swing proximal end up → anastomose to tunica with 5-0 PDS.

Deep Dorsal Vein Anastomotic Shunt Procedure (Barry Shunt)
 • 4-cm longitudinal incision at the penile base → identify superficial or deep dorsal vein → mobilise, ligate
   distally, transect → excise tunical ellipse sized to the vein → tension-free anastomosis with 5-0 PDS.

Open Penoscrotal Decompression Procedure
 • Described by Morey: glans-sparing, rapid detumescence.
 • Transverse penoscrotal incision → skin-hook retraction, isolate and incise each corpus, holding sutures →
   proximal corporotomy → paediatric Yankauer suction passed distally and proximally to evacuate clot →
   close corporotomy → repeat on the contralateral side → layered closure.

 • Complications of shunting: penile oedema, haematoma, urethral injury, urethral fistula, penile necrosis,

Concurrent Penile Prosthesis Placement at the Time of Shunt Procedure
 • Shunts are not always successful; each hour and each procedure adds smooth muscle damage →
   necrosis, fibrosis, ED, sometimes ↓sensation.
 • Early implantation avoids fibrosis; matured fibrosis makes dilation difficult or impossible, forcing
   aggressive dilation with cavernosal or urethral perforation.
 • Benefits of early placement: preserved length, easier insertion, shorter operating time, fewer
   reoperations.

 KEY POINTS: SURGICAL THERAPIES
   • Because priapism duration is highly correlated with ED risk, surgical treatment should be promptly
     performed when conservative measures fail.
   • Distal shunt (Winter, Ebbehoj, T-shunt and Al-Ghorab) procedures create corpus cavernosal–corpus
     spongiosum fistulae to facilitate penile detumescence.
   • The penoscrotal decompression procedure is performed via a penoscrotal incision, using a proximal
     corporotomy to achieve detumescence.
   • Corpus cavernosal snake ("tunnelling") manoeuvres can be used with proximal or distal shunt
     procedures to facilitate sustained detumescence.
   • Patients with prolonged ischaemic priapism and a high risk for ED should be considered for early penile
     prosthesis placement.
   • Early prosthesis placement has better outcomes and greater patient satisfaction than delayed
     placement.


Concept — Every shunt is the same operation at a different address — a controlled fistula escalating distal
→ tunnelling → proximal as invasiveness rises and success falls; duration, not the choice of shunt, sets
erectile prognosis, which is why prosthesis enters at 36 hours.

MANAGEMENT OF HIGH-FLOW (ARTERIAL) PRIAPISM
 • Persistent erection from unregulated cavernosal arterial inflow; first described by Burt et al. (1960).
 • Boys and men <55 years: trauma — the mechanisms listed under aetiology, plus kicks, birth-canal trauma
   in newborns, complications of penile diagnostics and surgery.
 • Men >55 years: often advanced malignancy eroding the cavernosal artery.
 • Most fistulae arise from the cavernosal artery or its branches.
 • Haemodynamics: laceration → direct, persistent, irregular flow into the lacunae → high-velocity turbulent
   flow → shear stress → endothelial NO release → arterial and trabecular dilatation.
 • Incomplete rigidity reflects incomplete smooth muscle relaxation.

Expectant Management
 • Not a medical emergency; no ischaemia → conservative management first-line; about 60% resolve
   spontaneously.
 • Penile compression, ice, observation → cavernosal artery vasospasm → clot → fistula closure.

   • Selective cavernosal arterial embolisation has high success rates in treating nonischaemic priapism that

SUMMARY
 • History, examination and corporal blood gas remain the diagnostic cornerstone; delay to resolution
   drives fibrosis, shortening and ED.
 • Ischaemic ladder: phenylephrine + aspiration ± irrigation → distal shunt ± tunnelling → proximal shunts
   (limited success); open penoscrotal decompression ± tunnelling is the newer proximal option with
   promising results.
 • Stuttering: episodes typically ≤1 hour; pharmacological prevention, plus self-administered
   intracavernosal phenylephrine at home for episodes after onset.
 • Nonischaemic: expectant management resolves most; angiographic embolisation next.
 • Research needs: molecular mechanisms of ischaemic and stuttering priapism; outcomes of concurrent vs
   delayed prosthesis.
