Chapter 120 — Aging and Geriatric Urology
 • Urology is a predominantly geriatric specialty: geriatricians aside, only ophthalmology and cardiology
   exceed it in geriatric care volume. Geriatric competencies are recommended for surgical training and
   board certification.
 • Age raises need and risk together: more need for care, more risk from surgery and medication. Age alone
   matters less than geriatric syndromes, frailty, function, mental status and socioeconomic factors.
 • Aim: mitigate risk factors, preserve freedom from frailty and vulnerability, minimise intervention risk. This
   includes altering goals of care in ways inappropriate for a younger patient with the same condition.
 • Ignatz Leo Nascher (1909) first used geriatrics as a clinical term; it is now an established specialty.
 • Conventionally ≥65, but chronological age is a poor descriptor: "elderly" starts anywhere from 50 to 80
   in the hip-fracture literature. The United Nations proposes defining it by years of life remaining, to reflect
   variation in life expectancy and access to care.
 • No typical 65-year-old: environment, health behaviours, family, gender and ethnicity shape ageing. UN
   Decade of Healthy Ageing 2021–2030, led by the World Health Organization (WHO).
Concept — The chapter replaces chronological age with physiological and functional age. Function, frailty
and geriatric syndromes are the tools for measuring it.

DEMOGRAPHICS OF AGING

Aging and Population Trends
 • Adults ≥65 are the fastest-growing US segment: they will nearly double to 94.7 million between 2016 and
   2060.
 • By 2034, people ≥65 will outnumber children for the first time in US history.
 • People ≥85 will triple, from 6 to 19 million, by 2060.
 • Drivers: longer lives, falling birth rates and medical advances.
 • Figure 120.1 — US population pyramids for 1980, 2022 and the 2060 projection (US Census Bureau; Vespa
   et al., 2020). The base narrows and the older bands widen as the pyramid becomes a column.

BIOLOGY AND PRINCIPLES OF AGING
 • Ageing is the accumulation of molecular and cellular insults. It leads to declining physical and mental
   capacity, rising disease risk and, finally, death.
 • Decline is not linear with chronological age. It may be gradual or rapid, which explains the wide
   functional diversity in old age. Environmental and modifiable factors, including life transitions such as
   retirement, rehousing and bereavement, strongly shape healthy ageing.
 • Cellular senescence (a cell stops replicating) is the fundamental driver of biological ageing; senescent
   cells accumulate with age. Telomere-driven arrest defines the Hayflick limit (1961), which correlates with
   lifespan.
 • Senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP): cytokines, growth factors and matrix
   metalloproteinases → tissue remodelling, tumorigenesis, chronic inflammation.
 • Senescence spreads: SASP cells age adjacent tissue. Senescent cells transplanted from old into young
   mice spread senescence to the host.

Healthy Aging
 • WHO definition: "the process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables wellbeing
   in older age."
 • Functional ability is the capability to be and do what one values. It has five parts: (1) meet basic needs;
   (2) learn, grow and make decisions; (3) be mobile; (4) build and maintain relationships; (5) contribute to
   society.
 • Functional ability comes from intrinsic capacity interacting with environment. Intrinsic capacity is the
   person's mental and physical capacity; environment is home, community and society.
 • Purpose: gives clinicians a goal or target for care.
Concept — Healthy ageing is defined by function, not by the absence of disease. That is why the clinical
evaluation that follows begins with function.

CLINICAL EVALUATION OF THE GERIATRIC UROLOGY PATIENT
 • Older adults have more frequent and more complex health problems. Physiological ageing is
   compounded by interrelated geriatric syndromes (for example frailty), which do not affect younger adults
   and act synergistically. They are more complex than simple comorbidity.
 • The result is loss of functional reserve capacity and an impaired response to stressors such as infection,
   surgery, chemotherapy and urological disease or treatment.
 • Chronic disease burden: about four in five adults over 65 have at least one chronic condition, and about
   one in four have four or more.
 • First step: assess the ability to function independently.

Functional Assessment
 • Baseline functional status is vital. It predicts remaining life expectancy, morbidity and mortality.
   Heterogeneity is wide, so factors other than chronological age drive disability.

Activities of Daily Living
 • Basic activities of daily living (BADLs, ADLs) are graded from complete independence to full assistance;
   their loss predicts dependency on caregivers.
 • Screening question: can the patient shower or bathe independently? This is an early sign of general
   decline.
 • ADLs predict postoperative outcome, and identifying difficulty is still worthwhile when no surgery is
   planned. Occupational therapy (home or community) restores some independence, social participation
   and mobility.

goes before feeding. Each step down costs life expectancy and money.

Surgical Risk and Medical Optimization
 • Frailty (impaired compensation for stressors) can bias surgeons and patients against surgery without
   anyone measuring it.
 • Age by itself should not be a limiting factor; comorbidity may matter more, and optimisation can
   minimise complications even over 80.
 • Recognising comorbidity and geriatric syndromes is the surgeon's responsibility; to "clear" a patient is a
   misnomer.
 • Chronological age is seldom an independent predictor of surgical morbidity or mortality. Predictors:
   longer operating time, poor American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, urgent or emergent
   priority.
 • Society for Perioperative Assessment and Quality Improvement (SPAQI) sequence: frailty screen before
   elective major surgery → if positive, diagnostic frailty assessment → comprehensive geriatric assessment
   where feasible.

Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment
 • Comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA): a multidisciplinary assessment, usually led by a geriatrician.
   No standardised definition yet; it generally covers functional assessment and an inventory of the
   patient's geriatric syndromes.
 • Sequence: frailty assessment (commonly the Fried criteria) → physical performance (standing balance, a
   timed 360° turn as fast as possible, three chair rises, picking a pen up from the floor, putting on and
   removing a jacket) → laboratory tests (serum albumin, creatinine, brain natriuretic peptide) → respiratory
   function, forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1).

Concept — Optimise, do not "clear." Surgical risk comes from physiological reserve and operative context
(ASA score, urgency, duration), not from date of birth.

Major Geriatric Syndromes and Urology
 • Definition: ageing-related dysfunctions spanning multiple organ systems. They are associated with
   reduced life expectancy, disability, dependent care, hospitalisation and mortality, and are often poorly
   understood by urologists.
 • Interrelated and synergistic: frailty is linked to urinary incontinence (UI) and falls, and improving frailty
   reduces both. Falls increase frailty, and rushing to the toilet links incontinence to falls.

 • Major syndromes: frailty, depression, delirium, falls, incontinence, polypharmacy, vision impairment and
   hearing impairment. They worsen cardiovascular, diabetic, pulmonary, malignant and neurological
   disease.
 • Lifelines Cohort Study (Netherlands, adults ≥60): one or more geriatric syndromes raise the risk of a new

 • Balance and cognitive impairment raise the need for dependent care; chronic pain does not.

Polypharmacy and Medication Optimization
 • Polypharmacy means taking multiple medicines. It is not dangerous in itself, but with advanced age and
   harmful interactions it becomes a geriatric syndrome that worsens the others.
 • Inappropriate medicines cause side effects and non-medical harm, including financial toxicity. A key
   driver is the "prescribing cascade."

Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause
 • Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) (term coined 2014) affects 27–84% of postmenopausal
   individuals and is a geriatric-specific risk factor for urgency and urgency urinary incontinence (UUI).
 • More than vaginal atrophy: besides dyspareunia, pain, dryness and UTI, it causes urgency, frequency and
   UI, and is a common contributor to transient incontinence.
 • Treating atrophy with vaginal oestrogen may relieve some urge incontinence: urgency or UUI
   accompanies GSM in about 29%.
 • Hormone deprivation drives overactive bladder (OAB). Women on oestrogen-deprivation therapy for
   breast cancer have a markedly higher OAB hazard. Men on androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) and men
   with BPH on 5α-reductase inhibitors also have more OAB than α-blocker-treated BPH or healthy controls,
   and the effect grows as ADT approaches 5 years.
 • Before vaginal oestrogen, ask about previous breast or endometrial cancer. A hormone-sensitive
   malignancy is not necessarily a contraindication to non-systemic vaginal oestrogen, but it requires
   consultation with the oncologist.
 • Urethral caruncle: a GSM sequela; urethral tissue protrudes, presenting as spotting on underclothing. It is
   usually painless and benign, mimics postmenopausal bleeding, and affects voiding only if large.
 • True postmenopausal bleeding may be an early sign of endometrial or cervical cancer: work-up finds
   endometrial cancer in 9%.
 • Caruncle treatment: topical oestrogen → contraction and involution in most. Excision is rarely necessary
   but effective.

Fecal Incontinence
 • Fecal incontinence (FI) is perhaps the most dreaded geriatric syndrome. Urologists are often involved
   incidentally although it is not strictly urological.
 • It is under-reported: fewer than 30% of affected women, and probably fewer men, tell caregivers. Ask
   specifically about bowel function and constipation.
 • Greater dependency predicts more constipation and FI.
 • Prevalence rises with age and dependency: 14.4% in the US population, 17% of community-dwelling
   women over 85 and 43% of women in nursing homes.

Treatment of Incontinence in the Geriatric Patient
 • Treatment of UI in older patients differs in several ways from treatment in younger patients.
 • Behavioural therapy: see the American Urological Association (AUA)/Society for Urodynamics and Female
   Urology (SUFU) stress urinary incontinence guideline (2017), the AUA/SUFU idiopathic OAB guideline
   (2024) and Chapter 110.

Pharmacotherapy
 • Antimuscarinics improve OAB by 20–70% over placebo, but discontinuation is high. In older adults,
   fewer than half still take them at 6 months and about a quarter or fewer at 12 months.
 • Persistence is better with M3-selective agents, more severe UUI and a well-informed patient. Cost is a
   major reason for stopping.
 • Agents differ in lipophilicity, molecular size, receptor-subtype selectivity, half-life and metabolism. The
   ageing blood–brain barrier is more permeable, amplifying cognitive effects.
 • Retention is theoretical and uncommon, but consider checking post-void residual (PVR) after starting.
   Never exceed the maximum dose.

Incontinence in the Nursing Home Setting
 • Very common in long-term care residents ≥65: in the US, about half of short-stay and three-quarters of
   long-stay residents. More than half of residents elsewhere are affected, and combined urinary and fecal
   incontinence is frequent.
 • It correlates with a worse Functional Independence Measure score and with cognitive impairment, each
   of which independently drives long-term-care residence.
 • UI is a key driver of nursing home admission; the attributable fraction is larger in men than in women.
 • New UI after admission is common, suggesting that existing incontinence is unmasked at intake.
 • Reducing catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI) is a priority in acute and nursing-home care. Standard
   diagnostic definitions and appropriate catheter use lower catheter use, CAUTI incidence and cost.
 • Colonisation affects up to 98% of indwelling catheters in older nursing-home residents.
 • Treating asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) is harmful. The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA)
   advises against it in healthy non-pregnant adults, so standardised criteria for starting antibiotics are
   critical: Loeb's minimum criteria and the National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) criteria.

Urinary Tract Infections and Asymptomatic Bacteriuria
 • UTI is one of the commonest conditions in older adults, especially women. More than 50% of women
   and 12% of men have a UTI in their lifetime.
 • Older adults present atypically, including with mental status change. However, a causal link between
   confusion and UTI is not supported.
 • Annual UTI incidence in women rises steeply with age: several-fold higher at 75–84 and ≥85 than at 45–
   54 (Urologic Diseases in America Project).
 • First task: distinguish symptomatic UTI from OAB and from ASB.
 • ASB: bacteria on urine culture, regardless of pyuria, without signs or symptoms of UTI. Prevalence
   reaches 22% in non-institutionalised older women and about 10% in community-dwelling older men.
 • ASB raises serum and urinary inflammatory cytokines but still does not require antibiotics; outside
   pregnancy it carries little risk.
 • Immunosuppressed transplant recipients: evidence is inconclusive, with no evidence of increased graft
   failure from ASB.
 • Further reading: AUA core curriculum on recurrent UTI (2023); AUA/Canadian Urological Association
   (CUA)/SUFU guideline on recurrent uncomplicated UTI in women (2022); Chapter 26.

Prostate Cancer in the Elderly
 • Cancers in general affect older people far more than younger people. Prostate cancer treatment accounts
   for about 11% of US cancer treatment costs, possibly because of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) early
   detection.
 • USPSTF grade D: no PSA screening in men >70.
 • Estimated remaining life expectancy <10 years → a plan without curative intent.
 • See Chapter 153 and Chapters 158–166.

Bladder Cancer in the Elderly
 • Age is the single greatest risk factor, and bladder cancer is mainly a disease of older people. It is the
   fourth commonest cancer and the eighth leading cause of cancer death in US men.
 • AUA guidelines exist for non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) and for non-metastatic muscle-
   invasive bladder cancer (MIBC; AUA/ASCO/ASTRO/SUO), with index case scenarios.
 • Guidelines are often not followed in older patients. Restaging resection, recommended for T1
   intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC, was rarely done in Medicare recipients; it was more frequent with
   high grade and high stage. Restaging improved 5-year cancer-specific mortality.

age.

END OF LIFE CARE AND UROLOGY
 • Attitudes to palliative and end-of-life care strongly shape urological health in older adults. Family
   members may not share the patient's goals, so the goals-of-care conversation comes first.
 • It often starts during an admission, led by the multidisciplinary team. When the urological problem drives
   the admission, the urologist must be involved.
 • Palliative care is not withdrawal of care: when cure is no longer possible, treatment moves to palliation.
   It is no less focused or intense, and aggressive symptom management is a hallmark. Urologists play a key
   role at the end of life in genitourinary disease.
 • Hallmarks of high-quality palliative care: pain and symptom management; realising personal goals of
   patient and family; coordination of care.

Resources for Providers
 • AGS: information for clinicians and families. Families often face this responsibility for the first time and are
   unsure even of the goals.
 • Urology Care Foundation (AUA-affiliated): for patients and caregivers.
 • Organisations link families with part-time and full-time caregivers. Medicare, state Medicaid and local
   county and municipal resources are hard to navigate for patients and families.
Concept — Palliation changes the target from cure to symptoms, not the intensity of care, and it starts with
agreeing goals, which may differ between patient and family.

SUMMARY
 • A comorbidity list classifies disability and dependency poorly. Geriatric syndromes capture the negative
   synergy unique to older adults, and treating one syndrome often improves others. Care is tailored to
   health, comorbidity and environment.
