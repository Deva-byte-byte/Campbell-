Chapter 79 — Neoplasms of the Testis
• Over 95% of testicular neoplasms are germ cell tumours (GCTs), divided into seminoma and non-
  seminomatous germ cell tumour (NSGCT) because their natural history and treatment differ.
• Rare: 1–2% of cancers in adult males in the United States; 95% arise in the testis, 5% are extragonadal.
• The model of a curable neoplasm: cisplatin-based chemotherapy integrated with surgery has made GCT
  the paradigm of multidisciplinary cancer treatment.
• Cure of advanced disease rose from 5–10% (pre-cisplatin) to 80–90% long-term survival for metastatic
  GCT today.
• Because most patients are cured, the central objective is minimising treatment toxicity without
  compromising curability.
• Deaths arise from inherent platinum resistance and failure to eradicate residual disease early in the
  treatment course.
• Non-GCT tumours are rare: sex cord–stromal tumours, lymphoid and haematopoietic tumours, tumours
  of the collecting duct and rete testis, and adnexal tumours.

GERM CELL TUMOURS
Epidemiology
• Burden: in 2023 an estimated 9190 men were diagnosed with testis cancer in the United States and 470
  died of it.
• Commonest malignancy in males aged 20–40; second commonest (after leukaemia) at 15–19.
• Age curve: most cases at 15–55; incidence rises steeply after puberty, peaks at 25–35, then declines so
  that men of 50–54 have the same incidence as boys of 15–19.
• Bilateral GCT ≈2%; most are metachronous (mean interval 5 years), and histology is discordant between
  the two tumours in 30–50%.
• Geography: highest in Scandinavia, Western Europe and Australia/New Zealand; intermediate in the
  United States and United Kingdom; lowest in Africa and Asia.
• Ethnicity (United States): incidence in non-Hispanic White men is 3.6 times that in Black men, 2.5 times
  that in Asian men and slightly above that in Hispanic men.
• Incidence is rising worldwide for both seminoma and NSGCT (United States age-adjusted rate about 3.7
  per 100,000 in 1975 rising to about 6.4 in 2014); the rise among US Hispanic men has been marked, with
  no significant trend in other groups.
• Stage migration through awareness and earlier diagnosis: localised disease at diagnosis rose from 55% to
  68% (1973–2014); only about 13% present with distant metastases.

Risk Factors
• Five established risk factors: White race, cryptorchidism, family history, personal history of testis cancer,
  and germ cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS, also termed intratubular germ cell neoplasia, ITGCN).
• Infertile and subfertile men also have a higher incidence.
• Birth-cohort effects explain the recent rise, implying a major role for diet and environment; early
  exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals is associated, although no specific agent is proven.

 • Cryptorchidism, as the examinable pair:
     ◦ Undescended testis: relative risk (RR) 4–6 in the affected gonad, falling to 2–3 if orchidopexy is
       performed before puberty.
     ◦ Contralateral descended testis: slightly increased risk (RR about 1.7, meta-analysis).
 • Family history: a first-degree relative raises risk substantially and lowers median age at diagnosis by 2–3
   years; RR 8–12 with an affected brother versus 2–4 with an affected father.
 • Personal history: twelvefold risk of contralateral GCT, yet the 15-year cumulative incidence is only 2%.
 • GCNIS is the precursor of most GCTs. Exceptions: prepubertal GCTs (rarely after puberty), ovarian cystic
   teratomas, dermoid cysts and spermatocytic tumours.
 • GCNIS lies adjacent to invasive GCT in 80–90% and carries a 50% risk of invasive GCT within 5 years and
   70% within 7 years.
 • Contralateral GCNIS in 5–9% of men with GCT, rising to about 36% with testicular atrophy or
   cryptorchidism.
 • Prenatal origin: gene expression profiling shows GCNIS develops before birth from an arrested gonocyte.
 • Microlithiasis: in men with previous GCT, contralateral microlithiasis signals higher GCNIS risk; in the
   general population its significance is unclear — it is common, and few affected men develop GCT.

 KEY POINTS — EPIDEMIOLOGY AND RISK
   • GCT is the commonest solid malignancy in males aged 20–40.
   • Bilateral GCT in 2%; metachronous presentation is the rule.
   • Incidence highest in White and lowest in Black individuals.
   • Cryptorchidism, personal or family history of GCT and GCNIS are risk factors; prepubertal orchidopexy
     lowers risk.
   • All postpubertal GCTs except spermatocytic tumour carry extra copies of 12p [isochromosome 12p,
     i(12p)]: diagnostic for GCT in carcinoma of unknown primary and for somatic malignancy arising from
     teratoma.

Concept — GCT is a disease of the arrested fetal gonocyte: GCNIS forms before birth, so the risk factors
(cryptorchidism, family history, previous contralateral tumour, atrophic testis) mark an abnormal
developmental field, and early orchidopexy reduces the risk without abolishing it.

Histologic Classification
 • Postpubertal GCTs are GCNIS-derived or non-GCNIS-derived; the vast majority are GCNIS-derived.
 • Clinically, GCNIS-derived tumours divide into seminoma (52–56%) and NSGCT (44–48%).
 • NSGCT subtypes: embryonal carcinoma (EC), yolk sac tumour, teratoma and choriocarcinoma, pure or
   mixed; most NSGCTs are mixed.
 • Any NSGCT element makes the tumour an NSGCT, however small its proportion alongside seminoma.

Germ Cell Neoplasia in Situ
 • Undifferentiated, seminoma-like germ cells lying basally within seminiferous tubules; spermatogenesis
   reduced or absent.
 • Precursor of every postpubertal invasive GCT except spermatocytic tumour.
 • No prognostic significance for relapse when found in the orchidectomy specimen.
 • Much less frequent in paediatric GCTs.

Seminoma
 • Commonest GCT; older patients than NSGCT (fourth–fifth decade).
 • Gross: soft, tan–white, diffuse or multinodular; necrosis focal and less prominent than in other GCTs.
 • Microscopy: sheets of polygonal cells with clear cytoplasm, nests separated by fibrovascular septa
   containing lymphocytes; lymphocytic and granulomatous reactions common, with an association with
   sarcoidosis.
 • Syncytiotrophoblasts (human chorionic gonadotrophin [hCG]-positive) in about 15%, of no clear
   prognostic significance.
 • Mimics: solid-pattern EC, yolk sac tumour, Sertoli cell tumour.
 • Immunohistochemistry: CD30-negative, CD117-positive, strongly placental alkaline phosphatase (PLAP)-
   positive.
 • "Anaplastic seminoma" is no longer recognised — no biological or clinical significance.
 • Seminoma is the common precursor of NSGCT subtypes and can transform into NSGCT elements, which
   shapes management of non-responders.

Spermatocytic Tumour
 • Formerly "spermatocytic seminoma"; now a distinct entity, less than 1% of GCTs.
 • Not GCNIS-derived, not linked to cryptorchidism or bilaterality, no i(12p), never part of a mixed GCT —
   sharing GCNIS independence with juvenile yolk sac tumour and teratoma.
 • Negative for OCT3/4, PLAP and glycogen (periodic acid–Schiff, PAS); round nuclei; minimal lymphocytes;
   three cell types (small lymphocyte-like, medium eosinophilic, large mono- or multinucleated).
 • Peak in the sixth decade.
 • Benign — orchidectomy almost always curative; metastasis exceptionally rare.
 • Exceptions: spermatocytic tumour with sarcoma, and the anaplastic variant — widely metastatic,
   chemoresistant, poor prognosis.

Embryonal Carcinoma
 • Undifferentiated primitive epithelial cells with crowded pleomorphic nuclei; tan–yellow with haemorrhage
   and necrosis; solid, papillary, glandular-alveolar or tubular growth; syncytiotrophoblasts sometimes
   present.
 • Aggressive, with a high metastatic rate — often with normal tumour markers.
 • Most undifferentiated, totipotent NSGCT: can differentiate into any other NSGCT type, including
   teratoma, in the primary or at metastatic sites.
 • Presence and proportion of EC predict occult metastasis in clinical stage (CS) I NSGCT.
 • Immunohistochemistry: AE1/AE3, PLAP and OCT3/4 positive; c-KIT negative.

Choriocarcinoma
 • Rare, aggressive; presents with extremely high hCG and disseminated disease — usually poor risk (stage
   IIIC) through hCG level and/or non-pulmonary visceral metastases.
 • Haematogenous spread to lungs, liver and brain.
 • Syncytiotrophoblasts (hCG-positive) and cytotrophoblasts; prominent haemorrhage and necrosis.
 • Haemorrhage — spontaneous or immediately after chemotherapy starts — can be catastrophic in lung
   or brain.

 • Endocrine effects: hCG shares an identical α-subunit with thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and
   luteinising hormone (LH) → hyperthyroidism and raised androgen production; hyperprolactinaemia
   reported.

Yolk Sac Tumour
 • Pure form rare in adult testis and retroperitoneum, more common in mediastinal and paediatric GCTs;
   frequent within mixed GCTs.
 • Reticular network of cuboidal cells; glandular, papillary or microcystic patterns.
 • Schiller–Duval body (resembling an endodermal sinus) in about half; eosinophilic hyaline globules in
   most.
 • Produces alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), not hCG.

Teratoma
 • Elements of at least two of the three germ layers, intermixed. Mature versus immature has no clinical
   significance in postpubertal males and is not reported.
 • May contain bone, cartilage, teeth, hair and squamous epithelium ("monster tumour"); solid and cystic.
 • Markers normal or AFP mildly raised.
 • Present in about half of adult mixed GCTs; pure teratoma uncommon.
 • Benign histology, malignant biology: aneuploidy, i(12p), variable proliferation; cyst fluid often contains
   hCG and AFP.
 • Chemoresistant → residual masses after chemotherapy require resection; limits every chemotherapy-
   alone strategy.
 • Unresected teratoma may grow uncontrollably and become unresectable, undergo malignant
   transformation, or cause late relapse — each potentially lethal.
 • Teratoma with somatic-type malignancy (malignant transformation): rhabdomyosarcoma,
   adenocarcinoma, primitive neuroectodermal tumour; chromosome 12 abnormalities or i(12p) prove GCT
   origin; aggressive and chemoresistant. Only 4% arise in the testis — most arise at metastatic sites 3–4
   years after chemotherapy from unresected teratoma.
 • Expert pathology review is worthwhile: second-opinion review changes histologic subtype in about one-
   third and lymphovascular invasion status in about one-fifth, altering treatment.

Concept — Management follows the least differentiated and most chemoresistant element: any NSGCT
component makes the tumour an NSGCT, EC drives occult metastasis, choriocarcinoma drives haematogenous
spread and haemorrhage, and teratoma — histologically benign but genetically malignant and
chemoresistant — is the reason surgery stays integral to cure.

Initial Presentation
Signs and Symptoms
 • Painless testicular mass is the commonest presentation; any solid testicular mass on examination or
   imaging is malignant until proven otherwise.
 • Acute pain (less common) comes from rapid expansion through intratumoural haemorrhage or infarction
   — commoner in NSGCT, which is more vascular and faster-growing.
 • A history of trauma is common but incidental — it merely draws attention to the mass. Vague scrotal
   discomfort or heaviness may be reported.
 • Metastases at diagnosis: about two-thirds of NSGCT versus 15% of pure seminoma; metastatic
   symptoms are the presenting complaint in 10–20%.
 • Metastatic features: retroperitoneal bulk (palpable mass, abdominal pain, flank pain from ureteric
   obstruction, back pain from psoas or nerve-root involvement, leg swelling from inferior vena cava
   compression, gastrointestinal symptoms); lung (dyspnoea, chest pain, cough, haemoptysis);
   supraclavicular node (neck mass).
 • Gynaecomastia in about 2%: raised hCG, reduced androgen or raised oestrogen (most often Leydig cell
   tumours).
 • About two-thirds have reduced fertility, but it is rarely the presenting complaint.

Physical Examination
 • Examine both testes — relative size, consistency, intra- and extratesticular masses; atrophy is common,
   especially after cryptorchidism.
 • Any firm area within the testis is suspicious and must be investigated.
 • A hydrocele obscuring the testis → scrotal ultrasound.
 • Examine the abdomen, inguinal nodes (especially after previous inguinal or scrotal surgery), breasts,
   supraclavicular fossae and chest.

Differential Diagnosis
 • Epididymo-orchitis, torsion, haematoma, paratesticular neoplasm; hernia, varicocele and spermatocele
   are usually distinguishable clinically.
 • A firm intratesticular mass is cancer until proven otherwise → scrotal ultrasound.

 • Presumed epididymo-orchitis → re-examine 2–4 weeks after completing oral antibiotics; persistent mass
   or pain → ultrasound.

Diagnostic Delay
 • Common, contributed to by patients and physicians; up to one-third are first misdiagnosed as
   epididymitis or hydrocele.
 • Young men delay through denial, ignorance or poor access; physicians may fixate on metastatic symptoms
   → unnecessary laparotomy, neck dissection or mastectomy for unsuspected GCT.
 • Delay is associated with higher stage, poorer chemotherapy response and reduced survival. A survival
   penalty for long delay was shown before the cisplatin era but not within it; unnecessary laparotomy
   delaying treatment beyond 30 days → more intensive chemotherapy needed.
 • Consider GCT in any male aged 15–50 with a firm testicular mass, a midline retroperitoneal mass or a
   left supraclavicular mass.
Concept — The testis is often examined because of the metastasis, not the reverse: GCT strikes young men
who delay, and misattributed metastatic symptoms lead to the wrong operation — hence the rule that a
midline or left supraclavicular mass in a young man mandates testicular examination, ultrasound and
markers.

Diagnostic Testing and Initial Management
Scrotal Ultrasound
 • An extension of the physical examination — available, inexpensive, non-invasive; 5–10 MHz transducers
   detect lesions of a few millimetres and separate intra- from extratesticular pathology.
 • GCT is hypoechoic and may be multifocal: heterogeneous echotexture → NSGCT; homogeneous →
   seminoma.
 • Colour Doppler flow suggests malignancy, but its absence does not exclude GCT.
 • Microlithiasis alone does not warrant further evaluation.
 • Scan both testes: bilateral GCT 2%, though synchronous bilateral tumours are rare (0.5%).
 • Advanced GCT with a normal testicular examination → ultrasound for a "burned-out" primary
   (impalpable scar or calcification). GCT is among the tumours most prone to spontaneous regression —
   seminoma most often.
 • Intratesticular lesion (discrete nodule, stellate scar, coarse calcification) → radical orchidectomy,
   because GCNIS and residual teratoma are frequent; normal testes on examination and ultrasound →
   primary extragonadal GCT.
 • Small (<10 mm) impalpable lesion with normal markers and no metastases is a dilemma: most are
   benign (cysts, infarcts, Leydig cell nodules, small Leydig or Sertoli cell tumours) but 20–50% are small
   GCTs, usually seminoma; risk rises with size — about 50% below 1 cm, 80% or more at 1–2 cm.
 • Options: inguinal orchidectomy; testis-sparing inguinal exploration and excision with frozen section; or
   serial ultrasound with exploration of growing lesions. Intraoperative ultrasound localises the lesion.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging
 • Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) when ultrasound is equivocal or suboptimal —
   distinguishes testicular from extratesticular and neoplastic from non-neoplastic lesions.

 • Remove the testis and spermatic cord to the internal inguinal ring through an inguinal incision.

 • Trans-scrotal orchidectomy or biopsy is contraindicated: it leaves the inguinal cord and alters lymphatic
   drainage → local recurrence and pelvic or inguinal nodal metastasis.
 • Avoid delays beyond 1–2 weeks — GCT grows rapidly.
 • Establishes histology and pT stage, gives prognostic information, and is curative alone in 80–85% of CS I
   seminoma and 70–80% of CS I NSGCT.
 • Pathology report: histologic type (each component and its proportion in mixed GCT), size, multifocality,
   local invasion (rete testis, tunica albuginea, tunica vaginalis, epididymis, cord, scrotum), primary tumour
   (pT) stage in the tumour, node, metastasis (TNM) system, GCNIS, lymphovascular invasion (LVI) and
   margin status.
 • Expert review recommended: second-opinion review at a high-volume centre changed pathological stage
   in about a quarter (mostly LVI or cord invasion), downstaging many pT2 and pT3 tumours.

Testis-Sparing Surgery
 • No role for a suspected neoplasm when the contralateral testis is normal.
 • Consider for organ-confined tumours <2–3 cm (up to 30% of testicular volume) with synchronous
   bilateral tumours or a solitary testis with adequate androgen production; also for suspected benign or
   indeterminate lesions — up to 80% of lesions <3 cm with symptoms for over 6 months are benign.
 • Seldom feasible above 3 cm — complete excision leaves too little parenchyma.
 • Technique: inguinal exploration → excision → frozen section (reliably separates benign from malignant) →
   biopsies of adjacent parenchyma for GCNIS.
 • GCNIS → adjuvant radiotherapy ≥20 Gy to the residual testis: prevents GCT while preserving Leydig
   function, but causes permanent sterility, and up to 40% eventually need testosterone.
 • German Testicular Cancer Study Group: no local recurrences with adjuvant radiotherapy, versus
   recurrence in most men who omitted it.
 • Radiotherapy may be deferred to allow paternity, with close follow-up.

Contralateral Testis Biopsy
 • Contralateral GCNIS in 5–9%; up to 36% with an atrophic testis, cryptorchidism or age under 40.
 • Open inguinal biopsy may be considered with these risk factors or a suspicious contralateral ultrasound.

Suspected Extragonadal Germ Cell Tumour
 • About 5% of GCTs are extragonadal: consider GCT in any young male with a midline mass and normal
   testes.
 • Only one-third of metastatic GCT without a testicular mass is truly extragonadal; most are burned-out
   testicular primaries (GCNIS in 30–50%; scar or coarse calcification on ultrasound in one-third).
 • Raised AFP and/or hCG with a normal testicular evaluation is diagnostic — biopsy is not needed before
   treatment; normal markers → biopsy.
 • Poorly differentiated carcinoma with no identifiable primary: consider extragonadal GCT with malignant
   transformation; i(12p) in the biopsy supports it.
 • Orchidectomy at some point during treatment if the metastatic pattern fits a right- or left-sided testicular
   primary, or ultrasound shows a burned-out tumour.

 KEY POINTS — PRESENTATION AND DIAGNOSIS
   • A solid intratesticular mass in a postpubertal male is GCT until proven otherwise.
   • With rare exceptions, inguinal orchidectomy with high cord ligation.
   • Testis-sparing surgery only in highly selected patients in whom the preserved testis gives sufficient
     androgen.

Concept — Inguinal orchidectomy is at once diagnosis, staging and, for most stage I patients, cure; every
departure from it (testis sparing, chemotherapy before orchidectomy) must be justified by androgen
preservation or survival, and the testis must still be dealt with because it is a chemotherapy sanctuary.

Clinical Staging
 • Clinical stage rests on primary histology and pT stage, post-orchidectomy markers, and examination and
   imaging.
 • AJCC/UICC consensus classification (1997) first added the marker (S) category to anatomic staging; the
   2002 update made LVI in an organ-confined tumour pT2.
 • CS I = confined to testis; CS II = regional (retroperitoneal) nodes; CS III = non-regional nodes and/or
   visceral metastases.

Staging Imaging Studies
 • Predictable spread: lymphatic → retroperitoneal nodes → distant sites; choriocarcinoma is the exception
   (haematogenous). The retroperitoneum is the first site in 70–80%.
 • Right-to-left drainage: crossover from the right landing zone is common; left → right is rare and usually
   reflects bulky disease.
 • Caudal (iliac, inguinal) deposits reflect retrograde spread from bulk or, rarely, aberrant drainage.
 • Lymph drains to the cisterna chyli behind the right renal artery and right crus (hence retrocrural nodes)
   → thoracic duct → posterior mediastinum → left supraclavicular fossa.

Clinical Staging of the Abdomen and Pelvis
 • All patients: computed tomography (CT) of abdomen and pelvis with oral and intravenous contrast —
   also shows anomalies relevant to RPLND (circumaortic or retroaortic left renal vein, lower-pole renal
   artery, retrocaval ureter). MRI is an alternative (slower, costlier, less available).
 • Nodes enlarged on CT in 10–20% of seminomas and 60–70% of NSGCTs.
 • The retroperitoneum is hardest to stage: 25–35% of CS I NSGCT with a "normal" CT have pathological
   nodes, unchanged over four decades. No agreed size criterion; a 10-mm cut-off gives many false
   negatives. CS IIA–B is overstaged in 12–40% (negative nodes at RPLND).
 • Lower threshold in the landing zone: a 4-mm cut-off there (10 mm elsewhere) raises sensitivity above
   90% at the cost of specificity (about 50–60%) → 5–9-mm landing-zone nodes, especially anterior to the
   great vessels, are suspicious.
 • Base decisions on CT within 4 weeks of treatment; equivocal CT with normal hCG and AFP → repeat
   imaging in 6–8 weeks.

Pathological Staging of the Abdomen and Pelvis
 • Staging RPLND without curative intent is used for CS I–IIA NSGCT in select European centres and most
   laparoscopic series, to decide on chemotherapy.
 • Pathological N counts nodes: pN1 ≤5 nodes, none >2 cm; pN2 >5 nodes and/or any node 2–5 cm; pN3 any
   node >5 cm.
 • Relapse after RPLND tracks nodal burden: 10–30% for pN1 versus 50–80% for pN2–3.
 • pN cannot be applied after chemotherapy.

Chest Imaging
 • Chest imaging for all before management decisions. Thoracic metastasis without retroperitoneal disease
   or raised markers is uncommon (especially seminoma), so routine chest CT yields false positives that
   complicate care.
 • Chest radiograph first; CT if post-orchidectomy markers are raised, metastases are found on
   examination or abdominal CT, or the radiograph is abnormal or equivocal. Consider chest CT in CS I
   NSGCT with LVI or EC predominance (haematogenous lung spread despite a clear abdomen).
 • Mediastinal or hilar nodes without retroperitoneal disease → suspect lymphoma or sarcoidosis; confirm
   GCT by mediastinoscopy and biopsy before chemotherapy.
 • No routine bone scan or brain imaging — except brain imaging when hCG exceeds 10,000 IU/L
   (metastatic choriocarcinoma, which favours the brain).

Serum Tumour Markers (Staging)
 • Post-orchidectomy AFP, hCG and LDH for everyone: confirm decline according to half-life when levels
   were raised.
 • New or rising markers after orchidectomy = metastatic disease → induction chemotherapy.
 • Slow decline (not by half-life) with negative imaging → monitor until normal or rising.
 • Stable, slightly raised AFP or hCG → exclude other causes before acting.
 • Use markers measured within 4 weeks of treatment.

Prognostic Classification of Advanced Germ Cell Tumour
 • International Germ Cell Cancer Collaborative Group (IGCCCG), 1997: pooled analysis of 5202 men with
   advanced NSGCT treated with platinum (1975–1990) identified AFP, hCG and LDH at the start of
   chemotherapy, non-pulmonary visceral metastases (NPVM) and primary mediastinal NSGCT as
   independent predictors; in 660 advanced seminomas only NPVM mattered.
 • Assign an IGCCCG group to every metastatic GCT at diagnosis to choose chemotherapy. It does not apply
   to relapse and uses post-orchidectomy markers at the start of chemotherapy, not pre-orchidectomy
   values.
 • No poor-risk category exists for seminoma.
 • Survival has improved since: a meta-analysis of NSGCT treated after 1989 gave 5-year survival of 94%,
   83% and 71% (good, intermediate, poor) — especially better for poor risk, reflecting better therapy and
   experience.

 KEY POINTS — STAGING
   • Spread is predictable: primary → retroperitoneal nodes → distant sites.
   • Landing zone: left = PA and left renal hilar nodes; right = interaortocaval and paracaval nodes.
   • CT is optimal for the retroperitoneum, but with a 1-cm cut-off false negatives occur in 25–35% of CS I
     NSGCT and 14–20% of CS I seminoma.
   • Rising post-orchidectomy markers indicate metastatic GCT → chemotherapy.
   • IGCCCG classification gives prognosis and dictates chemotherapy.
   • Offer sperm cryopreservation to all before RPLND, chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

Concept — Staging is anatomy plus biology: the lymphatic map (side-specific landing zones, right-to-left
crossover, cisterna chyli to left supraclavicular fossa) tells you where to look and why CT under-stages, while
post-orchidectomy markers read against their half-lives are the only direct signal of occult systemic disease —
which is why the marker category, not node size alone, governs stage and risk group.

Treatment
Therapeutic Principles
 • Rapid growth plus near-universal curability → rapid diagnosis, staging and treatment, so that no patient
   dies unnecessarily or needs toxicity that earlier care would have avoided.
 • An aggressive approach is justified: chemotherapy is given despite low white cell or platelet counts;
   cisplatin is given even with moderate-to-severe renal impairment; all residual disease is resected after
   chemotherapy, even at multiple sites. Young, fit patients tolerate this.
 • Markers steer management: raised after orchidectomy → chemotherapy; rising during or after therapy →
   refractory or relapsed disease; levels at the start of chemotherapy set regimen and duration.
 • Centralise care: GCT is rare, algorithms are nuanced, RPLND evidence comes from a few high-volume
   surgeons, and most US urology residents perform two or fewer RPLNDs; survival is better at high-volume
   centres → treat there, with RPLND by experienced surgeons.

   • Seminoma is indolent, with less metastatic disease.

Germ Cell Neoplasia In Situ
 • Diagnosed on biopsy for infertility, contralateral biopsy in GCT, or within a testis-sparing specimen; treated
   because of the high risk of invasive GCT.
 • Options: radical orchidectomy (most definitive), low-dose radiotherapy (≥20 Gy), close observation —
   chosen by wish for paternity, state of the contralateral testis and wish to avoid testosterone replacement.
 • ≥20 Gy gives local control similar to orchidectomy while preserving endocrine function, because Leydig
   cells are relatively radioresistant compared with germinal epithelium; but up to 40% eventually need
   testosterone, so monitor androgen production. Doses <20 Gy → recurrent GCNIS.
 • Normal contralateral testis and wish for paternity → orchidectomy, because radiotherapy scatter impairs
   contralateral spermatogenesis.
 • Subnormal semen still adequate for assisted reproduction → surveillance with serial ultrasound,
   deferring treatment until pregnancy or GCT; alternatively testicular exploration, sperm harvest and
   cryopreservation, then orchidectomy and testosterone.
 • GCNIS with planned cisplatin chemotherapy: chemotherapy reduces but does not eliminate risk (about
   one-fifth by 5 years, nearly half by 10 years) → low-dose radiotherapy after chemotherapy, or biopsy 2
   years or more after chemotherapy and treat only if GCNIS persists.

 KEY POINTS — GCNIS
   • GCNIS is the precursor of GCT, with a 50% risk of invasive GCT within 5 years.
   • Radical orchidectomy and low-dose (≥20 Gy) radiotherapy are effective.

Concept — GCNIS treatment trades three testicular functions — germ cells (fertility), Leydig cells

Nonseminoma Germ Cell Tumours
Clinical Stage I Nonseminoma Germ Cell Tumour
 • About one-third of NSGCT patients are CS I with normal post-orchidectomy markers; long-term survival
   with surveillance, RPLND or primary chemotherapy approaches 100%, hence the controversy.
 • Occult metastases (retroperitoneal or distant) in only 20–30% → any post-orchidectomy intervention
   overtreats the 70–80% with disease confined to the testis.
 • Most centres use a risk-adapted approach; some offer surveillance to all regardless of risk.
Risk assessment
 • LVI and a predominant EC component are the main predictors of occult metastasis; "EC predominance"
   is defined variously as 45–90% EC.
 • In older series, occult metastasis ran at 45–90% with LVI and 30–80% with EC predominance; with neither
   factor, risk is under 20%.
 • Other predictors: advanced pT stage, absence of mature teratoma, absence of yolk sac tumour, any EC,
   MIB-1 staining, tumour size, age. A pooled analysis ranked LVI (odds ratio about 5), MIB-1 staining >70%
   (about 5) and EC predominance (about 3) strongest.
 • Factor in the CT: landing-zone nodes above 5–9 mm are suspicious, since a 1-cm cut-off misses many.
 • CS I low/high risk (by LVI and EC) is not the IGCCCG classification, which applies only to metastatic
   disease. Only one CS I model has been prospectively validated, and none incorporates staging CT.
 • Contemporary risks are lower: prospective series put occult metastasis with LVI or EC predominance at
   35–55% (relapse on surveillance about 50% with LVI, one-third with EC predominance); in the Albers
   RPLND trial only 18% were node-positive although 42% had LVI — attributed to closer CT scrutiny and
   stage migration.
Surveillance
 • Rationale: 70–80% are cured by orchidectomy, and relapses are salvaged by good-risk chemotherapy;
   survival matches RPLND and primary chemotherapy → a standard option that confines treatment to
   those proven to need it.
 • Disadvantages: highest relapse risk; follow-up beyond 5 years; SMN risk from repeated CT; relapse needs
   more intensive treatment than upfront therapy would have.
 • Most-used approach in the United States for CS IA (about three-quarters) and CS IB (about half).
 • Pooled series of over 3000 men: mean relapse about 28%, cancer-specific mortality about 1%.
 • Over 90% of relapses occur within 2 years; late relapse (>5 years) in up to 1% (up to 5% in some reports).
 • 65–75% of relapses are retroperitoneal, with or without raised markers. Most need induction
   chemotherapy (bulky >3 cm nodes, raised markers or distant disease); normal markers with nonbulky (<3
   cm) retroperitoneal relapse → RPLND is an option.

 • Schedule: no schedule proven superior; intensive in years 0–2, less frequent in years 3–5, and beyond 5
   years for late relapse (whether with CT is controversial). CT use ranges from 2 to 13 or more scans in 5
   years.
 • Medical Research Council (MRC) randomised trial: 2 versus 5 CT scans in years 1–2 → no difference in
   survival, IGCCCG group or stage at relapse.
 • Non-compliance with surveillance: 35–80%.
 • Relapses are found by CT or markers; chest radiograph and examination rarely detect them. With LVI,
   relapse is more often marker-detected; without LVI, more often CT-detected.
 • Late relapses on surveillance are treated as successfully as early ones.
Retroperitoneal lymph node dissection
 • Rationale for RPLND:
     ◦ retroperitoneum is the commonest site of occult disease, with low associated systemic risk
     ◦ teratoma in 15–25% of occult retroperitoneal metastases (chemoresistant)
     ◦ low abdominal–pelvic recurrence after full bilateral template → no routine surveillance CT
     ◦ high cure with RPLND alone for low-volume malignancy (pN1) and teratoma (pN1–3)
     ◦ ≥75% avoid chemotherapy if adjuvant therapy is reserved for pN2–3
     ◦ relapses salvaged by good-risk chemotherapy
     ◦ low short- and long-term morbidity with nerve sparing by experienced surgeons
 • In low-stage NSGCT the therapeutic focus is the retroperitoneum, and RPLND gives the best control with
   the least serious long-term morbidity.
 • Disadvantages: major abdominal surgery for all; needs experienced surgeons (not deliverable
   everywhere); highest rate of double therapy. US use is falling (about 15% for CS IA and IB).
 • Pathological stage II in 19–28%; 66–81% of these are cured by RPLND alone; cancer-specific survival (±
   adjuvant chemotherapy) approaches 100% with negligible late relapse; retroperitoneal recurrence under
   2%.
 • Teratoma only in the retroperitoneum → low relapse whatever the pN stage; no adjuvant
   chemotherapy.
 • Full bilateral template: abdominal–pelvic recurrence <2% and antegrade ejaculation >90% with nerve
   sparing → standard of care for primary RPLND; mandatory with suspicious nodes (CT or intraoperative)
   or somatic-type malignancy in the primary; a modified template is acceptable for clinically negative
   nodes.
 • Modified templates: right-sided may omit PA nodes below the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) —
   omitting PA nodes above the IMA is controversial; left-sided may omit paracaval, precaval and retrocaval
   nodes — omitting interaortocaval nodes is controversial.
 • RPLND cures 60–90% of pN1 and up to 100% of teratoma-only disease; relapse with pN2–3 exceeds
   50%.
 • Two adjuvant cycles (BEP×2 or EP×2) cut relapse to 1% or less. In the randomised adjuvant trial
   (Williams), relapse was 6% with adjuvant chemotherapy versus 49% with observation, with no overall
   survival difference → adjuvant chemotherapy and observation are both acceptable for pathological
   stage II; counsel on the trade-off.
Primary chemotherapy
 • Primary chemotherapy = chemotherapy after orchidectomy for CS I, as distinct from adjuvant
   chemotherapy after RPLND for pathological stage II.

 • Rationale: two cycles eradicate micrometastases in the adjuvant setting, and 20–25% need chemotherapy
   despite RPLND (adjuvant or at relapse). Goal: minimise relapse and avoid RPLND and later induction
   chemotherapy.
 • Advantages: lowest relapse of any single modality; deliverable in community centres.
 • Disadvantages: does not treat retroperitoneal teratoma (risk of chemoresistant or late relapse); long-
   term retroperitoneal CT surveillance still required; everyone is exposed to late toxicity, and no safe lower
   limit of exposure is known.
 • Used far less in the United States (about 13% CS IA, 37% CS IB) than in Europe.
 • With LVI and/or EC predominance, relapse falls from 30–60% to about 2–3%. Pooled series: relapse
   about 2.4%, mortality about 0.45% — but about 18% of relapsing men died: relapses after chemotherapy

   naive and almost always curable. Overall cure (>99%) is similar across approaches.
 • Relapses after primary chemotherapy are almost all retroperitoneal → CT surveillance of abdomen and
   pelvis.
 • Many European centres prefer primary BEP because RPLND there is mainly a staging procedure.
 • Guidelines now recommend BEP×1: over 1000 patients, relapse about 2.3%, mortality about 0.2% — not

 • Slightly raised, stable markers may be false positives — interpret cautiously.

Chemotherapy for good-risk NSGCT
 • Aim after BEP×4 became standard: less toxicity for good risk, better outcomes for intermediate and poor

 • Omitting bleomycin (pulmonary fibrosis, Raynaud phenomenon) is one of the few remaining

Chemotherapy for intermediate- and poor-risk NSGCT
 • BEP×4 standard since 1987.

 • Slow AFP/hCG decline (longer time to normalisation and longer half-lives) is adverse; intensifying
   chemotherapy for slow decliners improved PFS but not survival → BEP×4 remains standard first line.

   some guidelines observe, others resect if the pre-chemotherapy mass exceeded 3 cm.
 • Why resect: persistent GCT elements (teratoma or viable malignancy) in 50% or more — histology by
   setting in the table below.
 • Completely resected viable malignancy: 5-year survival 45–77%; left unresected it relapses, and only 25–
   35% achieve durable remission with second-line chemotherapy.
 • Teratoma at metastatic sites in 15% or more; unresected it risks growing teratoma syndrome,
   transformation or late relapse; long-term survival 75–90% after resection of residual teratoma.
 • In-field retroperitoneal relapse <2% after full bilateral template → abdominal imaging surveillance
   largely unnecessary.
 • Non-GCT malignancy (malignant transformation) in 6–8% of PCS specimens; rhabdomyosarcoma
   commonest; i(12p) or chromosome 12 abnormalities confirm GCT origin. Resistant to GCT chemotherapy
   → complete resection gives 50–66% survival, incomplete resection is usually fatal; histology-specific
   chemotherapy gave partial responses in selected patients.
 • Necrosis only → relapse ≤10%.
 • Predictors of necrosis: no teratoma in the primary, greater percentage shrinkage, small residual size — yet
   models carry a consistent ≈20% false-negative rate, so necrosis cannot be predicted well enough to omit
   PCS. No teratoma in the primary does not exclude retroperitoneal teratoma.
 • FDG-PET has no role for NSGCT residual masses: teratoma is not FDG-avid, and PET predicted histology
   no better than CT or markers.
 • After complete response (26–62%): residual masses ≤20 mm still harbour teratoma or viable malignancy
   (table), so size cannot exclude disease. Observation of complete responders gave 92% survival at 5–10
   years (Indiana), and recent series of residual masses <1 cm observed without PCS reported relapse 4–10%

 • Multiple sites (about one-third; retroperitoneum, chest, left supraclavicular fossa commonest) → resect
   all measurable disease; the authors stage infra- and supradiaphragmatic resections separately. Histology
   is discordant between sites in 22–46%; non-retroperitoneal sites show more necrosis.

Relapsed Nonseminoma Germ Cell Tumour
 • Treatment depends on previous therapy and, sometimes, relapse site; chemotherapy-naive relapse has a
   far better prognosis than relapse after chemotherapy.
Chemotherapy-naive NSGCT relapse
 • Follows surveillance or RPLND for CS I, or RPLND alone for CS IIA–B. Markers are raised in 60–75% of
   surveillance relapses.
 • Induction chemotherapy by IGCCCG group → cure >95%. Surveillance relapse confined to the
   retroperitoneum, nonbulky (<3 cm) with normal markers → induction chemotherapy or RPLND
   (especially if the primary contained teratoma); RPLND ± adjuvant chemotherapy cures nearly 100% and
   spares chemotherapy toxicity.
 • Relapse after RPLND is usually in the lungs or mediastinum, virtually always cured by first-line
   chemotherapy.
 • Most relapses occur within 2 years; relapse beyond 2 years with normal markers → biopsy or resection
   (teratoma likely). Unlike post-chemotherapy relapse, chemo-naive relapse after 2 years carries the same
   prognosis as earlier relapse.
Post-chemotherapy NSGCT relapse: early
 • Relapse within 2 years of chemotherapy = early; treated with second-line (salvage) chemotherapy.
   Beyond 2 years = late relapse, which differs in prognosis and treatment.
 • Incomplete responders — no complete response to first line, or relapse within 6 months of one — fare
   worst.
 • Pooled analysis of about 2000 patients: median PFS 10 months, overall survival 41 months; adverse
   factors: incomplete response, primary mediastinal NSGCT (hazard ratio 3.0), NPVM, raised AFP and hCG.
 • Standard second-line regimens: VIP×4, VeIP×4 (after prior etoposide) and TIP×4 — never compared
   randomly; long-term remission 23–35% with VIP/VeIP, relapse-free survival 36–47% with TIP.
 • HDCT as second or third line: the Indiana series (two HDCT cycles) reported 63% continuously disease-
   free (70% second line, 45% third line); a matched-pair analysis suggested about 10% benefit; but the
   randomised trial (VIP/VeIP×3 + one high-dose cycle vs VIP/VeIP×4) showed no difference in response,
   event-free or overall survival.
 • Why the discrepancy: selection bias in single-arm series; expertise not reproducible elsewhere; a
   suboptimal trial schedule (three standard cycles, one HDCT cycle; only 73% received HDCT; toxic deaths
   7% vs 3%) versus the Indiana approach (early transfer to HDCT, two cycles, 94% completion, 2.7%
   treatment deaths).
 • Three negative randomised trials (two first line, one second line) → HDCT is not standard; offer only at
   specialised centres.

Management of postsalvage chemotherapy residual masses
 • Serological complete response with residual masses → postsalvage chemotherapy surgery (PSCS).
 • Versus PCS after first line: complete resection only 56–72% (vs ≥85%); more viable malignancy and less
   necrosis and teratoma (table above); 5-year survival 44–61%; viable malignancy in PSCS specimens
   carries a poor prognosis not improved by post-operative chemotherapy.
Desperation surgery
 • Rising markers despite first- and second-line chemotherapy but resectable disease at a single site
   (usually retroperitoneum) → salvage ("desperation") surgery.
 • Markers normalise in 47–60%; long-term survival 33–57% (± post-operative chemotherapy); up to half
   have only necrosis or teratoma.
Post-chemotherapy NSGCT relapse: late
 • Late relapse = more than 2 years after chemotherapy; about 3% of NSGCT. Biopsy to confirm, especially
   with normal AFP and hCG.
 • Histology: viable malignancy 54–88% (yolk sac tumour commonest), teratoma 12–28%, malignant
   transformation 10–20% (adenocarcinoma commonest).
 • Risk factors: previous relapse; teratoma in PCS specimens (possibly incomplete resection).
 • Usually a single site: retroperitoneum 50–72%, lungs 17%, mediastinum 9%, neck 7%, pelvis 4% → failure
   to control the retroperitoneum initially is a major risk factor.
 • AFP raised in about half, hCG in about a quarter; marker-only relapse → monitor until measurable
   disease appears.

   chemoinsensitive, viable disease is platinum-resistant). Contemporary data suggest cure rates may
   approach those of early relapse. Chemotherapy rarely achieves complete response, whereas initial surgery

   and multifocal disease predict worse survival.

 KEY POINTS — NSGCT
   • CS I: surveillance, primary RPLND and primary BEP chemotherapy are all accepted, with survival
     approaching 100%.
   • Full bilateral nerve-sparing template for suspicious nodes; modified template when nodes are clinically

Concept — Relapse prognosis tracks previous chemotherapy exposure: chemo-naive relapse is simply first-

Seminoma
Clinical Stage I Seminoma
 • About 80% of seminomas are CS I — the commonest presentation of testis cancer.
 • Accepted options: surveillance, primary radiotherapy and single-agent carboplatin, each with long-term
   cancer control approaching 100%, largely validated by randomised trials.
 • Current aim is reducing therapeutic burden: platinum and infradiaphragmatic radiotherapy raise late
   cardiovascular and SMN risk; radiotherapy fields and doses have been cut; carboplatin is less neuro-, oto-
   and nephrotoxic than cisplatin, but its late cardiovascular and SMN risks are largely unknown.
Primary radiotherapy
 • Historical mainstay: retroperitoneum plus ipsilateral pelvis ("dog-leg", DL, field); optimal dose undefined
   — most centres used 25–35 Gy in 15–20 fractions. Cancer-specific survival ≈100%; progression-free 95–
   97%.
 • In-field recurrence <1% → no routine abdominal–pelvic CT. Inguinal metastasis uncommon without
   previous inguinal or scrotal surgery. Relapse typically in the thorax and left supraclavicular fossa, virtually
   always cured by first-line chemotherapy; isolated inguinal relapse → radiotherapy or excision.
 • Follow-up after DL radiotherapy: clinical assessment, chest radiograph, markers.
 • Acute toxicity: mild self-limiting nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea; grade II–IV haematological toxicity 5–
   15%.
 • Late toxicity: moderate and severe late gastrointestinal toxicity (dyspepsia, peptic ulcer) in 5% and <2%;
   scatter to the contralateral testis despite shielding → persistent oligospermia in 8%; SMN risk about 18%
   at 25 years — leukaemia with radiotherapy and chemotherapy; upper gastrointestinal, bladder and
   possibly pancreatic cancer with radiotherapy.
 • Shrinking field: DL → para-aortic (PA) strip, because pelvic nodes are involved in only 1–3% without
   previous inguinal or scrotal surgery; PA may reduce SMN and speeds early recovery of spermatogenesis,
   but its small pelvic recurrence risk mandates routine pelvic CT, with its cost and radiation.
 • Shrinking dose: 20 Gy matches 30 Gy with less acute gastrointestinal toxicity, leukopenia and lethargy
   (equal by 12 weeks); durability still under follow-up. Trial results in the table below (EORTC, European
   Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer).

Surveillance
 • Rationale: late toxicity of DL radiotherapy, 80–85% cure by orchidectomy, and >90% chemotherapy cure
   of advanced seminoma.
 • Harder than NSGCT surveillance: markers rarely detect relapse, and 10–20% of relapses occur 4 years or
   more after diagnosis → long-term CT. About 60% of contemporary US CS I seminoma patients are under
   surveillance.
 • 5-year relapse-free survival 80–86%; cancer-specific survival ≈100%. 84–100% of relapses are
   retroperitoneal; 18–24% have bulky or distant disease at relapse. DL radiotherapy treats 73–88% of
   relapses, curing 70–90%; relapses outside the retroperitoneum are cured by first-line chemotherapy.
 • Median time to relapse: seminoma 14 months versus NSGCT 4–8 months; more seminoma relapses
   occur after 5 years.
 • Schedule: assessment (examination, chest radiograph, markers, CT) every 2–4 months in years 1–3, 6-
   monthly in years 4–7, then annually; CT every 4–6 months in years 1–3, 6-monthly in years 4–7, then
   annually. The MRC evidence for fewer scans comes from low-risk NSGCT; its applicability to seminoma is
   unclear. Long-term follow-up is mandatory.
 • Detection: CT finds >85% of relapses, hCG <5%, chest radiograph and examination virtually none.
 • Risk factors (pooled 1980s surveillance series): tumour >4 cm and rete testis invasion; LVI is not
   predictive in seminoma (unlike NSGCT). 5-year relapse 12%, 16%, 32% with 0, 1, 2 factors; about one-fifth
   had both. Treating all "high-risk" patients would still overtreat two-thirds; not prospectively validated.
Primary chemotherapy with single-agent carboplatin
 • One or two cycles; rationale: complete response in 65–90% of advanced seminoma and less toxicity than
   cisplatin. Published series: no seminoma deaths; 3–5-year relapse-free rates 91–100%.
 • MRC TE19/EORTC 30982 (1477 men): one cycle of carboplatin equivalent to PA radiotherapy (table
   below), with less lethargy and time off work; grade III–IV haematological toxicity 4%.
 • Relapse after carboplatin is salvageable (pooled analysis: 5-year overall survival 98%), not unduly
   chemoresistant; 15% of relapses occur after 3 years → at least 5 years of surveillance.
 • Underdosing concern: relapse is higher with one than two cycles and with inadequate dosing. A Swedish–
   Norwegian Testicular Cancer Group (SWENOTECA) study found one dose only about halved relapse in men
   with risk factors (tumour >4 cm: roughly 19% → 10%; rete testis invasion: roughly 14% → 9%); a German
   study likewise found relapse 5% after one cycle versus 1.5% after two. Guidelines are split on one versus
   two cycles, and some question carboplatin for stage I altogether.
 • Calvert formula: carboplatin dose (mg) = 7 × (glomerular filtration rate [GFR, mL/min] + 25); never use
   estimated GFR → base a single cycle on isotope GFR, or give two cycles.
 • Guidelines prefer surveillance (low overall relapse risk, no validated high-risk markers, late toxicity of
   alternatives): 80–85% avoid treatment and relapses are mostly salvaged with DL radiotherapy;
   surveillance must run beyond 5 years with frequent CT. Non-compliant or unwilling patients →
   radiotherapy or carboplatin ×2.

Relapsed Seminoma
Chemotherapy-naive seminoma relapse
 • Follows surveillance (CS I) or primary radiotherapy (CS I–IIB).

 • Relapse after single-agent carboplatin counts as chemo-naive → first-line cisplatin-based chemotherapy.
Post-chemotherapy seminoma relapse: early
 • 15–20% of advanced seminoma relapse after induction (10% after an initial complete response);

Post-chemotherapy seminoma relapse: late
 • Pure seminoma accounts for under 8% of late relapses in most series; one large series found 41%, mostly

 KEY POINTS — SEMINOMA
   • CS I: surveillance, primary radiotherapy (20–30 Gy to the PA region ± ipsilateral pelvis) or carboplatin,
     each with survival approaching 100%.
   • Given the low risk of occult metastasis, surveillance is the recommended approach for CS I.
   • CS IIA–B nonbulky (<3 cm): DL radiotherapy, first-line chemotherapy (BEP×3 or EP×4) or RPLND; bulky
     (>3 cm) and/or multifocal nodes → first-line chemotherapy.
   • CS IIC and III: cisplatin-based chemotherapy, regimen and cycles by IGCCCG.
   • Discrete residual masses >3 cm → FDG-PET; PET-positive → resection; PET-negative or <3 cm → safe
     observation.

 KEY POINTS — BRAIN METASTASES
   • Brain metastases are associated with choriocarcinoma; suspect them with a very high hCG.
   • Choriocarcinomas are highly vascular and haemorrhage during chemotherapy, causing intracranial
     haemorrhage.

Concept — Brain metastasis is a choriocarcinoma problem: hCG identifies the risk, haemorrhage is the
immediate danger, and relapse in the brain after complete response signals resistant disease with a worse
outlook than brain involvement at diagnosis.

Treatment Sequelae
 • Divided into early and late. Main problems after RPLND: midline scar, ejaculatory dysfunction, small-
   bowel obstruction and perioperative complications; hypogonadism is increased after orchidectomy.

Early Toxicity
 • Cisplatin-based chemotherapy: fatigue, myelosuppression, infection, peripheral neuropathy, hearing loss,
   reduced renal function, death.
 • Toxic death rate: 0–2.4% in good-risk and 3–4.4% in intermediate/poor-risk first-line chemotherapy.
 • Most men father children after treatment, but paternity is lower after radiotherapy and/or
   chemotherapy.
 • Early radiotherapy effects: fatigue, nausea and vomiting, leukopenia, dyspepsia.

 KEY POINTS — TREATMENT SEQUELAE
   • All GCT treatments (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy) carry early and late toxicity.
   • The most concerning late complications are cardiovascular disease and secondary malignancy.
   • As most patients are cured, even with advanced disease, minimising toxicity without compromising
     cure is a key objective.

Concept — Survivorship is now the deciding endpoint: because cure is expected, late cardiovascular disease
and second cancers — rising with the number of modalities used and with cumulative radiation, including
surveillance CT — decide between otherwise equally curative options.
