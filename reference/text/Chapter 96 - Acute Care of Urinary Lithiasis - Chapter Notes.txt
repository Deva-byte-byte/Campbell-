 Chapter 96 — Acute Care of Urinary Lithiasis
 INITIAL PRESENTATION
   • Classic presentation: unilateral severe flank pain progressing toward the lower abdomen and genitalia.
     It is not positional — the patient cannot find a comfortable position and writhes — and waxes and
     wanes, usually described as excruciating. Less commonly it is a dull pressure.
   • A dull-pressure presentation should raise suspicion of a more chronic obstruction.
   • Stone approaching the vesicoureteral junction → pain at the tip of the urethra, dysuria, persistent
     frequency and urgency. Sudden relief of pain suggests passage into the bladder.
   • Nausea and vomiting in about half of acute cases.
   • Haematuria (microscopic or macroscopic) in about 90%, gross in about one-third. Its absence does not
     exclude a stone — more than one in ten proven passages have none.
   • Fever and chills are uncommon; when present they suggest an infected obstructing stone and the
     need for more urgent intervention.

 Office Versus Hospital Setting
   • Most present to urgent care or the emergency department with classic renal colic; milder cases
     present to the office — maintain a high index of suspicion.
   • Office patients seen after the initial colic may report dull pressure or lower urinary tract symptoms
     (dysuria, urgency, frequency), attributable to a calculus that has migrated to the ureterovesical junction
     (UVJ).

 Laboratory Tests
   • Routine tests suffice for most: serum chemistry and urinalysis; a complete blood count (CBC) with
     differential because systemic infection may be present; a pregnancy test in every patient of
     reproductive age.
   • Results are usually normal and renal function preserved, unless there is significant dehydration,
     bilateral obstruction, or an absent or abnormal contralateral kidney.
   • Raised neutrophils and white blood cells (WBCs) may be a stress response, not necessarily urinary
     tract infection (UTI).
   • Microscopic haematuria is present in more than 90% of acute stones, yet haematuria is a poor
     predictor of a stone — sensitive, not specific.
   • WBCs, leucocyte esterase and nitrites raise suspicion of infection but are not diagnostic. More than 50
     WBCs per high-power field is associated with a positive culture in the majority; nitrites increase the
     likelihood of concurrent UTI.

 Imaging Studies
   • Non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT; CT) of abdomen and pelvis is the gold standard,
     especially in first-time stone formers. American College of Radiology Appropriateness Criteria:
     sensitivity 97%, specificity 95%, the examination of choice.
   • NCCT evaluates kidneys, ureters and bladder, measures true stone volume, size, location and skin-to-
     stone distance, and shows secondary signs — hydronephrosis, hydroureter, perinephric stranding,
     suspected calyceal rupture.

   • Radiation — ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable), the radiation-protection principle since 1977.
     United States Nuclear Regulatory Commission occupational whole-body limit 50 mSv/year. Standard
     NCCT delivers roughly 8–16 mSv, varying widely by protocol and facility; reduced-dose protocols are
     used in only a small minority of renal-colic studies.
   • Low-dose NCCT (≤3 mSv; effective dose about 0.5–2.8 mSv): sensitivity and specificity around 90% or
     better; weak below 2–3 mm and in obesity, but comparable to standard NCCT for stones ≥3 mm (meta-
     analysis).
   • STONE score: five criteria grouped into low, moderate and high risk, designed to identify uncomplicated
     ureteral stones, estimate the likelihood of an alternative diagnosis and limit NCCT use. External
     validation failed to show accuracy sufficient to dispense with NCCT; criterion changes have been
     proposed.

 PAIN MANAGEMENT IN RENAL COLIC
 Mechanism of renal colic:

     Stone enters ureter → obstruction → stretch of renal pelvis and capsule → local prostaglandin release
     → diuresis and vasodilation → rising intrarenal pressure; prostaglandins also act directly on the ureter
     → smooth-muscle spasm.
   • Shared innervation → pain referred to intestine, groin and genitalia; a distal ureteral stone refers to
     the ipsilateral testis, labium or groin.
   • Prostaglandins (arachidonic acid metabolites) drive the pain and inflammation. Non-steroidal anti-
     inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) inhibit cyclo-oxygenase (COX) and so stop their production, without the
     respiratory and consciousness depression of opioids; intravenous NSAIDs act immediately, reducing
     smooth-muscle stimulation and ureteral spasm.
   • Bleeding and renal-function concerns are not clinically significant at common doses.
   • NSAIDs versus opioids: comparable efficacy (no difference at 30 and 60 minutes in a large systematic
     review), with less rescue analgesia and less vomiting with NSAIDs; one systematic review found greater
     pain reduction with NSAIDs.

 OPIOIDS
   • History: morphine isolated from opium 1806; chemical formula deduced 1847; hypodermic needle
     1853 → widespread clinical use.
   • Three classical receptors: δ (DOR), κ (KOR) and μ (MOR); MOR is the most clinically relevant.
     Morphine is the reference MOR agonist and also acts on the other two; opioids remain the benchmark
     against which all analgesics are measured.
   • Adverse effects: reduced consciousness, respiratory depression, constipation, nausea and vomiting,
     urinary retention, pruritus, muscular rigidity, miosis, dysphoria; euphoria underlies abuse potential.
   • Opioid crisis: most United States overdose deaths involve an opioid, quadrupled since 1999; United
     States crisis declared 2017, with public-health emergencies declared elsewhere.
   • Dependency in opioid-naive patients rises with each additional day prescribed from day 3, most
     sharply after day 5. Highest long-term abuse probability with long-acting opioids, then tramadol.
     Younger patients (<45 years) given two prescriptions rather than one roughly double their risk.
   • Stewardship: multimodal non-opioid protocols cut opioid prescribing at the cost of more admissions
     and repeat emergency visits; surgeon-level protocols did not change other prescribers' habits →
     system-wide stewardship needed.
   • Current recommendation: opioids only for intractable pain or pain unresponsive to NSAIDs and
     paracetamol, with the NSAID continued.

 tied to prescription length and formulation — argues for the fewest days possible.

 MEDICAL EXPULSIVE THERAPY
   • Medical expulsive therapy (MET): medication to facilitate passage of a ureteral stone into the bladder;
     α-blockers are the principal agents.
     α-Blocker mechanism: less ureteral spasm and lower force and frequency of peristalsis → larger urine
     bolus per peristaltic wave → higher pressure proximal to the stone.
   • NSAIDs alone reduce inflammation and oedema but do not improve passage.
   • American Urological Association (AUA) and EAU: MET for distal ureteral stones <10 mm. Tamsulosin is
     the most studied; other α-blockers offer the same benefit.

 trials with the positive meta-analyses.

 SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

 Pregnancy
   • Parous women under 50 have more than double the stone incidence of nulliparous women, rising
     with parity.
   • Physiological changes of pregnancy counterbalance one another, so prevalence of urolithiasis does not
     increase in pregnancy; symptomatic stones affect under 1% of pregnancies but carry adverse birth
     outcomes — low birth weight, pre-eclampsia, preterm birth — especially when surgery is needed.
   • Intervention needed in roughly one-fifth to one-third (about 35% in the chapter's key figure): stents
     most often, then ureteroscopy, then nephrostomy. Preterm delivery rises stepwise: conservative
     management < procedural intervention < nephrostomy (highest).
 Imaging
     US first (all guidelines) → MRI second (AUA, EAU) → low-dose NCCT when necessary (endorsed by the
     American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists [ACOG]; shared decision, after other modalities).
   • Physiological hydronephrosis in ~90% (second and third trimesters) makes hydronephrosis unreliable;
     it does not usually extend beyond the iliac vessels, so distal ureteral dilatation points to a stone. An
     absent unilateral ureteric jet helps but occurs without obstruction.
   • Intrarenal resistive index (RI): mean RI ≥0.7 (vs ≤0.6) is specific but insensitive; a difference of ≥0.06
     from the contralateral kidney is highly sensitive and specific for obstruction.
   • MRI: useful positive predictive value despite poor stone visualisation. Gadolinium is teratogenic in
     animals but not in human studies, and usually unnecessary; ACOG: use only where benefit clearly
     outweighs risk.
   • CT: rarely first line; teratogenic risk greatest before or during the first trimester; fetal abnormality risk
     not significant below 50 mSv; low-dose protocols ≤3 mSv, some <1 mSv — well within the safe range.
 Management
   • First line: conservative management. α-Blockers are insufficiently studied in pregnancy — used by
     fewer than half of urologists, against near-universal use otherwise.
   • Up to 30% of symptomatic patients need intervention (ureteroscopy, stent or nephrostomy) — shared
     decision and individualised plan.
   • Stent vs nephrostomy by hydronephrosis, preference and gestational stage. Both encrust → change
     every 4–6 weeks. Each stent exchange needs anaesthesia, so the earlier the pregnancy, the more
     nephrostomy is preferred. Stents also cause stent colic, preterm labour soon after placement, and
     early induction in over 40% for poor stent-pain control.
   • Ureteroscopy: former advice to wait for the second trimester is superseded — ACOG Committee
     Opinion No. 775 (2019): no teratogenicity of anaesthetic agents at standard concentrations, no fetal
     brain effect with exposure under 3 hours; never deny or delay medically necessary surgery in any
     trimester. Meta-analysis: low complication rate, mostly Clavien I–II, no rise in UTI, low preterm labour
     — lower than stenting in some series → safe, and avoids repeated tube exchanges.
   • Fluoroscopy: minimal exposure, pelvic shield, and C-arm on manual exposure (automatic mode
     increases dose) — agree with staff beforehand. Stones in pregnancy are covered further in Chapter 21.

 Frailty and the Elderly
   • Same initial management, but atypical presentation is more likely.

   • Mean age at first stone about 40; a first obstructing stone after 60 is less likely → broaden the
     differential to acute myocardial infarction and aortic aneurysm or dissection.
   • Older patients more often have atypical or no pain, fever, diarrhoea, pyuria, UTI and bacteraemia; more
     atypical composition including uric acid; and a lower chance of spontaneous passage.
   • Imaging: always CT — diagnosis and exclusion of alternatives.
   • Comorbidity often precludes NSAIDs; antiplatelets and anticoagulants complicate procedures; reduced
     reserve demands high suspicion for infection and early drainage.

 Transplant Kidney
   • Rare — low single-digit incidence of stones and obstruction in allografts.
   • Denervated allograft → obstruction is painless; missed cases present with hydronephrosis, fever and
     acute renal failure → regular follow-up and high suspicion.
   • Endoscopic management preferred; if retrograde stenting is difficult, percutaneous access under US or
     CT guidance.

 Concept — Each special population removes one assumption of standard care: pregnancy removes free

 denervation removes the pain signal — so in each, vigilance must replace the missing cue.

 CONCLUSION
   • Initial management is individualised; accurate diagnosis gives prognosis and manages expectations.
   • Ideal: conservative management — pain control and spontaneous passage; when intervention is
     needed, decide swiftly to minimise morbidity.
