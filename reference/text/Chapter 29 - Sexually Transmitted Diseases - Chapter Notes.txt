Chapter 29 — Sexually Transmitted Diseases
EPIDEMIOLOGY OF SEXUALLY TRANSMITTED DISEASES
 • “Hidden epidemics”: a 1997 Institute of Medicine (IOM) report described sexually transmitted diseases
   (STDs) as hidden epidemics of major health and economic consequence, under-recognised by the public
   and by professionals; this remains true two decades later.
 • Resurgence: syphilis and gonorrhoea, previously declining, are rising again. Against 2016 Centers for
   Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) data, gonorrhoea rose 45% and syphilis 52%, but congenital syphilis
   rose 235% — the steepest rise.
 • Burden: about 67.6 million prevalent infections in the United States (US) in 2018 — roughly one adult in
   five has an STD; direct treatment costs nearly $16 billion a year.

Clinical Prevention Guidance
 • Five major strategies: (1) accurate risk assessment, education and counselling; (2) pre-exposure
   vaccination against vaccine-preventable STDs; (3) identification of symptomatic and asymptomatic
   infected persons; (4) effective diagnosis, treatment, counselling and follow-up; (5) evaluation, treatment
   and counselling of sex partners.
 • The first step is identifying the at-risk population. The “Five P’s” give a gender-inclusive and sexual
   orientation–inclusive history: partners, practices, protection against STDs, past STD history, pregnancy
   intention.
 • Risk factors: more lifetime partners, condomless sex, substance misuse, risky partners.
 • Men who have sex with men (MSM): human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and bacterial STDs fell in this
   group in the mid-1990s, but syphilis, gonorrhoea and chlamydia are rising again; MSM account for about
   half of male primary and secondary syphilis.

Screening Recommendations
 • Risk-tailored: a 2014 multi-organisation report advised assessing behavioural and biological risk and
   tailoring counselling and screening to it. The nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) is the standard
   screening assay.

Reporting Sexually Transmitted Diseases
 • Six conditions are reportable in every US state: syphilis (including congenital), gonorrhoea, chlamydia,
   chancroid, HIV infection and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS). Other STDs vary by state; local
   health departments list them.

Prevention Methods
 • Methods: pre-exposure vaccination, abstinence and fewer partners, male and female condoms, cervical
   diaphragms, topical microbicides and spermicides.
 • Male condoms used consistently are highly effective against HIV: in heterosexual serodiscordant couples
   (one partner infected), consistent use made the negative partner about 80% less likely to acquire HIV.
 • Other STDs: condoms reduce risk and, by limiting lower genital tract inflammation, may reduce pelvic
   inflammatory disease (PID). Against human papillomavirus (HPV), herpes, syphilis and chancroid they
   protect only where the site of exposure is covered.
 • Quality: regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as medical devices with random sampling;
   breakage about 2 per 100, slightly higher in anal intercourse.

URETHRITIS
 • Presentation: urethral discharge, dysuria, urethral stinging or itching, penile tip irritation — but often
   asymptomatic.

Etiology
 • Complications of the estimated 2.8 million annual cases: acute epididymitis, orchitis, prostatitis.
 • Gram-negative intracellular diplococci → presumed Neisseria gonorrhoeae.
 • Nongonococcal urethritis (NGU): Chlamydia trachomatis is the commonest identifiable cause;
   Mycoplasma genitalium is the second commonest and the leading identified cause of persistent or
   recurrent urethritis.
 • Less common: Trichomonas vaginalis, herpes simplex virus (HSV), Gardnerella (formerly Haemophilus)
   vaginalis, Neisseria meningitidis, adenovirus.

Diagnosis
 • Threshold lowered: the old Gram stain criterion of ≥5 white blood cells (WBC) per high-power field (hpf)
   missed a significant proportion of chlamydia, gonorrhoea and M. genitalium → CDC now uses ≥2 WBC per
   oil immersion field.
 • Gram stain sensitivity depends on operator and collection device; yield rises swab → loop → spatula.
 • Urethritis is documented by any one of: mucoid, mucopurulent or purulent discharge; Gram stain ≥2
   WBC per oil immersion field; positive leucocyte esterase on first-void urine; ≥10 WBC/hpf in first-void
   urine.
 • No point-of-care (POC) Gram stain → NAAT for all males; urine is the preferred male specimen. NAAT is
   also indicated when ≥2 WBC per oil immersion field are seen without intracellular gram-negative or
   purple diplococci.

Gonococcal Urethritis
 • Rising: reported cases in 2020 were more than double those of 2009. Gram-negative diplococcus;
   incubation 3–14 days.
 • Sex asymmetry: males are usually symptomatic (urethritis, epididymitis, prostatitis, proctitis; rarely
   disseminated gonococcal infection); females are usually asymptomatic and present with sequelae — PID,
   tubal scarring, infertility, ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain.
Diagnosis of Gonococcal Urethritis
 • Diagnostic in symptomatic males: Gram stain showing polymorphonuclear leucocytes with intracellular
   gram-negative diplococci. A negative Gram stain does not exclude gonorrhoea (low sensitivity).

 • NAAT is preferred for gonorrhoea and chlamydia (higher sensitivity); POC NAAT (GeneXpert) exists. NAAT
   runs on first-catch urine or urethral swab — first-void urine is preferred; culture requires a urethral swab.
   FDA-approved NAATs cover urine, endocervical, vaginal, male urethral, rectal and pharyngeal specimens;
   follow each manufacturer’s insert.
 • Culture is still indicated for suspected treatment failure and resistance monitoring. In children, use
   culture or only Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-validated, FDA-cleared NAATs, given
   the implications of the diagnosis.
 • Sampling order: when several specimens come from one site, take the gonococcal culture first to
   maximise yield.
Treatment of Gonococcal Urethritis
 • Why it is complex: accumulating antimicrobial resistance and less culture (so fewer susceptibility data).
   The CDC Gonococcal Isolate Surveillance Project (GISP), established 1986, monitors susceptibility.

Nongonococcal Urethritis
 • Proportions: gonorrhoea causes 5–20% of urethritis; the remainder is NGU. C. trachomatis leads (more so
   in younger males), M. genitalium follows. Many males never receive a definitive diagnosis — no
   pathogen is found in up to half even with extensive testing.

 • Recurrent or persistent symptoms after empiric treatment → test for M. genitalium.
 • Minor pathogens: Ureaplasma urealyticum (weak association, young males with few partners; consider
   when nothing else is found); T. vaginalis (consider in high-prevalence areas or after empiric failure); also
   N. meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae, Corynebacterium, HSV-1, HSV-2, adenovirus.
 • Treatment: pathogen-directed when possible, otherwise presumptive. Doxycycline 100 mg orally twice
   daily for 7 days; alternatives azithromycin 1 g single dose, or 500 mg then 250 mg daily for 4 days.
   Doxycycline covers chlamydia well and has some M. genitalium activity. Consider concurrent gonorrhoea
   treatment in at-risk patients or high-prevalence areas.
Chlamydia
 • Most frequently reported infectious disease in the US, peaking under 24 years; incubation 3–14 days.
 • Male sequelae: epididymitis, reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter syndrome). Treating males also protects
   female partners from ascending infection — tubal scarring, PID, pelvic pain, infertility.
 • First-catch urine NAAT is the most sensitive male test and is recommended.
 • Treatment: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days; azithromycin regimens are alternatives. This
   changed from 2015, when doxycycline and azithromycin were equal first-line options; the 2015 European
   guideline is under review. Consider concurrent gonorrhoea treatment.
 • Aftercare: abstain for 7 days after single-dose therapy, or until the 7-day course is complete and
   symptoms have resolved; test for gonorrhoea, HIV and syphilis; no test of cure (retesting within 3 weeks
   may detect residual nucleic acid) but retest at 3 months; partners from the preceding 60 days.
Mycoplasma genitalium
 • Share of disease: first identified in the early 1980s; causes a substantial minority of NGU, a larger share of
   non-chlamydial NGU, and the largest identified share of persistent or recurrent urethritis.
 • Transmission: mainly genital–genital mucosal contact; penile–anal transmission documented; oral–genital
   unlikely (low oropharyngeal concentration).
 • Diagnosis: no cell wall → cannot be Gram stained; culture takes up to 6 months → NAAT is the only
   clinically useful test (FDA-approved on urine, urethral, meatal, endocervical and vaginal swabs). Test
   males with recurrent NGU.
 • Resistance: macrolide resistance is widespread and rising; resistance-guided therapy achieves cure in
   over 90%. European 2021 guidance: every positive NAAT is followed by a macrolide-resistance assay.

Trichomonas vaginalis
 • Flagellated parasite of the male urethra and the female urethra, vagina and vulva; mostly minimal or no
   symptoms; about 1.5-fold increased HIV acquisition.
 • Testing in males is limited: no FDA-cleared rapid test; NAATs are replacing wet mount and culture, but
   many are not cleared for males. Not part of the initial NGU work-up (low prevalence) — test male
   partners of infected females and males in high-prevalence areas.
 • Treatment (male): metronidazole 2 g orally once, or tinidazole 2 g orally once.
Persistent or Recurrent Nongonococcal Urethritis
 • Three causes: resistance, non-adherence, re-exposure to an untreated partner. Think of resistant M.
   genitalium and T. vaginalis.
 • Urological evaluation rarely finds a cause; check for pain elsewhere in the pelvis suggesting chronic pelvic
   pain syndrome rather than localised urethritis.

EPIDIDYMITIS
 • Definitions: acute = pain, swelling and inflammation of the epididymis for <6 weeks, sometimes with
   orchitis (epididymo-orchitis); chronic = scrotal, testicular or epididymal discomfort for ≥6 weeks,
   infectious (commonest Mycobacterium tuberculosis) or non-infectious. Full discussion in Chapter 27.
 • Keep testicular torsion in mind — the presentations overlap. Concurrent urethritis is common but often
   asymptomatic.

Diagnosis of Epididymitis
 • Clinical: unilateral testicular pain and tenderness, reactive hydrocele, palpable epididymal swelling.
 • Ultrasonography is not used to diagnose epididymitis — a negative scan would not change management;
   useful only when torsion is suspected.
 • Requires one of: Gram or methylene blue stain of urethral secretion with ≥2 WBC per oil immersion field;
   positive leucocyte esterase on first-void urine; ≥10 WBC/hpf on spun first-void urine.
 • All suspected acute cases → NAAT for chlamydia and gonorrhoea. Urine culture in sexually transmitted
   enteric infection and in older males with bacteriuria.

Treatment of Epididymitis
 • Start empiric therapy before results.

GENITAL, ANAL, AND PERIANAL ULCERS
 • Commonest causes in young sexually active US patients: genital herpes (most common), then syphilis;
   less often chancroid and donovanosis. Non-infectious: carcinoma, yeast, trauma, psoriasis, fixed drug
   eruption.
 • History and examination are inaccurate → every ulcer gets: syphilis serology plus darkfield examination
   or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) if available; HSV culture or NAAT plus type-specific HSV serology;
   Haemophilus ducreyi NAAT or culture in endemic areas; HIV test if status unknown.
 • About a quarter remain undiagnosed; biopsy ulcers that fail initial therapy or look unusual.

Genital Herpes Simplex Virus Infections
 • Virology: HSV-1 and HSV-2 are double-stranded DNA viruses. Most genital herpes is HSV-2, but HSV-1
   now leads initial genital herpes in high-income countries, especially in females and in MSM and
   transgender females under 25 who have sex with males.
 • Unrecognised infection is the rule: about one in six US adults is HSV-2 seropositive, but only a minority
   recognise genital herpes. Shedding is intermittent, occurring on about 10% of days even in asymptomatic
   HSV-2 infection. Transmission is greatest during lesional recurrence or prodrome.
 • HSV-2 increases HIV acquisition two- to threefold → test all patients with genital herpes for HIV.
Pathophysiology of Genital Herpes
 • Sequence: epithelial cells → nerve endings → retrograde axonal transport to sacral ganglia → latency →
   reactivation: virus travels down the axon to skin and mucosa → shedding, with ulcers or, more often,
   asymptomatically.
 • Recurrence is the hallmark: median 4 recurrences in year one after HSV-2 vs 1.3 after HSV-1; HSV-2
   recurrences then fall by a median of 2 a year, with wide variation.
 • Reactivation triggers: local trauma including surgery, ultraviolet light, immunosuppression, fever.
Presentation of Genital Herpes
 • Classic course: painful papule → vesicle → ulcer with local adenitis, 4–7 days after exposure. Many
   lesions are atypical — fissures, furuncle-like lesions, vulvar erythema.
 • Primary HSV-1 and HSV-2 are clinically indistinguishable → serological typing needed.
 • Complications: aseptic meningitis; autonomic dysfunction causing urinary retention.
 • Recurrences are usually preceded by a prodrome, then resemble the primary lesions.
Diagnosis of Genital Herpes
 • Type-specific confirmation is required because prognosis and counselling differ between HSV-1 and
   HSV-2.
 • Cell culture and NAAT are preferred, swabbing the ulcer base; unroof vesicles with a needle or scalpel.
   FDA-cleared HSV NAATs are highly sensitive and specific.
 • A negative culture or NAAT does not exclude HSV — shedding is intermittent, especially without active
   lesions.
 • HSV PCR is the test of choice for central nervous system (CNS) and systemic infection; blood PCR is not
   used for genital herpes unless dissemination is suspected.
 • Tzanck preparation is insensitive — do not rely on it.

Treatment of Genital Herpes
 • Every symptomatic first episode receives antiviral therapy, because severe or prolonged illness can
   follow.
 • Recurrence: nearly all symptomatic first-episode HSV-2 recurs; HSV-1 recurs less often.
 • Two strategies for recurrent disease: suppressive — reduces recurrence frequency and reduces HSV-2
   transmission to partners; episodic — shortens lesions but must start within 1 day of lesion onset or
   during the prodrome.
 • Counselling of patients and partners is central (full topic list in CDC 2021).

Syphilis
 • Organism: Treponema pallidum, a coiled spirochaete; systemic disease, acquired or congenital.
 • Epidemiology: MSM are disproportionately affected, but female primary and secondary syphilis rose
   steeply (147%) from 2016 to 2020 — the driver of congenital syphilis.
 • Transmission: sexual (vaginal, anogenital, orogenital); rarely blood products, organ donation, occupational
   exposure. Over half of sexual contacts of early syphilis acquire infection.
 • HIV link: all primary or secondary syphilis → HIV test; offer pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) if negative.
   Syphilis is far more common in people with HIV and increases HIV shedding in plasma and genital tract.
   HIV can produce unusual serological responses — when clinical suspicion persists despite non-reactive or
   unclear serology, use biopsy or darkfield examination.

 • Darkfield examination: T. pallidum cannot be cultured in vitro; darkfield of lesion exudate or tissue is the

Granuloma Inguinale (Donovanosis)
 • Organism: intracellular gram-negative Klebsiella granulomatis (formerly Calymmatobacterium). Rare in
   the US; reported from Papua New Guinea, South Africa, India and Brazil.
 • Lesion: incubation about 50 days; painless, slowly progressive ulcers on genitals or perineum without
   regional lymphadenopathy — despite the name, only 10% involve the inguinal region. Highly vascular
   (beefy red), bleed easily. Extragenital spread: mouth commonest; also pelvis, intra-abdominal organs,
   bone.
 • Diagnosis: dark-staining Donovan bodies on tissue crush preparation or biopsy; no FDA-cleared
   molecular test. Test for HIV.
 • Biopsy if antibiotics fail — penile squamous cell carcinoma mimics granuloma inguinale.

 • Serovars: C. trachomatis is an obligate intracellular pathogen with 15 serovars in three groups —

ANOGENITAL WARTS
Human Papillomavirus
 • Virology: small non-enveloped double-stranded DNA viruses (Papillomaviridae); over 200 types, half
   genital. HPV is the most prevalent STD in the world.
 • High-risk types 16, 18, 31, 33, 35 cause most cervical, penile, vulvar, vaginal, anal and oropharyngeal
   cancers and precancers; 16 and 18 cause >70% of cervical cancer.
 • HPV 6 and 11 are non-oncogenic and cause about 90% of anogenital warts.
 • Burden: infection is often unrecognised; most sexually active people are infected at least once. HPV
   causes roughly 57,000 cancers a year in females and 60,000 in males worldwide; in the US in 2009, about
   35,000 new HPV-associated cancers and 355,000 new cases of warts.

Presentation and Diagnostic Considerations
 • Condylomata acuminata: usually asymptomatic, painful or pruritic depending on size and site; flat,
   papular or pedunculated. Common at the vaginal introitus, under the foreskin (uncircumcised) and on the
   shaft (circumcised); also cervix, vagina, urethra (meatal warts), perineum, perianal skin, anus, scrotum.
 • Vaccination has reduced wart incidence in adolescents, young females and heterosexual males.

Diagnosis of Human Immunodeficiency Virus
 • Screen everyone aged 15–65 at least once (opt-out: informed, may decline); screen everyone seeking
   STD care; high-risk persons at least annually.
 • Tests: serological (antibodies to HIV-1 and HIV-2) and virological (antigen or RNA). Screening
   immunoassays are highly sensitive and specific and detect all HIV-1 subtypes; most detect HIV-2. Rapid
   tests turn reactive later than laboratory assays → may miss recent infection.
 1. Laboratory HIV-1/HIV-2 antigen–antibody combination immunoassay.
 2. Repeatedly reactive → HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay; positive confirms the diagnosis and
    identifies HIV-2 (different monitoring and treatment).
 3. Discordant (reactive immunoassay, negative differentiation assay — possible in very early infection) →
    HIV RNA to identify acute infection.
 • Partners: encourage notification (health department services). Partners exposed to genital secretions or
   blood within 72 hours → postexposure prophylaxis (PEP) with combination antiretrovirals. HIV-negative
   persons at increased risk → consider PrEP, and PEP if the last exposure was ≤72 hours ago.

Decreasing the Transmission Risk
Male Circumcision
 • Three sub-Saharan African randomised controlled trials: circumcision cut heterosexual HIV acquisition
   by 50–60%, with similar reductions in high-risk HPV and genital herpes.
 • Mechanisms: abundant HIV target cells beneath the foreskin dermis; poorly keratinised inner foreskin
   prone to lacerations (entry sites); anaerobic preputial infection recruits Langerhans cells, which HIV
   targets.
 • Limits: no reduction in transmission from an HIV-positive male; not shown to protect MSM, although
   limited data suggest benefit for exclusively insertive partners.
 • Policy: the World Health Organization (WHO), the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS)
   and the US President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) recommend scaling up circumcision to
   prevent heterosexually acquired HIV.

Postexposure Prophylaxis
 • Landmark evidence: a 1997 case-control study of health care workers (HCWs) with percutaneous
   exposure found zidovudine prophylaxis reduced the odds of transmission by 81%. No randomised trial of

Urologic Manifestations of HIV/AIDS
HIV and Other Sexually Transmitted Diseases
 • Test for HIV in anyone with, or at risk of, an STD; HIV epidemiology mirrors that of other STDs.
 • Ulcerative STDs (herpes, syphilis, chancroid) increase both transmission and acquisition: bleeding ulcers
   and HIV in exudate increase infectivity; in HIV-negative persons ulcers breach mucosa and recruit HIV-
   susceptible immune cells.
 • Coinfection: HSV shedding increases in HIV; HSV-2 affects most HIV-positive patients (50–90%). Syphilis
   raises viral load and lowers CD4 count → rapid progression.

 • Frequency: uncommon generally (1–2%), commoner with HIV and more so with AIDS (pre-HAART figures).

Nephrolithiasis
 • Indinavir (a PI) is the classic culprit — stones in up to 22% of treated patients; autopsy stones in up to
   40% of HIV patients. Risk factors: hepatitis coinfection, PI exposure.
 • Mechanism: 20% of indinavir is excreted unchanged within 24 hours; it is soluble at pH <5 and
   precipitates in alkaline urine → radiolucent stones. Indinavir is present in only 29% of calculi; the rest are
   calcium oxalate, ammonium acid urate and uric acid. Newer PIs (lopinavir, atazanavir, amprenavir,
   nelfinavir) cause stones far less often.
 • Other contributors: malnutrition, diarrhoea, dehydration, urinary acidification, hypocitraturia,
   hyperuricosuria from cell lysis after chemotherapy for AIDS-associated lymphoma.
 • Work-up: complete metabolic evaluation — stone analysis, 24-hour urine, serum creatinine, uric acid,
   calcium.
 • First line for PI stones, if conservative management is possible: stop the drug and hydrate (successful in
   up to 70%).
Renal Dysfunction
 • Still a leading cause of death despite HAART; arises from infection and from treatment.
 • Causes: volume depletion (diarrhoea, vomiting), sepsis, metabolic dysfunction, nephrotoxins, HIV-
   associated nephropathy (HIVAN), ureteric obstruction (stones, clot, retroperitoneal lymphoma, fungal
   collections, fibrosis).
 • Nephrotoxic drugs: antibiotics; antifungals (amphotericin B, pentamidine); antivirals; antiretrovirals
   (indinavir, atazanavir, abacavir, tenofovir).
 • HAART era: longer survival → more chronic kidney disease, hypertension and diabetes (fourfold higher
   diabetes incidence on HAART); untreated HIV raises acute kidney injury risk.
 • HIVAN: raised creatinine with nephrotic-range proteinuria, often without oedema or hypertension;
   typically CD4 <200 and viral load >1000 copies/mL; about 12 times commoner in Black than White
   patients — third leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in Black patients aged 20–64. Biopsy:
   collapsing focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, tubular and visceral epithelial proliferation, tubular
   microcysts, oedema, interstitial fibrosis, leucocytic infiltrate. Dialysis within about 10 months; treat with
   HAART and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors; 1-year mortality about 50%.
Voiding Dysfunction
 • LUTS worsen as disease progresses: HIV is an independent risk factor for bothersome LUTS in MSM, and
   prior AIDS for severe LUTS.
 • Acute HIV: urinary retention and sacral sensory loss may herald seroconversion.
 • Later causes: recurrent or chronic UTI; CNS disease (HIV encephalitis, cerebral toxoplasmosis,
   demyelination, CMV polyradiculopathy, neoplasm, HIV dementia); peripheral neuropathy; HAART and
   other drugs.
 • CMV: reactivates in about 40%, risking bladder damage and life-threatening infection. Combined bladder
   and bowel dysfunction with back or sciatic pain → suspect CMV polyradiculopathy → lumbar puncture;
   prompt treatment may help.

Erectile Dysfunction
 • Prevalence: erectile dysfunction (ED) is commoner in HIV-infected males in every age decade; HIV
   infection was the strongest independent predictor on multivariate analysis, confirmed by meta-analysis.
   About half of HIV-positive men under 50 are affected.
 • Causes: neurological, vascular and hormonal comorbidity, psychological stress, depression,
   hypogonadism, age, BMI. Antiretrovirals, especially PIs (indinavir, ritonavir), worsen ED: weight gain,
   dyslipidaemia, hyperglycaemia and lower testosterone → endothelial dysfunction.
 • Other dysfunction: ejaculatory disorders commoner; libido lower with HIV and lowest on HAART.
 • Interaction: PIs inhibit CYP3A → sildenafil levels rise 3–10-fold → start at a low dose.
Malignancy
 • Excess cancer versus age-matched controls: reduced immune surveillance, viral protein effects, cytokine
   dysregulation, and behaviour (smoking two to three times commoner).

ECTOPARASITIC INFECTIONS
Pediculosis Pubis (Pthirus pubis): Pubic or Crab Louse
 • Transmission: sexual or close body contact, less often fomites; infests terminal hairs of the pubic and
   perianal areas. Adult lifespan <1 month; eggs hatch in 1 week; incubation usually up to a month.
 • Condoms do not prevent transmission. Pubic lice in children do not prove sexual contact. Evaluate for
   other STDs including HIV.
 • Clinical: genital itch; nits or lice attached to hairs. Symptoms 2–6 weeks after first exposure, sooner on re-
   exposure. Diagnosis needs live lice or viable eggs — empty shells persist for months.
 • Aftercare: no fumigation needed; treat partners from the previous month; review at 1 week and retreat if
   lice or eggs remain; switch to an alternative regimen if there is no response.

Scabies
 • Transmission: Sarcoptes scabiei by skin-to-skin contact, including intercourse, less often fomites. The
   female burrows into the epidermis and lays eggs that mature in 2 weeks; mites die off the host within
   24–36 hours.
 • Symptoms are immunological: type IV hypersensitivity to mites and products → symptoms 3–6 weeks
   after first infestation but 1–3 days after reinfestation.
 • Presentation: rash and itch, worse at night; burrows as tiny raised serpiginous lines. Scratching → S.
   aureus or β-haemolytic streptococcal infection → post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

Bacterial Vaginosis
 • Polymicrobial replacement: loss of hydrogen peroxide–producing Lactobacillus with overgrowth of
   anaerobes (Prevotella, Mobiluncus), Gardnerella vaginalis, Ureaplasma, Mycoplasma and fastidious
   anaerobes.
 • Commonest diagnosis in symptomatic females, yet most cases are asymptomatic. Associations: multiple
   partners, douching, no condoms, lactobacillus deficiency.
 • Consequences: increased risk of STDs, post-gynaecological-surgery complications, pregnancy
   complications and recurrence; increases HIV transmission to the male partner.
 • Gold standard: Gram stain quantifying lactobacilli against gram-negative and gram-variable rods and cocci
   and curved gram-negative rods.
 • Clinical diagnosis (Amsel) — three of four: (1) thin, white, homogeneous discharge coating the walls; (2)
   clue cells; (3) pH >4.5; (4) fishy odour before or after 10% KOH. BV NAATs only in symptomatic persons.
 • Treat symptomatic patients: metronidazole 500 mg orally twice daily 7 days; or metronidazole 0.75% gel
   5 g intravaginally daily 5 days; or clindamycin 2% cream 5 g intravaginally at bedtime 7 days (weakens
   latex for 5 days). Test for other STDs including HIV. Routine partner treatment is not recommended.

Trichomoniasis
 • Most prevalent non-viral sexually transmitted infection in the US (about 3.7 million); protozoan T.
   vaginalis. Mostly asymptomatic; otherwise diffuse, malodorous, yellow-green discharge with or without
   vulvar irritation. 1.5-fold HIV acquisition, preterm birth, other pregnancy complications.
 • Testing: NAAT is highly sensitive (vaginal, endocervical or urine specimens); culture is less sensitive than
   NAAT; wet mount is cheap but sensitivity is low and falls within an hour — read immediately.
 • Treatment — females: metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for 7 days (halves positive retests at 1 month
   compared with the old 2 g single dose) or tinidazole 2 g once. Males: metronidazole 2 g once.
 • Aftercare: no alcohol for 24 h after metronidazole, 72 h after tinidazole; retest all sexually active
   females within 3 months (high reinfection); refer all partners for presumptive treatment.
Concept — the two sexually transmitted causes of vaginitis share a raised pH and differ in inflammation, and
the partner rule follows transmission: trichomoniasis — treat all partners; BV — routine partner treatment
is not recommended.

 KEY POINTS
   • Patients with urethritis need treatment for both gonorrhoea and chlamydia. Because of higher
     sensitivity, NAATs are the preferred method for detecting N. gonorrhoeae and C. trachomatis; urethral
     swab is no longer indicated.
   • Males with recurrent urethritis should be tested for M. genitalium.
   • Most genital ulcers in the US are herpes (most common) or syphilis. Chancroid occurs in parts of the
     US; granuloma inguinale usually does not. LGV is increasing in MSM.
   • HPV vaccines are recommended for males and females through 26 years, preferably before sexual
     debut.
   • HIV testing is recommended for anyone with, or at risk of, an STD.
   • HIV is becoming a chronic disease; many associated problems now stem from ageing and chronic
     illness rather than immunosuppression.
