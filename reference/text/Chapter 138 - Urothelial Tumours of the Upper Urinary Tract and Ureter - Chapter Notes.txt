 Chapter 138 — Urothelial Tumours of the Upper Urinary
 Tract and Ureter — Chapter Notes
 INCIDENCE
   • Urothelial carcinomas are the sixth most common tumours in developed countries; upper tract
     urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) accounts for only 5–10% of urothelial tumours.
   • Annual incidence in Western countries ≈ 2 per 100,000; the Surveillance, Epidemiology and End
     Results (SEER) database gives an annual incidence of roughly 1.9–2.1 per 100,000 person-years.
   • Overall incidence is rising, driven by ureteric tumours (rising) while pelvicalyceal tumours are static or
     marginally falling.
   • Reasons for the rise: better diagnostic methods and longer survival after bladder cancer surgery (more
     patients live long enough to develop an upper tract tumour).
   • Stage and grade migration: proportion of early-stage tumours has risen markedly over three decades,
     and the proportion of high-grade UTUC has also risen.
   • Peak incidence: age 70–90, males, and the Balkan countries, where UTUC is 40% of all renal
     neoplasms.
 Distribution and multiplicity
  • Usually a single renal unit; bilateral in up to 5%.
   • Pelvicalyceal tumours about twice as common as ureteric tumours (elsewhere in the chapter stated as
     only slightly more common).
   • Multifocal in 10–20%; concomitant carcinoma in situ (CIS) in 11–36%; concurrent bladder cancer in
     17%.
   • Prior bladder cancer is far commoner in American than in Chinese males with UTUC; Asian patients
     present with higher-grade disease.
   • At diagnosis, ≈ 60% are invasive and ≈ 7–9% are metastatic.
   • The absence of a thick muscularis propria in the upper tract explains earlier upstaging and the high
     proportion of invasive tumours at presentation.
 Recurrence after treatment, and UTUC after bladder cancer
  • After UTUC treatment, recurrence involves the bladder in 22–47% and the contralateral upper tract in
    2–6%.
   • UTUC incidence is increased after treated bladder cancer: ≈ 7.5% after bladder-sparing surgery plus
     intravesical bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) versus 3–5% after radical cystectomy for muscle-invasive
     disease.

 SEX, RACE, AGE VARIATIONS, AND MORTALITY RATE
   • Male : female ≈ 2 : 1; Black individuals affected about twice as often as White individuals.
   • UTUC patients are older than bladder cancer patients and have a worse prognosis.
   • Disease mortality rises with age, male sex, Black non-Hispanic race and advanced stage; higher grade
     (III–IV vs I–II) and stage also raise mortality in recent SEER data.
   • Five-year disease-specific survival falls steeply with stage: in situ ≈ 95%, localised ≈ 89%, regional ≈
     63%, distant ≈ 17% (overall ≈ 75%).

 SOMATIC AND GERMLINE ALTERATIONS: GENOMIC CLASSIFICATION AND
 FAMILIAL PREDISPOSITION
   • UTUC and bladder cancer are genetically distinct; when one patient has both, the tumours are usually
     clonally related.
   • Five molecular subtypes: hypermutated, TP53/MDM2-mutated, RAS-mutated, FGFR3-mutated and
     triple-negative (no TP53/MDM2, RAS or FGFR3 mutation). The KRAS/RAS-mutated group has the
     worst prognosis.
   • Higher-stage disease: fewer RTK/RAS alterations, more TP53/MDM2 alterations. Molecular
     classification may in future direct targeted therapy.
   • Germline analysis serves two purposes: estimating the risk of subsequent bladder cancer and
     identifying Lynch syndrome (LS).
   • The Amsterdam criteria identify families likely to carry LS. Immunohistochemistry of LS-related UTUC
     shows loss of the protein of the mutated mismatch repair (MMR) gene in the vast majority; MSH2
     carriers have the greatest tumour tendency.
   • A germline MMR mutation is found in about 5–9% of screened UTUC patients.
   • Screen for LS: all UTUC patients under 60 and anyone with a family history of UTUC; the most recent
     recommendation extends molecular tumour testing to patients under 65 or with a personal or family
     history of LS-related cancer, followed by germline testing.

 Concept — UTUC is rare, rising and presents late: the thin upper tract wall lets tumours invade early, so
 the epidemiology is dominated by invasive disease, by the bladder as the commonest site of recurrence,
 and by a small hereditary (Lynch) group that must be actively sought in the young.

 RISK FACTORS

 Genetic Predisposition
   • Familial/hereditary UTUC = hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal carcinoma (HNPCC), i.e. Lynch
     syndrome, caused by mutations in the MMR genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6 and PMS2.
   • LS cancers: colon, urothelial, gastric, pancreatic, uterine, sebaceous, ovarian. The urothelial cancers
     are mainly upper tract; bladder involvement is uncertain and a bladder tumour in LS may simply be
     seeding from the upper tract.
   • LS confers ≈ 14-fold UTUC risk over the general population, rising to ≈ 75-fold in MSH2 carriers.
   • LS-associated UTUC: younger (< 60; mean ≈ 55) and with relatively more women than sporadic UTUC.
   • Suspect genetic predisposition in young patients, a personal history of LS cancer, or two first-degree
     relatives with an LS cancer (especially colon or endometrium) → short structured interview (Figure
     138.1) → DNA sequencing and family counselling in high-risk cases.

 Environmental Factors
 Tobacco Exposure
  • Smoking is the most important modifiable risk factor; it raises UTUC risk 2.5- to 7-fold.
   • Mechanism: aromatic amines (arylamine, benzopyrene, dimethylbenzanthracene) are activated to
     mutagenic N-hydroxylamine; detoxification by cytochrome P450 (CYP1A1), glutathione S-transferases
     and N-acetyltransferases is modulated by genetic polymorphisms.
   • Dose-response with cigarettes per day and years smoked — risk roughly triples from ≤ 20 to ≥ 60 pack-
     years.
   • Cessation for > 10 years reduces risk by 60–70%; former smokers carry about half the risk of current
     smokers.
   • The ureter is at greater smoking-related risk than the pelvicalyceal system.
 Coffee, Tea, and Yerba Mate
  • Coffee and tea: no significant association with UTUC; any apparent coffee effect is confounded by
    smoking. Water, coffee, tea and dairy intake were unrelated to urothelial cancer in a large prospective
    European cohort.
   • Yerba mate (South America) supplies polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and roughly doubles urothelial
     cancer risk, but has never been studied specifically for UTUC.
 Alcohol Consumption
  • Ever-drinkers have a modestly higher UTUC risk than never-drinkers, with a dose-response; the risk
    threshold is ≥ 15 g of alcohol daily.

 Occupation
  • "Amino tumours": UTUC from occupational aromatic amines — dyes, textiles, rubber, chemicals,
    petrochemicals, coal. Benzidine and β-naphthylamine are the implicated amines, banned since the
    1960s in most industrialised countries.
   • Average exposure needed ≈ 7 years; latency up to 20 years after exposure ends.
   • Raised risk also with the chemical, petroleum and plastics industries, coal or coke, and highest with
     asphalt or tar; polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and nitrosamines (dose effect); chlorinated solvents
     (trichloroethylene, tetrachloroethylene) in metallurgy and printing.
 Aristolochic Acid Nephropathy
  • Balkan endemic nephropathy (BEN): UTUC incidence 60–100 times higher in some rural Balkan areas;
    the excess fell sharply (from ≈ 57-fold to ≈ 11-fold) between 1988 and 1998.
   • BEN is a proximal tubular disorder with low-molecular-weight proteinuria and dense interstitial
     fibrosis.
   • Ochratoxin A was investigated for decades; it is carcinogenic in animals but its lesions do not match
     BEN tumours histologically.
   • Chinese herb nephropathy: Belgium, 1992–1993 — end-stage renal failure after slimming herbal
     products in which Stephania tetrandra was replaced in error by Aristolochia fangchi; nearly half later
     developed UTUC, histologically and genetically identical to BEN tumours.
   • Aristolochic acid (from A. fangchi and A. clematitis, a weed of wheat fields) is the common cause. BEN
     is familial but not inherited — a shared dietary exposure; bladder cancer is not increased in affected
     families.
   • Mechanism: the metabolite aristolactam forms aristolactam–deoxyadenosine DNA adducts (lasting
     exposure biomarkers) → A:T → T:A transversions, mainly on the non-transcribed strand, including TP53
     codon 139. This signature mutation predominates in BEN and Chinese herb nephropathy.
   • Fewer than 10% of exposed people develop UTUC.
   • High UTUC prevalence in Taiwan and China is linked to aristolochia-containing herbal remedies.
   • Worse prognosis in aristolochic acid UTUC: female sex, tumour > 3 cm, stage T3–T4.
 Analgesics
  • Phenacetin first implicated in 1961; carcinogenesis mainly indirect, through nephrotoxicity and
    papillary necrosis (the confirmed mechanism) rather than direct mutagenesis. Withdrawn about 30
    years ago and replaced by paracetamol; phenacetin-related UTUC is declining. Mean latency ≈ 22
    years.
   • Analgesic abuse is an established risk factor: history of abuse in 22% of pelvic and 11% of ureteric
     tumours. Most abusers take combinations (caffeine, codeine, paracetamol, salicylates).
   • Pathognomonic histology: basement membrane thickening (capillarosclerosis) and papillary scarring
     — present in up to 15% of UTUC; its presence should raise suspicion of analgesic abuse and of
     contralateral disease.
   • Tumour grade correlates with the degree of papillary scarring; scarring is not linked to squamous
     metaplasia.
 Arsenic
  • Inorganic arsenic in artesian well water (south-west coast of Taiwan, Chile) is associated with UTUC; up
    to a quarter of Taiwan's UTUC arises in this region.
   • Chronic exposure causes blackfoot disease — a peripheral vascular disease with dry gangrene of the
     extremities.

   • Features of UTUC in blackfoot-endemic areas: women affected twice as often (arsenic fumes during
     cooking — inhalation plus ingestion), younger onset (55–60), ureter involved twice as often as the
     pelvis.
   • The causal link is not established for UTUC (it is stronger for bladder cancer): many patients never have
     blackfoot disease, and north-east Taiwan has a high UTUC rate without it.
 Chronic Inflammation and Infection
  • Chronic urinary infection weakens the urothelium; stasis and urinary irritants promote carcinogenesis;
    risk raised ≈ 1.5–2-fold.
   • Urolithiasis history in 5–8% of UTUC, but tumour incidence in stone formers remains low; risk higher in
     women, with renal pelvic calcification, and after stone-related admission.
   • Chronic infection + stones + obstruction → squamous cell carcinoma (less often adenocarcinoma).
 Iatrogenic Factors
  • Alkylating chemotherapy (cyclophosphamide) → acrolein → UTUC, with epidermoid pathology on
    chronic exposure.
   • Anthranoid and chemical laxatives used > 1 year: strong association; mechanism unknown.
   • Pelvic radiotherapy for gynaecological cancer: raised risk, but lower than after chemotherapy.

 Concept — Almost every UTUC risk factor is a urine-borne carcinogen concentrated in the upper tract
 (tobacco amines, aristolochic acid, arsenic, phenacetin metabolites, acrolein); the exposure-driven forms
 behave as a field disease — earlier, bilateral and multiply recurrent — which is why they bear directly on
 treatment choice.

   • > 90% of upper tract tumours are urothelial; pure non-urothelial cancers (squamous cell carcinoma,
     adenocarcinoma) are rare.
   • Histological variants in ≈ 25%; they correspond to high-grade tumours with a worse prognosis.

 Renal Pelvis and Calyces
  • Transitional epithelium over two muscle layers plus fibrous tissue; the muscle is spiral/helical,
    originating in the minor calyces.
 Ureter
  • Two continuous thin spiral layers: outer layer tightly spiralled, inner layer looser; a third outer
    longitudinal layer in the lower third.
   • These merge with the three bladder layers (inner longitudinal, middle circular, outer longitudinal). The
     serosa (adventitia) of loose connective tissue carries vessels and lymphatics.

 Urothelial Neoplasms
 Benign Lesions: Papillomas and von Brunn Nests
  • Papillomas, inverted papillomas and von Brunn nests are benign, but synchronous or metachronous
    UTUC cannot be excluded → close surveillance.
   • Malignancy found in ≈ 18% of ureteric inverted papillomas. Type 1 benign; type 2 potentially
     malignant — but the two cannot be told apart, so all inverted papillomas are followed for at least 2
     years.
 Metaplastic and Dysplastic Lesions
  • UTUC often develops through hyperplasia → dysplasia → CIS.
   • CIS spreads in patches, up to the collecting ducts. Severe dysplasia predicts a worse prognosis
     through higher recurrence in the distal ureter and bladder.

 UROTHELIAL MALIGNANT LESIONS

 Urothelial Carcinoma
   • > 90% of upper tract tumours. Morphology as in the bladder: flat (CIS), papillary or sessile; unifocal or
     multifocal.
   • Histologically like bladder cancer, but the thin muscle allows earlier muscle invasion, and invasion of
     renal parenchyma or periureteric adventitia is more likely than in the bladder.
   • CIS is hard to diagnose: it may look like a whitish plaque, epithelial hyperplasia or a smooth red patch
     (increased submucosal vascularity).
   • Collecting duct carcinoma shares features through common embryology but is classed as kidney
     cancer, not UTUC.

 Micropapillary Variant of Urothelial Carcinoma
  • Variants (up to 25%): squamous, glandular, sarcomatoid, micropapillary, neuroendocrine,
    lymphoepithelial — all linked to aggressive behaviour, although not independently prognostic once
    other clinicopathological features are adjusted for.
   • Micropapillary UTUC is rare and usually advanced at presentation (most pT3, frequent CIS and nodal
     involvement); it is an independent predictor of worse progression-free and cancer-specific survival.

 Concept — Upper tract and bladder urothelium look the same but sit on different walls: the thin
 mesodermal muscle is the single fact that explains earlier invasion, the dominance of invasive disease and
 the limited value of superficial biopsy in staging.

 DIAGNOSIS
 Symptoms and signs
  • Haematuria is the commonest sign (56–98%), gross or microscopic. Yield of UTUC is low: ≈ 0.75% of
    gross and ≈ 0.17% of microscopic haematuria.
   • Flank pain is second (20–32%): usually dull from gradual obstruction and hydronephrosis; colic when
     clots pass. Flank pain is commoner than with bladder tumours (10–40%).
   • Palpable lumbar mass ≈ 10%; asymptomatic, incidental ≈ 15%.
   • Systemic symptoms (anorexia, weight loss, fatigue, fever, bone pain, night sweats, abdominal or flank
     mass) → immediate metastatic work-up; they carry a worse prognosis.
   • UTUC is a rare autopsy finding — most are diagnosed in life.
 Gross features
  • Ureteric tumours are low grade and low stage in 55–75%.
   • 85% papillary, 15% sessile. Invasion (T1–T2) in 50% of papillary vs > 80% of sessile tumours.
   • Invasive tumours are commoner in the ureter than in the bladder; renal pelvic tumours invade
     lamina propria or muscle in 50–60%.

 Endoscopy of the Lower Tract
 Cystoscopy
  • Cystoscopy always — UTUC frequently coexists with bladder cancer.
   • Positive cytology + negative cystoscopy (including urethra) → evaluate the upper tracts.

 Endoscopy of the Upper Tract
 Ureterorenoscopic Evaluation
  • Flexible ureteroscopy (URS) is the key tool for visualising ureter, pelvis and collecting system, and for
    biopsy.
   • Adding URS raises diagnostic accuracy from 75–85% (excretory or retrograde urography alone) to ≈
     90%.
   • Pyelovenous/pyelolymphatic dissemination from URS was reported historically but not confirmed
     with modern technique → URS whenever needed.
   • Biopsy (forceps or brush) determines grade in ≈ 90%, with a low false-negative rate regardless of
     sample size; biopsy–specimen correlation 78–92%. Visual grading alone is right in ≈ 70% → biopsy
     needed.
   • Undergrading and understaging are common: specimens are small and rarely include deep layers;
     loop biopsies contain lamina propria far more often than cup biopsies; a Ta biopsy is frequently
     upstaged to T3 at resection. A grade 3 biopsy predicts stage accurately in > 90%.
   • Hence, EAU: stage cannot be assessed from URS biopsy; stratify by grade + visual appearance +
     imaging (hydronephrosis) + cytology, and follow kidney-sparing patients intensively.

   • URS allows selective in-situ cytology sampling.
   • Indications for diagnostic URS: diagnostic uncertainty; kidney-sparing treatment being considered;

   • URS before radical nephroureterectomy (RNU) increases intravesical recurrence, particularly with

   • Enhanced imaging: narrow-band imaging is promising but preliminary; optical coherence tomography
     and confocal endomicroscopy (Cellvizio) correlate well with final histology for grade and stage.

 Antegrade Endoscopic Evaluation
  • Percutaneous nephroscopy, antegrade URS and urography allow visualisation, biopsy and resection/

   • EAU: consider for low-risk renal pelvic UTUC inaccessible or difficult with flexible URS. Largely

   • Risk: tumour seeding of the retroperitoneum and nephrostomy tract.

 Radiologic Imaging
   • Computed tomography urography (CTU) has the highest diagnostic accuracy (pooled sensitivity ≈ 92%,
     specificity ≈ 95%). Thin isotropic sections allow multiplanar reconstruction; accuracy equals intravenous
     pyelography with less labour; drawback is high radiation dose.
   • Suggestive signs: radiolucent filling defect (seen in about 50–75%), obstruction or incomplete filling,
     non-visualisation of the collecting system.
   • Differential of a filling defect: stone, blood clot, bowel gas, extrinsic compression, sloughed papilla,
     fungus ball. Calcification on ultrasound or unenhanced CT identifies stones.

 Cytology and Tumor Markers
   • Overall urine cytology sensitivity ≈ 65%, strongly grade-dependent: ≈ 20% grade 1, ≈ 45% grade 2, ≈
     75% grade 3.
   • Voided cytology gives no site of origin; it is less sensitive for UTUC than for bladder cancer. Selective
     sampling or ureteric washing by catheter (in situ in the collecting system) is more accurate; saline
     washings raise the cell yield. False results remain common.
   • Brush biopsy + cytology raises sensitivity to ≈ 90% (high for low grade, near-complete for high grade),
     at the cost of haemorrhage and perforation.
   • Take cytology before any contrast — ionic high-osmolar agents degrade the specimen.
   • Fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) is as sensitive as in bladder cancer; best suited to low-grade
     disease under surveillance after kidney-sparing therapy, but not routinely used.

   KEY POINTS: DIAGNOSIS
    • Localised disease: haematuria, dysuria and flank pain.
    • Advanced disease: flank or abdominal mass, weight loss, anorexia and bone pain.
    • Cystoscopy should always be performed.
    • Flexible URS with biopsy is the key diagnostic approach.
    • CT urography has the highest diagnostic accuracy.
    • Urine collection or ureteric washing via a ureteric catheter gives the most accurate cytology.

 Concept — Every UTUC test is good at grade and poor at stage: biopsy is shallow, CT misses flat and small
 invasive lesions, and cytology tracks grade — so stage is inferred from a composite (grade, appearance,
 hydronephrosis, cytology), and that composite drives the choice between kidney-sparing treatment and
 RNU.

 Involvement of the Ureter or Renal Collecting System
  • Ureteric tumours: 70% distal, 25% mid, 5% proximal — attributed to downstream implantation.
   • Synchronous or metachronous bilateral disease: 1.6–6% of sporadic UTUC.
   • Stage-adjusted, ureteric and/or multifocal tumours do worse than renal pelvic tumours.
 Synchronous and Asynchronous Localizations
 Upper tract recurrence after bladder cancer.
   • Incidence 0.75–6%, late (median > 3 years); traditional series 2–4% over 17–170 months, some up to
     25% (selection, grade, stage and dysplasia explain the spread). After cystectomy ≈ 0.75–6.4% in the
     largest meta-analysis.
   • Risk factors: tumour at the distal ureter/ureteric orifice, multifocal or recurrent bladder cancer, non-
     muscle-invasive bladder cancer, CIS; risk rises steeply with the number of factors present, and is
     minimal with none.
   • Also higher with T1 vs Ta, high grade, trigonal/periureteric location, vesicoureteric reflux, and CIS vs
     papillary disease (including cystectomy for CIS vs for invasive cancer).
   • Recurrences are usually superficial and confined to the distal ureter in some series, yet more than half
     present with symptoms, most at advanced stage, 18–33% metastatic; historical BCG-treated cohorts
     show high UTUC mortality.
   • Ureteric recurrence is commoner and earlier than pelvic recurrence.
   • Risk is stable over long follow-up → rigorous, prolonged upper tract surveillance.
   • BCG-refractory disease treated by cystectomy, and bladder CIS treated with BCG: ≈ 30% UTUC →
     annual upper tract evaluation in these and all high-risk bladder cancers.
   • Meta-analysis after cystectomy: type of urinary diversion does not affect risk; detection is usually
     symptom-driven; CT should replace cytology and excretory urography for follow-up.
 Bladder recurrence after upper tract tumours.
   • Bladder recurrence 15–75% within 5 years (22–47% in pooled contemporary data), usually within 2
     years, often at the site of bladder trauma from ureterectomy.

   KEY POINTS: STAGING AND CLASSIFICATION
    • Ureteric tumours: distal 70%, mid 25%, upper 5%. Bilateral disease 3–5%.
    • Recurrence prognosis after RNU depends on tumour location and stage.
    • Upper tract recurrence after bladder cancer 2–4%, over 17–170 months.
    • Bladder recurrence after UTUC 15–75% within 5 years.
    • CIS brings a higher incidence of bilateral and multifocal disease.
    • Spread is typically by direct extension, lymphatics and bloodstream.

 Concept — Recurrence is asymmetric because urine flows downhill: seeding runs antegrade, so bladder
 recurrence after UTUC is common and early while upper tract recurrence after bladder cancer is
 uncommon and late; the field effect explains the rest, and CIS is its most concentrated form.

 PROGNOSIS

 TNM Classification
   • Classification and morphology mirror the bladder: non-invasive papillary tumours (papillary urothelial
     neoplasm of low malignant potential, low- and high-grade papillary carcinoma), flat lesions (CIS) and
     invasive carcinoma. Variant histology is an adverse factor.
   • Regional nodes: pelvis and upper ureter → hilar and retroperitoneal; mid and distal ureter →
     intrapelvic. Laterality does not affect N category.
   • Renal pelvic pT3 subclassification: pT3a = microscopic renal parenchymal infiltration; pT3b =
     macroscopic infiltration or peripelvic fat invasion (worse).

 Architecture, grade and histology
  • Benign papillomas do well regardless of treatment. PUNLMP — reassigned from 1973 grade 1 to the
    low-grade category — is managed as low-grade disease.
   • Papillary tumours do better than sessile; sessile architecture is a strong predictor of poor outcome.
   • Renal pelvic tumours are slightly commoner than ureteric; after conservative treatment, recurrence is
     antegrade (downstream) in 33–55%, proximal recurrence uncommon.
   • Atypia, dysplasia or CIS in 60–95% of RNU specimens for pelvic tumours; concomitant CIS in organ-
     confined UTUC and prior bladder CIS raise recurrence and cancer-specific mortality.
 Size and necrosis
  • Size is not an established criterion, but > 3–4 cm is linked to worse survival and bladder recurrence;
    each additional centimetre raises the risk of ≥ pT2; 2 cm is the best cut-off for predicting ≥ pT2 at RNU.
   • Extensive necrosis (> 10% of tumour area) is an independent predictor after RNU, tracking advanced
     stage, high grade, nodal disease, CIS and sessile architecture; its effect on survival is debated.
 History and multifocality
  • Metachronous bilateral UTUC is rare (≈ 3%), occurs in the elderly and carries short survival.
   • History of bladder tumour in 15–50% of UTUC; CIS of the distal ureter in 7–25% of cystectomy
     specimens; upper tract recurrence after bladder cancer 2–4% (mean interval ≈ 70 months), 3–9% after
     cystectomy.
   • Environmental, analgesic and LS-related UTUC recur more often multiply and bilaterally. Balkan
     tumours are usually low grade, but coexisting renal insufficiency limits kidney-sparing options.
     Papillary scarring from phenacetin predicts higher grade and progression, dose-dependently.
 Pattern of spread
  • Most metastases appear within 2–3 years; sites: lung, liver, bone, regional nodes.
   • Stage T3 renal pelvic tumours outlive stage T3 ureteric tumours — the renal parenchyma acts as a
     barrier, whereas periureteric spread enters a rich vascular and lymphatic plexus; ureteric tumours
     have higher local and distant failure at equal stage and grade.
 Lymph Node Involvement
  • Node metastasis and extranodal extension are powerful, independent predictors of poor survival; up
    to 40% harbour nodal disease depending on stage and grade.

   • Lymph node dissection (LND) at RNU gives optimal staging; its curative role is debated. Lymph node
     density ≥ 30% predicts poor outcome.
   • Clinically node-positive UTUC → systemic chemotherapy first, surgery if there is a response.

 Lymphovascular Invasion
  • Lymphovascular invasion (LVI) in ≈ 20%; independent predictor of recurrence and survival in node-
    negative or node-unknown patients, not in node-positive patients.
   • Not identifiable on URS biopsy — a postoperative factor only; must be reported on every UTUC
     specimen.

 Surgical Margins
   • Positive soft-tissue margin after RNU predicts recurrence; above pT2, pathologists should report
     margins at the ureteric transection, bladder cuff and around the tumour.

 Other Pathological Factors
   • Extensive necrosis (> 10%); pathological downstaging after neoadjuvant chemotherapy → better
     overall survival; sessile architecture; bladder CIS history and concomitant CIS; peripelvic fat or
     macroscopic invasion (pT3b) worse than microscopic parenchymal invasion (pT3a).

 Molecular Markers
   • UTUC and bladder cancer share some genetic and epigenetic features but are divergent entities.
     Evidence is retrospective and small: currently insufficient for routine clinical use of any marker, though
     some are promising.
   • Microsatellite instability (MSI) and hypermethylation are the key differences from bladder cancer. MSI
     is an independent prognostic marker, flags germline mutations (LS), and is linked to inverted growth
     pattern; MSI testing on tumour and normal tissue, established for colon cancer, can be applied to UTUC
     meeting LS criteria. A urine MSI panel detected most UTUCs.
   • Chromosome 9 alterations in ≈ 50%. Comparative genomic hybridisation shows concordant losses (2q,
     8p, 9q, 11p, 13q, 17p, 18q) and gains (1q, 6p, 8q, 17q) between pelvic and bladder tumours, with
     similar gene expression. Other alterations: +1p36, +6p22, +7, +8q22, −9p21, +11q, −13q, +17, +19q13,
     +20q.
   • PD-1 and PD-L1 expression is prognostic in high-grade UTUC.

 Predictive Tools
   • Two preoperative models: one predicts nodal involvement (guiding whether and how extensively to
     do LND at RNU); one identifies non-organ-confined disease for RNU.
   • Five postoperative nomograms use standard pathology; one uses only four variables with high
     accuracy for high-grade UTUC after surgery. None is yet established in practice.

 Risk Stratification
   • Clinical staging is unreliable, so pre-RNU models predict ≥ pT2/non-organ-confined disease to select
     patients for neoadjuvant chemotherapy; pre- and postoperative nomograms aid counselling, follow-up
     and perioperative chemotherapy decisions.
   • Kidney-sparing selection uses low- vs high-risk stratification (Figure 138.6).

    • Use the TNM classification and staging system.
