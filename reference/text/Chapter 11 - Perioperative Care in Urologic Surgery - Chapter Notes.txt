Chapter 11 — Perioperative Care in Urologic Surgery
 • Perioperative care keeps evolving with new tools and an ageing population; economics drive more
   outpatient surgery, shorter stays and fewer complications.
 • Standard of care: even complex urological operations are admitted on the day of surgery and discharged
   the day after.
 • Despite the shift to office-based and non-surgical care, the diversity of genitourinary disease requires
   every urologist to command perioperative principles.

PREOPERATIVE EVALUATION
 • Patient acuity is rising with age and comorbidity.
 • Surgeon and patient share responsibility for evaluation by the wider team, so the patient arrives in, and
   leaves, theatre optimised.
 • Appropriate specialist consultation improves outcomes and reduces cancellations.

PRESURGICAL TESTING
 • Goal: find an undiagnosed comorbidity, an undertreated condition, or a significant exacerbation that may
   affect the operative course.
 • Individualise by age, history, examination and procedure.
 • Routine testing beyond history and examination has never been shown cost-effective, and predicts
   morbidity less well than American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) status or American Heart
   Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) risk guidance; a systematic review found no
   support for routine testing before elective non-cardiac surgery.
 • Typical panel: full blood count; basic metabolic panel; prothrombin time (PT), partial thromboplastin time
   (PTT), international normalised ratio (INR) (controversial); electrocardiogram (ECG); ABO/Rh group and
   antibody screen; chest radiograph.

American Society of Anesthesiologists Classification and Risk
 • About 27 million US operations a year; about 8 million (30%) in patients with significant coronary or
   other cardiac disease — hence the cardiovascular focus.
 • ASA classification (1961, revised to six classes; Box 11.1) grades overall physical status, not surgical risk;
   subjective yet an independent predictor of mortality.
 • Multivariate scores: Goldman's criteria (Table 11.1) sum weighted points; the Modified Cardiac Risk Index
   (Table 11.2) simplifies to six predictors.

PREOPERATIVE CARDIOVASCULAR EVALUATION
 • Initial history, examination and ECG seek coronary disease, heart failure, symptomatic arrhythmia,
   pacemaker/implantable defibrillator, and orthostatic hypotension; define severity and stability.
 • Cardiac risk is modified by functional capacity, age, diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, renal
   dysfunction and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).
 • ACC/AHA guidelines use three categories: clinical markers, functional capacity, and type of surgery.

Concept — Cardiac risk is the product of three independent axes: how sick the heart is (clinical markers), how
much reserve the patient has (METs), and how much stress the operation imposes (surgery type). A patient
with good reserve undergoing low-stress surgery needs no further testing whatever the history.

PULMONARY EVALUATION
 • Most important for thoracic and abdominal surgery.
 • Pulmonary function testing (forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV₁), forced vital capacity, diffusing
   capacity for carbon monoxide) for known lung disease, significant smoking history or symptoms — gives a
   baseline.
 • FEV₁ <0.8 L or <30% predicted → high risk.
 • Specific risk factors: COPD, smoking, sputum production, pneumonia, dyspnoea, obstructive sleep
   apnoea.
 • Smokers: 4-fold postoperative pulmonary morbidity and up to 10-fold mortality.
 • Restrictive fares better than obstructive disease — preserved maximal expiratory flow allows an effective
   cough with less sputum.
 • General factors: age, low albumin, obesity, impaired sensorium, prior stroke, immobility, acute renal
   failure, chronic steroids.

HEPATOBILIARY EVALUATION
 • Advanced liver disease survival has improved, so these patients come to surgery more often; mild–
   moderate disease is often asymptomatic and must be sought.
 • History: prior hepatitis, timing and precipitants (important if staff have a blood-borne exposure); pruritus,
   bleeding, distension, weight gain.
 • Examination: jaundice/scleral icterus visible at bilirubin >3 mg/dL; caput medusae, palmar erythema,
   spider naevi, clubbing; severe — ascites, encephalopathy, asterixis, cachexia.

SPECIAL POPULATIONS
Elderly
 • By 2050 Americans >65 will more than double to 89 million, >20% of them over 85; octogenarian and
   nonagenarian surgery is increasing.
 • Whether age or comorbidity drives risk is unsettled, but age independently predicts morbidity and
   mortality; after radical cystectomy, 90-day mortality climbs across the <70, 70–80 and >80 bands.
 • Over 80: hypertension and dyspnoea are the commonest comorbid risks; preoperative transfusion,
   emergency surgery and weight loss best predict morbidity; longer operative time raises mortality in
   octogenarians.
 • Delirium — often dismissed as "sundowning" — may be the first sign of metabolic or infective
   complication; assess cognition, function and mood preoperatively.
 • Reduce delirium: minimise noise, familiar surroundings, consistent sleep, regional anaesthesia where
   possible, minimal opioids.

Morbid Obesity
 • Weigh operative risk against natural history when timing surgery; BMI ≥45 kg/m² costs 8–13 years of life
   expectancy.
 • Cardiac symptoms are non-specific and examination underestimates dysfunction; >3 coronary risk factors
   → consider non-invasive cardiac evaluation.
 • Comorbidities: ASCVD, heart failure, hypertension, sleep-apnoea pulmonary hypertension,
   hypoventilation, arrhythmias, deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, poor exercise capacity;
   ventilation–perfusion mismatch.
 • Positioning injury and wound infection risk → consider laparoscopy when appropriate.

Pregnancy
 • Commonest reason: renal colic/stones, found on fetal ultrasound or during colic.
 • Fetal radiation risk highest from preimplantation to ~15 weeks; malformation threshold 10 cGy →
   ultrasound first (CT abdomen ~1 cGy; intravenous pyelogram ~0.3 cGy).
 • Delay surgery until the fetus is mature unless mother or fetus would be harmed.
 Trimester                     Anaesthetic/surgical risk
 First                         Teratogenic exposure to anaesthetic agents; delay semi-elective surgery beyond it
 Second                        Safest — organogenesis complete; almost no risk of anaesthetic malformation or
                               miscarriage
 Third                         Preterm labour; fetal hypoxaemia from altered uterine flow and maternal acid–base change

 • Involve obstetrician, perinatologist and anaesthetist; fetal heart rate and tocometry before and after.
 • Analgesia: opioids acceptable (no human birth defects at normal doses; wean early — fetal dependency);
   avoid NSAIDs — premature closure of the ductus arteriosus.

Nutritional Status
 • Malnutrition impairs host defence, wound healing, immunity, bowel recovery and organ function.
 • Assessment: weight loss plus lymphocyte count and albumin (visceral protein); >20 lb (~9 kg) loss in 3
   months = severe malnutrition; validated tools include the Subjective Global Assessment.

Concept — Each special population reshapes the same risk calculation: the elderly lose reserve and present
atypically (delirium), the obese mask cardiac disease, pregnancy adds a second patient with a time-
dependent vulnerability, and malnutrition removes the capacity to heal — so the gut should be used
whenever it works.

PREPARATION FOR SURGERY
Preoperative Optimisation Strategies
Optimisation of Comorbid Illness
Cardiac.
 • Of nitrates, calcium-channel blockers and β-blockers, only β-blockade improved outcome.
 • Mangano (NEJM, 1996), landmark: prophylactic atenolol improved outcomes after non-cardiac surgery; a
   very large retrospective study linked perioperative β-blockade to lower mortality in high-risk major non-
   cardiac surgery.
 • Goal-directed therapy (judicious fluids, inotropes, oxygen to physiological targets) further reduces risk; an
   early trial showed a large fall in mortality.
Pulmonary.
 • Stop smoking ≥8 weeks before surgery. Stopping <8 weeks may raise risk — loss of smoke irritation
   reduces coughing and pulmonary toilet. >6 months' abstinence → risk equals non-smokers.
 • COPD: preoperative bronchodilators dramatically reduce complications; treat infection with antibiotics;
   steroid pretreatment for asthma; epidural/regional anaesthesia, physiotherapy and continued
   bronchodilation help.
Diabetes.
 • Hyperglycaemia impairs healing and raises infection; hypoglycaemia under anaesthesia may go
   unrecognised.
 • Stop long-acting oral hypoglycaemics; prefer short-acting agents or sliding-scale insulin; target glucose
   80–250 mg/dL; frequent fingerstick checks postoperatively; resume usual regimen when eating.

Management of Medications — Antithrombotic Therapy
 • Warfarin and antiplatelets cause significant postoperative bleeding (unlike venous thromboembolism
   (VTE) prophylaxis) → balance bleeding against thrombosis.
 • Warfarin (atrial fibrillation, mechanical valves, prior VTE): half-life 36–42 h → stop 5 days before surgery
   for INR <1.5.
 • Direct oral anticoagulants (apixaban, dabigatran, rivaroxaban) differ pharmacologically; the surgeon must
   know each.
 • Bridging depends on thrombotic risk (Table 11.4): 2012 American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) —
   bridge moderate- and high-risk patients with therapeutic subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin
   (LMWH) or IV unfractionated heparin (UFH).

rules are counterintuitive: late smoking cessation, premature antiplatelet withdrawal and routine mechanical
bowel preparation can each do more harm than good.

INTRAOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT
Patient Environment
 • Hypothermia is harmful in elective surgery (therapeutic only in some trauma/brain injury).
 • Mechanism: induction → peripheral vasodilatation redistributes core heat (early drop); thereafter
   radiation and conduction account for most loss.
 • Normothermia = core 36–38°C; even 1–2°C of hypothermia is harmful — mild hypothermia increases
   blood loss and transfusion (meta-analysis of RCTs) via impaired clotting enzymes and platelet aggregation.
 • SSI risk rises with mild hypothermia (34–36°C) — impaired immunity and vasoconstriction-driven tissue
   hypoxia.
 • Kurz (1996), landmark, 200 colorectal patients: hypothermia → 3× wound infection and +2.6 days' stay.
   The Surgical Care Improvement Project (SCIP) includes normothermia.
 • Warming: blankets, warmed IV fluids, warmed irrigation (transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP),
   long endoscopy), warmed humidified CO₂ in laparoscopy, higher theatre temperature.

Patient Safety
 • Harvard Medical Practice Study (Brennan, 1991): 48% of adverse events accompanied an operation →
   inspired the Institute of Medicine's To Err Is Human. The operating theatre is the commonest venue of
   preventable surgical injury; the surgeon is "captain of the ship" but every member must attend to detail.
 • Three immediately preventable injuries: retractor, thermal and positioning.
 • Self-retaining retractors → femoral neuropathy: lateral blades must cradle rectus only, never compress
   psoas; reinspect periodically.

 • Trap: most urological operations entering the urinary tract are clean-contaminated; transrectal biopsy is

 • Keys once prophylaxis is chosen: the right agent, correct timing, correct dose.
 • Agent: procedure dictates pathogens (TURP → urinary organisms; sigmoid diversion → anaerobes); local
   resistance matters — level 1 evidence supports fluoroquinolones for endoscopy but E. coli resistance is
   changing practice; use the monthly hospital antibiogram. AUA amendment (2012) for prostate biopsy:
   cephalosporins and/or aminoglycosides in resistant communities.
 • Timing: Classen (1992), pivotal — within 2 h of incision; SCIP — within 60 min; a multicentre trial
   favoured ≤30 min over 31–60 min and confirmed that giving it at or after incision roughly doubles SSI.
 • Duration: ≤24 h without established infection (longer → C. difficile, resistance, cost).
 • Dose: by weight, renal and hepatic function; redose if the case exceeds 4 h. Compliance with AUA best
   practice remains <60%.

VENOUS THROMBOEMBOLIC PROPHYLAXIS
 • Postoperative VTE is the second commonest cause of excess stay, charges and mortality among surgical
   discharges.
 • Urology incidence without prophylaxis historically 10–40%; now 1–5% with mechanical prophylaxis and
   early ambulation; in the prospective @RISTOS study VTE followed open urological surgery despite high
   prophylaxis rates; radical cystectomy carries the highest incidence.
 • VTE is the most important cause of non-surgical mortality in urology.
 • Mechanical prophylaxis is near-universal; pharmacological only after weighing bleeding (Table 11.6). In
   general surgery, pharmacological prophylaxis increased minor bleeding (bruising, haematoma) but not
   major bleeding; TURP and partial nephrectomy bleed more.
 • Surgical factors: general vs neuraxial anaesthesia; supine vs lithotomy; abdominal vs pelvic ±
   lymphadenectomy; open vs laparoscopic. Patient factors: Box 11.5.
 • ACCP 2008: hospitals need a formal VTE strategy; moved from individual risk models to routine group-
   specific prophylaxis for each surgical group. AUA (2009) combines individual risk with procedure type —
   high-risk patient/low-risk surgery or low-risk patient/high-risk surgery may each warrant drugs (Table
   11.7).

ANAESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS
 • Anaesthesia delivers hypnosis, amnesia and analgesia with good operating conditions; most urology is
   done under monitored anaesthesia care, regional or general anaesthesia, combining inhalational and IV
   agents, analgesics and benzodiazepines.
 • Risk is now very low: ambulatory all-cause mortality of the order of 1 in 50,000.

Selection of Mode of Anaesthesia
 • Urologist helps choose the mode: patient factors (comorbidity, airway, preference) and procedural factors
   (complexity, duration, site, expected fluid and blood loss).

 • Conscious sedation under an anaesthetist, ranging from minimal sedation to brief general anaesthesia;

SKIN PREPARATION
 • Antiseptics: alcohol, povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine; apply in concentric circles from the centre and let
   dry before incision.
 • Cochrane: some evidence that 0.5% chlorhexidine in methylated spirits beats alcohol-based povidone-
   iodine for clean surgery; no conclusive superiority overall.
 • CDC recommends preoperative showering, but antiseptic bathing does not reduce infection.
 • Hair removal, if done: immediately before surgery, with clippers (shaving breaches skin → colonisation).

TRANSFUSION CONSIDERATIONS
 • Urological organs are vascular; know transfusion risks and alternatives (e.g. Jehovah's Witnesses).
 • Before the acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) epidemic: transfusion for haematocrit <30%;
   infection fears → National Institutes of Health consensus (1988) → ASA guidelines (2006).

 KEY POINTS
   • Proper preoperative evaluation prevents cancellations and reduces complications.
   • Cardiac testing depends on functional capacity, cardiac risk factors and surgery-specific risk.
   • SSI is a leading complication; prophylaxis is indicated in virtually all procedures.
   • VTE: AUA recommends mechanical or pharmacological prophylaxis (both for the highest risk).
   • Anaesthetic pharmacology lets the urologist help choose the mode.
   • Transfuse at haematocrit <21% in most, unless cardiopulmonary risk.
   • Hypothermia of even 1–2°C increases blood loss and SSI.
