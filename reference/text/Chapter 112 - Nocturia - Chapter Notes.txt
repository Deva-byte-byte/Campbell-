Chapter 112 — Nocturia
 • Nocturia is voiding during the hours of intended sleep. It carries substantial morbidity: a risk factor for
   falls and fractures, a major disruptor of normal sleep architecture and a potential harbinger of serious
   systemic disease.
 • Aetiology is multifactorial — "Sleep CALM": Sleep disorders, Comorbidities, Actions (modifiable
   behaviours and lifestyle factors), Lower urinary tract dysfunction, Medications.
 • One common mechanism: every Sleep CALM factor disturbs the balance between nocturnal urine
   production, storage capacity and normal sleep architecture — and that balance is best characterised by
   the bladder diary.
 • Evaluation: systematic yet individualised — history and examination → tailored ancillary testing.
 • Management: optimise the Sleep CALM factors first; pharmacotherapy targeting excess nocturnal urine
   production is reserved for appropriately selected patients refractory to conservative management.

NOCTURIA—WHAT, WHEN, AND WHY?
 • ICS definition (2019): the number of times urine is passed during the main sleep period — from the time
   a person intends to sleep until the time they intend to rise.
 • The main sleep period is defined by the individual's own sleep schedule, not by societal norms or the
   solar cycle (sunset to sunrise). "Night-time" is used for convenience but technically means the individual's
   sleep period.
 • Shift workers: nocturia is pervasive across all work and sleep schedules, and similar mechanisms operate
   independent of the solar cycle.
 • Current terminology does not exclude patients who void only once per night and does not incorporate
   bother.

RATIONALE FOR EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT

Nocturia Is Common
 • Older age is among the most robust correlates, but nocturia is common across ages, races and sexes
   worldwide.

 • 1 in 5–6 people aged 20–40 years void ≥2 times per night; up to 3 in 5 people >70 years void ≥2 times
   nightly.
 • Boston Area Community Health (BACH) survey (landmark): higher prevalence in Black and Hispanic than
   White respondents; partly mediated by socio-economic status — a disparity in urological practice
   requiring investigation.

Nocturia Is Bothersome
 • Among the most bothersome urinary complaints in the general population; bother is linked to reduced
   quality of life (QoL) and more depressive symptoms.
 • High bother → more difficulty initiating sleep, returning to sleep after a void, and more morning fatigue.
 • Most people with ≥2 voids per night report significant bother; but frequency and bother correlate only at
   population level — one void per night can be highly bothersome.
 • Bother drives help-seeking; many with nocturia never seek help. Nocturia may dominate the interview
   and mask other LUTS.
 • Any nocturia that brings a patient to urological care is, by definition, clinically significant, irrespective of
   the number of voids.

Impact of Nocturia
Sleep
 • Harm is mediated largely through sleep architecture; urge-driven awakening is the commonest cause of
   adult sleep disruption.
 • Affects sleep latency (time to fall asleep after lights out), sleep maintenance and sleep efficiency
   (percentage of time in bed spent asleep).
 • An independent predictor of insomnia and reduced sleep quality in older adults; worsens wakefulness in
   baseline insomnia. Polysomnography confirms an independent association with poor sleep across the
   whole night.
 • Harm far exceeds the minutes lost to toileting — because of timing.

Falls and fractures
 • Nocturia is among the LUTS most strongly associated with falls and hip fractures; meta-analysis shows
   increased risk of both.
 • Older adults are doubly exposed: most affected, and ageing erodes coordination, strength, vision and
   cognition — hazardous when hurrying to the toilet in the dark. Weigh fall risk in them and other high-risk
   groups.

Beyond Bother—Nocturia, Morbidity, and Mortality
 • Nocturia may be a hallmark of cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, hepatic, endocrine, neurological,
   psychiatric or immunological disease.
 • Evidence is strongest for cardiometabolic and vascular disease: hypertension, diabetes mellitus,
   hyperlipidaemia, obesity, metabolic syndrome, coronary artery disease, arrhythmias, cerebrovascular
   disease, stroke and post-stroke sequelae.
 • Mortality: in a nationally representative US sample, ≥2 voids per night was associated with worse survival
   than 0–1, particularly in those <65 years; meta-analysis shows increased mortality in both sexes.
 • Causality is not established; the link is likely mediated by falls, poor sleep and underlying comorbidity
   (falls history further raises mortality risk).

 KEY POINTS: RATIONALE FOR EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT
   • Common (highest in older adults), bothersome, the commonest cause of adult sleep disruption; hits
     deep sleep.
   • Linked to falls, fractures and systemic disease; management matters beyond bother.

Concept — Nocturia matters because of when it strikes and what it signals. The first void falls in the early,
restorative slow-wave sleep, and the symptom may be the visible edge of systemic disease. Its harm is
therefore out of proportion to the number of voids.


CAUSES OF NOCTURIA: "SLEEP CALM" AND THE BLADDER DIARY
 • Extensive differential. The relationship with poor sleep is bidirectional: sleep studies of patients with
   disordered sleep (no urinary complaint) find bathroom trips at the time of awakening.
 • Nocturia may share a pathophysiological pathway with systemic disease; lifestyle, lower urinary tract
   dysfunction (LUTD) and medications may cause or aggravate it.
 • Idiopathic nocturia is small but real. Most real-world cases are caused by one or more of the five Sleep
   CALM factors.

Bladder Diaries: Connecting Causal Factors to Clinical Symptoms
 • The Sleep CALM factors look heterogeneous, but all disrupt the balance between nocturnal urine
   production, functional bladder storage capacity and normal sleep architecture. The bladder diary — a
   guideline cornerstone — reveals this relationship objectively.
 • Urine production is continuous. Even after emptying at bedtime, volume climbs towards maximum
   capacity; when it is reached there are only two endpoints: wake to void, or enuresis.
 • So sleeping through requires that nocturnal production not exceed maximum functional capacity. A
   production–storage mismatch arises from excess volume (polyuria) and/or insufficient capacity.

 • Sleep architecture is the third arm: primary sleep disorders produce voids independent of bladder
   volume ("convenience" voids while already awake). Disordered sleep may also increase nocturnal
   production and/or lower the arousal "set-point" at which bladder volume overcomes sleep.

Nocturia Severity and Sleep
 • "Going to bed intending to sleep" and "rising intending to rise" bookend the main sleep period; voids
   between them = ANV.
 • Intent governs: the last void of the night does not end the sleep period if the patient stays in bed
   intending to sleep, even if sleep never returns. A void at or just after the end of the period is the first
   morning void (daytime frequency).
 • Diary-based ANV is a gold standard for nocturia severity. Symptom questionnaires correlate only
   modestly with diaries: respondents more often overestimate, especially with severe symptoms, while
   those with low frequency may underplay it.
 • Interview-reported severity correlates with the diary in some populations, but inconsistently, and less
   well as ANV rises; overestimation at standardised interview persists however the question is phrased.
 • FUSP is the most important sleep measure in the diary. It indexes how long the patient stays in early
   slow-wave sleep uninterrupted.
     ◦ FUSP is inversely related to the number of night-time voids.
     ◦ Prolonged FUSP marks successful treatment, with better whole-night sleep and daytime function;
       improved blood glucose has been reported after FUSP prolongation.
     ◦ Hence FUSP is an important outcome measure and treatment target.
 • Total sleep duration cuts both ways: nocturia can truncate sleep (patient gets up for the day after a late
   void), and treatment that lengthens FUSP lengthens total sleep; conversely, impaired efficiency may keep
   patients in bed longer — longer total sleep (>8 h) is more common in those with ≥2 voids. Total sleep
   time is needed for calculations but has limited clinical utility on its own.

Nocturnal Urine Production
 • NUV = all nocturnal voids + first morning void. Rationale: the untested assumption that people void
   before bed and shortly after waking, so first-morning urine was made during sleep.
 • NPi = NUV/TUV (often ×100 as a percentage); NUP = NUV/hours of main sleep period.

 • Krimpen study (landmark; community-dwelling older Dutch men): NPi >33% in 92% of men with ≥2
   voids but also 70% of men without nocturia — the reason NPi discriminates poorly.
 • No universal NP cut-off exists. Whatever the definition, excess nocturnal production contributes to most
   adult nocturia, increasingly so as severity rises.
 • Global polyuria: >40 mL/kg/24 h (e.g. >2.8 L at 70 kg); inordinate output by day as well as night. A
   relatively infrequent cause of nocturia, but its differential is rich in serious disease — neoplastic,
   traumatic, inflammatory, immunological, psychiatric disorders of water homeostasis. Always investigate
   global polyuria as a potential harbinger of disease beyond the urinary tract.

Functional Bladder Storage Capacity
 • Small functional capacity may be night-time-specific or span 24 h (mirroring NP versus global polyuria).
 • True capacity = voided volume + post-void residual (PVR); in practice NMVV (nocturnal) and 24-h MVV
   are reasonable proxies.
 • No consensus on diminished capacity: normal usually >300–400 mL; 150–300 mL recognised as abnormal
   in recent literature.

Quantifying the Relationship Between Nocturnal Urine Production and Storage
 • Urge-driven waking depends on the relationship between production and capacity, not either alone.
 • Ni = NUV/24-h MVV — production expressed as a function of the patient's own capacity. Ni >1 = NUV
   exceeds capacity; higher = greater mismatch. Ni correlates more strongly with nocturia frequency than
   production or capacity alone.
 • NBCi quantifies the gap between the patient's own maximum capacity and the volumes at which night
   voids actually occur.
       ◦ PNV = Ni − 1: the voids expected if every void occurred exactly at maximum capacity. One is
         subtracted because the last nocturnal volume leaves as the first morning void, which is not a nocturia
         episode.
       ◦ NBCi = ANV − PNV.
Worked example: NUV 800 mL, MVV 200 mL → Ni = 4 → PNV = 3 (three night voids of 200 mL, plus 200 mL
at first morning void) → diary shows 6 night voids → NBCi = 6 − 3 = 3.
 • NBCi = 0: every night void at full capacity. NBCi >0: voids occur below MVV; higher values = greater
   discordance.

 • Convenience voids (little or no urgency on the diary — woken by something other than urge) partly
   explain night-specific small capacity; they are significantly smaller than urge-driven voids.
 • But convenience voiding is uncommon in the general population and remains infrequent even in clinic
   patients with depression, anxiety or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Night-specific diminished
   capacity is a small but important entity.
 • NP, global polyuria and small capacity are not mutually exclusive.
     ◦ Classical NP: high night production offset by low day production, so 24-h volume is normal.
     ◦ Current ICS NP criteria (NPi >33% or NUP >90 mL/h) do not preclude concurrent global polyuria (>40
       mL/kg/24 h).
     ◦ A small nocturnal capacity may coexist with daytime capacity that is normal, increased, or small but
       further reduced at night.
 • Capacity has no bearing on the polyuria definitions, and small capacity is diagnosed independently of
   urine volume. Whether high diuresis alters perception of filling is undemonstrated.
 • Multiple diary abnormalities are common, and more so as nocturia frequency rises.

Bladder Diaries: Practical Considerations
 • Standard of care in all guidelines, yet under-used: completed by fewer than half of patients seen by
   urologists and about a third seen by general practitioners.
 • Adherence is the pervasive obstacle: interference with lifestyle and routine ("too busy", "took too long",
   "at work that day"). In a urogynaecology sample, unemployment and fewer children independently
   predicted completion — professional, family and social commitments make diaries cumbersome.

 KEY POINTS: CAUSES OF NOCTURIA
   • Sleep CALM factors act on production, storage and sleep; the diary quantifies them, and FUSP tracks
     sleep quality and response.
   • NP in most; global polyuria uncommon but serious; Ni is the strongest severity determinant.
   • 3–7-day diaries more reliable but less adhered to; night-only diaries unvalidated.

Concept — Every cause of nocturia works through one equation: production against capacity, gated by
sleep. The diary measures both sides, and the ratio (Ni) predicts severity better than either side alone.


EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT
 • Diaries define the precise production–capacity–sleep relationship. Sleep CALM organises the initial
   evaluation: history, comprehensive examination and basic testing in all → individually tailored
   adjunctive testing and multidisciplinary care as indicated (Table 112.2).
 • Guidelines support a validated disease-specific questionnaire at baseline and for monitoring; none is
   preferred. TANGO ("Targeting the individual's Aetiology of Nocturia to Guide Outcomes") covers the Sleep
   CALM factors and aids multidisciplinary assessment.
 • Because Sleep CALM factors cause nocturia by the same mechanisms, first-line management is patient-
   specific optimisation of every Sleep CALM abnormality found.

Sleep CALM: Sleep Disorders
 • Relationship is bidirectional: urge-driven waking damages early deep sleep; conversely obstructive sleep
   apnoea (OSA), insomnia, restless legs syndrome and parasomnias may themselves underlie nocturnal
   toileting.
 • OSA (sudden cessation of breathing from airway obstruction) is the best-described link and a common
   cause of nocturia. OSA incidence rises with nocturia frequency; severe sleep-disordered breathing →
   more nocturia; high nocturia frequency predicts severe OSA in some studies — hence calls to add
   nocturia to OSA screening questionnaires.

Melatonin and behavioural treatment of insomnia
 • Melatonin (pineal hormone regulating the sleep cycle) may be a primary treatment for some
   subpopulations, particularly older adults. Age correlates with both disordered sleep and nocturia, and
   older adults are most vulnerable to sedative-hypnotics, anticholinergics and antidiuretic therapy.
 • Evidence for melatonin is limited, with modest results in some subpopulations; defining ideal candidates
   is a pressing research need.
 • Behavioural treatment of insomnia: individualised sleep-hygiene education improved sleep in older
   adults with chronic insomnia (small randomised trial); participants with baseline nocturia also had fewer
   nocturnal voids. But nocturia dampened the sleep-quality benefit.
 • So an integrated cognitive-behavioural intervention for insomnia with nocturia has been proposed by a
   multidisciplinary sleep panel; efficacy is under investigation.
 • Know when to involve sleep experts in a multimodal plan.

From the guidelines
 • EAU Female LUTS (strong, LoE 1a): women with nocturia and a history suggestive of OSA → refer to a
   sleep clinic for CPAP assessment.
 • EAU Male LUTS panel on nocturia (grade C, LoE 2): sleep-promoting agents may be used to aid return to
   sleep.
Concept — OSA is the model for the whole chapter. One disorder raises production (ANP), lowers capacity
(detrusor instability) and fragments sleep, which is why treating the sleep disorder yields one of the largest
nocturia responses of any intervention.

Sleep CALM: Comorbidities
 • Nocturia is intertwined with cardiovascular, endocrine, renal, hepatic, neurological and immunological
   disease; nocturia management is being studied within cardiology, endocrinology, neurology and chronic
   kidney disease care.
 • These conditions cause nocturia chiefly through excess urine production. There is growing evidence that
   atherosclerosis-mediated bladder vascular damage and ischaemia reduce functional capacity. Sleep
   disorders are highly prevalent in medical disease — comorbid insomnia may be commoner than primary
   insomnia.

Volume Overload
 • By day, intravascular fluid "third-spaces" into the interstitium and collects in dependent legs; it is not
   excreted by day but mobilised when lying down, adding to NUV — even in healthy adults.
 • Exaggerated in hypervolaemic states such as congestive heart failure, of which third-spacing is cardinal.
 • Acute decompensated heart failure: more nocturia before admission ↔ more leg oedema and greater
   NUV; diuretic treatment reduced nocturia alongside oedema and NUV. Similar findings in cardiology
   outpatients with overt overload treated to best practice.

Hypertension
 • Normal circadian dip of 10–20% in blood pressure during sleep. Non-dipping hypertension occurs in up
   to 40–50% of hypertensives and independently predicts target-organ damage and adverse cardiovascular
   outcomes.
 • Non-dipping probably mediates the nocturia–hypertension link: nocturia is independently associated with
   higher night-time pressure and blunted dipping.

 • Mechanism: renin–angiotensin–aldosterone dysregulation → excess aldosterone → nocturnal

the posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis) → released to osmotic and volume stimuli (rising serum

permeability → water reabsorbed down osmotic gradients until isotonic.

Sleep CALM: Actions
 • Assess excess fluid, caffeine and alcohol, particularly in the 3–4 h before bed (Figure 112.7).
 • Epidemiology is inconsistent — large cohorts show no clear association, and some find nocturics less
   likely to take evening caffeine. This probably reflects cross-sectional design: patients self-regulate intake
   as self-treatment.
 • Interventional evidence is thin:
     ◦ Crossover trial in OAB: 25% fluid reduction reduced nocturia, urgency and daytime frequency, but the
       nocturia gain was of modest clinical significance.
     ◦ Men with 24-h output >30 mL/kg guided to <30 mL/kg: about one fewer night void, correlating with
       the fall in nocturnal volume; no adverse events.
     ◦ Bottom line: benefit varies; patients with higher baseline intake gain most.
 • Physiology is the stronger evidence: urine output tracks total intake.

Sleep CALM: Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction
 • Nocturia is often isolated, but is also a component of bladder outlet obstruction (BOO), OAB, detrusor
   underactivity, neurogenic bladder, dysfunctional voiding and the genitourinary syndrome of menopause
   (GSM). It is commoner in men with clinically significant BPH and is present in up to 85% of OAB trial
   participants.
 • Historically nocturia was just one LUTS attributed to BPH (and a core OAB feature) long before it was
   studied in its own right.
 • Isolated nocturia is not a LUTS and would not be expected to respond to lower urinary tract drugs.
   Those drugs — historically the backbone of treatment — seldom give clinically meaningful benefit, but
   remain reasonable when LUTD is justifiably suspected.

Benign Prostatic Obstruction
 • Relieving BOO should help nocturia by lowering PVR, increasing functional capacity, reducing global
   frequency and reducing afferent input from the bladder neck and prostatic urethra.
 • α-blockers: inhibit noradrenaline at prostatic smooth muscle → ↓ prostatic tone and outlet resistance.
   α1-receptors also lie in bladder, urethra and CNS, and α-blockers reduce CNS afferent activity. Available:
   alfuzosin, doxazosin, tamsulosin, terazosin, silodosin.
 • 5α-reductase inhibitors (5ARIs), dutasteride and finasteride, block formation of serum and intraprostatic
   dihydrotestosterone (DHT) → prostatic involution and ↓ volume.
 • Nocturia data come almost entirely from secondary or post hoc endpoints in BPH/LUTS trials, in which
   nocturia did not determine eligibility.

Overactive Bladder Syndrome
 • Antimuscarinics (historical cornerstone): M3 receptors mediate detrusor contraction; antagonism of post-
   junctional excitatory receptors → relaxation. Muscarinic receptors in the urothelium and afferent
   pathways may also contribute to OAB.
 • Proposed nocturia mechanisms: ↓ afferent activity (less nocturnal urgency), ↑ nocturnal capacity, longer
   inter-void interval. Agents: darifenacin, fesoterodine, oxybutynin, propiverine, solifenacin, tolterodine,
   trospium.
 • Evidence mostly secondary/post hoc; significance inconsistent. Nocturnal urgency and nocturnal voids
   are among the most refractory OAB symptoms — good daytime response does not predict night-time
   benefit.

Multicomponent Therapy
 • Most nocturia has more than one cause, and the Sleep CALM factors interact:
     ◦ Poor health and comorbidity burden are the strongest predictors of sleep disturbance; many chronic-
       disease drugs cause insomnia.
     ◦ LUTS severity is linked to cardiovascular risk, inflammation and systemic disease.
     ◦ Older age brings more disease, adverse sleep changes and polypharmacy.
 • A multifactorial cause needs a multimodal plan — the basis of the EAU Male LUTS "Shared Care
   Pathway", which addresses sleep, medical and lower urinary tract factors concurrently.
 • Small trials show benefit from multicomponent, aetiology-directed treatment; an individualised plan
   addressing several Sleep CALM factors in older male US veterans improved frequency, bother and sleep
   outcomes.

 KEY POINTS: EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT
   • Optimise every Sleep CALM factor; sleep is bidirectional — CPAP, melatonin, insomnia therapy may
     help.
   • Head-to-toe work-up: nocturia may be the sole sign of disease, and treating disease helps nocturia.
   • Evening fluid, alcohol, caffeine, sodium restriction: variable evidence, low risk.
   • LUTD drugs: modest benefit; β3 vs antimuscarinic unknown; oestrogen in GSM.
   • Reconcile medications; multicomponent treatment is likely additive.

Concept — Sleep CALM is a diagnostic checklist and a treatment plan at once. Each factor causes nocturia
through production, capacity or sleep, so identifying it is already the start of treating it. Because the factors
coexist, they are treated together, not in turn.

Refractory Nocturia: A Stepwise Approach
Figure 112.8 — Sleep CALM diagnostic algorithm (Monaghan et al., 2023).
History + physical examination + individualised ancillary testing → identify modifiable Sleep CALM factors →
optimise Sleep CALM → conservative management → pharmacotherapy in carefully selected patients —
with the voiding diary running alongside every step.
 • Even refractory nocturia starts conservatively: lifestyle measures have variable evidence but minimal
   risk. Drugs are for nocturia refractory to Sleep CALM optimisation and conservative management.
 • In the USA, bedtime antidiuretic replacement is the only drug specifically indicated for nocturia — for
   ≥2 voids per night due to NP. Guidelines also discuss timed diuretics (e.g. early afternoon), which shift
   urine output into the day and reduce NUV.
 • Why target nocturnal volume even when capacity is small:
     ◦ Production and capacity both determine the mismatch, but excess nocturnal production is the
       stronger correlate of frequency and of treatment response.
     ◦ NP patients benefit most; but even patients without NP respond to volume-reducing treatment.
     ◦ In patients with impaired nocturnal capacity, production-lowering therapy helps them void closer to
       capacity. In diary-confirmed small capacity, improvement came with falling NUV and unchanged
       capacity.

     ◦ Production-lowering therapy improves nocturia in BPH and OAB, as monotherapy or add-on.
 • EAU Male LUTS panel on nocturia (grade A, LoE 3): discuss lifestyle changes to reduce nocturnal volume
   and episodes and improve sleep.

Antidiuretic Replacement Therapy
 • Endogenous AVP normally rises during sleep, conserving water, lowering NUV and counteracting bladder
   filling. A blunted nocturnal AVP rise occurs in some NP samples — leading some to regard NP as partial
   central DI.
 • AVP is technically difficult to measure, and its role may differ between populations; but synthetic
   antidiuretics predictably ↑ water permeability and reabsorption, dilute extracellular fluid and
   concentrate urine.
 • Bedtime antidiuretic → ↓ NUV: a rational option for refractory nocturia without other identifiable
   contributory factors.
 • Desmopressin (1-deamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin, DDAVP): selective V2 agonist — antidiuretic action
   of AVP without pressor activity. Used since the 1980s; approved for nocturia in >80 countries.
 • US-approved low-dose formulations for NP with ≥2 voids per night: desmopressin acetate nasal spray
   (DANS) and orally disintegrating tablets (ODT). They offer greater bioavailability, faster onset and a
   better efficacy–side-effect balance, notably for hyponatraemia — the most clinically significant side
   effect.
 • Antidiuretic therapy is supported by level 1b evidence from multiple phase III placebo-controlled
   randomised controlled trials (RCTs) (Table 112.7).

 • Hyponatraemia risk is most strongly associated with older age, particularly with low baseline sodium,
   and is dose-dependent. It was seen mainly with earlier high-dose oral tablets in older patients (sodium
   <130 mmol/L in up to about 5%). Risk with DANS or ODT is lower.
 • Low-dose rationale: the median effective dose (therapeutic response in 50%) is far lower than the dose
   for maximal antidiuresis. Low doses still achieve 33–50% improvement in many patients. Supra-threshold
   plasma levels prolong antidiuresis, so low doses also avoid effects on fluid handling beyond the sleep
   period.

 • Population studies link diuretic use and nocturia. Furosemide, the commonest US diuretic, increases

 • Taken in the hours before sleep, furosemide causes NP; taken >6 h before bed, it does not raise NUV. So

   • Desmopressin: the only US-approved nocturia drug — DANS/ODT for ≥2 voids with NP, lower
     hyponatraemia risk than old tablets.
   • Women more sensitive (sex-specific ODT); hyponatraemia is age- and dose-dependent, mostly within

Concept — Refractory nocturia is treated by moving urine production out of the night. Desmopressin
suppresses night-time water diuresis; timed diuretics finish the sodium diuresis before bed. Either way the
price is sodium balance, so dose, age and monitoring define safe use.

CONSIDERATIONS

Desmopressin Acetate Nasal Spray
 • Approval rested on two phase III placebo RCTs (pooled): adults ≥50 years reporting ≥2 episodes and
   confirmed ≥2.16 nightly voids over 2-week screening; 1.66 or 0.83 mcg/0.1 mL (1.11 mcg in one study
   only) vs placebo, 12 weeks.
 • Co-primary endpoints: change in frequency; ≥50% responder rate. Both doses beat placebo on both (see
   Table 112.7). Sodium checked every 2 weeks.
 • Hyponatraemia (≤125 mmol/L, or <130 with symptoms): 0% at 0.83 mcg, 1.1% at 1.66 mcg, 0.2%
   placebo.

Desmopressin Orally Disintegrating Tablets
 • Women are more sensitive to DDAVP — greater effect on NUV and longer action.
     ◦ Animal studies: greater renal V2 expression and response in females.
     ◦ Human physiology: men show greater osmotic sensitivity of AVP release but lower renal sensitivity to
       AVP.
     ◦ Possible basis: the V2 receptor gene is on the X chromosome (differential expression with X-
       inactivation); sex hormones may modulate.
 • Consequence: women do as well on a lower dose and are at risk of hyponatraemia at higher doses.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS
 • Patient-centred diagnosis: user-friendly electronic bladder diaries, remote visits, comorbidity-driven
   questionnaires for multidisciplinary care.
 • Single-void nocturnal urine samples (volume, osmolality) collected at the first night void and analysed on
   standard laboratory equipment — a possible alternative way to diagnose NP.
 • Point-of-care biomarkers: urinary AVP in early-morning urine as a potential NP marker, especially with
   impaired circadian AVP rhythm; plasma copeptin predicts DDAVP response in children with NP-related
   enuresis — possibly relevant to adults.
 • Nocturnal urine composition is heterogeneous: patients differ in the balance of water and sodium
   diuresis — the basis for individualised treatment.
