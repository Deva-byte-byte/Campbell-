Chapter 2 — Evaluation of the Urologic Patient: History
and Physical Examination
 • History and examination come first; laboratory and imaging tests are chosen on their findings to narrow
   the differential.
 • A reproducible, organised system identifies cause, contributing factors and information relevant to
   treatment, and builds the rapport needed for later counselling.
 • Diagnostic confirmation bias: relying on prior records, "chart lore", aberrant laboratory values or
   incidentalomas without the patient's input misdirects evaluation — e.g. a haematuria referral whose
   penile tumour is found only on examination.
Concept — The history and examination are the primary diagnostic test; prior data inform them but never
replace them.

HISTORY
 • Foundation of evaluation: a good history often gives the diagnosis or directs to it.
Setting
 • Warm, non-threatening environment with easy intake — frustration with parking or front-office staff
   agitates the patient before the encounter.
 • Gender-sensitivity training for all staff: preferred name and pronouns.
 • Review vital signs and records before entering; knock and introduce yourself.
 • Face-to-face layout, computer to the side, so the patient does not feel secondary to it.
 • Non-verbal communication conveys most emotion and affect; eye contact and posture alter disclosure,
   and sitting rather than standing improves satisfaction and outcomes and reduces litigation.
Comprehension
 • Plain language; health literacy is often misjudged across a language barrier, and patients may decline
   interpreters out of embarrassment.
 • Teach-back method — the patient restates the information in their own words — confirms and reinforces
   understanding.
Companions
 • A companion supplies history the patient lacks and, since patients stop listening effectively after difficult
   news, can take notes and relay information once the shock passes; also aids recall of long risk–benefit
   discussions.
Components
 • Complete history: chief complaint, history of present illness, past medical and surgical history, allergies,
   social and family history, review of systems.
 • Direct, focused questioning: let the patient voice concerns, then steer toward what diagnosis and safe
   treatment require.

Chief Complaint
 • Why the patient came, and the urologist's primary focus.
 • Address it even when a more serious problem is found: a patient with frequency and an incidental renal
   mass regards care that treats only the mass as ineffective.
 • Start the differential from the complaint; narrow it with the history of present illness.

History of Present Illness
 • Timing, severity, nature, and exacerbating and relieving factors of the chief complaint; an efficient
   history of present illness (HPI) narrows the differential diagnosis.
Pain
 • Location, nature, onset, duration, prior episodes; the patient points with one finger to the maximal site.
 • Stone-naïve patients misattribute renal colic to gut or musculoskeletal causes; previous stone formers
   recognise it and present promptly.
 • Pain versus tenderness: pain is reported; tenderness is elicited by palpation. Pain without tenderness
   suggests referred pain — testicular pain with a non-tender testis may come from an ipsilateral obstructing
   ureteric stone rather than orchitis.
 • Severity: mild–moderate–severe or 0–10; the Wong–Baker FACES scale (1988) uses faces of increasing
   distress; record before and after intervention.

 • Shared innervation explains associated features: the coeliac plexus supplies both foregut and kidneys, so
   renal irritation causes paroxysmal nausea and vomiting; the ureter shares its nerve supply with the
   ipsilateral testis or labium, so ureteric pain is referred there.
 • Pain from tumour infiltration of periparenchymal nerves is rare and indicates advanced disease.
Acute pain
 • Clear cause: obstruction or inflammation → prostaglandins and chemokines → nerve stimulation →
   central nervous system, with amplification or damping en route.
 • Analgesia acts at either end: block the mediator (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs] inhibit
   prostaglandin synthesis) or act on central opioid and other receptors.
Renal pain
 • Ipsilateral costovertebral angle, just lateral to the spine and below the 12th rib.
 • Collecting-system obstruction → colicky pain; parenchymal inflammation or infection → flank pain with
   costovertebral angle tenderness (CVAT).
 • Radiates anteriorly across the flank toward the midline, or down to the ipsilateral scrotum or labium.

Ureteric pain
 • Acute obstruction: immediate onset, ipsilateral lower quadrant.
 • Mechanism: acute distension and hyperperistalsis → prostaglandin accumulation → ureteric spasm and
   lactic acid production → irritation of A-delta and C fibres → T11–L1 dorsal root ganglia → pain perceived
   in those segments.
 • Gradual or partial obstruction may be painless.
 • Referred to the ipsilateral scrotum or penis; ureterovesical junction obstruction causes irritative
   symptoms — urgency and frequency with small voids.
Vesical pain
 • Suprapubic pain from inflammation (cystitis, interstitial cystitis) or distension (obstruction, detrusor
   failure).
 • Inflammatory pain worst when full, eased by voiding.
 • Strangury: stabbing pain at the end of micturition from final contraction of an inflamed detrusor.
 • Acute retention in a sensate bladder is a history diagnosis — intense desire, inability to void. An atonic
   bladder retains large volumes painlessly, as chronic ureteric obstruction is painless.
Prostatic pain
 • Deep pelvic, poorly localised, mistaken for rectal pain; with irritative symptoms; acute or chronic,
   inflammatory and/or infective.
Penile pain
 • Flaccid penis: balanitis, paraphimosis, ulcerative lesions (carcinoma, herpes), or pain referred from cystitis
   or prostatitis. Erect penis: Peyronie disease or priapism.
Scrotal pain
 • Skin causes (e.g. an inflamed pustule from an ingrown hair) versus testis and cord.
 • Epididymitis and orchitis: pain may be relieved by testicular elevation.
 • Torsion of the testis or its appendages → acute vascular congestion; testicular torsion is a surgical
   emergency.
 • Varicocele: dull ache worse at day's end from venous congestion; eased by elevation and lying supine.
 • Renal and ureteric pain refers to the ipsilateral scrotum (shared embryology and nerve pathways).
Chronic pain
 • Central sensitisation (signal "imprinting"): pain without noxious stimulus — chronic pelvic pain,
   fibromyalgia. No urological cause after thorough evaluation → pain specialist.
Concept — Pain character localises pathology: colic means a distended hollow viscus, constant pain a
stretched capsule, and pain without tenderness referral along a shared nerve supply (T11–L1, coeliac
plexus).
Opioid Abuse Considerations
 • United States opioid epidemic: a national public health emergency was declared on 26 October 2017.
 • Diversion: exchanging prescribed controlled substances for money or illicit drugs.
 • Physicians and manufacturers implicated: synthetic opioids falsely promoted as "safer"; complacent
   overprescribing.
 • Treat the painful condition promptly; prefer non-opioid analgesia — NSAIDs, physical therapy,
   neuromodulation, acupuncture.
 • Pain without a urological cause → no opioids; refer to primary care or pain management.
 • Opioid adverse effects: euphoria, constipation or ileus, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, tolerance, physical
   dependence, respiratory depression.

 • Drug-seeking signs: unusual home locations; inconsistent behaviour (walks easily in the corridor, struggles
   in the room); poor follow-up; disinterest in non-opioid analgesia; brand-name requests; discrepant or
   rehearsed answers.
Haematuria
 • Up to a quarter of patients presenting with haematuria have a urological cancer, so it must always be
   evaluated; it is gross or microscopic.
Gross haematuria
 • Malignancy risk factors to elicit: age, irritative voiding symptoms, smoking, industrial chemical exposure;
   also alkylating chemotherapy, analgesic abuse, chronic foreign bodies in the urinary tract and family
   history of malignancy.
 • First exclude urinary tract infection (UTI), nephrological disease, trauma and recent instrumentation; then
   full evaluation per American Urological Association (AUA) guidelines. Age is a risk factor, but
   genitourinary (GU) malignancy occurs in children.
 • Commonest cause of gross haematuria over 50 years: bladder cancer.
 • Pseudohaematuria (dehydration, drugs, foods): differentiate by microscopy of a clean midstream
   specimen for red blood cells (RBCs); dipstick alone is insufficient — other solutes read positive, and
   evaluating dipstick-only "haematuria" is costly and low-yield.

 • Usually painless; pain arises when clots obstruct the upper tract (colic) or cause retention.
 • Clot shape: vermiform → upper tract; cuboid → bladder.
 • Response to a catheter: clears with bladder irrigation but recurs → upper-tract source; clears once a
   catheter is placed → prostatic source.
Microscopic haematuria
 • AUA definition: ≥3 RBCs per high-power field on microscopy of a properly collected midstream
   specimen; a positive dipstick is confirmed by microscopy.
 • Patients on anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy are evaluated in the same way as those who are not.
 • Treat benign causes (gynaecological, UTI), then repeat urinalysis for persistence; likely intrinsic renal
   disease → nephrology.

Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms
 • Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS): symptoms of the bladder and its outlet, from any combination of
   obstructive and irritative causes.
 • Consider a hypocontractile or acontractile bladder (e.g. diabetes). Bladder outlet obstruction (BOO)
   causes a spectrum from raised residual to complete retention.
 • No single post-void residual (PVR) volume defines retention; judge the PVR trend together with bother,
   bladder stones, haematuria, infection and renal injury.
 • Slow progression (e.g. benign prostatic hyperplasia [BPH]) and neuropathy make patients under-
   appreciate obstruction despite objectively weak flow and high residual. Chronic BOO itself causes
   detrusor irritability.
 • Bladder diary: timed intake and output volumes, with urgency and leakage episodes; usually 48 hours;
   gives functional capacity (normal adult 300–400 mL) and quantifies nocturia.
Irritative symptoms
  • Frequency, urgency and dysuria; causes besides chronic BOO: overactive bladder, cystitis, prostatitis,
    bladder stones, bladder cancer.
 • Frequency: more than five or six voids a day; the diary separates incomplete emptying, overactive
   bladder and polyuria (polydipsia, diabetes mellitus [DM], diabetes insipidus).
 • Urgency: difficulty postponing micturition — normal only after prolonged holding. Dysuria: painful voiding
   from bladder inflammation, felt along the urethra or at the meatus.
 • Irritative symptoms may be the only sign of occult bladder cancer, particularly carcinoma in situ (CIS) —
   especially over 50 years and in smokers → low threshold for cystoscopy.
 • Nocturia: number of times the patient wakes to void. Daytime frequency without nocturia →
   psychogenic (e.g. anxiety). Nocturia without daytime frequency → nocturnal polyuria: evening fluid
   intake; mobilisation of leg oedema when recumbent; age-related loss of concentrating ability with higher
   nocturnal renal blood flow.
Obstructive symptoms
 • Causes — anything narrowing the urethra: BPH, prostate cancer, urethral stricture, dysfunctional voiding,
   detrusor–external sphincter dyssynergia, severe phimosis or meatal stenosis.
 • Complete obstruction → acute retention; partial obstruction → obstructive LUTS: incomplete emptying,
   frequency (more often than 2-hourly), intermittency, weak stream, straining.
 • Weak detrusor + minimal obstruction can still cause obstructive LUTS or retention; a weak stream must
   be assigned to outlet or detrusor.
 • Hesitancy: flow normally starts about a second after sphincter relaxation; delayed in BOO. Intermittency:
   stop–start stream, distinct from double voiding.
 • Post-void dribbling: urethral urine not "milked back" into the bladder; often an early BPH symptom.
 • Straining: Valsalva or manual pressure (Credé voiding) — temporising until definitive treatment.
AUA Symptom Score and quality-of-life score
 • AUA Symptom Score (1992): seven symptoms over the past month, each 0–5 (Table 2.1). International
   Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) = AUA score + a quality-of-life (QoL) question.
 • Total 0–35: mild 0–7, moderate 8–19, severe 20–35.
 • Limitations: non-specific; sixth-grade reading level; unsuitable for some neurological patients. Value:
   standardised comparison before and after intervention.

 • Stress versus urge treatment: SUI → increase outlet resistance (Kegel pelvic floor exercises, urethral

Concept — Every incontinence type is a pressure–resistance imbalance or a bypass: stress lowers resistance,
urge and overflow raise bladder pressure, continuous bypasses the outlet, and functional incontinence has
neither — it is a problem of access.
Sexual Dysfunction
 • Common in women with LUTS; in men "impotence" is often a symptom of another problem.
Erectile dysfunction
 • Definition (AUA; National Institutes of Health [NIH] Consensus Conference on Impotence, 1993):
   inability to attain and/or maintain penile erection sufficient for satisfactory intercourse.
 • May affect more than half of men over 40. History identifies the type — vasculogenic, neurogenic,
   psychogenic, endocrinological, drug-induced — guiding testing and treatment.
 • Poorly controlled DM → neuropathy and/or vasculopathy (endocrinology referral); diabetic men are
   more often hypogonadal, reducing phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE5) inhibitor efficacy.
 • Drugs: antihypertensives, antidepressants, antipsychotics, testosterone-lowering drugs.
 • Psychogenic clues: situational erectile dysfunction (ED) — erections with one partner but not another, or
   with visual or manual stimulation — and preserved nocturnal tumescence.
 • ED can herald occult coronary artery disease (CAD) → cardiology consultation.
 • Questionnaires: International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) and the abbreviated five-item IIEF-5, the
   Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM) — erectile and orgasmic function, desire, intercourse and overall
   satisfaction.
Loss of libido
 • Multifactorial — onset, timing, situational nature. With ED, suspect hypogonadism (primary or
   secondary): early-morning serum testosterone on two separate occasions, at least 2 weeks apart.
 • Hypogonadal symptoms: altered sleep, emotional change, reduced strength, weight gain, infertility, ED.
 • Other causes: depression, medications, severe illness (e.g. cancer).
 • Questionnaires: Androgen Deficiency in the Aging Male (ADAM), Aging Male Survey, Massachusetts Male
   Aging Study (MMAS) questionnaire — these correlate poorly with serum testosterone; the
   Hypogonadism-Related Symptom Scale has better sensitivity and specificity against serum testosterone.

 • First-line: topical anaesthetics, the stop–start method and sexual counselling; alternatively a selective
   serotonin reuptake inhibitor, on demand or daily.
Loss of ejaculation
 • Low volume or anejaculation: androgen deficiency, sympathetic denervation, drugs, retroperitoneal,
   bladder neck or prostate surgery, midline prostatic or seminal vesicle cyst.
Absence of orgasm
 • Orgasm: euphoria with genital muscular contractions and endorphin release; usually accompanies
   ejaculation but can occur without erection or ejaculation (e.g. after prostatectomy).
 • Causes: psychogenic, psychiatric medications, impaired pudendal nerve function (e.g. diabetic
   neuropathy).
 • Management: neurological evaluation (vibratory testing of penile sensation), hormonal treatment and/or
   sexual counselling.
Concept — Sexual complaints are symptoms, not diagnoses: the history sorts erectile, libido, ejaculatory and
orgasmic failure into vascular, neural, hormonal, drug and psychogenic causes — and ED additionally flags
cardiovascular risk.
Haematospermia
 • Blood in the ejaculate; almost always non-specific inflammation of the prostate or seminal vesicles,
   resolving spontaneously; follows prolonged abstinence or prostate biopsy.
 • Persistent beyond several weeks → genital examination, digital rectal examination (DRE), prostate-
   specific antigen (PSA), cystoscopy and urine cytology to exclude GU tuberculosis or cancer.
Pneumaturia
 • Gas in the urine, interrupting flow like urethral flatus.
 • Commonest cause: fistula between bowel and bladder → screen for diverticular disease, Crohn disease,
   enteritis, and recent abdominal surgery or radiation.
 • Rarely, gas-forming organisms → ask about severe UTIs and immunocompromise; urinalysis and culture.
Urethral Discharge
 • Commonest symptom of sexually transmitted infection (STI); screen for high-risk sexual behaviour.
   Bloody discharge → consider urethral carcinoma.
Fevers and Chills
 • Subjective or measured, and by which route (oral, rectal, auricular, temporal) — accuracy against core
   temperature matters in children. Rigors, even without fever, suggest bacteraemia.
 • Cystitis rarely causes fever; pyelonephritis usually requires fever for the diagnosis.
 • Fever with chills may signal sepsis → evaluation, possibly admission; worse in the elderly and
   immunocompromised.
 • Constitutional ("B") symptoms — fever, chills, night sweats, anorexia, weight loss, fatigue, lethargy — a
   term borrowed from lymphoma staging; suggest advanced inflammatory, infective or malignant disease
   (GU tuberculosis, advanced bladder cancer).
Emotional Distress
 • Establish bother: a patient with nocturia three times nightly who is not bothered, and has no sequelae of
   obstruction (renal injury, recurrent UTI, bladder stones), may be monitored rather than treated — best
   captured by the IPSS QoL score.
 • Conversely, a patient distressed by benign haematospermia may choose full evaluation over reassurance.

Concept — In benign conditions bother, not the symptom itself, decides treatment — unless objective
sequelae (renal injury, infection, stones) override it.

Medical History
 • Know each comorbidity's severity and treatment compliance, not just its name — in DM, control, insulin
   use and neuropathy; ask whether a recent glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) is available.
 • Sources: family, primary care notes, the medication list — though drugs have several indications
   (hydrochlorothiazide for hypercalciuria or hypertension) and adherence varies.
 • Seek undiagnosed disease with treatment implications: occult CAD (no "heart attack", but exertional
   dyspnoea or chest pain on prompting); obstructive sleep apnoea (large collar size; spouse reports
   snoring, irregular night breathing, daytime fatigue).

Performance Status
 • Functional ability reflects fitness for surgery or chemotherapy.
 • Activities of daily living (ADLs): dressing, eating, ambulating, toileting, hygiene. Instrumental ADLs:
   housekeeping, managing money, preparing meals, shopping, community participation.
 • ADL deficiency predicted complications after percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) better than the
   Charlson Comorbidity Index or the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score.
 • Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) and Karnofsky scales (mainly oncological) grade strenuous
   and non-strenuous activity, self-care, time out of bed and moribund status; they convey overall health to
   other providers.

Past Surgical History
 • Prior surgery → adhesions and obliterated planes.
 • Extensive prior abdominal surgery → Hasson (open) or Visiport (optical) access rather than Veress
   needle for laparoscopy.
 • Read the operative note, not just its title: a recorded "TURP" may have been a channel TURP only, so a
   further TURP may help; notes also reveal anatomy and difficulties encountered.

Medications
 • Reveals disease severity, drug-induced symptoms and perioperative targets.
Medications with urological side effects
 • Relate the timing of drug starts and stops to symptom onset.
 • Common culprits: antihistamines, anticoagulants, antiplatelets, antidepressants, anxiolytics.
 • Examples: several diabetic drugs and high-dose insulin imply severe DM and probable neuropathy;
   tamsulosin → anejaculation, light-headedness; haematuria on antithrombotics → temporary cessation
   plus full evaluation.
 • Know the indication: low-dose aspirin for primary CAD prophylaxis versus prevention of coronary stent
   thrombosis determines whether and when it may be stopped.

Allergies
 • Ask at the first visit; verify the list at every visit.
Medication allergies
 • Record drug and reaction; patients confuse allergy with side effects (vancomycin red man syndrome,
   antibiotic dyspepsia). Flag true allergies in the electronic medical record (EMR).
Intravenous contrast allergies
  • Intravenous contrast is often essential for CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), except for stones
    and a few other indications.
 • Contrast-allergic patients: choose ultrasound or MRI; if CT is essential, premedicate with
   diphenhydramine and a corticosteroid. Shellfish or "iodine" allergy is not a specific cross-reactivity with
   iodinated contrast.
 • Endourology requires intraluminal contrast to outline anatomy and instruments.
 • Retrograde and antegrade pyelography with iodinated contrast is safe in patients with known iodine,
   shellfish or contrast allergy, including prior anaphylaxis, with or without prophylaxis (retrospective
   cohort).

Social History
Tobacco
 • Chewed, smoked or vaped; agent and method both matter. Cessation support, including nicotine
   replacement, is appropriate for chewers too; nicotine derivatives themselves promote malignant change
   in urothelial and lung cell lines, of unknown long-term clinical significance.
Alcohol
 • Raises cancer risk and liver disease (wound healing); affects LUTS and sexual function.
 • Admitted heavy drinkers risk life-threatening withdrawal without prophylaxis — Clinical Institute
   Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol (CIWA) protocol or alcohol replacement; they understate intake, so
   anticipate.

Recreational drug use
 • Marijuana, cocaine, opioids, methamphetamines, anabolic steroids; the agent and the route predict
   challenges in care, and intravenous use carries communicable-disease risk.
 • Stigma causes under-reporting → build trust, or review prior records.
Sexual relations
 • Do not presume a monogamous, heterosexual relationship; ask non-accusatory questions ("men, women
   or both? one partner or several?") to assess STI risk and social support.
Domestic situation
 • Household and nearby support plan care; little support → poor course after complex surgery; reveals
   unacceptable options (a sole carer may refuse long convalescence). Screen for domestic violence.
Occupation
 • Worldview, socioeconomic status and industrial exposure to carcinogens.

Family History
 • Defined inheritance: autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, tuberous sclerosis, von Hippel–
   Lindau disease, renal tubular acidosis, cystinuria.
 • Familial clustering without a defined pattern: prostate cancer, urolithiasis.
 • A relative's age at diagnosis can set screening age.
 • Prostate cancer risk rises with an affected brother, and further with an affected father and brother
   (large Swedish population study) — the number of affected relatives matters, not only presence.

Review of Systems
 • Comprehensive system-based checklist; identifies important problems unrelated to the chief complaint.

   • Thorough characterisation of symptoms may reveal causes and treatment plans.
   • An accurate medical history is critical: comorbidities, medications and surgical history may have
     urological implications.
   • Performance status, assessed by ADLs, helps counsel patients on surgical risk.


PHYSICAL EXAMINATION
 • A complete examination often selects the most appropriate next step.
 • Offer gender-specific chaperones on request, given the sensitive nature of GU examination.

Vital Signs
 • Temperature, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate and pain score identify the critically ill; out-of-
   range values → immediate physician triage (all staff have a role); profound hypotension or tachycardia →
   emergency department.
 • Distress screening as a "sixth vital sign", with prompt social work, mental health or pastoral support,
   reduces waiting-room anxiety.

General Observations
 • Four modes of examination: inspection, auscultation, percussion, palpation; inspection begins at first
   contact.
 • Observe distress, nutrition, dress, strength, mobility, skin, dentition, and apparent versus stated age.

Markers of Frailty and Nutritional Status
 • Frailty (American Geriatrics Society): excess vulnerability to stressors, with reduced ability to maintain or
   regain homeostasis after a destabilising event.
 • Office tests: gait speed, get-up-and-go test, grip strength; calf circumference is inversely related to
   frailty.
 • Dentition reflects health; moist mucosa, hydration. Obese or deformed patients: rehearse the operative
   position in clinic.
 • Poor nutrition: unintended weight loss (wound-healing risk), chronic wounds, temporal wasting; low
   albumin, transferrin, prealbumin predict poor surgical outcomes.
General Examination (Neurologic, Cardiovascular, Pulmonary, Abdominal, Dermatologic)
 • Sets a baseline. Neurological: mental status, cranial nerves, motor (bulk, tone, coordination, gait),
   sensory — especially with pain. Cardiorespiratory: murmurs, heart failure, pulses, oedema.
 • Abdominal: four quadrants; scars are clues in poor historians; examine the painful area last; light then
   deep palpation for masses, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly; peritonitis (rigidity, guarding, rebound
   tenderness) → urgent transfer to the emergency department.
 • Skin: rashes, lesions, chronic wounds → infection, malnutrition, occasionally malignancy.

Urologic-Specific Considerations
Renal
 • Retroperitoneal, bounded by psoas, diaphragm, oblique muscles and peritoneum; normally not visible
   and difficult to palpate, except very large masses in small children or very thin adults.
 • The right kidney lies lower than the left (liver); its lower pole may be felt on deep inspiration.
 • Inspect flank skin for superficial causes of "flank pain"; test pain (pin or spoke-wheel), temperature (cool
   object), light touch (brush); herpes zoster gives prodromal hyperaesthesia before vesicles.
 • Bimanual palpation (renal ballottement, Fig. 2.1): non-examining hand posteriorly at the costovertebral
   angle → examining hand through the anterior abdominal wall → deep inspiration displaces the kidney
   downward.
 • Neonates and infants: thumb anteriorly on the abdomen, four fingers posteriorly at the costovertebral
   angle; large flank masses in neonates are usually renal.
 • Transillumination: fluid (cyst, hydronephrosis) → dull red glow; solid (tumour, polycystic kidney) → no
   glow.
 • Renal bruit (renal artery stenosis, large arteriovenous fistula) is theoretically audible but non-specific →
   Doppler ultrasound or angiography.
 • Renal "percussion" tests for pain with the base of a closed fist at the costovertebral angle; be gentle — a
   light tap elicits a positive sign.

Bladder
 • Palpable above the pubis only when it holds more than about 150 mL; visible as a lower midline mass in
   thin patients at about 500 mL.
 • Percuss from the symphysis toward the umbilicus: note dull changes to resonant above the bladder.
 • Ballottement aids palpation.
 • Bimanual examination (Figs. 2.2 and 2.3) assesses bladder mobility and is the standard of care after
   resection of large bladder tumours; even in the CT and MRI era it improves prediction of pT3 disease
   and is an independent predictor of pT3 on multivariable analysis.
Prostate / Digital Rectal Examination
 • Indications: size, prostatitis, cancer screening; bimanual under anaesthesia after TURBT; gentle DRE in
   neonates with differences of sex development for Müllerian structures (uterus).
 • Screening of average-risk men: the chapter cites offering screening at 55–69 years every 2 years (AUA,
   2013); the current AUA/Society of Urologic Oncology guideline (2023) advises a baseline PSA at 45–50
   years and regular screening at 50–69 years every 2–4 years.
 • Technique: patient standing, feet shoulder-width apart, bent about 90 degrees at the waist, hands or
   elbows on the table — or lateral decubitus with hips and knees flexed → reassure throughout → non-
   dominant hand spreads the gluteal folds; inspect the anus for haemorrhoids and lesions → lubricated
   gloved finger advanced gently, non-dominant hand on the anterior thigh or lower abdomen for counter-
   traction → advance until the prostate is palpable → provide cleansing supplies and privacy.
 • Normal prostate: chestnut-sized, rubbery, like the contracted thenar eminence. Nodular firmness, like a
   flexed knuckle → biopsy.
Perineum
 • Anus to scrotal base; access to the posterior urethra and transperineal biopsy. Perineal pain may be
   prostatitis; inspect for infection and fistula; tenderness may mean a tight pelvic floor.
Genitourinary Examination
Phallus
 • Inspect hair distribution, skin lesions, nodules, masses and foreskin; record Tanner stage in children.
 • Lesions: superficial vesicles → herpes simplex; ulcers → other sexually transmitted diseases or penile
   squamous cell carcinoma; warts (condylomata acuminata) → human papillomavirus (HPV). Prominent
   superficial veins are usually normal.
 • Retract the foreskin: most penile tumours involve the prepuce or glans.
 • Meatus: position, stenosis and discharge (characterise and sample if STI is suspected). Hypospadias
   (ventral ectopic meatus): record site and calibre (e.g. megameatus). Epispadias (dorsal meatus): rare,
   usually with major anomalies such as bladder exstrophy.
 • Palpation: dense subcutaneous plaques on the underlying fascia → Peyronie disease (with a history of
   curvature on erection); a tender urethra → periurethritis; spongiofibrosis is palpable only when severe
   (relevant to urethral reconstruction).
Scrotum and testes
 • Scrotal skin: infections common (tinea cruris, cellulitis, pustules) — erythema and tenderness, or
   fluctuance and pus in an abscess; malignancy rare (squamous cell carcinoma, classically in chimney
   sweeps).
 • Neglected or immunocompromised → Fournier gangrene: necrotic black skin, foul odour, "dishwater"
   discharge, pain out of proportion to examination, crepitus.

Female Genitourinary Examination
Pelvic examination
 • Indications: pelvic organ prolapse, urinary incontinence, dyspareunia, blood per urethra or vagina,
   anterior vaginal masses.
 • Sequence: undress from the waist, gown → stirrups flex and abduct the thighs and flex the knees →
   chaperone offered (for all pelvic examinations, male or female) → inspect genitalia and introitus (atrophy,
   erosions, ulcers, discharge, warts) → separate labia; inspect urethra (prolapse, caruncle, hyperplasia,
   cysts) → with a full bladder, Valsalva to elicit SUI → half-speculum shows one vaginal wall; Valsalva shows
   prolapse of bladder, apex or rectum → palpate urethra (mass or expressed discharge → urethral
   diverticulum) → bimanual: two fingers of the dominant hand in the vagina (one if the introitus is small),
   non-dominant hand over the lower abdomen.
 • Performed on clinical suspicion, not for screening (screening → gynaecologist); children, adolescents
   and young women rarely need a urological pelvic examination.
Urethral mobility
 • Lack of urethral support, especially at the mid-urethra, is the key factor in SUI.
 • Q-tip (cotton-swab) test: dorsal lithotomy → lubricated swab gently to the bladder neck → Valsalva →
   deflection of 30 degrees or more from the horizontal indicates urethral hypermobility.
 • A positive Q-tip test is not required to diagnose SUI.
Prolapse
 • Symptoms range from none to pelvic or back pressure, pain, a bulge, change in SUI, and difficulty voiding
   or defecating.
 • Sequence: dorsal lithotomy → cough or Valsalva: note leakage and obvious prolapse → palpate levator ani
   and obturator internus for pain and voluntary recruitment (Fig. 2.5, after Meister et al., 2019) → speculum

Paediatrics
 • Infants to young adults (sometimes up to 26 years). Toddlers: history from parents; adolescents:

Elderly
 • Carer input valuable, privacy preserved; low tables, Hoyer lifts, blankets and pillows.

 • Privacy and confidentiality are critical to the physician–patient relationship.

TELEMEDICINE OPPORTUNITIES AND LIMITATIONS
Improved Access to Care
 • Pre-pandemic barriers: reimbursement, regulation, resistance, technology; COVID-19 distancing
   accelerated adoption — urologists' telehealth use rose sharply 2019–2020 (AUA Census).
 • Benefits: underserved and low-socioeconomic patients; shorter waits, less travel, shorter visits; proxies
   join remotely; sustained satisfaction.

Limitations
 • No examination or in-person tests (vital signs, cystoscopy, PVR); needs good connectivity and a HIPAA-
   compliant EMR platform simple enough for non-technical patients.
 • No assistant pre-gathers results or questionnaires; convenience increases no-shows and late
   cancellations.
 • Billing: Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) waivers expired with the end of the federal
   COVID-19 public health emergency; continued insurer reimbursement is required.
 • Licensure is state-based: physicians usually need a licence in the patient's state of residence; pandemic
   relaxations lapsed, though some states added telehealth licences or exemptions; the Interstate Medical
   Licensure Compact (37 states) grants several licences through one application.

 KEY POINTS
   • Telemedicine has been associated with improved appointment wait times and continued patient
     satisfaction.
   • A telehealth platform may be challenging for elderly or non-technologically adept patients.

Concept — Telemedicine trades examination for access: it suits history-driven care but cannot replace a visit
where examination or office testing decides management.
