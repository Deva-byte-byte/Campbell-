Chapter 136 — Benign Renal Tumours
 • Incidence has risen with incidental detection: wider use of abdominal imaging has increased the number
   of benign renal tumours found.
 • Up to about a quarter of surgically treated small renal masses (SRMs) prove benign on final pathology.
   This challenges routine surgery for every suspicious lesion, given the morbidity of intervention.
 • The benign differential of the SRM: renal cysts of varying anatomical complexity; papillary adenoma;
   oncocytoma; metanephric tumours (metanephric adenoma, metanephric adenofibroma, metanephric
   stromal tumour); mixed epithelial and stromal renal tumours; and adult renal mesenchymal tumours —
   angiomyolipoma (AML), renal haemangioblastoma, juxtaglomerular cell tumour and renomedullary
   interstitial cell tumour.
 • No imaging modality fully separates benign from malignant — not ultrasonography (US), computed
   tomography (CT) or even magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).
 • Two benign lesions are usually diagnosable on imaging: the simple cyst (smooth, non-enhancing wall)
   and AML (macroscopic fat). Clinical pointers to benignity — small size, female sex, older age — are not
   reliable, so resection is sometimes required for diagnosis.
 • Renal mass biopsy spares intervention in a significant proportion of patients with benign histology; the
   American Urological Association (AUA) guideline gives it a role in SRM decision-making.
 • Management follows the perceived risk of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and metastasis: no dedicated
   follow-up (simple cyst) → selective embolisation (larger AML) → surgical extirpation (solid or complex
   cystic mass).

RENAL CYSTS

Epidemiology, Pathophysiology and Natural History
 • Fluid-filled masses, usually asymptomatic and found incidentally as SRMs.
 • Common, commoner in men, and increasing with age: about one in ten trauma CT scans shows an
   incidental cyst; on population MRI about one-third of men and one-fifth of women have cysts, rising to
   around half of men and over two-fifths of women aged ≥70 years. Average size is under 1.5 cm.
 • Risk factors: increasing age, male sex and renal dysfunction.
 • Larger cysts only infrequently cause flank or abdominal pain, a palpable mass or haematuria.
 • Mostly sporadic with a benign natural history; slow growth of about 1–2 mm per year.

Evaluation
 • Most incidental masses on non-renal-protocol imaging are determinate; cysts form the majority of
   benign renal masses.
 • Simple cyst: any size, homogeneous low attenuation ≤20 Hounsfield units (HU) (fluid density) → no
   further imaging.
 • Non-simple features raise concern for malignancy — heterogeneity, attenuation >20 HU, septa, wall
   thickening, mural nodules, calcification — and prompt further imaging and Bosniak stratification.
 • Bosniak classification: described by Morton Bosniak in 1986 on CT criteria; later refined to add category
   IIF (“F” = follow-up); updated as version 2019 to include MRI, chiefly to address the high proportion of
   benign findings among resected Bosniak III lesions.

 • Performance (systematic reviews): high sensitivity, modest specificity for malignancy. Malignancy is
   uncommon in I, II and IIF, about half of III and the great majority of IV.
 • Bosniak III has a low positive predictive value (PPV) → substantial overtreatment. If the outcome that
   matters is avoidance of metastasis, the PPV is very low and the number needed to treat very high —
   supporting surveillance of Bosniak III.

Management
 • Canadian Urological Association (CUA) 2023 guideline endorses Bosniak version 2019; developed with a
   modified Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.
 • Bosniak I and II: growth is expected and is not a sign of malignancy; malignant transformation is
   exceedingly rare.
 • Large (≥3 cm) hyperdense cysts, formerly Bosniak IIF, are no longer IIF under version 2019.
 • Shared decision-making governs the III–IV choice, weighing circumstances, values and preferences.
 • Cystic RCC has a more favourable natural history than solid RCC: much of the measured volume is fluid,
   so tumour burden is lower and size correlates poorly with outcome → proposed stage pT1c (“c” = cystic).
 • Surveillance of cystic masses up to 4 cm parallels AUA surveillance of solid SRMs presumed to be RCC;
   evidence is from small retrospective series, with prospective studies ongoing.

Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease
 • Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD): systemic disorder with progressive cystic
   transformation of both kidneys → gross enlargement and distortion; hypertension from raised plasma
   renin and aldosterone and intrarenal ischaemia.
 • Slowly progressive chronic kidney disease (CKD); one of the commonest causes of end-stage renal
   disease (ESRD); about 12.5 million people worldwide, about 1 in 1000–2500.
 • Extrarenal cysts: liver, pancreas, spleen, ovaries, seminal vesicles, arachnoid.
 • Genetics: PKD1 (chromosome 16) → polycystin-1; PKD2 (chromosome 4) → polycystin-2. These integral
   membrane proteins regulate ciliary function on tubular cells; the two are proposed to form a receptor–
   channel complex that senses external stimuli and transmits a local calcium-mediated signal.
 • Diagnosis by US with age-specific criteria: in first-degree relatives from PKD1 families aged 15–29 years,
   ≥3 renal cysts confirms the disease. A cyst-free scan at this age does not reliably exclude it;
   ultrasonographic exclusion is secure from 40 years (fewer than two cysts). Molecular genetic diagnosis is
   available.
 • Window for intervention: defined mutations, dominant inheritance, measurable cyst burden and slow
   progression permit measures to preserve kidney function.
 • Care is mainly nephrological: blood pressure control and slowing of renal decline — non-pharmacological
   (healthy lifestyle, weight management, no smoking) and pharmacological (antihypertensives, statins,
   analgesics).
 • Tolvaptan (Jynarque): Food and Drug Administration (FDA)–approved selective vasopressin V2-receptor
   antagonist; slows kidney function decline in adults at risk of rapid progression.
 • Urological referral: nephrolithiasis, flank pain, gross haematuria, cyst haemorrhage, cyst infection.
   Haematuria and cyst haemorrhage are usually self-limiting within days.
 • RCC risk is not higher than in the general population, but RCC in ADPKD presents earlier, is more often
   sarcomatoid and is harder to diagnose → haematuria work-up follows guidelines (AUA).
 • Cyst infection: fever, abdominal pain, raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate or C-reactive protein; 18F-
   fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (PET) aids confirmation. Treatment is usually
   conservative with empirical antibiotics — fluoroquinolones or trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole.

   embolisation) or surgical (cyst fenestration, nephrectomy). Routine nephrectomy is not recommended

 • ESRD → renal transplantation is the treatment of choice.

Acquired Cystic Kidney Disease
 • Acquired cystic kidney disease (ACKD): multiple cysts in ESRD without hereditary kidney disease; cysts
   are thought to arise from the proximal convoluted tubule, not the distal tubule.
 • Cystic change tracks the duration of dialysis-dependent ESRD: up to 80% have cysts by 10 years.
 • World Health Organization (WHO) 2022 classification lists “acquired cystic disease-associated RCC” as a
   distinct entity.

 KEY POINTS — RENAL CYSTS
   • Cysts are the commonest renal mass; most are asymptomatic and incidental.
   • The CT-based Bosniak system now includes MRI (version 2019).
   • Bosniak I and II need no follow-up; III and IV management includes both surveillance and resection.
   • ADPKD: bilateral progressive cystic transformation with slowly progressive CKD.
   • ACKD: multiple cysts in small or normal kidneys in ESRD, with increased RCC risk.


ONCOCYTOMA

Epidemiology, Pathophysiology and Natural History
 • The commonest benign enhancing renal mass, accounting for up to half of benign SRMs <4 cm.
 • Usually an asymptomatic, unilateral, cortical mass; hard to distinguish from RCC on imaging, especially
   chromophobe RCC.
 • Mostly sporadic; also occurs in Birt–Hogg–Dubé syndrome (BHDS): autosomal dominant, folliculin gene
   on chromosome 17, variable penetrance → cutaneous fibrofolliculomas (face, upper trunk), pulmonary
   cysts with spontaneous pneumothorax, chromophobe RCC and oncocytomas.

 • Renal oncocytosis: extremely rare, distinct from BHDS; the kidney is diffusely replaced and compressed by
   numerous bilateral oncocytic nodules → CKD. It may harbour chromophobe RCC and hybrid oncocytic
   tumours, which are not benign and are indistinguishable on cross-sectional imaging.

Evaluation
 • Hypervascular, with a central, sharply defined stellate scar — not specific enough to diagnose
   oncocytoma or exclude RCC.
 • Lesion-to-cortex attenuation: lower attenuation in the corticomedullary phase and more gradual
   enhancement favour oncocytoma.
 • MRI: well demarcated; lower T1 and higher T2 signal than cortex. Diffusion-weighted imaging and the
   apparent diffusion coefficient show promise.
 • Technetium-99m (99mTc)-sestamibi single-photon emission CT/CT (SPECT/CT): possible future role; not
   yet accurate enough for routine use.
 • Figure 136.3: US — 4 cm well-circumscribed hypoechoic mass with a central hyperechoic stellate scar;
   dynamic CT — heterogeneous enhancement with delayed enhancement of the scar and segmental
   enhancement inversion (highly and less-enhancing segments in the corticomedullary phase reverse in the
   early excretory phase), a pattern common in oncocytoma.

Management
 • Oncocytoma frequency plus imaging limits have driven uptake of percutaneous renal mass biopsy.
 • AUA: biopsy “should be obtained” whenever the result may change management (expert opinion). EAU:
   biopsy reduces overtreatment of benign masses and adds grade and subtype for informed decisions (level
   of evidence 3).
 • But biopsy is unreliable for oncocytoma: a systematic review found a modest PPV, with a substantial
   minority proving RCC at surgery; selection bias explains part of this.
 • Two reasons: inadequate sampling, and overlapping histology with chromophobe RCC, which shows
   intratumoural heterogeneity with oncocytoma-like areas → EAU: definitive histopathological diagnosis of
   oncocytoma cannot be made on a needle core.
 • With sufficient representative tissue, immunohistochemistry with cytokeratin 7 (CK7) and cytogenetics
   help (table below).
 • Figure 136.4: well-marginated eosinophilic neoplasm; nested uniform round-to-polygonal cells with dense
   eosinophilic cytoplasm in hypocellular hyalinised stroma; CK7 single-cell positivity; KIT positive.

   • Benign; up to half of benign SRMs <4 cm; the commonest benign enhancing renal mass.
   • May be indistinguishable from malignant masses on imaging.
   • Mostly sporadic; also part of BHDS (autosomal dominant, folliculin, chromosome 17, variable
     penetrance).
   • Histology overlaps with chromophobe RCC; biopsy misleads unless tissue is sufficient and
     representative.


ANGIOMYOLIPOMA

Epidemiology, Pathophysiology and Natural History
 • Benign mesenchymal tumour of dysmorphic blood vessels (angio), smooth muscle (myo) and adipose
   tissue (lipo), arising mainly from the renal cortex or medulla.
 • Sporadic or syndromic — most commonly tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) and
   lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM).
 • Prevalence about 0.13%, female predisposition, peak in the fourth and fifth decades; mostly
   asymptomatic and incidental.
 • In TSC, AML occurs in 55–90%, presenting earlier than sporadic AML.
 • Belongs to the family of tumours of perivascular epithelioid cells.
 • Driver: biallelic inactivation of TSC2 (tuberin, chromosome 16p13) or, less often, TSC1 (hamartin,
   chromosome 9q34), found in the great majority. Mechanisms: point mutations, small insertions/
   deletions, large deletions, copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity (commoner in TSC; small mutations
   commoner in sporadic AML). Multiple tumours in one patient are clonally independent (different second
   hits).
 • TSC is autosomal dominant with variable penetrance; sporadic mutations are common.
 • Hamartin and tuberin together inhibit the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR); their loss →
   unregulated mTOR → cell growth and angiogenesis.

Management
 • Treatment turns on type (sporadic or TSC), symptoms and spontaneous bleeding risk, which correlates
   with size.
 • The historical 4 cm threshold (in people of childbearing age) was highly sensitive but poorly specific for
   bleeding → many false positives and overtreatment of most patients treated on size alone. A threshold of
   >6 cm has been proposed; bleeding risk rises steeply with size and is highest above 6 cm. A 4 cm criterion
   should probably not be a general trigger. AMLs <4 cm are expected to be asymptomatic and unlikely to
   bleed → surveillance.
 • Intralesional aneurysm ≥5 mm predicts haemorrhage → thin-slice CT angiography when observing an
   AML ≥4 cm.
 • Pregnancy: bleeding risk may be higher and consequences graver for mother and fetus; prophylactic
   intervention is proposed in women planning pregnancy soon (case reports and small series).
 • Active-treatment prompts: size, symptoms, residual suspicion of RCC, or epithelioid AML. Four options:
   embolisation, surgery, thermal ablation, mTOR inhibition.

PAPILLARY ADENOMA
 • Unencapsulated, low-grade tumour of papillary or tubular architecture, ≤15 mm (WHO 2016 and 2022),
   arising from the renal cortex.
 • Nearly always incidental — on cross-sectional imaging or in nephrectomy specimens removed for larger
   masses.
 • Incidence rises with age; commoner in men. Associations with ESRD, acquired cystic disease and sporadic
   RCC may reflect detection bias.
 • Resembles papillary RCC microscopically, genetically and immunohistochemically; may be a precursor:
   shared KMT2C pathogenic mutation; trisomy 7 and 17 and loss of Y, as in papillary RCC.
 • Until 2015, defined as ≤5 mm; not requiring treatment and often found alongside RCC in nephrectomy
   specimens.
 • Virtually no metastatic capacity; in a donor kidney, not a contraindication to transplantation.
Concept — Papillary adenoma is papillary RCC in miniature — the same genetics and staining — and its size
ceiling, now 15 mm, is what defines it as benign.

 KEY POINTS — PAPILLARY ADENOMA
   • Low-grade papillary or tubular tumour ≤15 mm; formerly ≤5 mm (until 2015).
   • Now mostly incidental on cross-sectional imaging.

METANEPHRIC ADENOMA
 • Metanephric adenoma (MA): rare benign epithelial tumour, <0.5% of adult renal neoplasms; a few
   hundred reported cases.
 • Early series: nearly half symptomatic — flank pain, haematuria and polycythaemia (erythropoietin and
   other cytokines). Recent series: mostly incidental; peak in the fifties; commoner in women.
 • CT: solid, hypovascular, with prolonged, homogeneous, mild enhancement — less than normal
   parenchyma in all phases and less than typical RCC. Suggestive but not specific enough to exclude
   malignancy → often excised, ideally by NSS.
 • Biopsy may help, but its accuracy for MA is not established (a systematic review of renal biopsies
   contained no MA).
 • Gross: circumscribed, unencapsulated, solid.
 • Histology mimics papillary RCC: small epithelial cells in tightly packed small acini, scant cytoplasm,
   inconspicuous nucleoli, essentially no mitoses.
 • Immunohistochemistry: Wilms tumour 1 (WT1) and CD57 positive; CK7 and alpha-methylacyl-CoA
   racemase (AMACR) negative.
 • Fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) for chromosomes 7, 17 and Y: MA lacks the gains of 7 and 17
   and loss of Y typical of papillary RCC.
Concept — Metanephric adenoma looks like papillary RCC but does not stain or karyotype like it: WT1 and
CD57 positive, CK7 and AMACR negative, without the 7/17/Y changes that papillary adenoma shares with
papillary RCC.

 KEY POINTS — METANEPHRIC ADENOMA
   • Rare benign epithelial tumour, mostly incidental.
   • CT: solid, hypovascular, reduced enhancement — not sufficiently diagnostic.
   • Histology resembles papillary RCC; FISH helps: MA lacks +7, +17 and −Y.


MIXED EPITHELIAL AND STROMAL TUMOURS
 • Mixed epithelial and stromal tumour (MEST) and adult cystic nephroma (ACN), traditionally separate,
   share epidemiology, imaging and histology → WHO 2022 places both in the MEST family.
 • The family also contains paediatric cystic nephroma, a distinct entity defined by DICER1 (microRNA-
   processing gene) mutations — not found in ACN — with downstream effects on p53 function and RAS
   pathway activation.
 • Middle-aged women, usually incidental; solitary, unilateral, well-circumscribed, fluid-dense,
   multiseptated masses indistinguishable from cystic RCC → historically excised, preferably by NSS.
 • MEST histology: cysts lined by cuboidal and columnar epithelium with occasional urothelium, in a stroma
   of spindle cells, smooth muscle fascicles and collagen bands. Stroma: oestrogen and progesterone
   receptor positive, plus vimentin, CD34 and desmin; epithelium CK7 positive.
 • ACN histology: variably sized cysts lined by flat, cuboidal and hobnail epithelium (nuclei projecting
   smoothly from the surface); stroma likewise oestrogen and progesterone receptor positive.

 • Manage by current guidelines for cystic renal masses; consider biopsy.
 • Figure 136.8: gross — well-circumscribed mass of variably sized cysts separated by thick white septa;
   microscopy — hobnailed lining and spindle-cell stroma.
Concept — MEST and ACN are one lesion of middle-aged women with hormone-receptor-positive stroma;
because they image as complex cysts, they are managed through the Bosniak pathway rather than by name.

 KEY POINTS — MEST AND ACN
   • Uncommon, benign, mostly in middle-aged women; usually incidental.
   • One family (with paediatric cystic nephroma) under WHO 2022.
   • Solitary multiseptated cystic masses that may resemble cystic RCC.
   • Manage per cystic-mass guidelines; consider biopsy.


LEIOMYOMA
 • Very rare benign smooth muscle tumour of unknown aetiology, mostly incidental; about 0.3% of primary
   renal masses; female predominance 2:1.
 • Commonest origin: the renal capsule; renal pelvis and renal vein origins also reported.
 • CT: small, well-circumscribed, homogeneous, exophytic solid mass with uniform enhancement. MRI:
   hypointense on T1 and T2 (smooth muscle relaxation times), relatively homogeneous enhancement. Still
   not reliably distinguishable from RCC.
 • Many reported leiomyomas prove to be lipid-poor AML.
 • Gross: usually small (mean about 3 cm; up to 7 cm reported); well circumscribed, tan to white, bulging
   whorled cut surface.
 • Histology: smooth muscle cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and blunt-ended, cigar-shaped nuclei; no
   hypercellularity, pleomorphism, mitoses or necrosis. SMA, desmin and caldesmon positive.
 • Lipid-poor AML and leiomyoma are mutually misdiagnosed: both can be HMB-45 positive; Melan-A is
   positive in AML but not leiomyoma — the decisive stain.
 • Management: per current SRM guidelines (AUA), individualised to symptoms, location, values and
   preferences. Biopsy can supply tissue. If surgery → NSS preferred (small, benign, excellent prognosis).
 • Figure 136.9: small mass arising from the capsule; uniform spindle cells with thin cigar-shaped nuclei;
   strong SMA staining, absent in adjacent normal tubules.
Concept — Leiomyoma is defined against its mimic: lipid-poor AML shares its smooth muscle and even
HMB-45, and Melan-A decides.

 KEY POINTS — LEIOMYOMA
   • Very rare benign smooth muscle tumour, most often from the renal capsule.
   • Hard to separate from RCC radiologically; biopsy helps.
   • Lipid-poor AML and leiomyoma can be mistaken for each other.
   • Manage per solid-mass guidelines; NSS if surgery.
