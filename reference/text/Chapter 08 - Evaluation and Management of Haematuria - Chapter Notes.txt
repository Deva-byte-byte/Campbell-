 Chapter 8 — Evaluation and Management of Haematuria
   • Haematuria has been recognised as a sign of illness since antiquity and is now one of the commonest
     indications for urological evaluation.
   • Up to 65% of patients with haematuria have a urological or renal condition, many needing treatment
     — hence the differential diagnosis, principles of evaluation and management strategies must be
     known.
   • Presentation spans asymptomatic microhaematuria to refractory haemorrhagic cystitis, with
     implications for health-system resources and difficult clinical decisions.
   • Knowing the risk factors for genitourinary malignancy in microscopic haematuria allows timely,
     appropriate evaluation; severe upper and lower tract bleeding is often life-threatening in elderly,
     comorbid patients.

 CLASSIFICATION AND TIMING OF HAEMATURIA
   • Classified by visibility, timing within the urinary stream, and associated symptoms.
   • Gross haematuria (GH) — also frank, macroscopic or visible haematuria — is visible to the naked eye.
     Its timing within the stream suggests the source:
  Timing of GH                      Usual source
  Initial                           Urethra
  Terminal                          Bladder trigone, bladder neck or prostate
  Total                             Bladder or upper urinary tract
  Cyclic (female)                   Gynaecological cause (e.g. endometriosis)

   • Pigmenturia must be excluded: endogenous pigments (bilirubin, myoglobin, porphyrins), foods
     (beetroot, rhubarb), drugs (phenazopyridine) or dehydration — distinguished by urinalysis (UA) with
     microscopy.
   • False-positive dipstick for haemoglobin: myoglobinuria, semen and bacterial peroxidases →
     microscopy is required to confirm haematuria.
   • Exclude vaginal bleeding: careful menstrual history, specimen collected outside menstrual or
     gynaecological bleeding, or, if necessary, a catheterised specimen. In incontinent patients GH may
     present as blood spotting on undergarments.
   • Once vaginal bleeding and mimics are excluded, a urological source must be suspected.
   • Microhaematuria (MH) is a sign, not a symptom: a laboratory diagnosis of red blood cells (RBCs) on
     urine microscopy, not visible to the eye.
   • Prevalence about 6.5% in healthy screened adults, higher in cohorts with more men, older patients
     and smokers.
   • MH is categorised by presence or absence of symptoms and quantified as RBCs per high-power field
     (HPF).
 Concept — Timing within the stream localises the source, and microscopy — not colour, not dipstick —
 establishes that the red cells are real.

 MICROSCOPIC HAEMATURIA
 Criteria for Diagnosis of Microhaematuria
   • A few RBCs enter the urine normally, and sexual activity and exercise can produce minor MH. Together
     with the high prevalence and low malignancy yield, this makes a threshold with both high sensitivity
     and high specificity unattainable.
   • Threshold ≥3 RBCs/HPF is used by most studies and guideline panels.
   • Sensitivity–specificity trade-off (large observational study correlating degree of MH with malignancy):
     0–2 RBCs/HPF is fully sensitive but has no specificity; 3–10 gives moderate sensitivity and specificity;
     higher thresholds progressively lose sensitivity and gain specificity.
   • The American Urological Association (AUA)/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and
     Urogenital Reconstruction (SUFU) guideline (2020) defines MH as ≥3 RBCs/HPF: higher thresholds
     would miss treatable urological conditions.
   • A single positive UA is sufficient to prompt evaluation, because MH from significant disease, including
     malignancy, can be intermittent.
   • Other national guidelines vary (Table 8.1), reflecting different balances of benefit, harm and cost; none
     has been robustly validated.
   • Laboratory reporting must match the threshold: a normal range extending above 3 (e.g. ≤5 RBCs/HPF)
     defeats it, and current reporting cut-offs are variable and often discordant with guideline risk groups.
   • Automated UA (e.g. flow cytometry) increasingly replaces microscopy, but the ≥3 RBCs/HPF threshold
     is based on microscopic sediment; the correct automated threshold is unknown.

 Differential Diagnosis
   • One-third to two-thirds of patients evaluated for MH have a demonstrable cause — commonly
     benign prostatic enlargement (BPE), calculi and urethral stricture (Table 8.2).
   • MH may arise anywhere: consider neoplastic, infectious and anatomical causes in kidney, ureter,
     bladder, prostate and urethra, plus medical renal disease and mimics such as vaginal bleeding.
   • Many benign findings need no treatment: non-obstructing nephrolithiasis, pelvic organ prolapse,
     vaginal atrophy, BPE.
   • Malignancy is found in roughly 3% of MH evaluations, varying with population, threshold and
     completeness of evaluation.
   • Meta-analysis: bladder cancer is found in a few per cent (more in high-risk cohorts), whereas upper
     tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) are each found in about 1 in 1,000
     — upper tract malignancy is very uncommon in MH.
   • Yield rises with degree of MH (>25 RBCs/HPF), GH and malignancy risk factors (Box 8.1).

 Bladder Cancer Detection and Risk Factors for Delayed Diagnosis
   • United States: over 82,000 new bladder cancers and over 16,700 deaths annually; most new cases are
     non-muscle-invasive.
   • Male-to-female incidence 4:1, yet 5-year survival is worse in women — partly from delayed
     diagnosis: women present at higher stage and their MH is more often misattributed to UTI or
     gynaecological causes.
   • In muscle-invasive disease, treatment delay worsens survival → prove rather than presume a benign
     cause, and repeat the UA after treatment to confirm resolution.

   • Black patients: lower incidence but higher mortality, likewise attributed to differences in referral and
     timely diagnosis.
   • Anticoagulants and antiplatelets (warfarin, heparins, aspirin, clopidogrel, non-steroidal anti-
     inflammatory drugs): haematuria prevalence and cancer yield are no different from non-users; these
     drugs may unmask lesions at an earlier stage; a population study found users more than twice as
     likely to be diagnosed with bladder cancer within 6 months of haematuria → risk-based evaluation
     exactly as for non-users.
   • Under-evaluation is pervasive: fewer than 25% of patients with haematuria are referred and fewer
     than 10% receive complete evaluation (cystoscopy and imaging), even when high risk.
   • Contributors: disparate guidelines, low yield, cost of imaging and invasiveness of cystoscopy. Proposed
     remedies: electronic record decision support, telemedicine consultation, one-stop haematuria clinics.

 Rationale for a Risk-Based Evaluation Algorithm
   • One-third to two-thirds of MH evaluations are negative; given cost and patient risk, an individualised
     selection for evaluation is sought.
   • Kaiser Permanente data: patients with risk factors (age ≥50, GH history, tobacco, male sex, >25 RBCs/
     HPF) had roughly ten-fold higher malignancy yield than intermediate-risk patients; low-risk yield was
     near zero.
   • ACOG/AUGS (2017): withhold evaluation in never-smoking women aged 35–50 with <25 RBCs/HPF,
     whose malignancy risk is <0.5%.
   • AUA/SUFU (2020) defined low, intermediate and high risk by age, sex, smoking and degree of MH
     (Table 8.3), matching evaluation intensity to cancer risk.
   • Not externally validated at publication, but built on systematic review confirming each factor; later
     models use the same factors. Subsequent validation confirmed a stepwise rise in malignancy across
     the three strata.

 EVALUATION OF PATIENTS WITH MICROHAEMATURIA
   • Many guidelines and risk models exist; the chapter follows the AUA/SUFU guideline (Fig. 8.1).

 Initial Evaluation
   • History and examination focused on genitourinary malignancy risk factors (Box 8.1), medical renal
     disease and benign causes: infection, trauma, nephrolithiasis, BPE and urethral stricture in men,
     urethral diverticulum and pelvic organ prolapse in women; directed examination, including female
     pelvic examination, as needed.
   • Measure blood pressure and serum creatinine — both detect medical renal disease; creatinine also
     guides the choice of imaging where cross-sectional imaging is indicated.
   • Suspected benign cause → confirm it, treat, repeat UA. Suspected UTI → urine culture, then follow-up
     UA after treatment; persistent MH → further evaluation. Omitting these steps delays bladder cancer
     diagnosis, raises stage and lowers cancer-specific survival.
   • Benign conditions needing no treatment (non-obstructing stones, vaginal atrophy), or where treatment
     will not clear MH (medical therapy for BPE) → shared decision-making on further evaluation.

     evaluation.

 Low-Risk Patients With Microhaematuria
   • Malignancy risk 0–0.4%. More than 85% of malignancies found on MH evaluation are bladder
     cancers; UTUC and RCC are uncommon overall and particularly rare in low risk.
   • Low yield against cost, false positives, complications and discomfort makes benefit debatable; the
     purpose of stratification is largely to identify a group safe to forgo evaluation.
   • Shared decision-making: repeat UA within 6 months vs cystoscopy + RUS, discussing low yield, false
     positives, cystoscopy complications, discomfort, cost, preference and risk tolerance.
   • Repeat UA still positive → cystoscopy and imaging, with modality set by the new stratum (3–25 RBCs/
     HPF intermediate; >25 high), assuming no new irritative symptoms.
   • Omitting evaluation in some and substituting RUS for CT urography reduces burden, risk and cost
     without compromising cancer detection.

 Intermediate-Risk Patients With Microhaematuria
   • Malignancy risk 1–2.5%; UTUC risk remains very low → cystoscopy + RUS.
   • RUS retains sensitivity for renal cortical tumours while avoiding ionising radiation and iodinated
     contrast, and reduces incidental findings and cost.

 High-Risk Patients With Microhaematuria
   • Malignancy about 6% in validation, higher in men, smokers and those with GH history.
   • UTUC is uncommon overall but likely most frequent in this stratum; many guidelines use RUS for all,
     but RUS is poorly sensitive for UTUC → AUA/SUFU: cystoscopy + axial upper tract imaging with
     excretory views.

 Upper Tract Imaging in the Diagnostic Evaluation of Microscopic Haematuria
   • CT urography has the highest sensitivity and specificity for renal parenchymal and upper tract lesions,
     is very sensitive for calculi and shows extra-urinary disease.

   • Limitations: pregnancy, iodinated contrast allergy, chronic kidney disease; radiation 20–30 mSv
     (variable between centres), with potential carcinogenesis.
   • Cost-effectiveness modelling: CT urography instead of RUS costs over US$1 million per additional
     cancer detected, with very few extra cancers, because UTUC is rare; across large populations, radiation-
     induced cancers, contrast complications and false positives become substantial.
   • Some organisations therefore use RUS for all; AUA/SUFU instead reserves axial imaging for the high-
     risk subpopulation, whose upper tract cancer risk justifies it.
   • CT urogram phases: pre-contrast (stones, hyperdense lesions) → contrast-enhanced parenchymal
     (cortical tumours) → delayed excretory images from kidneys to bladder (urothelium).
   • RUS sensitivity for UTUC is poorly defined but appears lower than CT — only two studies, with
     conflicting results; CT urography shows high sensitivity and very high specificity for UTUC and RCC
     combined.
   • CT risk mitigation: split-bolus protocols (three series reduced to two) and radiation dose adjusted for
     body mass index.
   • Contrast-associated acute kidney injury (AKI): an American College of Radiology (ACR)/National Kidney
     Foundation consensus states the risk has been overstated, recommending hydration when glomerular
     filtration rate (GFR) <30 mL/min/1.73 m².
   • CT urogram contraindicated → MR urography; MRI contraindicated (some pacemakers, metal
     implants, severe claustrophobia) → non-contrast CT or ultrasound of the parenchyma + retrograde
     pyelography of calyces, pelvis and ureters.
   • Nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF) after gadolinium is small and falling: exceedingly rare at GFR ≥30;
     below that, group II or III gadolinium agents may be used.
   • Genetic predisposition to renal cortical tumours (e.g. von Hippel–Lindau disease) → upper tract
     imaging regardless of stratum.
   • Persistent or recurrent MH after negative RUS (low or intermediate risk) → cross-sectional or
     retrograde urography may be used.

 Natural History of Microhaematuria in Patients With a Negative Initial Evaluation
   • MH resolves in about one-third over months to years — but these cohorts were young and often
     incompletely evaluated.
   • Re-evaluation series found a small malignancy yield, but initial evaluations were often incomplete and
     most cancers came from CT in patients not previously CT-imaged.
   • After a complete initial evaluation, malignancy on a second evaluation is rare; long-term follow-up of
     men over 50 found only occasional bladder cancers, many years later.
   • AUA/SUFU (conditional): may repeat UA within 12 months and discontinue if negative.
   • Persistent or recurrent MH → shared decision on re-evaluation, acknowledging low yield.
   • GH, a significant rise in MH or new urological symptoms → re-evaluate: these suggest new or occult
     disease.
   • Cytology if carcinoma in situ risk factors develop (GH, irritative symptoms); cross-sectional or
     retrograde urography if initially imaged with RUS.
   • Untreated chronic benign causes (non-obstructing stones, BPE, prolapse) → judgement and shared
     decision on whether and when to re-evaluate.
   • Low-risk patients who skipped evaluation → risk-based evaluation if MH persists or recurs.

 GROSS HAEMATURIA
   • The differential is the same as for MH, but the yield of significant lesions rises with the degree of
     haematuria: about half of GH patients have a demonstrable cause.
   • DETECT I (prospective, UK, 40 hospitals, 3,556 referred patients): urinary tract cancer in 13.8% with GH
     vs 3.1% with MH; 3.5% of GH patients under 45 had cancer → investigate GH without an age
     threshold.
   • IDENTIFY (international prospective, 10,896 patients, 110 hospitals, 26 countries): cancer in 26% with
     GH vs 6.38% with MH.
   • In both, bladder cancer was the commonest cancer; a systematic review likewise found bladder
     cancer, RCC and UTUC all several-fold more frequent with GH than MH.
   • Risk prediction models from DETECT and IDENTIFY both retain GH as a significant predictor of cancer.
   • GH without trauma or culture-documented UTI → urine cytology, cystoscopy and upper tract
     imaging, preferably CT urography.
   • GH with culture-documented UTI → treat, then repeat UA to confirm clearance.
   • Initial assessment as for MH, plus: haemodynamic stability, full blood count for anaemia, coagulation
     parameters if anticoagulated; bladder emptying (post-void residual ultrasound), with catheterisation
     and clot irrigation if clot retention is suspected.
   • Stabilise → diagnostic evaluation → cause-specific management.
   • Non-diagnostic evaluation: follow the MH schedule as a reference; full repeat evaluation if GH recurs.

 HAEMORRHAGIC CYSTITIS
   • Spectrum from transient to life-threatening, often in elderly, infirm, comorbid patients → central
     tenet: weigh the risk of the current state against the risk of escalating treatment.
   • Diffuse inflammation and bleeding of the bladder mucosa; causes in Box 8.2.
   • Bacterial infection: a common cause of GH; resolves with appropriate treatment.
   • Viral: children and immunosuppressed adults, especially after renal or bone marrow transplantation.
     BK virus (polyomavirus) is the commonest; adenovirus types 11 and 35 in children and renal
     transplant recipients. Treatment is supportive (hydration, diuresis, bladder irrigation); antiviral success
     is anecdotal.
   • Oxazaphosphorines (cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide): frequent and dose-dependent. Liver produces
     acrolein → renal excretion → urothelial contact → mucosal sloughing → oedema and fibrosis. Onset
     typically within 48 hours.
   • Mesna (2-mercaptoethane sulfonate) binds acrolein and inactivates it as prophylaxis, yet a substantial
     minority still develop cystitis; whether mesna beats hyperhydration with forced diuresis and/or
     continuous bladder irrigation (CBI) is unresolved.
   • Pelvic radiotherapy: moderate-to-severe haematuria in about 5%, dose- and technique-dependent,
     onset 6 months to 10 years after treatment; reported incidence varies with population and follow-up,
     rising with long follow-up after post-prostatectomy radiotherapy, and anticoagulant/antiplatelet use
     increases risk.
   • Radiation mechanism: vascular endothelial damage → inflammation → fibrosis → ischaemia →
     progressive obliterative endarteritis → necrosis and mucosal sloughing; secondary infection further
     impairs healing.
   • Morbidity is substantial: repeated admissions, transfusion, cystoscopic fulguration and clot evacuation,
     and some deaths.
   • Cyclophosphamide and pelvic radiation are bladder cancer risk factors → exclude bladder cancer
     before labelling haemorrhagic cystitis.

   • Clinically stable patients, particularly after radiation or cyclophosphamide → hyperbaric oxygen
     therapy (HBOT) is the next step.
   • 100% oxygen at 2–3 atmospheres, about 90 min, 30–40 sessions → ↑tissue oxygen tension →
     ↓oedema, neovascularisation, wound healing.

   • Response 80–90%, sustained up to about 2.5 years. RICH-ART randomised trial (radiation cystitis):
     HBOT significantly improved the Expanded Prostate Cancer Index Composite (EPIC) urinary score (about
     10 points) vs standard care.
   • Durability is limited: most relapse with longer follow-up, with only about a quarter in complete
     response at 5 years.
   • Complications: claustrophobia, otalgia, barotrauma, myopia, rarely seizures.
   • Continued bleeding during HBOT → bilateral nephrostomies ± ureteric occlusion, removing urokinase-
     containing urine from the bladder to aid haemostasis over the long HBOT course.
 Angioembolisation
   • Clinically unstable patients or intractable bleeding → internal iliac artery angioembolisation (first
     reported 1974); unilateral or bilateral, low risk even in debilitated patients.
   • Selective bilateral embolisation of the anterior division is usually needed (gelatin microspheres,
     polyvinyl alcohol particles, coils). Avoid the posterior division → superior gluteal artery occlusion →
     gluteal pain.
   • Initial control about 90%, but durability unknown; series are small and include pelvic tumours;
     reliability in radiation cystitis, where mucosal ischaemia is the pathology, is uncertain.
 Cystectomy and Urinary Diversion
   • Failed non-surgical management → cystectomy with urinary diversion.
   • Supravesical diversion (e.g. ileal conduit) without cystectomy: complications, chiefly pyocystis, and
     frequent readmission → remove the bladder at diversion if feasible.
   • Patients are elderly and deconditioned → perioperative complications higher than cystectomy for
     cancer; yet definitive surgery reduces subsequent admissions, procedures and transfusions → balance
     surgical risk against deconditioning from prolonged conservative care.
 Emerging Options
   • Tranexamic acid (antifibrinolytic): intravenous use lowers transfusion at radical cystectomy; benefit,
     route and risk of clot retention in haemorrhagic cystitis unestablished. Laser ablation of mucosal
     bleeding: feasible, needs study.

 HAEMATURIA FROM PROSTATE ORIGIN
   • A diagnosis of exclusion after complete GH evaluation (cytology, upper tract imaging, cystoscopy).
     Severity ranges from self-limiting episodes to obstructing, transfusion-dependent bleeding.
   • Causes: benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), prostatitis, prostate cancer (Fig. 8.3).
 Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia
   • Commonest cause of prostatic bleeding and of GH in men over 60; the only pathology found in about
     one-fifth of haematuria series.
   • Mechanism: increased microvessel density in hyperplastic tissue, driven by vascular endothelial
     growth factor (VEGF).
   • Usually mild and self-limiting → expectant management with hydration.
   • GH was historically a surgical indication; understanding the VEGF pathway has introduced targeted
     medical therapy.
   • Androgen suppression (oestrogens, antiandrogens; case reports) represses androgen-driven
     angiogenesis and induces apoptosis.
   • Finasteride (5α-reductase inhibitor (5-ARI); blocks testosterone → dihydrotestosterone) ↓VEGF
     expression, microvessel density and prostatic blood flow; improvement or resolution in about 90%,
     including anticoagulated patients. A randomised trial vs expectant management showed markedly
     fewer recurrences and no surgery for bleeding with finasteride. Onset 2 weeks to 9 months.
   • Randomised trial: finasteride and cyproterone acetate equally reduce recurrence vs watchful waiting.
     Dutasteride (type I and II inhibition) produces similar microvessel changes.
   • 5-ARIs have the best data and fewest side effects. AUA BPH guideline (2021, Expert Opinion): 5-ARIs
     appropriate for refractory prostatic haematuria after excluding other causes, and may be considered
     to reduce intraoperative bleeding and transfusion at transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) or
     other BPH surgery (randomised data conflicting).
   • Clot or retention → large-bore catheter, complete clot evacuation → CBI until clear → failure →
     endoscopy under anaesthesia: clot evacuation, electrocautery or laser.
   • Intravesical agents (e.g. aminocaproic acid) have little supporting evidence here.
   • Refractory bleeding → TURP, particularly if other surgical indications coexist; alternatives include
     photoselective vaporisation of the prostate (PVP), holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP)
     and open simple prostatectomy — the principle is removal of friable hyperplastic transition-zone
     tissue.
   • Bleeding despite TURP → prostatic artery embolisation (PAE): bilateral, preferably superselective with
     gelatin microparticles (or anterior division of internal iliac); high initial success, bleeding stops within
     days, durable at 12 months. AUA 2021 cites PAE as an adjunct for refractory haematuria.
   • Radical prostatectomy or cystoprostatectomy: last resort in usually poor candidates.

 Prostatitis
   • Haematuria presents in a small minority; mechanism unclear, probably inflammatory. Culture-proven
     bacterial prostatitis → antibiotics; significant recurrent haematuria in non-bacterial prostatitis is
     uncommon — antibiotics plus supportive care suggested.
 Prostate Cancer
   • Bleeding usually reflects locally advanced disease with bladder base or trigonal invasion (T4);
     haematuria is the commonest local symptom of advanced symptomatic prostate cancer.
   • Confirm the prostatic source endoscopically, especially after radiotherapy — exclude radiation cystitis
     or second bladder malignancy.
   • Patients are elderly and unwell → palliative intent. Mild bleeding → catheter ± CBI.
   • Not acutely life-threatening, radiation-naive → palliative external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) ±
     androgen deprivation therapy (ADT): good early response, poor durability.
   • Unfit for local therapy or recurrent after local therapy → ADT, which reduces prostatic vascularity.
   • Persistent bleeding, especially with bladder outlet obstruction → cystoscopy with fulguration and/or
     limited ("channel") TURP; selective angioembolisation possible but scant data and limited durability.
   • Escalating bleeding → urinary diversion, first percutaneous nephrostomy → then palliative
     extirpation, usually cystoprostatectomy with conduit, depending on status.

 Medical Renal Disease
   • Glomerular diseases (acquired or inherited) → haematuria, proteinuria with hypoproteinaemia and
     oedema, reduced GFR. RBC casts, dysmorphic RBCs and proteinuria indicate a glomerular source.
   • Tubulointerstitial diseases affect structures outside the glomerulus. Sickle cell nephropathy: sickled
     cells ↓medullary flow → ischaemia, microinfarction → papillary necrosis. Analgesic nephropathy →
     papillary necrosis → chronic interstitial nephritis.
   • Percutaneous renal biopsy when glomerular or tubulointerstitial disease is suspected.
   • Complete risk-stratified haematuria evaluation even when medical renal disease is suspected and
     nephrology referral is made.

 Vascular Conditions Affecting the Urinary Tract
   • Ureteroiliac artery fistula: rare, life-threatening; predisposed by pelvic or vascular surgery, pelvic
     irradiation, extensive ureteric mobilisation and chronic ureteric stenting. Open repair carries high
     mortality → angiographic localisation with endovascular stenting preferred.
   • Renal AVM: congenital or acquired; acquired forms are 75% (renal biopsy, partial nephrectomy,
     nephrolithotomy, trauma). Arteriography with selective angioembolisation is the primary diagnostic
     and therapeutic step, preserving parenchyma; aim is occlusion of the arteriovenous communication.
     GH after a recent renal procedure → expeditious angiography.
   • Renal artery aneurysm: linked to connective tissue disorders, usually asymptomatic; hypertension in
     up to 90%; dissection → flank pain and GH. Aneurysm and pseudoaneurysm: blood pressure control →
     endovascular repair if refractory but stable → surgery if unstable.
   • Nutcracker syndrome (renal vein entrapment): left renal vein compressed between aorta (posterior)
     and superior mesenteric artery (anterior) → ↑left renal vein pressure → rupture of thin-walled
     capillaries into the collecting system. Surgery: left renal vein transposition, superior mesenteric artery
     transposition, nephrectomy; endovascular stenting more recently.
