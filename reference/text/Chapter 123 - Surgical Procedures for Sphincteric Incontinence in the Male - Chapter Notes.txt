 • Male sphincteric urinary incontinence (SUI) significantly impairs quality of life (QoL); causes include
   congenital anomalies, injury and genitourinary surgery.
 • Radical prostatectomy (RP) for prostate cancer (CaP) is a leading cause: storage fails because external
   sphincter resistance is insufficient.
 • US burden is high because RP is common. It may decline with better technique, fewer RPs after the 2012
   U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommendation against prostate-specific antigen (PSA)
   screening, and more active surveillance.
 • QoL impact drives treatment-seeking; slings and the artificial urinary sphincter (AUS) increase outlet
   resistance. Physician and patient perceptions are barriers. Decisional regret is highest after conservative
   treatment, so outreach is needed.
 • AUS = gold standard, because it treats all degrees of SUI. Bulbar slings suit mild-to-moderate urinary
   incontinence (UI), with long-term data now available.

CLASSIFICATION, PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND AETIOLOGY
 • Continence needs bladder, internal sphincter (bladder neck, prostatic urethra) and external sphincter
   (rhabdosphincter). Male SUI requires both sphincters to be impaired.

 • Reduced compliance, detrusor overactivity (DO) and urgency urinary incontinence (UUI) complicate
   diagnosis — evaluate carefully.
 • Prevalence rises with age: 11% at 60–64, 31% at >85; incidence tracks the causative procedures.
 • Post-RP rates vary widely (patient, surgeon, data method); self-report gives higher rates than chart or
   physician record. Continence improves for up to 2 years.
 • Nerve sparing (preserving pudendal innervation of the rhabdosphincter) gives faster, not better final,
   continence (meta-analyses). Autonomic afferent denervation and reduced membranous urethral
   sensitivity are associated with SUI.
 • Other mechanisms: rhabdosphincter, pelvic floor and bladder neck damage; incontinent men differ in
   urethral pressure profile, length and sphincter morphology. Posterior reconstruction speeds recovery,
   not long-term continence.
 • Bladder neck stenosis lowers continence, mainly via secondary procedures damaging the sphincter.
   Vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis (VUAS) may impair coaptation, and its treatment can cause SUI
   when the contracture reaches the membranous urethra (endoscopically: lost coaptation, radiating folds).

Concept — Male SUI needs failure of both sphincters. RP removes the internal sphincter by design, so after
RP continence rests entirely on the rhabdosphincter, and any insult to it — nerve, muscle, stricture treatment
— is exposed.

MECHANISM OF CONTINENCE WITH SURGICAL DEVICES
Compression Devices
 • AUS (AMS 800): fluid-filled cuff giving circumferential compression about 2 cm long. The pressure-
   regulating balloon (PRB) sets the compression, chosen for tissue and cuff site. Standard bulbar PRB 61–
   70 cm H2O balances occlusion against erosion; lower pressure trades continence for less erosion.

 • An open cuff lowers resistance, so men without useful detrusor contraction void by straining or Credé
   manoeuvre.
 • ProACT (Adjustable Continence Therapy): two silicone balloons beside the bladder neck, filled with saline
   through scrotal ports.
Fig. 123.1 — AMS 800: cuff, pump and PRB. The 3.5-cm cuff has a unique three-cushion design.

Male Slings
Nonadjustable Slings
 • Transobturator slings reposition a residual rhabdosphincter: leak point pressure rises without
   obstruction; fluoroscopy and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) show urethral elongation and elevation
   of bladder neck, posterior wall and rhabdosphincter.
 • Virtue: four-armed mesh; prepubic arms compress against the urogenital diaphragm.
Adjustable Slings
 • ATOMS: central cushion under the bulbar urethra, titanium–silicone scrotal port for saline adjustment.

EVALUATION AND DIAGNOSIS
 • Core: history, examination, postvoid residual (PVR), urinalysis; cystoscopy and pressure-flow
   urodynamics (UDS) as indicated for VUAS, residual sphincter and storage.

History
 • Focus: UI type and severity; prior surgery, radiotherapy, radiation cystitis; neurological symptoms.
 • Most important step: classify the UI — SUI (stress or activity-related), UUI, mixed or overflow.
 • Incontinence during sleep and nocturia help separate severe SUI from urgency.
 • In men treated for CaP, establish disease status, the impact of planned adjuvant therapy, and life
   expectancy.

Physical Examination
 • Examine abdomen, back, genitalia, perineum, rectum and nervous system. Assess manual dexterity if
   AUS is considered.
 • Treat skin breakdown or infection before surgery. Note incisions for PRB planning, and inguinal hernia or
   repair if the external-ring route is planned. Scrotal pathology (hydrocele, hernia, mass) affects pump
   placement.

Patient Reported Outcome Measures
 • Severity and bother guide the operation; patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) add to history
   and examination.
 • UCLA Prostate Cancer Index and Expanded Prostate Cancer Index Composite (EPIC) capture post-RP SUI
   and UUI, mainly in research; clinical performance and responsiveness are not formally assessed.
   Incontinence Symptom Index and Incontinence Impact Questionnaire-7 have measured AUS outcomes
   but were not developed for male SUI.

Voiding Diaries
 • 2–7 days: UI episode frequency, bladder capacity, and urgency or UUI (separates SUI from UUI).

Pad Weight Testing
 • Objective severity measure, since pad counts and change thresholds vary: pre-weighed pads worn
   (usually 24 hours) → reweighed → difference = urine loss.
 • Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine & Urogenital Reconstruction (SUFU) Pad Test:
   questionnaire on number, size and wetness of 24-hour pads — correlates with actual loss, not validated
   to predict surgical outcome.

Standing Cough Test
 • Office test (stand, cough forcefully, observe); correlates with 24-hour pad weight, which rises with grade.
   Office Valsalva also demonstrates SUI.

Laboratory
 • Urinalysis and culture mandatory; investigate abnormalities; eradicate urinary tract infection (UTI).
 • Creatinine and PSA (renal function, cancer status); renal insufficiency needs evaluation first.

Cystoscopy
 • Mandatory if VUAS, bladder neck contracture or stricture is suspected — unrecognised obstruction
   worsens SUI surgery.
 • Grades residual sphincter function to select for a transobturator sling; poorly coapting sphincter → AUS.
 • Also used after AUS (mechanical failure, leak, atrophy) and after explant for erosion, before
   reimplantation, to exclude stricture or diverticulum.

Urodynamics
 • Capacity, compliance, contractility; intrinsic sphincter deficiency in nearly all.
 • Not routine; use when diagnosis is in doubt: mixed UI, SUI not shown in clinic, overflow, suspected poor
   compliance.
 • Mixed UI with DO: counsel that UUI may persist.
 • Poor compliance is the more serious finding (high-pressure storage harms kidneys). It does not predict
   worse AUS continence — so good continence may hide silent upper-tract deterioration.
 • Detrusor hypocontractility → AUS: the bladder may not overcome a sling's added resistance.

 KEY POINTS — EVALUATION
   • History and examination characterise type, severity and capacity in most.
   • Uroflowmetry and PVR screen for obstruction.
   • Obstruction, overactivity or poor contractility → UDS; urethral, vesicourethral or bladder neck
     pathology → cystoscopy; urinalysis ± culture before surgery.

Concept — Evaluation asks three questions before any device: is this really SUI, is the bladder safe to store
against more resistance, and how much sphincter remains? The answers select the device.

INDICATIONS FOR SURGERY
 • Indication: irreversible sphincter incompetence with UI impairing QoL.
 • Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) ± biofeedback or electrical stimulation works after RP: shortens time
   to continence, improves 1- and 3-month continence, no benefit beyond 12 months.
 • Do not delay surgery for severe or gravitational SUI unimproved at 6 months, especially with a
   cystoscopic sphincter defect.
 • Shared decision-making; weigh QoL gain against performance status and life expectancy.
 • First-line: AUS and perineal slings. Bulking agents are outside the post-RP and post-transurethral
   resection of the prostate (TURP) algorithm — only for small-volume leakage in men unfit for more.
 • Choice also depends on cognition, dexterity, body mass index, prior radiotherapy, prior surgery including
   urethroplasty, and bladder function (e.g. neurogenic).

Compression Devices
Artificial Urinary Sphincter
 • Standard for moderate-to-severe SUI with adequate cognition and dexterity; works without detrusor
   contraction and after radiation. AMS 800 has the longest follow-up; social continence = 0–1 pad/day
   (outcomes in table below).
 • Success predictors: surgeon experience, volume, intact compliance. Reoperation is common.
ProACT
 • Fewer pads, better QoL. Versus male sling (randomised): similar continence, more erosion; worse than
   sling after radiotherapy.

Slings
 • Perineal slings — mild-to-moderate SUI, nonadjustable or adjustable. Commonest: Advance / Advance
   XP (transobturator). Adjustable slings have limited data; ATOMS beats ProACT on dryness,
   improvement, satisfaction and pad use.

 • Sling failure: radiation, severity, previous surgery.
 • Severity thresholds (24-hour pad weight): mild <150 g; moderate <400 g. Transobturator slings are
   primarily for mild-to-moderate UI.
 • MASTER trial — randomised controlled trial (RCT) of AUS versus male sling. Primary result: the sling was
   non-inferior to AUS for continence at 12 months. The chapter's clinical emphasis is AUS superiority
   where 24-hour pad weight is >250 g or after radiotherapy.
 • Slings as an alternative in more severe SUI: men who refuse AUS for fear of infection, erosion or
   mechanical failure, and men with limited physical or cognitive capacity.

Concept — The AUS works regardless of sphincter or detrusor function but demands a competent operator
and healthy urethra. The sling demands a residual sphincter, an unirradiated urethra and a contractile
bladder. Each contraindication is the mirror of the other device's requirement.

SURGICAL DECISION MAKING IN THE HIGH-RISK PATIENT
 • "Fragile urethra" = increased risk of AUS cuff erosion. Established risk factors: prior pelvic radiotherapy,
   previous failed AUS (erosion or infection), previous urethroplasty, hypogonadism.

Prior Pelvic Radiotherapy
 • Microvascular injury to the spongiosum, despite modern delivery: erosion about 3–33% irradiated
   versus 1–13% not; irradiated men erode sooner (median about 1 versus 3 years).
 • Radiation predicts the first explant, not failure of later implants — those are driven by prior erosion and
   urethroplasty.

Previous Erosion/Infection
 • Coronary artery disease (CAD) and age >80 associated with erosion; diabetes and smoking not shown
   independently (small series — still optimise).
 • Prior erosion: 5-year repeat erosion approaches 50%.

Previous Urethroplasty
 • Greater risk than radiation (multi-institutional); 10-year revision-free below 50%.
 • Transection divides the bulbourethral arteries (dual blood supply lost); benefit of non-transecting
   urethroplasty is unproven.

Low Testosterone
 • Independently predicts erosion in small series (androgens support urethral health). Androgen
   deprivation therapy not shown to raise risk; testosterone supplementation not shown to lower it.

Strategies to reduce erosion in the high-risk patient
 • Medical optimisation. Erosion site disputed: mostly ventral or lateral in one series; equal dorsal, ventral,
   lateral in a multi-institutional study.
 • Transcorporal (TC) AUS (George Webster, 2002): tunica albuginea interposed between dorsal urethra
   and cuff, preserving vascularity and bulking the thinnest (dorsal) spongiosum; erosion benefit mixed.
 • PRB 51–60 cm H2O or cuff 0.5 cm larger than measured — scarce data. Many men still prefer repeated
   AUS attempts to pads, catheter or diversion.

Concept — Erosion is a problem of spongiosal blood supply under constant pressure. Every risk factor
(radiation, transection, prior erosion, low androgen) reduces perfusion, and every countermeasure (TC cuff,
lower PRB pressure, larger cuff) either adds tissue or lowers the pressure.

TECHNIQUE OF IMPLANTATION OF CURRENTLY APPROVED AND AVAILABLE DEVICES
Operative Preparation for All Implant Surgeries
 • General or spinal; low lithotomy; shave; chlorhexidine–alcohol preparation of lower abdomen, genitals,
   perineum.
 • Intravenous antibiotics within 60 minutes of incision (AUA Best Practice Statement): aminoglycoside 5
   mg/kg + first- or second-generation cephalosporin or vancomycin.
 • Chlorhexidine scrub + iodine povacrylex–alcohol (DuraPrep) cut skin culture rates versus povidone-
   iodine, but not implant infection. Optional urethral catheter aids identification of the urethra.

Artificial Urinary Sphincter
Bulbar Cuff Artificial Urinary Sphincter
 • Site: proximal bulbar urethra, perineal incision, proximal to corporal fusion — safe circumferential
   dissection, cuff protected from opening on sitting, largest spongiosal diameter.
Sequence: incise skin → Colles fascia → bulbospongiosus → incise Buck fascia where it reflects off the
urethra onto the diverging corpora → create a 2-cm tunnel (cuff width) under vision, dorsal to Buck fascia
over the urethral roof → pass a right-angle clamp → measure the circumference around the spongiosum →
choose cuff (usually 4.0 or 4.5 cm) → place → pass tubing through bulbospongiosus into the deep perineal
space beneath Colles fascia.
 • Sizing: a consistent method matters more than catheter in or out. Cuff is slightly tighter than the tape.
   Clearly wrong → next size. Less tight cuffs give better continence. Best test = visual and endoscopic
   appearance.
 • Sizes 3.5–11 cm (0.5-cm steps to 7.5, then 8, 9, 10, 11); large sizes for bladder neck.
 • 3.5-cm cuff: for atrophy after previous AUS or very distal placement; raises erosion after radiation →
   prefer TC and/or 51–60 cm H2O PRB.

Control Pump Placement
 • Anterior scrotum, subdartos. After abdominal PRB: dissect deep to Scarpa fascia → invaginate scrotal

Making the Connections
 • In the abdominal incision when used (less wear, easy re-exploration); quick connectors.
 • Cycle; urethroscopy: closed cuff → slight blanching; open cuff → minimal coaptation; excessive
   coaptation when open → larger cuff (Fig. 123.7).
Tandem Cuff Artificial Urinary Sphincter
 • Up to 11% leak significantly after one cuff; for incomplete occlusion, a second cuff helps. Not first-line:
   distal cuff erodes more and tandem uses up revision sites.
Sequence: perineal incision → dissect the distal bulbar urethra at least 1.5–2 cm distal to the primary cuff →
place new cuff → divide existing cuff tubing → join with a metal Y-connector → add 3 mL fluid → leave
deactivated for 6 weeks.
Transcorporal Cuff
 • Indications: difficult first or revision surgery; urethra <3.5 cm. After prior erosion or urethroplasty, place
   the TC cuff at a different site along the bulbar urethra.
Sequence: parallel longitudinal incisions in the tunica albuginea of each corpus near the spongiosal
interface → dissect through the intercorporal septum from side to side → the ventral tunica stays attached
to the dorsal spongiosum → imbricate the lateral tunical edges behind the urethra (reduces haematoma) →
measure → place cuff (Figs. 123.9D, 123.10).
 • Erections usually preserved in sexually active men despite theoretical risk.
 • Advantages: less urethral injury at reoperation (prior erosion, injury, urethroplasty, rectourethral fistula
   repair); better fit from added bulk; lower erosion unproven. Ideal when atrophy leaves a small distal
   urethra (≤3.5-cm cuff) — a 3.5-cm cuff, prior radiation and prior erosion each raise erosion risk.

 KEY POINTS — IMPLANTATION
   • Bulbar cuff proximal to corporal convergence; PRB 61–70 (abdominal, subinguinal or perineal).
   • Deactivate for 4–6 weeks after surgery — essential to heal without erosion.
   • Non-mechanical revision may need TC, 3.5-cm or tandem cuff.

Concept — Implantation is a trade between urethral bulk and future options. Go as proximal as possible,
where the spongiosum is thickest. Keep distal sites in reserve. When bulk is lacking, borrow it (TC tunica)
rather than squeeze harder (higher PRB or smaller cuff).

MALE SLINGS
Transobturator Bulbourethral Sling
 • Rehder and Gozzi (2007). For SUI after CaP treatment, TURP and laser enucleation. Advance XP (better
   anchoring) replaced Advance.
Sequence: perineal incision → open bulbospongiosus, free it from spongiosum to the perineal body →
mark, then divide perineal body 1–2 cm to release the proximal bulb → open spongiosum–corpus space
until inferior pubic ramus palpable behind each crus → stab incision one fingerbreadth below adductor
longus in groin crease; spinal needle finds foramen → helical trocar outside-in (two "pops"), exiting onto the
fingertip at the spongiosum–ramus apex → draw mesh out without tension; proximal edge at marked
perineal body → four absorbable sutures to spongiosum → tension arms equally under cystoscopy until
the bulbar mucosa coapts (bulb moves 3–4 cm proximally and cephalad; Fig. 123.11) → remove sheaths,
trim and bury arms → close in layers.

ARTIFICIAL URINARY SPHINCTER COMPLICATIONS
Urinary Retention
 • Rare; commoner with TC and 3.5-cm cuffs.
Sequence: confirm cuff deactivated → small urethral catheter for 24–48 hours → fails a second trial at 48
hours → suprapubic cystostomy (to cut erosion risk), under ultrasound or fluoroscopic guidance so the PRB
is not punctured or contaminated → retention lasting several weeks → cuff undersized → reoperate and
replace the cuff; review preoperative urodynamics.
 • Late-onset retention → endoscopy and UDS to exclude proximal obstruction, erosion or detrusor failure.

Artificial Urinary Sphincter Infection
 • 1–3% at first implant; up to 10% high-risk. Skin flora — Staphylococcus epidermidis, S. aureus.
   InhibiZone (rifampicin + minocycline) has not cut AUS infection as it did for penile implants.
 • Late (>4 months): indolent inoculum or haematogenous spread → prophylaxis before urinary tract
   manipulation.
 • Scrotal pain is the key early sign (± erythema, oedema, pus). Antibiotics fail → almost always explant;
   traditionally reimplant months later.
 • Immediate salvage (non-eroded): remove all → seven-solution antiseptic irrigation (penile-prosthesis
   protocol) → new device at once; successful in most.
 • Contraindications to salvage: sepsis, ketoacidosis, necrotising infection, device erosion,
   immunosuppression, gross pus at explant.

Urethral Erosion
 • 2–15%; up to 35% with multiple risk factors (see risk table). Delayed activation lowered erosion,
   especially at reimplantation.
 • Remove all components — assume infected, though most left-in PRBs stay clean. Wound treated as
   infected: loose closure or secondary intention.
 • Stricture after erosion: 8–35%; prior radiation, not erosion extent, predicted it in one series.

 • Chronic compression of spongy tissue; insidious, unlike device failure; a commonest cause of revision.

 • Atrophy disputed: one centre treats all failures as mechanical, same-size cuff, acceptable results; pressure

Mechanical Failure
 • Device life 7–10 years. Within 3 years (no infection or erosion): replace the failed part — usually pump
   or cuff, median ≤1 year.
 • >3 years: PRB is the usual culprit → replace entire device; slow PRB leaks hide at surgery. Ohmmeter or
   burette testing of the PRB may spare total replacement.

 KEY POINTS — AUS COMPLICATIONS
   • Second failed trial at 48 hours → suprapubic cystostomy. Erosion management graded to severity and
     contamination. TC ± low-pressure PRB after erosion. Replace whole AUS if >3 years old.

Concept — Every complication is managed to protect the urethra for the next device. Suprapubic rather than
urethral drainage, total explant at erosion, a new cuff site, and whole-device replacement after 3 years all
serve the same aim: a salvageable urethra.

SPECIAL CIRCUMSTANCES
Managing Device Implantation With Spongiosal Vascular Insufficiency
 • Protect spongiosal blood supply: non-transecting urethroplasty for strictures before AUS; TC AUS, lower-
   pressure PRB; possibly testosterone replacement if hypogonadal (unproven).

Urethral and Vesicourethral Anastomotic Stricture
 • Stricture at the cuff (compression or ischaemia) may herald erosion; periodic dilation has worked.
   Proximal to the cuff → balloon dilation under vision or holmium laser via flexible scope.
 • ROBUST III RCT — U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved paclitaxel-coated urethral balloon
   versus direct vision internal urethrotomy (DVIU) or dilation in recurrent bulbar stricture: 1-year freedom
   from intervention 83.2% versus 21.7%; 67% functional success at 3 years. No data yet for VUAS.
 • Post-erosion stricture → urethroplasty → repeat AUS (often TC): about a quarter re-erode within 2
   years.
 • VUAS after AUS → balloon or ureteroscopic laser. Large scope (22–24 Fr) proximal to cuff → surgically
   uncouple the cuff. Refractory → robotic reconstruction, sparing the cuff.
 • Timing: traditionally treat VUAS before AUS; but non-obliterated VUAS (<12 Fr) dilated synchronously
   showed no difference in retreatment or erosion.

Urinary Incontinence After Cystectomy and Neobladder Reconstruction
 • SUI after orthotopic neobladder: 33–95%; continence drives QoL.
 • AUS helps about three-quarters, but 60% need revision (about 2 years), over a third for erosion or
   infection — radiotherapy, bacterial colonisation, clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC).
 • PRB ectopic, lateral counter-incision, behind rectus — avoids neobladder or bowel.

Dual Implantation of Artificial Urinary Sphincter and Inflatable Penile Prosthesis
 • Synchronous = metachronous AUS + inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) for device survival and reoperation
   (meta-analysis); high satisfaction. Dual implants are revised more than either alone (about 2× IPP, 1.7×
   AUS).

Climacturia
 • 20–68% after RP, including leakage on arousal. Condom and pre-coital voiding help little; authors use
   sling or AUS by severity.
 • Mini-jupette at IPP: 3-cm corporotomies → graft sutured to medial edges → inflation tightens graft

   • VUAS after AUS → balloon or ureteroscopic laser; synchronous AUS + IPP safe; mini-jupette for

Concept — In each special case the cuff is the fixed asset. Instrumentation is minimised, the cuff is uncoupled
or bypassed, and other devices are routed away from it, because anything that stresses the bulbar urethra
risks losing continence.

Registries
 • Long-term data are small, retrospective, single-institution → registries and prospective trials (AUS versus
   sling, ATOMS versus AUS, Virtue).

Surgical Training
 • >150,000 men implanted, yet many urologists never do SUI surgery; fellowships and simulation should
   widen access.

SUMMARY
 • Demand persists with ongoing surgery for localised CaP. Work-up adds pad test, uroflowmetry, PVR;
   cystoscopy and UDS selectively.
 • AUS = preferred for moderate-to-severe and complex SUI. Slings for mild-to-moderate UI without prior
   urethral surgery, radiotherapy or detrusor acontractility; unproven for neurogenic or post-traumatic UI.
 • AUS–sling comparison is confounded by severity, endpoints, follow-up and selection bias; MASTER is the
   only RCT.
 • Nearly 90% improve in urinary QoL, whatever the severity. Erosion and mechanical failure drive
   innovation.
Concept — The chapter reduces to one decision: how much sphincter, how healthy a urethra and how
capable a patient. A residual sphincter and good tissue allow a sling; anything less, or radiation, points to the
AUS. Everything after implantation is about preserving the urethra for the next revision.
