Chapter 160 — Open Radical Prostatectomy                                                                      Introduction

Chapter 160 — Open Radical Prostatectomy
 • Most radical prostatectomies (RPs) in the United States and Europe are now robot-assisted, despite no
   evidence of better cancer control, fewer technical complications, or better continence or potency.
 • Open surgery still matters: its lessons transfer to robotic surgery, it serves systems without a robot, and it
   is the fallback on conversion.
 • Total surgical removal is the best cure for clinically localised prostate cancer. RP is the only treatment
   shown in a randomised controlled trial (Bill-Axelson; Scandinavian Prostate Cancer Group-4, SPCG-4) to
   reduce metastasis and prostate cancer death.
 • Better understanding of periprostatic anatomy → less bleeding, fewer technical complications, better
   continence and potency.
 • Goals, in order of importance: cancer control → minimise technical complications → continence →
   potency.
 • Open radical retropubic prostatectomy (ORRP) is among the hardest operations in urology, demanding
   skill in both patient selection and technique.
Concept — The order of goals settles every intraoperative trade-off: when nerve sparing and a clear margin
conflict, the margin wins.

RADICAL RETROPUBIC PROSTATECTOMY: SURGICAL ANATOMY

Venous and Arterial Anatomy
Venous drainage
 • Prostatic veins drain into the Santorini plexus. Knowing it precisely is what gives a bloodless field at the
   membranous urethra and apex.
 • Deep dorsal vein: leaves the penis beneath Buck's fascia between the corpora → pierces the urogenital
   diaphragm → three branches: superficial branch + right and left lateral venous plexuses.
 • Superficial branch: central vein between the puboprostatic ligaments over the bladder neck and prostate;
   seen early; communicates over the bladder and into the endopelvic fascia; lies outside the anterior
   prostatic fascia.
 • Common trunk and lateral plexuses: hidden beneath the prostatic and endopelvic fascia. The plexuses
   run posterolaterally and communicate freely with the pudendal, obturator and vesical plexuses; near the
   puboprostatic ligaments small branches pierce the sidewall muscle to the internal pudendal vein.
 • Lateral plexus → inferior vesical vein → internal iliac vein. Because the veins anastomose freely, any
   laceration of these friable veins can bleed heavily.
 • The common trunk lies directly over the urethra — the site where the dorsal vein is transected.
Arterial supply
 • The inferior vesical artery supplies the prostate. After small branches to the seminal vesicle, bladder base
   and prostate, it ends in two groups:

Pelvic Plexus
 • Autonomic supply to pelvic organs and external genitalia. Formed by parasympathetic preganglionic
   visceral efferents from the sacral centre (S2–S4) + sympathetic fibres from the thoracolumbar centre via
   the hypogastric nerve.
 • Location: retroperitoneal beside the rectum, 5–11 cm from the anal verge; a fenestrated rectangular
   sagittal plate with its midpoint at the seminal vesicle tip.
 • Inferior vesical vessels perforate the plexus → ligating the "lateral pedicle" in its midportion cuts both
   vessels and the nerves to prostate, urethra and corpora.
 • Visceral branches: bladder, ureter, seminal vesicles, prostate, rectum, membranous urethra, corpora.
   Somatic motor axons also pass through to the levator ani, coccygeus and striated urethral muscle.
 • All these nerves stay outside the capsule: prostatic nerves run outside the capsule and Denonvilliers
   fascia until they pierce it; branches to the membranous urethra and corpora run in the lateral pelvic
   fascia, dorsolateral to the prostate, between prostate and rectum.
 • Neurovascular bundle (NVB), "NVB of Walsh": capsular vessels + cavernous nerves. The nerves are
   invisible; the vessels mark where they are.
 • Cavernous branches join the capsular vessels in a spray-like pattern, forming the NVB 20–30 mm distal
   to the prostatovesical junction, in the lateral pelvic fascia between prostatic and levator fascia.
 • At the apex, branches to the cavernous bodies and sphincter spray anteriorly and posteriorly, with wide
   variation, some leaving the vessels to run anteriorly — the anatomical basis of high anterior release.
 • Beyond the urogenital diaphragm the nerves pass behind the dorsal penile artery and nerve into the
   corpora.

Striated Urethral Sphincter
 • Not a horizontal "sandwich" but a vertical tubular sheath (Oelrich) around the membranous urethra.
 • Why: in utero it runs unbroken from bladder to perineal membrane; the prostate, growing from the
   urethra, invades and thins it, and part atrophies.
 • Adult: horseshoe-shaped fibres at the apex form a tube, fusing in the posterior midline near the apex. The
   prostate does not sit "like an apple on a shelf" on a flat diaphragm; the sphincter attaches broadly over
   the apical prostatic fascia — which is why apical dissection and urethral reconstruction decide
   continence.
 • At the apex the NVBs lie posterolateral to the circumferential sphincter.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Preoperative Preparation
 • Assessment: focused systems review; full medical, surgical and anaesthetic history; all medicines,
   prescribed or not; examination; routine blood tests.
 • Rare life-threatening risks — myocardial infarction, stroke, arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism,
   haemorrhage, anaesthetic reaction. Identify at-risk men to reduce risk or offer alternatives.
 • Factors that make surgery harder but do not contraindicate it: previous abdominal or pelvic surgery or
   radiotherapy, transurethral surgery, extensive biopsies, significant inflammatory bowel disease, hernia
   mesh, and prostate size.
 • Inguinal hernia: about 15% have one on proper preoperative examination; up to 20% develop one after
   ORRP. Most were silent before, so ORRP seems to unmask them → examine standing with Valsalva and
   repair preperitoneally at ORRP. Standing Valsalva ultrasonography finds even more; with this approach,
   symptomatic hernias afterwards are rare.
 • Defer surgery 6 weeks after needle biopsy and 12 weeks after transurethral resection of the prostate
   (TURP), so adhesions and haematoma settle and planes return to near normal — crucial for NVB
   preservation and avoiding rectal injury.

 • No autologous donation: transfusion is rare and donated units go unused unless anaemic, so donation
   only deepens postoperative anaemia. Preoperative erythropoiesis-stimulating agents raise haematocrit
   and reduce anaemia; higher discharge haematocrit speeds recovery.
 • Prophylaxis when the urinary tract is entered: first- or second-generation cephalosporin, or
   aminoglycoside + metronidazole or clindamycin.
 • Antiplatelet and anticoagulant plans are made with the internist or cardiologist. Drug-eluting coronary
   stents pit stent thrombosis (usually catastrophic) against bleeding; low-dose aspirin often gives enough
   cover without extra bleeding at ORRP.
 • Bowel preparation: clear fluids the day before → half a bottle of magnesium citrate that evening →
   enema that morning; same-day admission.
 • Pretreatment magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can guide how much NVB and membranous urethra to
   preserve.

Special Instruments
 • Fibreoptic headlight: essential — much of the operation is beneath the pubis, where the view is poor.
 • Balfour retractor with narrow and wide malleable blades: helpful for lymphadenectomy, required for RP
   (cranial and posterior retraction of peritoneum and bladder).
 • Optional: coagulating forceps, small, fine and standard right-angled clamps, Metzenbaum and Jamison
   scissors, 2.5–4.5× loupes (help the apical dissection).

Anaesthesia, Incision, and Lymphadenectomy
 • General endotracheal anaesthesia. Relative hypotension (systolic ≤100 mm Hg) and crystalloid ≤1500
   mL until the prostate is out.
 • Supine; flex the table in obese men to lengthen the umbilicus–pubis distance.
 • 16-Fr silicone (Silastic) Foley, 20 mL balloon, closed drainage — the small calibre eases mucosal suturing.
   A right-handed surgeon stands on the patient's left.
 • Incision: extraperitoneal lower midline, pubis towards umbilicus → anterior fascia to pubis → recti
   separated → transversalis fascia opened → space of Retzius → peritoneum lifted off external iliacs to the
   common iliac bifurcation, vas left intact → Balfour placed.
 • Leave the tissue over the external iliac artery: it carries the leg lymphatics; dividing it risks leg oedema
   and lymphocele.
 • Exposure: narrow malleable blade under the vas lifts the peritoneum; deep Deaver retracts the bladder
   medially.
 • Pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND) precedes RP for intermediate- and high-risk disease; omitted for
   well-differentiated or low-volume favourable intermediate-risk disease (Gleason Grade Group 1–2).
 • PLND sequence (dominant-tumour side first): divide adventitia over the external iliac vein, sparing the
   arterial lymphatics → under the vein to the sidewall → down to the femoral canal, ligating lymphatics
   (node of Cloquet need not be removed) → up to the common iliac bifurcation, clearing the external iliac–
   hypogastric angle → obturator nodes, protecting the obturator nerve; obturator vessels skeletonised,
   ligated only if bleeding → down to the pelvic floor, exposing hypogastric veins → repeat contralaterally.
 • Extended dissection removes more nodes than a limited one → better staging and possible therapeutic
   benefit.

Exposure
 • Malleable blade lifts the peritoneum and gently displaces the bladder back, exposing the anterior
   prostate.

Incision in the Endopelvic Fascia
 • Clear fibroadipose tissue → expose pelvic fascia, puboprostatic ligaments, superficial dorsal vein.
 • Open the endopelvic fascia at its sidewall reflection, well away from bladder and prostate, where it is
   transparent over the levator ani.
 • Too medial → lateral Santorini plexus → persistent venous bleeding; the prostatic arteries and pelvic
   plexus branches lie beneath it.
 • Extend anteromedially towards the puboprostatic ligaments → the lateral prostate becomes palpable.
 • Pudendal perforators through the pelvic muscle → clip, don't coagulate: the pudendal artery and nerve
   lie just deep, along the pubic ramus.

Division of the Puboprostatic Ligaments
 • Tease fibrofat off the superficial vein and ligaments → free the vein from the medial ligament edge →
   coagulate and divide it → sponge stick pushes the prostate back → divide each ligament with scissors.
 • Go deep enough to expose the apex–anterior DVC junction, where the DVC will be divided.
 • Spare the pubourethral component: it anchors the striated sphincter anteriorly to the pubis.

Preservation of Accessory Pudendal Arteries
 • Arterial insufficiency contributes to erectile dysfunction after nerve sparing; accessory pudendals over
   the anterolateral prostate are one cause.
 • Prevalence depends on the method: about 70% in cadavers, about 7% on selective angiography; large
   visible vessels in about 4%.
 • Releasing them can bleed briskly, forcing prompt DVC ligation → complete the opposite side first when a
   prominent vessel is unilateral.
 • Sequence: divide endopelvic fascia lateral to the vessel and the puboprostatic ligament (vessel runs
   beneath it) → vessel loop → free the artery sharply with a right-angled clamp, lifting the anterior prostatic
   fascia → divide venous tributaries (bleeding controlled by running 4-0 absorbable) → continue caudally
   past the planned DVC division.

Ligation and Division of the Dorsal Vein Complex
 • Aim: divide the DVC with minimal bleeding, without injuring the sphincter or entering the anterior apex.

Division of the Dorsal Vein Complex
 • Sponge stick presses the prostate down → Metzenbaum or No. 15 blade, starting at the left edge, where
   the apical junction shows best. The anteriorly fixed distal DVC opens the plane between prostate and
   striated muscle; loupes and resting the scissors on the anterior apex help find it.
 • Anterior apex = commonest site of positive margins, because its surface is hard to see.
 • Caveat: near the apex an apical vessel tethers the NVB medially beneath the sphincter, so cutting too
   close to the apex risks it → divide the lateral sphincter edges obliquely, midway between apex and
   pelvic floor.
 • Venous control must be absolute so the rest is bloodless: the kept 3-0 Monocryl (5/8-circle) oversews the
   distal sphincter–DVC edge with a loosely held driver, forming a hood over the anterior urethra;
   circumflex veins at 5 and 7 o'clock → clips.
 • Proximal back-bleeding: oversew the proximal DVC edges on the prostate in a V with the preplaced 2-0 —
   this also spreads tension evenly over the prostatic fascia, easing high release.

Division of the Urethra and Placement of Urethral Sutures
 • Standard nerve release → divide the urethra first; high anterior release → after nerve release.
 • Sequence: prostate pushed back → release lateral sphincter bands at their midpoint → right-angled
   clamp around urethral smooth muscle close to the apex (transect as near the apex as possible) → divide
   anterior two-thirds, sparing the catheter → place sutures → remove catheter, place 6-o'clock suture →
   divide posterior urethra.
 • The clamp separates posterior urethra from posterior sphincter and, in a dry field, shows the sphincter–
   NVB relationship; the intact posterior sphincter shields the NVBs.
 • Six 3-0 Monocryl sutures (5/8-circle tapered): text gives 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 o'clock; operative figures show 12,
   2, 5, 6, 7, 10 o'clock.
 • Mucosa and submucosa only — smooth-muscle bites delay continence. The 16-Fr catheter makes the
   mucosa easy to see.
 • Flimsy urethra → include the DVC in the first (anterior) suture for strength. The first sutures lift the
   mucosa, easing the rest.
 • Placed outside → in; if inside → out is easier, a French-eye needle passes them from the bladder side at
   the end. Towels over the sutures prevent traction.
Posterior striated sphincter complex (skeletal muscle + fibrous tissue) — precise division matters for four
reasons:
 • (1) apical margins; (2) the right plane on the anterior rectum, so all of Denonvilliers fascia is excised; (3)
   no blunt trauma to the NVBs just behind it; (4) continence.
 • Sequence: clamp under the left edge midway between apex and urethra (here the NVB has fallen back
   beneath the clamp) → divide → right edge → centre last.
 • Never divide it all from one side — that endangers the contralateral NVB; a clamp too close to the apex
   endangers the NVB.

Identification and Preservation of the Nerve Bundle
 • Both NVBs can usually be preserved safely; excising both is rarely needed. With modern technique and
   phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) inhibitors, most healthy potent men under 65 stay potent.
 • Extracapsular extension rarely exceeds 1–2 mm — and that much tissue often remains even with the
   NVB preserved.

 • Excising the NVB does not prevent positive margins, because most margins are elsewhere: apex →
   posterior → posterolateral; about a fifth are multiple. Margin rates are the same with and without nerve
   sparing.
 • Walsh series: bilateral preservation 87%, one NVB excised 13%, overall positive margins about 5%.
   Penetration severe enough to need both excised implies probable distant spread and incurability; in
   practice one side usually proves free.
 • The decision is never final before surgery. Inputs: erectile status — continence is the same after bilateral
   or unilateral sparing and whether or not erections return, so NVBs are spared less aggressively in men
   already impotent; palpable apical lesion; high predicted penetration on Partin tables or MRI.
 • Intraoperative triggers for wide excision on that side: induration in the lateral pelvic fascia once the
   endopelvic fascia is open; too little tissue over the posterolateral surface after removal (NVB excised
   secondarily).

Identification of the Neurovascular Bundle
 • Touch is open surgery's advantage: induration → excise; bundle fixed to the prostate on gentle release
   → don't preserve. Touch is dulled laparoscopically and absent robotically.
 • No upward traction; roll the prostate side to side. With the catheter out the prostate is softer and the
   plane easier to find.

Standard Preservation of the Neurovascular Bundles
 • Starts after urethral division, suture placement and posterior sphincter division.
 • Sequence:
     ◦ Babcock or sponge stick rolls the prostate over, so the prostate is released from the bundle, not the
       bundle from the prostate (less traction).
     ◦ Right-angled clamp under the superficial levator fascia from the bladder neck (dividing its thick band
       there frees the prostate at once) to the apex; loupes confirm the prostatic fascia is intact and allow
       gentler dissection. Releasing laterally first makes the posterior apical release easier.
     ◦ Locate the NVB by a subtle posterolateral "groove" → trace it to the apex, where the NVB turns
       caudolaterally to the urethra.
     ◦ Find the medial border at the apex → develop the midline plane back to the rectum.
     ◦ Release from the rectal surface, apex → base, spreading a right-angled clamp → clip and divide apical
       vessels → to the midpoint → to the seminal vesicle.
     ◦ Posterior arterial branch from the NVB over the seminal vesicle to the prostatic base → ligate and
       divide each side → the NVBs fall back, untethered.

 • Apical kinking artefact: with the prostate rolled across the midline the NVB looks kinked and can be cut
   by mistake; it straightens once apical vessels are divided.
 • NVB vessels: small clips parallel to the bundle; never thermal energy (monopolar, bipolar, harmonic).
   The prostatic side of tiny vessels is simply cut with fine scissors.
 • Fixation not explained by vessels → excise the NVB.
 • Why start at the apex: branches are seen before they are cut; an anteriorly placed bundle could have its
   groove mistaken for the prostatorectal space; lateral veins joining Santorini plexus to NVB are easier to
   control.
 • Working from the rectal side keeps Denonvilliers and prostatic fascia on the gland (only levator
   fragments come away) → iatrogenic margins in organ-confined disease become rare, and a mobile bundle
   can be felt and inspected → partial excision with negative margin and preserved potency is possible
   even with extension near the NVB.

High Anterior Release of the Neurovascular Bundles at the Apex
 • Purpose: faster recovery of potency and continence by less traction on nerves to the cavernous bodies
   and sphincter, and by not cutting small anterior branches.
 • Risk: less apical soft tissue → possibly more positive margins, so select men at low risk of extension near
   the NVB.

 • Passing the clamp lateral → medial makes rectal injury more likely.
 • NVB and lateral pelvic fascia come away under direct vision, generously covering the tumour.

Posterior Dissection and Division of the Lateral Pedicles
 • Replace catheter → light upward catheter traction (possible now the NVBs are free) → divide the
   rectum–Denonvilliers attachment in the midline, leaving all Denonvilliers layers over the seminal
   vesicles.
 • Sponge stick on the catheter, never on the prostate — displaced tissue can create a false-positive
   margin.
 • Divide the lateral pedicle on the lateral seminal vesicle (NVB already clear) in three layers: superficial →
   middle → deep; all at once risks cutting into the prostate. Clip arterial bleeders — more tissue stays on
   the prostate and the NVBs are protected.
 • Continue onto the anterolateral bladder–prostate junction → divide Denonvilliers fascia over the seminal
   vesicle tips; many surgeons now divide the vasa and free the seminal vesicles.

Division of the Bladder Neck and Excision of the Seminal Vesicles
 • Sequence: incise anterior bladder neck at the prostatovesical junction → through mucosa → deflate
   balloon, clamp catheter ends together for traction → widen → divide inferior vesical branches at 5 and 7
   o'clock → find the plane between anterior seminal vesicles and posterior bladder → divide posterior
   bladder neck hugging the seminal vesicles, watching the ureteric orifices → Allis on bladder neck → clip
   and divide vasa → free seminal vesicles → ligate tip arteries → divide residual Denonvilliers attachments
   → remove specimen.
 • Pelvic plexus lies on the lateral seminal vesicle → dissect under direct vision, especially laterally, staying
   on the seminal vesicle and clipping small arteries.
 • Complete seminal vesicle excision is essential for cancer control.
 • Inspect the specimen: doubtful posterolateral margin → excise that NVB.
 • Near the NVB, clips not cautery: a rectovesical haematoma inflames the NVBs and delays sexual
   recovery → a small peritoneal window in the rectovesical cul-de-sac lets any haematoma decompress.

Bladder Neck Reconstruction and Anastomosis
 • Tennis-racquet closure: running or interrupted 2-0 absorbable, full thickness (including mucosa prevents
   haematuria), posterior midline forwards, until the neck matches the urethra.
 • Ureteric orifices: earlier indigo carmine helps; ureteric catheters usually unnecessary.
 • Mucosal eversion: 4-0 absorbable advances mucosa over raw muscle → rosette → mucosa-to-mucosa
   anastomosis. 6-o'clock suture left long for urethral suture placement.
 • Intussusception — buttressing sutures stop the neck pulling open as the bladder fills:
     ◦ Remove nearby clips (they can fold into the neck → contracture) → 2-0 Maxon in the posterior
       bladder wall edges about 2 cm from the neck, tied midline → slacken the malleable blade to show
       loose perivesical tissue → second figure-of-eight 2-0 Maxon anteriorly, about 2 cm lateral to the neck
       each side, tied.
     ◦ End point: "turtle sign" — neck poking out beneath the anterior hood like a turtle's head. Saline fill →
       competent, little leak → empty with forceps through the neck.
     ◦ With intussusception, most men are pad-free by 3 months and nearly all by 1 year.

 • Margin–continence trade-off: this technique favours ample apical margins and may take more sphincter,
   so some men take longer to be pad-free. Preserving the puboprostatic ligaments and dissecting closer to
   the apex may spare sphincter but risks inadequate apical margins.
 • Anastomosis: check haemostasis → new 16-Fr silicone Foley → pass each 3-0 Monocryl through the
   bladder neck at its matching position, inside → out (6-o'clock traction everts mucosa; French-eye needle
   for 12, 2 and 5 o'clock) → irrigate, test balloon, pass catheter, inflate 15 mL → Babcock on the anterior
   bladder holds the neck down while tying — no catheter traction → anterior suture tied first, then 2, 5,
   10, 7, 6 o'clock.
 • No tension; if present, free the bladder from the peritoneum. Babcock coaptation has markedly reduced
   bladder neck contracture.
 • Closure: check catheter not caught → irrigate → small suction drain through fascia away from midline,
   between (not through) the recti → running No. 2 nylon, skin clips → catheter taped to thigh.

Postoperative Management
 • Usually uneventful. The Walsh technique cut mean stay to about 1.7 days, with urine leak, transfusion,
   ileus and re-exploration for bleeding all well under 1%.
 • Mobilise the evening of surgery; discharge day 1–2. Clear fluids that evening, low-fat diet next day. Single
   closed-suction drain out at discharge or when <50 mL/day off suction.
 • Analgesia: intravenous patient-controlled opioid (hydromorphone, fentanyl, morphine) or ketorolac,
   preferred to spare opioids; intravenous paracetamol works similarly but is costly. The combination
   reduces ileus.
 • Ketorolac (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, NSAID) only if creatinine <1.2 mg/dL, blood loss <1500
   mL, no uncontrolled diabetes, no postoperative haematuria; given before recovery transfer or next
   morning.
 • Catheter: very bothersome, delays return to activity and work → remove as early as safe; the risks are
   retention and bladder neck contracture. Never with significant extravasation.
 • Most anastomoses are watertight on cystography by day 4, yet removal then causes frequent acute
   retention — so not recommended.
 • Delayed healing is almost always a perianastomotic haematoma distracting the anastomosis.
 • Removal at 1 week, three ways: routine cystography (early removal, identifies the few needing longer
   drainage); transperineal saline sonocystography (no contrast cost or radiation); or no imaging if
   haematocrit falls <6% from recovery to discharge.
 • Bladder spasms usually settle with reassurance; if severe, oral oxybutynin 5 mg or oral diazepam 5–10
   mg.
Concept — Every step does one of three jobs: secure absolute venous control before touching the apex; keep
the right fascia on the specimen; keep traction and heat off the NVB. Apex-first, side-to-side, clip-not-cautery
and stapled or sutured DVC control all follow from these.

Postoperative Complications
 • Life-threatening delayed haemorrhage is rare. Significant bleeding = needing acute transfusion to
   support blood pressure. Most are managed expectantly.
 • Explored vs conservative: similar blood use, but shorter stay after re-exploration. Left alone, the
   haematoma may drain through the anastomosis → bladder neck contracture and long-term
   incontinence; only a minority of explored men have prolonged mild incontinence.
 • Signs of major bleeding in recovery — hypotension, tachycardia, falling urine output, lower abdominal
   distension → early exploration to evacuate the haematoma, preventing prolonged ileus, anastomotic
   dehiscence, prolonged catheterisation and contracture. Immediate exploration after resuscitation often
   still allows discharge on day 1–2.

Thromboembolic Events
 • Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) with pulmonary embolism (PE) is the major potential cause of death after
   RP.
 • Risk highest with PLND and peaks 14–28 days after surgery → stay alert for 4 weeks.
 • Prevention: careful positioning to avoid compressing leg veins, intermittent pneumatic compression,
   early ambulation. Some centres add mini-dose heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH); the
   authors do not, as their DVT and PE rates are very low.
 • An informed patient is the best protection: repeated verbal and written advice to call at once with calf or
   leg swelling or pain, pleuritic chest pain, haemoptysis, breathlessness, or sudden weakness or fainting.

Bladder Neck Contracture
 • Historically 0.5–10%, now much lower.
 • Causes: poor approximation, urinary extravasation, haematoma distracting the bladder neck.
 • Typically presents at about 3 months with a poor stream or regressing continence.
 • Treatment ladder: cystoscopic dilatation → cold-knife incision at 3, 6 and 9 o'clock + short-term
   intermittent self-catheterisation → recalcitrant: triamcinolone acetonide 200 mg in 5 mL injected after
   incision → very rarely, reconstruction with an artificial urinary sphincter.

Urinary Incontinence
 • Mechanism: usually intrinsic sphincter deficiency. Few men over 65 (population registry data) need an
   incontinence operation within 2 years.
 • Main cause: sphincter injury during DVC ligation and division. Contributors: a poorly developed
   sphincter, or a thinner, more collagenous one in older men; smooth muscle damaged by large deep
   anastomotic sutures or denervated by NVB injury; contracted or over-wide bladder neck.
 • Prevention: preserve the sphincter at the apex; tension-free anastomosis; small, supple bladder neck;
   precise mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis.
 • Pre-existing outlet obstruction (detrusor hypertrophy, poor compliance) slows recovery → avoid
   excessive bladder traction intraoperatively.
 • Until dry: less fluid; no caffeine or alcohol; stop α-adrenergic antagonists taken for hypertension or
   benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). By severity and cause: imipramine, phenylephrine, anticholinergics or
   adrenergic agonists.
 • Continence defined as 0–1 pad per 24 h.

Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms
 • ORRP improves lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in men with moderate-to-severe International

 • Benefit lasts up to 15 years → RP appears to alter the natural history of BPH and LUTS.
Concept — Most late complications trace to one event, the pelvic haematoma: it distracts the anastomosis,
causes contracture and incontinence, and inflames the NVBs. Hence early re-exploration, clips near the
bundles and a peritoneal window.

SUMMARY OF OPEN RADICAL RETROPUBIC PROSTATECTOMY
 • More men are diagnosed younger with curable disease, and live longer: a man aged 65–70 has a 50%
   chance of 15 more years.
 • ORRP is ideal for men who can be cured and will live long enough to benefit — who also have the best
   postoperative quality of life.
 • Robot-assisted RP has spread over the last decade; its main advantage is less blood loss, and even
   proponents concede no meaningful overall advantage. Some of the authors still operate only open.

 KEY POINTS: ANATOMIC OPEN RADICAL RETROPUBIC PROSTATECTOMY
   • Total removal = best cure for organ-confined cancer.
   • Goals: cancer → complications → continence → potency.
   • Defer 6 weeks after biopsy, 12 weeks after TURP.
   • Fibreoptic headlight: most useful instrument (surgery beneath the pubis).
   • Grade Group 1–2, low-volume favourable → no PLND.
   • Anterior apical nerve branches → high anterior release before DVC division → earlier potency.
   • Both NVBs usually preserved; bilateral excision rarely needed.
   • Induration → excise bundle; fixed bundle → don't preserve; touch dulled laparoscopically, absent
     robotically.
   • Transfusion for hypotension → explore, evacuate haematoma (prevents contracture and
     incontinence).
   • Thromboembolism = major cause of death; positioning, compression devices, early ambulation.
