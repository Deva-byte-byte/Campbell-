ANATOMIC CONSIDERATIONS
Male Urethra
 • Length 18–20 cm. The International Consultation on Urological Diseases (ICUD, Morocco, 2010) divides it
   into seven areas: meatus, fossa navicularis, penile, bulbar, membranous, prostatic urethra, and bladder
   neck (Fig. 15.1 numbers the six beyond the meatus).
 • By common usage the anterior urethra = fossa navicularis, penile (pendulous) and bulbar; the posterior
   urethra = membranous, prostatic and bladder neck.

Female Urethra
 • About 4 cm, lying within the anterior vaginal wall.

INDICATIONS FOR LOWER URINARY TRACT DRAINAGE
 • Drainage is either indwelling (Foley) or straight (intermittent). Straight catheterisation serves temporary
   drainage in acute retention and sterile urine collection, especially in children and women.

TABLE 15.1 LIST OF COMMON INDICATIONS FOR PLACEMENT OF AN INDWELLING URETHRAL CATHETER
 Purpose                               Indications
 Relieve retention or poor             Acute retention; chronic retention; incomplete emptying; urosepsis with
 emptying                              incomplete emptying
 Bleeding                              Gross haematuria and clot retention
 Protect against high pressure         High-pressure voiding; high-pressure (poorly compliant) bladder

 Postoperative healing                 After urologic surgery, e.g. urethroplasty, prostatectomy

 • The list reduces to four aims — empty, protect the upper tracts, monitor, allow healing.

Contraindications
 • Absolute: known or suspected urethral injury. Suspect it with pelvic fracture, gross haematuria, blood at
   the meatus, perineal ecchymosis, and a high-riding prostate on digital rectal examination (DRE) →
   retrograde urethrogram (RUG) before any Foley. Blind passage is avoided except in exceptional
   emergencies — then with a well-lubricated catheter by an experienced team member.
 • Relative: an artificial urinary sphincter (AUS), which must be deactivated first; urethral stricture, which
   may need additional manoeuvres.

TECHNIQUE OF URETHRAL CATHETERISATION
Preparation for Catheterisation
 • Standard tray: sterile gloves, catheter, sterile lubricant, antiseptic, drapes, sterile water, collection bag
   with tubing. Specialty catheters and wires are not in basic kits — have them ready according to the
   indication.
 • Sequence: open tray sterilely, glove → (optionally test the balloon with sterile water — not routine) →
   antiseptic onto swabs → sterile field with drapes → prepare the genitalia.

Urethral Catheterisation in Men
 • Supine → retract foreskin, sterilise glans → inject 10–15 mL lubricant into the meatus, pinch glans to
   prevent reflux → the non-dominant hand is now non-sterile → dominant hand inserts with the penis
   straight and taut → slight resistance at sphincter or prostate is normal; slow, steady pressure → advance
   to the hub (balloon port at the meatus) → confirm urine flow → inflate with sterile water → withdraw to
   seat the balloon → replace the foreskin (prevents paraphimosis).
 • Urine may return before the bladder neck is passed: inflating then places the balloon in the prostatic
   urethra — insert fully and confirm flow before inflation.
 • Beyond the bladder neck with no urine → lumen plugged by lubricant → irrigate with sterile water or
   saline.
 • Adjuncts: 2% lidocaine gel for apprehensive patients or failed attempts; distraction to pass the external
   sphincter.

Urethral Catheterisation in Women
 • Usually easier — shorter urethra. Supine frog-leg → thumb, index and middle fingers of the non-
   dominant hand spread the labia minora, lifting anteriorly → sterilise meatus → insert lubricated catheter;
   little or no resistance → inflate once urine returns.
 • Poor view → Trendelenburg and assistant retraction.
 • Examinable pair: men — inflate only at the hub after urine; women — inflate on urine return.

Urethral Catheterisation of Children
 • Same principles. Physiological phimosis must not be forcibly retracted — move the foreskin ventrally or
   dorsally to find the meatus.
 • Boys may show intense sphincter contraction: hold steady pressure without force until it releases, often
   with a deep breath. Size by age (Table 15.2).

Types of Urethral Catheters
Catheter Sizing
 • Choose by patient factors, anatomy and indication — a young man in retention after hernia repair needs
   a different catheter from an elderly man in clot retention after transurethral resection of bladder tumour
   (TURBT).
 • Size is in French (Fr); French size and recommended balloon volume are printed on the balloon-port cuff.
   Diameter (mm) = Fr ÷ 3; larger catheters are also more rigid.

Diagnostic Catheters
 • Lapides diagnostic Foley: five radiopaque rings 1 cm apart — calibrates female urethral length.
 • Davis and Trattner catheters: two balloons (bladder neck + meatal seal) so contrast can be instilled into
   the urethra — diagnosed female urethral diverticula; largely replaced by magnetic resonance imaging
   (MRI) (Fig. 15.3).
 • Urodynamic catheter: three ports — pressure sensor, filling, balloon.
Drain Catheters
 • Malecot (Fig. 15.4): single lumen, no balloon; four-winged tip compressed for insertion, holds in place.
   Used postoperatively as nephrostomy or suprapubic (SP) tube, or placed into a conduit after continent
   diversion.
Condom Catheters
 • External drainage for men with limited mobility and no outlet obstruction.

 KEY POINTS: CATHETERS
   • There are many indications for urethral catheterisation and many sizes and types of catheter.
   • As French size increases, the catheter lumen diameter increases (Table 15.3).
   • Three-way haematuria catheters should be used if clot retention is suspected.

SUPRAPUBIC CATHETERISATION
 • SP tubes bypass the urethra, draining through the lower anterior abdominal wall; placed at the bedside or
   by minor surgery.
 • Indications: urethra inaccessible (disruption from trauma; acute retention with significant stricture);
   permanent catheterisation, where SP may be more comfortable.
 • Advantages: easy, comfortable exchange (immobility, contractures, difficult genital anatomy); avoids
   penile trauma, stricture, prostatic bleeding, traumatic hypospadias/urethral erosion, a patulous female
   urethra; less interference with sexual activity.
 • Complications: bowel injury or perforation; abdominal wall cellulitis or abscess.
 • First exchange at 4–6 weeks by experienced staff — a new cystotomy tract is easily lost; later exchanges
   can be done by almost anyone once the tract matures.

Techniques
 • Open vs percutaneous: percutaneous preferred — bedside, local anaesthesia.
 • Contraindications to percutaneous placement (absolute and relative together): prior abdominal or pelvic
   surgery violating the retropubic space, complex anatomy, bowel overlying the bladder, no bedside
   ultrasound, surgeon preference.
Minimally Invasive
 • Two kit types — trocar-based and self-contained Seldinger — same principles; ultrasound guidance for
   both. Sequence:
    1. Ultrasound confirms a full bladder with no overlying bowel.
    2. Trendelenburg — moves peritoneal contents cephalad.
    3. 1–2 cm horizontal midline incision, 1–2 fingerbreadths above the symphysis.
    4. Spinal needle into the bladder, aspirate urine; infiltrate local anaesthetic along the tract; confirm by
       ultrasound or cystoscopy.
    5. (a) Trocar: trocar and sheath (stainless-steel obturator and outer sheath, Fig. 15.5) into the bladder →
       urine returns → remove trocar → 16-Fr catheter through the sheath → remove sheath. (b) Seldinger:
       wire through needle → gentle tract dilation → catheter.
    6. Do not advance the catheter too far — it may enter the bladder neck or urethra.
Open Cystotomy
 • General anaesthesia or sedation → distend bladder to capacity → small midline or Pfannenstiel incision
   → expose and incise rectus fascia, spread rectus → palpate bladder, stay stitches lift it → small cystotomy
   → any catheter placed and anchored → haemostasis → secure to skin with permanent suture.
 • Lowsley retractor (adapted urethral sound): advanced per urethra into bladder → pushed anteriorly to
   the abdominal wall → skin incision, cautery down onto its tip → tip exposed → catheter tied to it (2-0 silk)
   → retractor and catheter pulled back out through the urethra and meatus → tie cut → catheter guided
   cystoscopically back into the bladder → balloon inflated → secured to skin with permanent suture.
 • Pair: percutaneous — bedside, local, ultrasound-guided; open — theatre, anaesthesia, direct vision,
   reserved for when percutaneous access is unsafe.

DIFFICULT CATHETERISATION
Prostatic Obstruction
 • Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is the commonest cause: adenoma narrows the prostatic urethra;
   with the male urethral angle this blocks advance or creates an iatrogenic false passage posterior to the
   prostatic urethra.
 • 14–16 Fr usually will not pass; 18 Fr is more rigid with more tip surface area. Suspected BPH → at least
   18-Fr coudé, marker kept anterior so the tip points anteriorly — usually sufficient.
 • False passage: suspect with blood in the port or on the tip after a difficult pass → cystoscopy rather than
   further blind attempts (which extend the passage) → traverse it visually, usually angling the camera
   anteriorly → guidewire into bladder → Councill catheter over the wire.

Urethral Stricture
 • Strictures occur anywhere; location estimated by the length of catheter passed before resistance. Gentle
   pressure may dilate a soft stricture.
 • Membranous strictures are most prone to false passage (urethral angle), though it can happen
   anywhere.
 • Sequence: atraumatic hydrophilic guidewire past the stricture → confirm in bladder by passing a 5-Fr
   catheter and aspirating urine (or place under vision with flexible cystoscopy) → exchange for a stiffer
   wire through the 5-Fr catheter → stepwise dilation, small to large, not too deep (posterior bladder wall
   perforation) → dilate one size above the planned catheter (16-Fr catheter → dilate to at least 18 Fr),
   since the catheter is less rigid than the dilator → Councill catheter over the wire.
 • Alternatives: balloon dilator, filiform dilators. Dense stricture or intolerance → theatre, or SP catheter.

Urethral Trauma
 • Suspected injury → RUG before catheterisation; blind passage avoided except in exceptional
   circumstances. Pelvic fracture urethral injury → SP tube is the preferred initial management.

FIG. 15.6 ALGORITHM FOR DIFFICULT CATHETER PLACEMENT
 • First attempt fails — prostatic obstruction suspected → 18-Fr coudé → fails → cystoscopy → stricture:
   guidewire, dilate, Councill over wire; false passage: super-stiff wire exchanged over 5-Fr catheter, Councill
   over wire.
 • Not suspected → smaller-diameter catheter → fails → glidewire → if placed: stricture → dilate, Councill
   over wire; false passage → Councill over wire → if glidewire fails → cystoscopy, same branches.
 • Footnotes: wire cannot be placed → SP tube; SP also for urethral disruption or undilatable dense
   stricture; dilation is not appropriate for every stricture.

Abnormal Anatomy
Posterior Urethral Valves
 • Newborn boys need urgent catheterisation. The catheter coils because of the high bladder neck. Try 6–8
   Fr; premature urethras may be too small → 5-Fr open-ended catheter over a glidewire → failure → SP
   tube or vesicostomy.
Obesity
 • The problem is exposure — most difficult female catheterisations are failures of exposure. Trendelenburg;
   assistants (and the awake patient) retract the suprapubic fat pad or pannus; headlamp. Female meatus
   still hidden → two fingers in the vagina, pass the catheter over them guiding anteriorly.

 • Buried penis: infrapubic manual pressure reveals the meatus; dense cicatrix → flexible cystoscopy to find
   it. High incidence of urethral stricture in buried penis — expect adjunctive manoeuvres.
Reconstructive Considerations
 • Full history, examination, imaging and operative notes before instrumenting a complex patient; after
   bladder neck closure urethral catheterisation is impossible.
 • Neobladder: usually catheterisable per urethra unless anastomotic stenosis or tumour recurrence. If not,
   SP placement is hazardous — the neobladder is intraperitoneal (bowel or pedicle injury) →
   interventional radiology-guided or open SP.
 • Continent reservoir: not catheterisable per urethra; reservoir usually right lower quadrant, channel there
   or at the umbilicus. Commonest failure: overdistension kinks the channel. Never force (perforation, false
   passage) → flexible cystoscope, ureteroscope or hysteroscope into the reservoir → wire → Councill;
   failure → SP with neobladder precautions.
Artificial Urinary Sphincter
 • Three-piece hydraulic device for male stress incontinence; catheterisation is a relative contraindication —
   cuff erosion. If essential: fully deactivate → cuff size unknown → smallest catheter, 12–14 Fr → remove
   as soon as possible; prolonged drainage → SP tube.

 KEY POINTS
   • The urologist should be prepared to attain lower urinary drainage in almost all situations.
   • Review the history and prior operative reports before instrumenting the lower urinary tract.
   • If BPH is expected, use an 18-Fr coudé catheter.
   • Avoid catheters with an AUS; if necessary, place a 12–14 Fr after deactivation and remove it as soon as
     possible.

Concept — every difficult catheterisation escalates the same way: blind → wire → vision → suprapubic;
repeated forceful blind passage is the one step never taken.

COMPLICATIONS OF LOWER URINARY TRACT DRAINAGE
Infection
 • Per the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC): UTIs are the commonest healthcare-associated
   infection, and 75% of hospital-acquired UTIs are catheter-associated. Evidence-based guidelines on
   catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI) prevention and treatment: CDC and Infectious Diseases Society of
   America (IDSA).

Iatrogenic Trauma
 • Usually from poor technique or aggressive attempts in the difficult patient → false passage, haematuria,
   AUS cuff injury or erosion; long-term indwelling → traumatic hypospadias, urethral erosion.
 • About 0.3% of hospitalised patients (one institution: about 3 per 1000 male admissions), usually
   presenting with penile bleeding. Occurs at insertion, balloon inflation, or removal with an incompletely
   deflated balloon.
 • Catheter safety training significantly reduced injuries — the injury is preventable, and education is the
   most important intervention.

Malignancy
