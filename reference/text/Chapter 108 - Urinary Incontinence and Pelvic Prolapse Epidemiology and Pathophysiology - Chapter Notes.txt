DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION OF URINARY INCONTINENCE
Introduction and Overview of the Lower Urinary Tract
 • Urinary incontinence (UI) affects millions of women and men and their families, at a cost of billions in
   direct and indirect healthcare spending.
 • UI is heavily under-reported. Visits and procedures have risen for decades, yet many symptomatic
   women are undiagnosed. In one managed-care population, over half of women with no UI recorded at
   their visit reported UI in the past year when asked directly, and over half of them graded it moderate to
   severe. Primary care must ask directly and refer.
 • The lower urinary tract (LUT) is the bladder and urethra, with complex neural innervation and
   musculofascial pelvic support.
 • Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), including UI, arise from anatomical abnormality or structural
   deterioration of the LUT or its surroundings, at macroscopic, microscopic or ultrastructural level (e.g.
   urothelium, detrusor, sphincteric unit).
 • Two normal functions: storing urine at low intravesical pressure and expelling it at socially appropriate
   times. Both are shaped by coexisting disease, ageing, demographic and environmental risk, lifestyle and
   genetics.

Signs, Symptoms, and Urodynamic Observations of Urinary Incontinence
 • LUTS are non-specific: different causes of LUT dysfunction (LUTD) give similar symptoms. Urgency occurs
   in overactive bladder (OAB), bladder outlet obstruction (BOO), pelvic organ prolapse (POP) and
   interstitial cystitis/painful bladder syndrome (IC/PBS).
 • Work-up is tailored to the condition, the risk of the proposed intervention and clinician and patient
   confidence. A definitive diagnosis targets treatment better, but extensive, expensive evaluation is not
   always needed first.

TERMINOLOGY OF LOWER URINARY TRACT SYMPTOMS AND INCONTINENCE
 • Terminology for female LUTD is standardised by a joint report of the International Continence Society
   (ICS) and the International Urogynecological Association (IUGA) (2010), which builds on the 2002 ICS
   standardisation. The same documents also cover POP terminology and how to perform and interpret UDS.
 • LUTS fall into five groups: (1) UI, (2) bladder storage, (3) bladder sensation, (4) voiding and (5) post-
   micturition.

Incontinence
 • UI is the complaint of involuntary loss of urine.
 • Assessment must establish the precipitant (urgency, a particular activity or action), severity, impact on
   QoL, duration and frequency.
 • No single tool answers all of these questions. Questionnaires, bladder diary, examination and UDS are
   used together.

EPIDEMIOLOGY OF URINARY INCONTINENCE IN FEMALES
General Comments
 • Prevalence estimates vary widely, mainly because definitions and methods differ. A single survey
   question in a mixed population, an incontinence-specific questionnaire in at-risk women, examination,
   UDS and diaries all give different figures. Comparisons between studies, and before and after
   intervention, are therefore error-prone. Societies have called for common outcomes and tools, but this is
   unresolved.
 • Questionnaire design drives the figure. The time frame matters most: "any leak in the past month" gives
   a higher prevalence than a narrower window. Bother-based definitions differ from presence-based ones,
   and in a non-morbid condition bother should be paramount. Leakage shown on examination or UDS
   differs again from questionnaire report.
 • The population matters: young nulliparous women and octogenarians in nursing facilities give very
   different figures. Incidence and remission are even more bias-prone, because prevalence is high relative
   to incidence and studies differ in interval, age, recall window and wording.

Pregnancy and the postpartum period
 • Pregnancy is probably the commonest cause of incident UI in young women, and risk rises with
   gestational age. Prevalence in pregnancy is ≈40%; SUI is the commonest type (about two-thirds).
 • Risk factors for SUI in pregnancy: obesity, pre-existing SUI, gestational diabetes, age, constipation, prior
   vaginal delivery, childhood enuresis.
 • Primiparous women have ≈3 times the risk of UI in pregnancy compared with age-matched nulliparous
   women, still ≈2.5 times at 1 year. Higher parity probably adds risk up to 1 year postpartum (data mixed).
 • Postpartum remission cannot be assumed: few women with UI at 3 months are dry at both 6 and 12
   years.
 • Protective measures: properly performed pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) during and after pregnancy
   reduces incident UI in pregnancy and early postpartum, but the benefit of antenatal exercise may be lost
   by 7 years. Postpartum weight loss speeds recovery. Supervised PFMT and electrical stimulation treat
   postpartum UI effectively (systematic review). A validated model makes vaginal delivery the strongest
   predictor of UI up to 8 weeks.

Hysterectomy
 • Hysterectomy can both cause and resolve UI. In a Swedish national registry, a minority of continent
   women developed de novo UI, predicted by obesity and prior vaginal delivery. Some with pre-existing UI
   were dry at 1 year, predicted by uterine weight >300 g, body mass index (BMI) <25 kg/m² and coexisting
   POP.
 • Vaginal and laparoscopic hysterectomy carry more UI than open hysterectomy (10-year Finnish
   prospective study).

Incidence and remission
 • Women aged 40–60: annual incidence 1–10%, ≈3% per subtype. Cohorts followed for 8–10 years show
   substantial cumulative new-onset UI.
 • SUI accounts for about half of new cases in younger cohorts. SUI incidence, but not UUI or severe UI,
   rises across the menopausal transition (48–54 years). In older cohorts, annual incidence is ≈10–20%.
 • White women are more likely than Black women to develop UI over 5 years, especially with higher
   baseline BMI or weight gain.
 • Annual remission is up to ≈12%. It is higher in younger women and milder UI, and ≈30% with prolonged
   follow-up. MUI remits least. The highest remission is reported in Black women by some studies and, for
   SUI, in Hispanic women by others.
 • Remission implies transient causes, as well as subjectivity and recall bias.

 KEY POINTS: INCIDENCE AND REMISSION OF URINARY INCONTINENCE
   • Pregnancy → incident UI in >40%. Hysterectomy resolves UI in 13%. Annual incidence 1–10% (≈3% per
     subtype). MUI remits least; men remit considerably more often than women.

Concept — Every UI prevalence figure depends on its definition, instrument, time frame and population.
Stated numbers are therefore only comparable within a study. In young women, the event that dominates
incidence is pregnancy.

RISK FACTORS FOR URINARY INCONTINENCE IN FEMALES
Aging
 • Ageing is a consistent independent risk factor, and the risk keeps rising after the menopause. Over a third
   of Medicare beneficiaries (all ≥65) report UI, and the gradient with age persists even within this group.
 • With age, UI shifts away from SUI towards MUI and UUI. In the Urologic Diseases in America study,
   overall prevalence was about half, with SUI peaking at 40–59 and UUI/MUI predominating over 60.
 • Over 75, UUI is linked to impaired mobility and SUI is not. Better lower-limb function (strength, walking
   speed, "get up and go") predicts less incident UI over 4 years.
 • In nonagenarians, UI is independently linked to frailty and is an independent risk factor for death, so
   treatment may be warranted even in the oldest patients.
 • Long-term care facility (LTCF) residence carries the greatest risk, with prevalence >40% at admission.
   Severe impairment of activities of daily living (ADL) is the strongest association. Poor nutrition,
   immobility and dementia correlate with severity. Becoming incontinent while resident is driven by
   cognitive impairment, poor rehabilitation potential, stroke, Alzheimer disease and Parkinson disease.

   declines; urgency and mixed UI keep rising into the ninth decade.
 • Fig. 108.2 — UUI (including MUI) by age in men and women (Milsom et al., Eur Urol, 2014): UUI rises with age in
   both sexes, and in ageing men sometimes exceeds the female prevalence.

Pregnancy
 • UI that develops in pregnancy raises the risk of future and persistent UI. Prevalence falls by 3 months,
   but >30% still have UI at 1 year, mostly SUI. Nearly two-thirds of women with UI at 3 months still report it
   at 6 and 12 years.
 • Persistence is associated with older age at pregnancy and obesity. First pregnancy after age 30 roughly
   triples the odds of later UI (systematic review). Alcohol and depression predict persistence; physical
   activity and Black race protect.

Aspects of Delivery
 • Caesarean section (CS) protects against later UI and SUI compared with vaginal delivery. Elective CS
   carries less risk than either CS after labour or vaginal delivery. Primiparous women delivered by CS have
   less UI at 5 years.
 • Vaginal delivery leads to later SUI, not UUI. CS protects only if every delivery is by CS; one vaginal
   delivery removes the benefit.
 • Number needed to treat (NNT) with CS to prevent one case of moderate-to-severe UI = 13.
 • Unproven: length of labour, forceps, anaesthesia, head circumference and episiotomy. There is no
   conclusive evidence to change obstetric practice. A long second stage is linked to immediate postpartum
   UI only.
 • Consistent: vaginal birth of a large baby. At least one child ≥4 kg gives an odds ratio (OR) of ≈1.5 for
   weekly UI later in life.

Parity
 • A single pregnancy and delivery raises later UI risk (OR ≈1.5). The effect of further deliveries is unclear
   and the data conflict; a meta-analysis shows a modest rise with each delivery (relative risk (RR) ≈1.4 →
   1.6 from one to three).

 • Age at first birth is disputed: most studies put the greatest risk at a first birth near age 20, whereas the
   systematic review above found risk raised by a first pregnancy after 30.
 • The parity association is strongest for SUI.

Demographic Factors: Race, Ethnicity, Socioeconomics, and Geography
 • White women have more UI and SUI, prevalent and incident, than Black and Asian women, possibly
   because of higher urethral closure pressure in Black women. The difference is less clear for OAB/UUI and
   against Hispanic women, and differences in MUI/UUI are probably inconsequential.
 • Rural residence carries more risk than urban. White and Black women seek care equally, modified by
   leakage severity. In the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), lower
   socioeconomic status carries the greatest risk of UUI.
 • Cross-cultural differences are unclear; worldwide prevalence in older women is ≈28%, with marked
   heterogeneity.

Obesity
 • The presence, duration and severity of UI are strongly associated with obesity, most strongly for SUI and
   MUI, but for all types.
 • BMI ≥30 kg/m² more than doubles UI risk. Overweight without obesity already raises risk, so the effect
   of weight gain is progressive. Visceral fat, metabolic syndrome (obesity, dyslipidaemia, raised blood
   pressure and fasting glucose) and years of obesity all add to likelihood and severity.
 • Weight-related UI is reversible. Surgical or structured weight loss improves UI for as long as the weight
   stays off. Non-surgical programmes (diet, exercise, drugs) give a significant but modest fall in daily
   episodes (meta-analysis).
 • Obesity has little effect on the success of SUI procedures, but may reduce that of third-line OAB/UUI
   treatments.

Smoking
 • The data are inconsistent, but consensus is growing for a link, strongest with urgency.
 • Current smoking → urgency and frequency, dose-dependently (heavy > light). Waterpipe smoking is also
   linked to OAB. UUI is linked especially in younger women. Severe UI is associated with current smoking in
   a large nurses' cohort, and smokers awaiting SUI surgery have more episodes.

Diet
 • Caffeine, especially coffee, has the most compelling link: UUI, MUI and OAB, in both sexes. Diet has no
   consistent link with SUI. Carbonated drinks and artificial sweeteners are linked mainly to urgency but
   remain unconfirmed.

Medical Conditions
 • Diabetes mellitus (DM) and depression are the principal medical associations.
 • Type 2 DM: up to twice the UI prevalence of age-matched non-diabetic women, with nearly half of
   diabetic nurses reporting at least monthly UI. Obesity amplifies the risk. Macroalbuminuria and
   peripheral neuropathic pain are independent risk factors (NHANES). Type 1 DM data are emerging but
   less consistent.
 • Depression is linked multifactorially and bidirectionally: UI lowers perceived self-health, and a shared
   pathophysiology is proposed. Depression raises the risk of concurrent and later UI.
 • Multimorbidity: hypertension, osteoarthritis and heart failure are each independently associated, and
   their cumulative effect can tip vulnerable women into UI.

 KEY POINTS: RISK FACTORS FOR URINARY INCONTINENCE IN FEMALES
   • Age: strongest non-modifiable factor; LTCF residence independent of age. Delivery: CS advantage lost
     with one vaginal birth. Parity: each delivery adds risk, mainly SUI.
   • Oral oestrogen → SUI; topical does not. Obesity: dose- and duration-dependent, reversible. Smoking:
     urgency and UUI; data on stopping are scant. Caffeine: OAB, not SUI. DM and depression: commonest
     medical associations.

Concept — The risk factors split into two groups. Obstetric injury and loss of support produce stress
incontinence (vaginal delivery causes SUI, not UUI). Ageing, neurological, metabolic and lifestyle factors
(smoking, caffeine, DM, depression, immobility) push towards urgency. This is why the dominant type of UI
moves from stress to urge across a woman's lifetime.

EPIDEMIOLOGY OF URINARY INCONTINENCE IN MALES
Prevalence, Incidence, Remission Rates
 • UI is considerably less prevalent in men. SUI is uncommon except after radical pelvic surgery (radical
   prostatectomy (RP), abdominoperineal resection), after surgery for benign prostatic enlargement, or with
   neurological disease.
 • UUI and MUI are commoner than SUI, often from BOO due to benign prostatic enlargement, other
   outlet disorders, OAB, or inflammation and infection.
 • Prevalence in older men: 11–34%; up to 11% have daily UI. Incidence depends on age: ≈1–10% per year
   at 60–70.
 • Remission is higher than in women, up to 40%, because male leakage is mostly urgency-related and often
   has reversible (infective or inflammatory) causes.

Risk Factors for Urinary Incontinence in Males
 • Age is the strongest independent risk factor, possibly more so than in women. Other factors: infection
   (acute cystitis, prostatitis), which also rises with age; obesity indicators; loss of grip and quadriceps
   strength; possibly chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Non-Hispanic Black men have more
   UUI than Hispanic and non-Hispanic White men.
 • RP, radical pelvic surgery and radiotherapy: UI 1 year after RP is 8–60%, depending mainly on the
   definition used.
 • Less UI after RP with: no pre-existing LUTS or previous transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP);
   bladder-neck, neurovascular-bundle and possibly Retzius sparing; younger age. The data conflict.

high remission rate.

DEFINITIONS AND CLASSIFICATION OF PELVIC ORGAN PROLAPSE
 • POP is the downward displacement of the pelvic organs, with protrusion of the uterus and/or vaginal
   compartments and the organs around them (bladder, rectum, small or large bowel). It results from loss of
   support of one or more vaginal compartments.
 • Describe POP by vaginal segment, not by organ. Avoid cystocele, rectocele, enterocele and
   urethrovesical junction prolapse, because the structure behind the bulge is uncertain, especially after
   previous POP surgery.

Symptoms of Pelvic Organ Prolapse
 • Most specific symptom: a vaginal bulge the patient can see or feel. Others: pelvic pressure or discomfort,
   discharge, splinting or digitation to void or defecate, low backache.

    • Urinary and bowel symptoms correlate poorly with POP site and severity. Frequency, nocturia, UI, faecal
      incontinence (FI), voiding and defecatory dysfunction and incomplete emptying should still be assessed,
      but they often reflect coexisting bladder, anorectal or pelvic floor dysfunction. Bowel symptoms
      correlate weakly at most.
    • Constipation without a bulge should rarely prompt posterior repair. Exception: stool caught "in a
      pocket" needing splinting, which suggests a rectocele defect.
    • Bulge and splinting correlate much better once the leading edge is beyond the hymen.
    • Establish when SUI began. SUI falls in prevalence and severity as POP passes the hymen, possibly
      because urethral kinking obstructs the urethra, so concomitant SUI treatment may be indicated in some
      women.
    • Sexual function: POP harms body image and sexual function, and treatment can improve both. Ask
      sexually inactive women whether they wish to resume, because this affects the choice of repair.
    • Never attribute abnormal or postmenopausal bleeding to POP; it needs gynaecological evaluation before
      any POP intervention.
    • There is little evidence that POP causes pain, so look for another source first.
    • A pessary trial is diagnostic: if symptoms improve, POP is the likely cause and surgery is more likely to
      help.

    • Traps: stage is set by the single most distal point. Aa and Ap cannot exceed +3. Ba and Bp equal C in total
      eversion. There is no D after hysterectomy. Pessary removal falsely lowers the measurements.

Imaging
    • History plus POP-Q examination is usually enough; imaging is generally unnecessary.
    • Pelvic ultrasound identifies pelvic organ abnormalities and mesh from previous POP or UI surgery.
      Ultrasound assessment of the anatomical defect is investigational.
    • Defecography ± dynamic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is used for incomplete bowel emptying,
      significant defecatory dysfunction, or examination–symptom mismatch. Rest and straining MRI is
      particularly useful for enterocele (Fig. 108.7: A, severe cystocele with uterine descent; B, defecatory
      dysfunction with severe enterocele).
    • Routine preoperative MRI has not been shown to be necessary, cost-effective or outcome-improving, so
      it is discretionary. Careful POP-Q examination, reassessed at surgery if needed, remains the cornerstone.

EPIDEMIOLOGY OF PELVIC ORGAN PROLAPSE
 • Prevalence is hard to estimate. Studies use symptoms or examination, rarely both. Definitions vary
   because normal and abnormal support are poorly defined, and symptoms correlate poorly with POP
   location and severity.
 • Seeing or feeling a bulge is the symptom most strongly correlated with POP beyond the hymen, and
   POP is often asymptomatic until it reaches the hymenal ring.
 • Some loss of support is found on examination in 43–90% of women.
 • Stage 1 anterior and posterior POP is probably normal. In the Pelvic Organ Support Study (POSST) of
   ≈1000 women at routine gynaecological care, only 24% had no POP; stage 1 was found in 38%, stage 2 in
   35% and stage 3 in 2%. Asymptomatic stage 1–2 POP is therefore normal and rarely needs treatment.
 • Women's Health Initiative (WHI) (postmenopausal women, examined): any POP in 41% with a uterus and
   38% after hysterectomy; anterior 34%, posterior 19%, uterine 14%.
 • By compartment: anterior > posterior > apical. However, apical POP is under-diagnosed, because it
   almost always accompanies significant anterior or posterior POP, especially anterior. Significant apical
   descent (C ≥ −3) becomes more common with each stage of anterior POP and is universal at stage 4.
 • When planning a cystocele repair, suspect apical POP and consider a concomitant vault suspension.
 • Symptomatic POP (sensation of bulge, pressure or protrusion, or a visible bulge): 3–12%. Questionnaires
   under-report compared with examination: only about a fifth of women with stage ≥2 report symptoms,
   partly from shame and reluctance to discuss it.
 • Bother varies: at stage 2, Hispanic and Native American women report more bother than non-Hispanic
   White women; at stage ≥3 there is no difference.

Natural history
 • WHI Estrogen Plus Progestin Trial subgroup (2–8 years): annual incidence per 100 woman-years of
   cystocele 9.3, rectocele 5.7, uterine prolapse 1.5. Spontaneous regression was common, especially for
   grade 1.
 • Symptomatic women choosing observation mostly stay unchanged; a minority progress over ≈1–1.5
   years and very few regress.
 • Progression risk: age >60, Ba ≥4 cm, genital hiatus ≥5 cm at baseline. Change in gh over time, not only its
   size, predicts new POP.
 • Natural history: very slow progression, if any, even in symptomatic advanced POP.

RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN URINARY INCONTINENCE AND PELVIC ORGAN PROLAPSE
 • UUI, SUI and POP share aetiological risk factors. 40–55% of women with SUI have significant anterior
   POP; ≈20% of women with OAB have anterior POP.

POP and OAB
 • OAB is commoner with POP. Frequency tracks anterior and apical POP; UUI tracks the severity of anterior
   POP.
 • OAB improves after POP surgery in 60–80%. Anterior repair reduces frequency, UUI and voiding difficulty,
   and anterior or apical repair helps OAB more than posterior repair. No reliable predictor exists of
   whether OAB will persist.
 • Mechanisms (unclear): BOO is probably the most important. Chronic bladder-wall stretch may cause DO.
   A severe cystocele may pull the bladder neck open, so urine enters the proximal urethra and triggers DO.

POP and SUI
 • More than half of women with SUI have POP. In the Trial of Mid-Urethral Slings (TOMUS) (National
   Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK); two mid-urethral sling (MUS) types):
   stage 2 POP in 49%, stage 3 in 6%. 78% with stage 3 were symptomatic vs 25% with stage 2, and 15% of
   asymptomatic stage 2 women had concomitant repair.
 • Missing POP at UI surgery increases later POP surgery. In Medicare claims, urologists did concomitant
   POP repair at sling surgery about half as often as gynaecologists, and their patients had ≈twice the 12-
   month rate of repeat prolapse surgery. Identify and manage symptomatic POP when evaluating UI.
 • Occult SUI is SUI seen only after the coexisting POP is reduced, in up to a quarter of advanced POP. De
   novo SUI after POP surgery is more likely with age >66, DM, or low urethral closure pressure on UDS.
 • Sequence: cystocele → urethral kink → obstruction masks SUI → repair relieves obstruction → SUI
   unmasked. Conversely, SUI often improves as POP worsens. UDS with the prolapse reduced identifies
   occult SUI.

CONSEQUENCES OF URINARY INCONTINENCE AND PROLAPSE
Societal Costs of Urinary Incontinence
 • Costs are hard to measure, because they include out-of-pocket items payers do not capture (pads,
   laundry, missed work) as well as tests, visits, drugs, surgery, and treatment of UTI and skin breakdown.
 • Female SUI alone: >$12 billion a year in the United States, >70% out-of-pocket. Severe UI carries
   substantial out-of-pocket care costs, which fall after surgery (Urinary Incontinence Treatment Network
   (UITN)). SUI/MUI means more visits and spending than in controls.
 • OAB: ≈42 million Americans, ≈$25 billion a year, with substantially higher direct and indirect costs than in
   people without OAB.

Social Impact of Urinary Incontinence
 • UI affects nearly every aspect of life. Relationships and sex suffer, and surgery for UI improves sexual
   function. Patients avoid travel, restrict food and fluid (sometimes to inappropriate dehydration), wear
   dark clothing and avoid strangers. Work productivity falls, and sleep disturbance reduces daytime
   alertness.
 • UI affects all ages. Young nulligravid women with UI report much lower well-being. In the elderly, severity
   rather than type determines the QoL impact. QoL declines progressively over 10 years in both sexes,
   across nearly all domains, possibly more than with arthritis or DM.
 • The most bothersome LUTS are urgency and UUI.

   • >$50 billion a year in direct and indirect costs of UUI plus SUI in the United States. Relationships, sex,
     travel, work, diet and sleep are all affected.

PHYSIOLOGY OF URINARY CONTINENCE
Overview of Normal Continence Mechanisms
 • Continence is maintained by an interplay of neural, structural and ultrastructural mechanisms in the LUT
   and the structures around it. A change in any one can cause UI, and diseases, their treatments and ageing
   often affect several at once.
 • Example — DM: hyperglycaemic polyuria first → microvascular change and oxidative stress → direct
   neurogenic and myogenic damage → UUI and SUI.

Bladder: An Organ Capable of Significant Expansion at Low Pressures
 • The bladder's position allows expansion while protecting the sphincter. The dome expands into the
   peritoneal cavity with virtually no opposing force, while the base and neck sit in a stable retropubic
   position. In men this position is fixed, so leakage needs a direct injury to the sphincteric unit (or a
   neurological, traumatic or operative event). In women, pelvic floor laxity alters bladder-neck position
   and function.
 • Poor electrical coupling between detrusor smooth muscle cells stops spontaneous single-cell
   contractions, triggered by stretch, from spreading into a coordinated contraction. A sustained contraction
   needs coordinated parasympathetic drive.
 • The multilayered urothelium stores urine efficiently: its "umbrella cells" flatten with filling.
 • The extracellular matrix is critical to compliance, in particular the collagen type (type I in normally
   compliant bladders) and the collagen-to-elastin ratio. Inflammation, infection and radiation alter
   compliance.
 • Tonic sympathetic, β-mediated detrusor relaxation further lowers intravesical pressure.

 KEY POINTS: FACTORS CONTRIBUTING TO LOW-PRESSURE RESERVOIR DURING BLADDER FILLING
   • Neural: parasympathetic suppression and sympathetic activation. Gross anatomy: the intraperitoneal
     dome expands freely.
   • Microanatomy: the multilayered mucosa expands and collapses; poor detrusor cell coupling dissipates
     aberrant contractions; the extracellular matrix maintains compliance.


Bladder Outlet and Sphincteric Mechanisms
 • Outlet resistance must exceed storage pressure; otherwise a low-pressure reservoir is useless.
 • A competent outlet has three components: the urethral mucosa, the internal sphincter (intrinsic
   properties of the bladder neck and proximal urethra) and the distal rhabdosphincter.
 • Longitudinal mucosal folds let the urethra distend for voiding and form a mucosal seal during filling. The
   vascular, spongy submucosa adds distensibility.

Male Sphincteric Mechanisms
 • The internal sphincter runs from the bladder neck to the distal verumontanum, and normally urine does
   not pass beyond the bladder neck. α-Adrenergic smooth muscle at the bladder neck and in the prostatic
   urethra extends towards the rhabdosphincter, where the greatest intraurethral forces are generated. α-
   Activation here, with β-mediated relaxation of the bladder body, keeps the patient dry, and the prostate
   adds resistance.
 • The external sphincter is concentric, mostly type I (slow-twitch) skeletal muscle with some smooth
   muscle. The slow-twitch fibres give tonic closure at >40 cm H₂O throughout filling, rising with voluntary
   contraction. Fast-twitch levator fibres add transient closure under stress. Pudendal injury or denervation
   weakens the sphincter and causes UI.
 • The pubourethral ligaments (anteriorly) and the musculofascial plate fusing at the perineal body
   (posteriorly) stabilise the sphincter against external forces.

Female Sphincteric Mechanisms
 • Continence arises mainly along the proximal and mid-urethra, and an incompetent bladder neck is
   common in continent women. Circular striated muscle compresses the mid-urethra almost
   circumferentially under tonic pudendal stimulation. In rats, striated fibres run transversely and




                                                         page 16 of 22
Chapter 108 — Urinary Incontinence and Pelvic Prolapse                                  PHYSIOLOGY OF URINARY CONTINENCE
   longitudinally and insert into the urethral wall. Pudendal denervation from prolonged labour is one route
   from delivery to UI.
 • Distally, the striated fibres lie ventrally as the compressor urethrae (from the perineal membrane) and
   the urethrovaginal sphincter (from the vaginal wall).
 • Seal: the spongy, oestrogen-sensitive submucosa brings the mucosal surfaces together and provides up
   to 30% of continence forces.
 • Support: the anterior vaginal wall is a firm backing for mid-urethral compression. The pubourethral
   ligaments limit proximal movement and keep abdominal force off the rest of the urethra. The
   urethropelvic ligaments anchor the urethra to the arcus tendineus. Lax vaginal support with intact
   pubourethral ligaments causes shear in the continence zone.
 • Continence needs support, intrinsic urethral properties and muscle together; losing one, and usually
   several, causes UI.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF STRESS URINARY INCONTINENCE IN FEMALES
 • Early theories focused on descent of the proximal urethra and bladder neck out of their intra-abdominal
   location with anterior POP.

Loss of Urethral Support
 • Urethral support comes from the endopelvic fascia, reinforced anteriorly by the pubourethral ligament
   and posteriorly by the vaginal wall. Together these normally prevent excessive urethral mobility.
 • Three theories build on this (see the table below): the hammock hypothesis (backing), the integral
   theory (ligamentous laxity) and ISD (urethral function).

Intrinsic Sphincteric Deficiency
 • Support does not explain everything. Some women with extensive relaxation and hypermobility are
   continent, while some with no hypermobility have severe SUI, typically those with a scarred urethra or
   neurogenic disease.
 • McGuire and Lytton (1978) introduced ISD in their landmark description of the autologous fascial
   pubovaginal sling for urodynamically severe urethral dysfunction, usually after previous urethral surgery.
   Most patients improved, with no apparent obstruction on early assessment.

 • Blaivas and Olsson (1988) classified ISD as type III UI, to distinguish it from mobility-related types. ISD
   means sphincter dysfunction, whether neural or structural.
 • "Pipestem" urethra: a fixed urethra with little intrinsic closure, usually iatrogenic. Subtler ISD coexists

 • Valsalva leak point pressure (VLPP) is the abdominal pressure that causes urethral leakage. It cannot be
   measured during a detrusor contraction or with altered compliance. A low VLPP (<60 cm H₂O) indicates

 • Most female SUI involves some ISD, even with hypermobility. This is why pubovaginal sling, MUS and
   even bulking work in hypermobile patients. The converse is not true: Burch colposuspension, which
   corrects hypermobility, is less effective with severe ISD and limited mobility.
 • DeLancey emphasises urethral function by noting that the MUS, the most studied support procedure,
   achieves only about 40% subjective success.

PELVIC FLOOR SUPPORT MECHANISM
     • The pelvic organs are supported by the endopelvic fascia, the perineal membrane, the levator ani
       complex and their attachments to the bony pelvis.
     • The levator ani complex (pubococcygeus, puborectalis, iliococcygeus) is the primary support, forming a
       stable base on which the organs rest. The levator hiatus is the opening through which the urethra, vagina
       and rectum pass.
     • The uterosacral and cardinal ligaments, which are condensations of the endopelvic fascia, hold the
       organs in the right position for the muscles to support them. POP follows disruption or dysfunction of any
       of these structures.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF PELVIC ORGAN PROLAPSE
  • The urogenital hiatus is the part of the levator hiatus ventral to the perineal body, and all POP passes
    through it. It is kept closed by tonic levator activity, which compresses the urethra, vagina and rectum
    against the pubis.
  • Levator trauma or denervation → loss of tone → widened urogenital hiatus → POP. The hiatus widens
    further as POP progresses.
  • Childbirth is the commonest cause of levator trauma. On MRI, up to 20% of primiparous women have
    levator defects, which are not seen in nulliparous women.
  • Complete levator avulsion (found in about a quarter of symptomatic women on 3D/4D transperineal
    ultrasound) is associated with POP, advanced POP (stage III–IV) and three-compartment POP.
  • Muscle and fascia work together, as a sequence: while the levator keeps the hiatus closed, the
    endopelvic fascia carries little load → once the muscle fails, the ligaments carry all of it → over time they
    fail → POP. Ligament injury or stretching occurs with vaginal delivery, hysterectomy, chronic straining and
    ageing.
  • Collagen metabolism is consistently altered in POP, possibly with a changed type I:III collagen ratio.
    Whether this is cause or effect is unclear, but Ehlers–Danlos and Marfan syndromes increase POP risk,
    which supports a connective-tissue role.
  • Neuropathic injury. The pelvic floor has two nerve supplies:
        ◦ The pudendal nerve supplies the urethral and anal sphincters and the perineal muscles.
        ◦ The nerves to levator ani (S3–S5) supply the main pelvic floor musculature.
      Levator electromyography in primiparous women shows neuropathy in about a quarter at 6 weeks, rising
      slightly by 6 months. There is almost none after elective CS, but similar injury after CS in labour.

 KEY POINTS: PELVIC FLOOR SUPPORT MECHANISM AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF PELVIC ORGAN PROLAPSE
   • Support comes from the endopelvic fascia, perineal membrane, levator ani and their bony
     attachments.
   • Level I (uterus and upper vagina to the sacrum and sidewall) → apical prolapse. Level II (middle third)
     → anterior wall prolapse. Level III (distal) → urethral hypermobility, distal rectocele or perineal
     descent.
   • POP is multifactorial: direct muscle trauma, denervation, and injury or stretching of the endopelvic
     fascia.

Concept — In POP the muscle fails first and the fascia second. An intact levator unloads the ligaments. Once
birth injury or denervation opens the hiatus, the ligaments carry the load alone and eventually fail. This is
why labour, not caesarean delivery as such, is the injury.
