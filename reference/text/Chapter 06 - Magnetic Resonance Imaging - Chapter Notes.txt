Chapter 6 — Magnetic Resonance Imaging
INTRODUCTION
Position relative to computed tomography
 • Computed tomography (CT) remains the mainstay of urological cross-sectional imaging, but the
   resolution gap between CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is narrowing.
 • The central advantage of MRI is excellent soft-tissue contrast resolution, in many situations without
   intravenous contrast.
How the image is generated
 • Sequence: patient on a gantry through the magnet bore → free water protons align along the z-axis
   (head-to-toe, through the bore) → a radiofrequency (RF) coil over the region transmits RF pulses →
   when the pulse stops, protons release energy, which is detected and processed into the image.
 • A pulse sequence exploits the different ways tissues absorb and release this energy. Most coils now
   transmit and receive (dual-channel RF).
Weighting
 • T1 weighting reflects the time to return to equilibrium along the z-axis (longitudinal relaxation); T2
   weighting reflects the time to return to equilibrium in the x–y plane (transverse relaxation).
 • Fluid is dark on T1 and bright on T2.
 • In the kidney, the cortex is brighter than the medulla on T1; on T2 the medulla is relatively brighter.
Advantages of MRI
 • No ionising radiation, and therefore no risk of radiation-induced second malignancy.
 • Modality of choice in pregnancy, renal insufficiency and iodinated contrast allergy.
Gadolinium-based contrast agents and nephrogenic systemic fibrosis
 • Gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs) are non-iodinated. Iodinated CT contrast absorbs X-rays;
   GBCAs shorten the relaxation times of water, raising signal (enhancement), usually assessed on T1.
 • Gadolinium is a toxic heavy metal; it is chelated to prevent cellular uptake, making it non-toxic in
   almost all patients with normal renal function.
 • Nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF): the historical concern with glomerular filtration rate (GFR) below
   30 mL/min/1.73 m² — gadolinium deposits in skin and muscle as an insoluble precipitate and causes
   systemic fibrosis. This led to gadolinium being avoided in severe renal dysfunction.
 • GBCAs are not all alike. The American College of Radiology (ACR) states that group II agents carry little
   to no NSF risk, even in end-stage renal disease: gadobenate dimeglumine, gadoteridol, gadoterate
   meglumine and gadobutrol.
Concept — MRI trades CT's speed and spatial resolution for tissue contrast without radiation, and the
historical contrast barrier in renal failure has largely fallen with group II agents.

ADRENAL MAGNETIC RESONANCE IMAGING
 • MRI characterises tissue without contrast; minute quantities of lipid separate benign adenoma from
   malignancy. Most adrenal masses are incidental and non-functioning.
 • Adenomas are usually under 3 cm and non-functional, with a high lipid content that makes them
   readily distinguishable from malignant lesions.

   • Three lipid techniques exploit the different behaviour of fat and water protons: inversion recovery,
     chemical shift imaging (CSI) and fat saturation. CSI is the commonest in urology. Spectral presaturation
     with inversion recovery (SPIR) and spectral presaturation attenuated inversion recovery (SPAIR) detect
     fat by selective excitation of fat protons and T1 relaxation.

 Adrenal Adenoma
   • Physics of CSI: in water, oxygen pulls the electron cloud away from hydrogen; in fat (–CH₂–) the less
     electronegative carbon has a smaller effect. The resulting difference in shielding gives different
     precession frequencies — the chemical shift.
   • In-phase (IP): echo time (TE) set so fat and water signals add within a voxel. Out-of-phase (OP): TE set
     so signals cancel, so a voxel containing both fat and water loses signal.
   • Signal loss on OP relative to IP = intracytoplasmic (microscopic) fat = adenoma. No change makes fat
     less likely.
   • Qualitative (visual) comparison is sensitive but poorly specific; the quantitative signal intensity (SI)
     index raises specificity to very high levels:
       ◦ SI index (%) = (SI in-phase − SI out-of-phase) / SI in-phase × 100
       ◦ No agreed threshold; reported cut-offs range from 1.7% to 20%, with 5% at 3 tesla performing best
         in one series.
   • Lipid-poor adenomas (10–30%) give indeterminate CSI. Adrenocortical carcinoma washes out slowly,
     so contrast-enhanced CT with washout may better separate lipid-poor adenoma from other masses.
   • In unilateral functioning adenoma, CT and MRI are limited for lateralisation; adrenal vein sampling is a
     useful adjunct.
   • Figure 6.1: IP T1 shows a left adrenal mass isointense to muscle; OP T1 shows signal drop-out; single-
     shot T2 shows a low-signal nodule.

 Adrenal Cortical Carcinoma
   • Diagnosis combines clinical and imaging features. About 62% are hormonally active.
   • Risk rises with size: lesions ≤4 cm account for 2% of adrenocortical carcinomas (ACCs); 4–6 cm, 6%;
     over 6 cm, 25%.
   • T2 heterogeneous and high; T1 with gadolinium heterogeneous enhancement; CSI low signal (no lipid
     drop-out).
   • Local venous thrombosis is associated with ACC and is detectable on MRI.
   • ACC is metabolically active: fluorodeoxyglucose–positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) separates
     ACC from adenoma with high sensitivity and specificity.
   • Figure 6.2: hyperintense on moderately T2-weighted short tau inversion recovery (STIR) but isointense
     on heavily T2-weighted single-shot fast spin echo — apparent T2 brightness depends on the degree of
     T2 weighting.

 Myelolipoma
   • Benign mass of mature fat and bone marrow elements; about 6.5% of incidentally detected adrenal
     masses. Lesions over 4 cm can mimic malignancy.
   • High T1 signal; signal suppression on frequency-selective fat suppression (macroscopic fat); India ink
     artefact — a dark line around the lesion or at organ interfaces on OP images, from voxels containing
     both fat and water.

 Metastasis
   • Suspect in the setting of a known primary. Large, irregular, heterogeneous, occasionally necrotic.
   • High T2 signal from higher fluid content than adenoma; heterogeneous gadolinium enhancement with
     delayed peak enhancement relative to adenoma; a time-to-peak cut-off of about 53 seconds or more
     characterises metastasis well.
   • Trap — lipid-containing primaries: a metastasis carries the histology of its primary, so it can be CSI-
     positive and mimic adenoma. Reported with clear cell renal cell carcinoma, liposarcoma and
     hepatocellular carcinoma.
   • Figure 6.4: metachronous right adrenal metastasis after nephrectomy for clear cell carcinoma — drop-
     out on OP (microscopic fat) and T2 hyperintensity.

 Pheochromocytoma
   • High T2 signal (the "light bulb" sign) was traditionally considered diagnostic, but about a third of
     pheochromocytomas are T2-dark.
   • Pheochromocytoma, ACC and metastasis can all be T2-bright, and brightness varies with T2 weighting
     — the sign is neither sensitive nor specific.
   • Figure 6.5: isointense on heavily weighted T2; bright on moderately weighted fat-suppressed T2;
     marked early enhancement on post-contrast T1.

 Haemorrhage and Other Pathologies
   • Adrenal haematoma changes with age as red cells break down and haemosiderin is deposited: early
     isointense to hypointense on T1 and low on T2 → at 1–7 weeks hyperintense on T1 fat-suppressed
     and T2; a low-signal haemosiderin rim on both T1 and T2.
   • Teratoma: mixed tissues (bone, teeth, hair, muscle) give mixed T1 and T2 signal; enhancement of the
     peripheral rim, solid components and internal septations.
   • Intermediate SI index on CSI: lymphoma, neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroma, haemangioma and
     granulomatous disease.

 RENAL MAGNETIC RESONANCE IMAGING
 Cysts
   • Simple cysts look alike on ultrasound, CT and MRI; MRI characterises complex cysts better.
   • Haemorrhagic cyst contents are T1-bright (paramagnetic blood-breakdown products —
     methaemoglobin); proteinaceous contents are also T1-bright; chronic haemorrhage gives a black ring
     on T2 along the wall.
   • Benign cysts, including benign complex cysts, do not enhance.
   • As an independent risk factor for renal cell carcinoma (RCC), wall enhancement is more sensitive and
     specific than wall calcification. On CT, calcification can cause artefact that hides enhancing mural
     nodules; on MRI calcium is a signal void and does not interfere, so MRI detects enhancement in a
     calcified complex cyst better than CT.
   • Pseudocapsule: a low-signal rim on T1 and T2, assessed better on MRI than CT. Absence of the
     pseudocapsule predicts pT3a disease.
 Subtyping renal cell carcinoma
   • Multiparametric approach: T1; multiplanar T2 with and without fat suppression; dynamic contrast-
     enhanced (DCE) phases (arterial, corticomedullary, nephrographic, excretory); diffusion-weighted
     imaging (DWI) with apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps; and CSI. This subtypes renal masses
     better than CT.
   • Clear cell RCC (ccRCC), the commonest subtype: heterogeneous high T2 signal from haemorrhage,
     necrosis and cysts (Fig. 6.6).
   • Papillary RCC (pRCC): homogeneous low T2 signal from haemosiderin in histiocytes (Fig. 6.7). A
     haemorrhagic cyst with an enhancing peripheral wall, or a solid hypo-enhancing T2-low mass,
     separates pRCC from other subtypes with high specificity.

   • DWI detects restricted water movement in intracellular and extracellular spaces; ADC ≥1281 × 10⁻⁶
     mm²/s favours clear cell over non-clear cell RCC.
   • Oncocytoma's central scar is T2-bright, but present in only 54–80%, and it is also seen in about 37% of
     chromophobe carcinomas — the scar does not discriminate (Fig. 6.8). Both tumours are usually
     peripheral and hypovascular relative to cortex. Necrosis is T2-high and T1-low, like the oncocytoma
     scar.
   • CSI: about 59% of ccRCCs contain intracytoplasmic lipid. Once angiomyolipoma (AML) has been
     excluded (macroscopic fat on standard sequences, Fig. 6.9), an SI drop of 25% or more is diagnostic of
     ccRCC among renal tumours. CSI is highly specific but insensitive for ccRCC.
   • Trap: RCC rarely contains macroscopic fat; fat plus calcification favours RCC over AML.
   • Enhancement: a relative SI increase of 15% or more on gadolinium-enhanced T1 counts as
     enhancement, separating cyst from RCC with 100% sensitivity; peak at 2–4 minutes. ccRCC enhances
     by more than 200% in all three phases, significantly more than chromophobe and papillary carcinoma.

 Evaluating Local Tumour Extent
   • Venous tumour thrombus: MRI was historically superior to CT for renal vein and inferior vena cava
     thrombus; current MRI and CT perform equally. Gadolinium separates tumour thrombus (enhances)
     from bland thrombus (does not).
   • Lymph nodes: MRI and CT both detect lymphadenopathy. Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles
     are under investigation: normal nodes phagocytose them and lose T2 signal; metastatic nodes do not.
   • Multiparametric MRI adds information on the tumour microenvironment.
 Concept — Enhancement is the single most important sign of malignancy in a renal mass, and MRI sees
 it through calcium where CT may not. Subtyping follows one pattern: clear cell is bright, hypervascular, CSI-
 positive and high-ADC; papillary is dark, hypovascular and low-ADC.

 UPPER TRACT AND LOWER TRACT MRI FOR TRANSITIONAL CELL CARCINOMA
 Upper tract
   • Magnetic resonance urography (MRU) is added to renal mass protocols and is useful when other
     modalities are contraindicated. Static MRU: heavily T2-weighted, urine bright. Excretory MRU:
     gadolinium-enhanced T1.
   • MRU and CT urography are equally accurate for obstruction.
   • Stones and calcification have no signal and appear as voids. Tumour, clot, gas and sloughed papillae
     appear as low signal or voids against bright urine on T2.
   • Fast acquisitions (about 2 seconds per image) separate true pathology from ureteric peristalsis.
   • Normalised ADC ≤451 × 10⁻⁶ mm²/s separates TCC of the renal pelvis from central RCC. ADC varies
     with b-value and TE, so cut-offs need institutional validation.
   • On DWI, upper tract TCC is hyperintense and homogeneous; RCC is heterogeneous.
 Bladder
   • MRI beats CT through greater contrast between bladder wall layers, separating invasive from
     superficial disease with good accuracy (Fig. 6.10).
   • The detrusor is the low-signal layer on T2; urine, mucosa and submucosa are all high signal and cannot
     be separated. Muscle-invasive tumour is high signal disrupting the low-signal muscle layer.
   • Bladder TCC is DWI-hyperintense against tissue and urine. Underdistension causes false negatives.
   • Vesical Imaging–Reporting and Data System (VI-RADS): a 5-point score for muscle invasion from T2,
     DWI and DCE findings.
   • Figure 6.10: a T2-low right wall filling defect; an enhancing polypoid mass on early DCE without wall
     invasion; transurethral resection of bladder tumour confirmed high-grade papillary urothelial
     carcinoma without muscle invasion.
 Concept — Urine is the contrast agent on T2, so anything within it — tumour, clot, stone — is dark; in the
 bladder wall the question is whether the dark detrusor line is intact, and VI-RADS formalises that.

 PROSTATE MAGNETIC RESONANCE IMAGING
 Evolution and landmark evidence
   • Prostate cancer (CaP) long lacked reliable imaging; the standard was transrectal ultrasound with an
     extended sextant (12–14 core) biopsy. Advances included 1.5 T → 3 T magnets and better protocols.
   • PROMIS (Prostate MR Imaging Study, 2017): level 1b evidence for MRI before biopsy in men with
     elevated prostate-specific antigen (PSA); a follow-up analysis showed cost-effectiveness.

   • Göteborg (Swedish screening study): MRI in a PSA-based screening protocol (threshold 2.8 ng/mL)
     roughly halved biopsies.
   • PRECISION (2018): in biopsy-naive men, MRI with or without targeted biopsy detected more clinically
     significant and less clinically insignificant cancer.
   • Cochrane review (2019): MRI sensitivity 0.91, specificity 0.37 for Grade Group (GG) 2 or higher —
     excellent at ruling out, poor at ruling in.
   • American Urological Association (AUA) and European Association of Urology (EAU) both include
     prostate MRI in the work-up of elevated PSA.
 Coils
   • "The currency of MRI is signal." Signal was historically boosted with surface coils and an endorectal coil
     (ERC), at the cost of discomfort and expense.
   • At 3 T the ERC is largely historical: image quality and detection of GG3 or higher are equivalent without
     it; GG2 detection is somewhat inferior. The ERC remains useful on older 1.5 T scanners. If used, give an
     antispasmodic to reduce rectal motion artefact.

 T2-Weighted Imaging
   • The anatomic sequence; should be triplanar (axial, coronal, sagittal) or equivalent (Fig. 6.11).
   • Normal peripheral zone (PZ) is high signal. The central gland with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)
     shows well-demarcated nodules of heterogeneous signal.
   • Low T2 signal is non-specific: cancer, prostatitis, atrophy, scar, post-biopsy haemorrhage and BPH
     nodules. Stromal BPH nodules within the PZ cause false positives.
   • T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) alone is specific but insensitive; adding functional sequences raises
     negative (NPV) and positive predictive value (PPV) above 90%.
   • T2WI assesses extraprostatic extension (EPE) and seminal vesicle invasion (SVI), both appearing as
     low signal.
   • Figure 6.11: a well-circumscribed heterogeneous BPH nodule within the PZ, with restriction on ADC
     and type 2–3 enhancement curves, was benign on fusion biopsy.

 Diffusion-Weighted Imaging/Apparent Diffusion Coefficient Maps
   • DWI measures Brownian motion of water. Higher cellularity → tighter packing → restricted water →
     high signal on DWI.
   • b-values set the threshold for detecting restriction; as the b-value rises, less-restricted tissue loses
     signal. Use multiple b-values, including at least one above 1000; some add b-2000, on which cancer is
     bright relative to the gland.
   • The ADC map is the quantitative counterpart; cancer is dark (low signal) on ADC.
   • ADC correlates inversely with Gleason score: higher cellularity → less extracellular space → more
     restriction → lower ADC.
   • Figure 6.12: a large anterior central gland GG 4+4 cancer after 16 negative biopsies — low ADC, bright
     b-2000, type 3 curve, elevated transfer constant and rate constant, found on MR–ultrasound fusion
     biopsy.

 Dynamic Contrast-Enhanced MRI
   • T1-weighted imaging with GBCAs measuring perfusion and vascular permeability over time — not
     anatomy, and not simply "enhances or not."

   • Temporal resolution ≤15 seconds. Under 10 seconds was once preferred, but no benefit was shown
     across 1.4–14.9 seconds.
   • Three levels of analysis: qualitative (visual early enhancement and washout); semi-quantitative (time–
     intensity curves); quantitative (Tofts pharmacokinetic model).
   • Transfer constant (K-trans): rate of transfer from the intravascular to the extracellular space
     (permeability and flow). Rate constant (K-ep): rate of efflux back to the vascular space. Not yet in
     routine workflow; being studied for decision-support software.
   • Sensitivity and specificity vary widely with patient selection, technique, pathology correlation and
     reader experience. DCE is secondary to T2WI and DWI in scoring.

 Multiparametric Magnetic Resonance Imaging
   • Anatomic: T1 and T2. Functional: DWI with ADC, DCE, and optionally spectroscopy.
   • MR spectroscopy is not routine: about 15 minutes, labour-intensive, and seldom changes
     interpretation.
   • T2 + DWI + DCE gives an NPV above 90%.
   • Quality needs magnet tuning, dedicated staff and pathology feedback; start from the ACR's Prostate
     Imaging–Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) v2.1. The technologist reviews images during the scan
     and repeats sequences as needed.
   • Uses: detection, quantification, staging, treatment planning; selection for active surveillance (good
     NPV); bone and nodal assessment at diagnosis (moderate-to-good nodal sensitivity and specificity).

 Biparametric Magnetic Resonance Imaging
   • T2 + DWI, without DCE: lower cost, shorter scan, no GBCA risk, but may limit diagnostic capability and
     limits assessment beyond the prostate (staging).
   • Accuracy varies with magnet strength and reader experience; a meta-analysis found no significant
     difference from mpMRI for CaP detection.
   • Reasonable for screening low-risk men when image quality is optimised and experienced readers
     interpret.

 Magnetic Resonance Fusion-Guided Prostate Biopsy
   • The dominant determinant of performance is mpMRI quality and the imaging team, not the fusion
     platform.
   • Targeted biopsy or mpMRI alone is not yet an alternative to the standard of care in screening.

   • mpMRI helps select men with elevated PSA for biopsy (PSA rises with inflammation, infection, BPH,
     manipulation); it can show BPH or inflammation and may spare a repeat biopsy after a negative 12-
     core biopsy.
   • mpMRI detects intermediate- and high-risk disease and misses low-grade, low-volume cancer —
     desirable, as it reduces overdiagnosis and overtreatment. Over a third of men with elevated PSA have
     no visible lesion, and 12-core biopsy then rarely finds clinically significant cancer.
   • Göteborg-2 (2022): targeted biopsy alone avoided overdetection of insignificant cancer and missed
     under 0.2% of intermediate-risk CaP.
   • Fusion reaches anterior and central gland lesions undersampled by systematic biopsy; 12-core biopsy
     also misses many PZ lesions.
   • Fusion outperforms cognitive targeting: a randomised trial showed higher overall and clinically
     significant detection with fusion plus template than cognitive plus template biopsy.
   • Cancer detection rate for fusion-targeted biopsy of moderate- to high-suspicion lesions: about 50–
     72%. Deep-learning predictions with PI-RADS and clinical variables are being developed into risk
     nomograms.
 Concept — Prostate MRI is a filter before the needle: T2 gives anatomy, DWI and ADC give cellularity and
 drive the score, and DCE is the tiebreaker. Quality (PI-QUAL) must be assured before scoring (PI-RADS),
 change is tracked with PRECISE, and the yield of targeting depends on the scan and the reader rather than
 the device.

   KEY POINTS
    • NSF is now largely historical; newer GBCAs can be used safely in severe renal insufficiency.
    • Pheochromocytoma, adrenal metastasis and primary ACC are all bright on T2.
    • In a renal mass on MRI, enhancement is the most important indicator of malignancy.
    • Prostate MRI has transformed the CaP screening and diagnostic pathway and improved cancer
      detection.
