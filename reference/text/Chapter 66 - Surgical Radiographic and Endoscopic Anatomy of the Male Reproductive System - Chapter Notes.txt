SCROTUM

Anatomy
Gross Structure
 • The testis and spermatic cord are invested in six tissue layers, a consequence of descent from the
   retroperitoneum; each layer is a surgical plane in scrotal surgery.
 • Scrotal skin: hair-bearing, pigmented, rugated; sebaceous follicles and sweat glands.
 • Median raphe marks the scrotal septum, running in the midline from the urethral meatus to the anus and
   dividing the testes into separate compartments.
 • Layer sequence, superficial to deep: skin → tunica dartos → external spermatic fascia → cremasteric
   fascia → internal spermatic fascia → tunica vaginalis (parietal, then visceral).

 KEY POINTS: SCROTUM
   • The smooth muscle of the dartos fascia under the scrotal skin is continuous with Colles fascia, Scarpa
     fascia and the dartos fascia of the penis.
   • Scrotal wall arteries run parallel to the rugae and do not cross the median raphe.
   • Branches of the ilioinguinal and genitofemoral nerves innervate the anterior scrotal wall.

Concept — The scrotum is abdominal wall carried into the perineum: every layer has an abdominal
counterpart, and nothing — artery, vein, lymphatic or nerve — crosses the median raphe, so each
hemiscrotum is a self-contained unit draining to its own groin.


TESTIS

Anatomy
Gross Structure
 • Paired organs with exocrine and endocrine function; volume 15–25 mL, longitudinal axis 4.5–5.1 cm.
 • Appendix testis: small pedunculated structure at the superior pole; a müllerian (paramesonephric) duct
   remnant, present in some males.
 • Capsule, external to internal: visceral tunica vaginalis → tunica albuginea → tunica vasculosa.
 • Tunica albuginea: thick fibroelastic capsule with smooth muscle cells through collagen → possible
   capsular contractility → proposed roles in regulating testicular blood flow and propelling seminiferous
   tubule fluid out of the testis.
 • Posteriorly the capsule projects inward as the mediastinum testis — an echogenic band parallel to the
   epididymis on ultrasonography.
 • The epididymis attaches on the posterolateral aspect.
Microanatomic Architecture
 • Septa radiate from the mediastinum, forming about 250 wedge-shaped lobules, each containing at least
   one centrifugal artery.
 • 600–1200 seminiferous tubules per testis, total length 280–400 m; long, coiled and looped, terminating
   in the rete testis.
 • Tubules contain developing germ cells and supporting cells: Sertoli cells, fibrocytes and myoid cells of the
   basement membrane.
 • Interstitium = 20–30% of testicular volume: Leydig cells, mast cells, macrophages, nerves, blood vessels,
   lymphatics.

 • Course: aorta → crosses psoas and ureter → inguinal ring → spermatic cord. Branching most often occurs
   within the inguinal canal; the number of branches entering the testis varies (single most commonly).
 • Countercurrent heat exchange with the pampiniform plexus → cord arterial blood 2–4 °C below rectal
   temperature.
 • Near the mediastinum the artery is highly coiled and branches; it runs inferiorly along the posterior
   surface within the parenchyma, supplying the anterior upper pole and the anterior, medial and lateral
   lower pole.
 • Centrifugal arteries pass in the septa → arterioles → intertubular and peritubular capillaries.
 • Testicular–deferential intercommunications may preserve viability after testicular artery ligation, yet
   atrophy and/or azoospermia have followed ligation in adults and children.
 • Surgical rules: no traction suture through the lower pole; biopsy the medial or lateral surface of the
   upper pole; the midportion carries fewer vessels than either pole.
 • Prior vasectomy → deferential artery may be compromised → in later cord or testicular surgery preserve
   the testicular artery and counsel on devascularisation risk.
Venous Drainage
 • Testicular veins are anomalous: they do not travel with their arteries.
 • Parenchymal veins drain to a mediastinal group or a surface group; these coalesce with the deferential
   veins as the pampiniform plexus.

 • Functional role still being defined; postulated roles in Leydig cell steroidogenesis and vascular tone.
 • “Trifecta complex” — three cord nerve distributions thought to drive chronic orchialgia: perivasal,
   posterior periarterial/lipomatous, intracremasteric. Microsurgical spermatic cord denervation targets
   these.
 • Some fibres cross to the contralateral pelvic plexus → may explain unilateral pathology affecting the
   contralateral testis (reported with varicocele and testicular tumour).
 • Genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve: sensation to the parietal and visceral tunica vaginalis and
   overlying scrotum; travels with the testicular artery, ramifies in the tunica albuginea, never enters the
   seminiferous tubules; the seminiferous epithelium is nerve-free.
Blood–Testis Barrier
 • Primary level: Sertoli–Sertoli tight junctions → basal and adluminal compartments; premeiotic germ
   cells separated from other germ cells.
 • Additional levels: capillary endothelium and peritubular myoid cells.
 • Evidence: fluid leaving the tubules and testis differs substantially from plasma and lymph — the anatomic
   component.
 • Functional component may isolate the haploid gamete or support meiosis; significance unproven (further
   in Chapter 67).

Imaging
Ultrasonography
 • Ultrasonography (US) is the primary modality for the scrotum and contents: 7.5–10 MHz transducers,
   grey-scale real-time, colour flow and power Doppler.
 • Indications: scrotal pain, varicocele recurrence after repair, equivocal examination, testicular mass,
   swelling, epididymitis or orchitis.
 • In scrotal pain most studies are normal or benign; only a small minority show a finding needing urgent
   surgery.
 • Technique: supine, warm room, warmed gel.
 • Normal findings: scrotal wall 3–4 mm, hypoechoic; small anechoic physiological fluid between the tunica
   vaginalis layers (more = hydrocele); mediastinum = posterior echogenic band parallel to the epididymis;
   testis fine, homogeneous, medium-level echoes, about 5 × 3 × 2 cm; colour Doppler shows vessels in
   most.
 • Waveforms: intratesticular and capsular arteries — low impedance, high diastolic flow (low testicular
   vascular resistance); supratesticular testicular, deferential and cremasteric arteries also low impedance.
 • Suspected torsion → prompt surgical exploration; Doppler may be used in the acute scrotum but must
   not delay it.
 • Infertility: US rarely indicated; used for varicocele and palpable masses. Varicocele study: cord examined
   erect with Valsalva; Doppler shows persistent reflux after repair.
 • Varicocele is a clinical diagnosis; no agreed significant vein size; US reserved for equivocal examination or
   when examination is limited (cord lipoma, fatty infiltration).
Computed Tomography
 • Computed tomography (CT) is not primary; useful for inguinal and cord pathology; scrotal findings often
   incidental. Shows air in Fournier gangrene and hydrocele fluid.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging
 • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a secondary modality for indeterminate or nondiagnostic US.
 • Clearly separates tunica albuginea, parenchyma and epididymis; distinguishes intra- from
   extratesticular, solid from cystic, complicated from simple collections; resolves structures greater than
   1 mm.
 • Advantage: both hemiscrota and the inguinal region in one plane; also characterises cord pathology.
 • Magnetic resonance angiography has identified inguinal testicular nubbin in the nonpalpable testis.
 • Example: nonobstructive azoospermia with bilateral testicular atrophy (volumes 5–6 mL, homogeneous
   signal) and normal cord structures.

 KEY POINTS: TESTIS
   • The seminiferous tubules contain developing germ cells.
   • Leydig cells produce testosterone; Sertoli cells support germ cell development.
   • Three arterial supplies: testicular, deferential and cremasteric.
   • Testicular lymphatics drain to the para-aortic and interaortocaval nodes.
   • Nerves implicated in chronic orchialgia: perivasal, posterior periarterial/lipomatous and
     intracremasteric complexes.
   • Sertoli tight junctions form the anatomic component of the blood–testis barrier.
   • US is the primary imaging modality for intrascrotal contents.

Concept — The testis is built for thermal and immunological isolation: countercurrent exchange keeps it cool,
Sertoli junctions sequester germ cells, and its retroperitoneal origin fixes its artery, vein, lymph and nerves to
the great vessels — every surgical rule in this section protects one of these arrangements.


EPIDIDYMIS

Anatomy
Gross Structure
 • Attached to the superior posterolateral testis; covered by a thin layer of tunica vaginalis.
 • Three portions: caput (head), corpus (body), cauda (tail).
 • 8–12 efferent ducts from the rete converge in the caput into one tightly coiled duct about 0.1 mm in
   diameter (the figure legend gives 12–20 efferent ductules).
 • Caput visibly darker — tightly compacted, narrow efferent ducts; corpus lighter — wider tubule. Thin
   septa separate the efferent-duct region from the tubule.
 • Distal tubule = sperm reservoir; thickens as it becomes the vas, surrounded by smooth muscle.

Arterial Supply
 • Testicular artery branch → head and body, dividing into superior and inferior epididymal branches.
 • Deferential artery → tail; cremasteric artery also contributes.

 • Arteries enter through the connective tissue septa; coiled vessels straighten into the microvascular bed.
Venous Drainage
 • Directly into the pampiniform plexus, or via veins communicating with the vasal veins; the vasal–caudal

Nerve Supply
 • Intermediate and inferior spermatic nerves from the hypogastric and pelvic plexuses.
 • Sympathetic fibres sparse proximally (caput, efferent ducts), forming a peritubular plexus near vessels.
 • Nerve density rises progressively from the mid-corpus to the cauda, matched by increasing smooth
   muscle.

Imaging
Ultrasonography
 • Adjunct to examination for equivocal findings; highly sensitive for cysts and dilation.
 • Normally homogeneous and isoechoic with the testis; body and tail may be hyper- or hypoechoic.
 • In a small minority the epididymis is inverted — caput at the inferior pole.
 • Mean diameters: head 9 mm, body 3.5 mm, tail 4.8 mm; appendix epididymis seen in a minority, about
   2 mm long.
 • Head size associates with normal sperm morphology; colour Doppler detects flow in all regions; veins
   may extend to either testicular pole.
Magnetic Resonance Imaging
 • Multiparametric MRI: high soft-tissue contrast, functional data, contrast-enhancement sensitivity; less
   operator-dependent → better standardisation.
 • Characterises benign and malignant masses; growing role in male infertility.
 • T1 signal intensity correlates with epididymal obstruction → predicts need for vasoepididymostomy
   rather than vasovasostomy.
 • Functional MRI may detect early spermatogenic impairment and predict sperm extraction success in
   nonobstructive azoospermia.

 KEY POINTS: EPIDIDYMIS
   • The epididymis consists of the caput, corpus and cauda.
   • Each region has a slightly different cellular structure, gene expression and epigenetic function.

Concept — The epididymis is a regionally specialised duct: cell mix, gene expression and innervation change
along its length to match function, from absorption and maturation proximally to storage and propulsion in
the cauda — hence nerves and smooth muscle increase toward the tail.


VAS DEFERENS

Anatomy
Gross Structure
Course: contiguous with the distal epididymis → convoluted for the first 2–3 cm → straightens, lying
posterior to the spermatic vessels in the cord → inguinal canal → separates from the gonadal vessels →
pelvic side wall → crosses anterior to the ureter → behind the bladder base, above the prostatic base →
ampulla → joins the seminal vesicle → ejaculatory duct within the prostatic urethra.
 • Total length 30–35 cm; outer diameter 1.5–4.5 mm in healthy fertile men; lumen 0.2–0.7 mm (mean
   0.43 mm).
 • Derives from the mesonephric (wolffian) duct, with the epididymis and ejaculatory duct.

Microanatomic Architecture
 • Outer adventitia carrying vessels and small nerves.
 • Thick three-layer smooth muscle wall: outer longitudinal → middle circular → inner longitudinal.
 • Mucosa: pseudostratified columnar epithelium — columnar principal cells with microvilli and small
   hemispherical basal cells — on loose connective tissue stroma.
Arterial Supply
 • Deferential artery from the superior vesical artery (the testis section also allows an inferior vesical
   origin).
Venous Drainage

Lymphatic Drainage
 • About one-fifth of cord lymphatics lie around the vas; drainage to external and internal iliac nodes.
Nerve Supply
 • Densely innervated — about half of the nerves in the cord and spermatic fascia cluster around the vas.
 • Sensory fibres may transmit pain in chronic scrotal pain.
 • Sympathetic fibres (hypogastric → presacral nerve; deferential and testicular plexuses) drive vasal
   peristalsis and vasoconstriction.
 • Adrenergic fibres in all three muscle layers, densest in the outer longitudinal layer; a substantial
   minority of fibres are cholinergic; fibres also contain neuropeptide Y and nitric oxide.
 • Somatostatin, galanin, enkephalin and vasoactive intestinal peptide present; function unclear.

Imaging
Vasogram
Sequence: partial vasotomy at the junction of straight and convoluted vas → 24-gauge angiocatheter or
ureteric catheter advanced into the lumen → inject → observe.

 KEY POINTS: VAS DEFERENS
   • The proximal vas is convoluted, then straightens as it travels into the scrotum and pelvis.
   • The vas has a muscular wall and is important for sperm transit.
   • The lumen measures 0.2–0.7 mm.
   • The superior vesical artery gives rise to the deferential artery, which supplies the vas and is important
     for collateral testicular flow.

Concept — The vas is a muscular, densely adrenergic conduit: its three-layer wall and sympathetic supply
exist to drive emission, and the same dense innervation makes it a pain source, while its artery is the
testicular collateral that vasectomy can sacrifice.


SEMINAL VESICLES AND EJACULATORY DUCTS

Anatomy
Gross Structure
 • Paired, tightly coiled tubular structures posterior to the bladder and distal ureters, superoposterior to
   the prostate.
 • Each fuses with the ampulla of the vas → ejaculatory duct → prostatic urethra at the verumontanum.
 • Ejaculatory duct orifices lie inferolateral to the seminal colliculus at 5 and 7 o'clock, covered by one-way
   valves.
 • Embryology: wolffian; seminal vesicles bud from the distal mesonephric duct at 12 weeks' gestation.
   Development is linked to the ureter and kidney → congenital anomalies commonly coexist.
Microanatomic Architecture
 • Retroperitoneal; loose adventitia; thin smooth muscle in circumferential and longitudinal orientation.
 • Muscle sheaths of seminal vesicle and vas merge with the prostatic capsule at the base.
 • Ejaculatory ducts: longitudinal smooth muscle only; middle layer of vesicles and ducts collagenous.
 • Mucosa: pseudostratified columnar; in the ejaculatory duct above a basal cell layer.
Arterial Supply
 • Vesiculodeferential artery (from the superior vesical) → anterior surface near the tip.
 • Prostatovesicular branch (internal iliac / inferior vesical) → additional supply; variants arise from the
   pudendal or superior vesical artery.
 • Ejaculatory duct: inferior vesical artery branches.
Venous Drainage
 • Follows the arteries: vesiculodeferential veins and inferior vesical plexus → pelvic venous plexus.
Lymphatic Drainage
 • Internal iliac nodes.
Nerve Supply
 • Parasympathetic from the pelvic plexus; sympathetic from the hypogastric and superior lumbar nerves;
   pelvic plexus also supplies the ejaculatory ducts.
 • Noradrenergic density exceeds cholinergic — appropriate for smooth muscle contraction during
   emission.

 • Axons also contain acetylcholine, neuropeptide Y and nitric oxide (as in the vas); parasympathetic role
   unclear, possibly secretomotor.
Seminal Fluid
 • Seminal vesicles supply about 70% of semen volume.
 • Fluid is alkaline; contains fructose (sperm energy source), prostaglandins, enzymes and semenogelin.
 • Semenogelin forms a gel matrix around sperm, preventing immediate capacitation; the gel is liquefied
   by prostate-specific antigen.
 • Seminal fluid proteomics: emerging diagnostic tool for fertility and oncology.

Imaging
Transrectal Ultrasound
 • Transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) is accurate for echogenicity, homogeneity, thickened septa, calcification and
   cysts.
 • The vesicle shrinks after ejaculation; post-ejaculation anteroposterior diameter above 16 mm and
   volume above 6 mL suggest dilation or obstruction.
 • Vasal ampulla also assessed (mean about 4.4 mm).
 • Vesicle volume and ampullary diameter rise with prostate volume → a large prostate may compress the
   ejaculatory ducts.
 • Vesicle aspiration or vesiculography with TRUS → sperm cryopreservation and assisted reproduction, or
   before endoscopic treatment of ejaculatory duct obstruction.
Computed Tomography
 • Vesicles show soft-tissue or fluid attenuation; CT best for stones and congenital anomalies; MRI better for
   noncongenital disease.
Magnetic Resonance Imaging
 • MRI is the modality of choice (high spatial resolution, multiplanar). Seminal fluid is T2 hyperintense
   within a cystic-appearing vesicle with thin intermediate-to-low T2 septa.
Endoscopy
 • Ejaculatory duct orifices at the distal lateral verumontanum, 5 and 7 o'clock; utricle orifice midline at the
   distal-most verumontanum.
 • Seminal vesiculoscopy through the native ducts, with or without laser fenestration or transurethral
   resection of the utricle.
 • Instrument: 4.5–6.9 Fr paediatric flexible ureteroscope; an adjunct to MRI before planned endoscopic
   intervention.

PROSTATE

Anatomy
Gross Structure
 • Lies just inferior to the bladder; base at the bladder neck, apex distal-most. The prostatic urethra
   narrows toward the apex at the striated external sphincter (urogenital diaphragm).
 • Internal urethral sphincter: bladder neck → through the prostate → external sphincter; longitudinal and
   circular smooth muscle; circular fibres thickest at the bladder neck, thinnest at the membranous
   urethra; key to continence at rest.

 • Forms under androgen control by masculinisation of the urogenital sinus: epithelial buds → lumens →

 • Normal prostate about 25 mL, 3 × 3 × 5 cm; enlarges with age, fastest in the transition zone.
Microanatomic Architecture
 • Stroma: smooth muscle within a collagen scaffold, rich in myosin, desmin and α-actin.
 • Glands contain basal, luminal and neuroendocrine cells; ducts and acini have basal epithelium; lumen
   lined by tall columnar secretory cells with microvilli.
 • Secretion path: acini → minor then major ducts → urethra; each acinus wrapped in connective tissue and
   smooth muscle; interstitial cells of Cajal may aid transport.
 • Neuroendocrine cells regulate exocrine secretion and growth; malignant change → primary
   neuroendocrine tumours.
 • Urothelium extends into prostatic ducts at their urethral junction in the transition zone.
 • Internal sphincter surrounds the small periurethral glands with longitudinal smooth muscle.
 • Benign hyperplasia in the transition zone → bladder outlet obstruction.
 • Microacinar proliferation with loss of the basal cell layer = malignancy, mostly peripheral zone.
Ejaculatory Fluid
 • Prostatic secretion is rich in zinc and citrate, with proteins and enzymes important for fertility.
 • Prostate-specific antigen (PSA): cleaves insulin-like growth factor binding protein-3 (possible local
   growth promotion); expression from tall columnar cells is androgen-dependent.
 • PSA normally does not cross the epithelial basement membrane; disrupted architecture (inflammation,
   malignancy) lets it leak into stroma and circulation → raised serum PSA.

   bundles with the cavernous nerves, ending at the pelvic diaphragm.
 • Prostatic artery origin is variable: terminal inferior vesical, anterior division directly, obturator or internal

Prostatic urethra
 • Urethral crest projects from the posterior midline along the full prostatic urethra and stops at the striated
   sphincter; all glands drain into prostatic sinuses on either side of the crest.
 • An angle at the midpoint (0–90°, variable) turns the urethra anteriorly and divides it into preprostatic
   (proximal) and prostatic (distal) segments — functionally distinct; all glandular elements open distal to
   the angle.
 • Verumontanum = widening and protrusion of the crest from the posterior wall.
 • Prostatic utricle (müllerian remnant) — a slit at the apex of the verumontanum; orifice cystoscopically
   visible, about 6 mm; the two small ejaculatory duct openings flank it.
Membranous urethra
 • Thin smooth muscle layer, then an outer layer of circular striated muscle, horseshoe-shaped on the
   anterior surface near the apex.
 • Striated muscle extends from bladder base and anterior prostate along the whole membranous urethra;
   signet-ring shaped, broad-based, narrowing through the urogenital hiatus of the levator to the apex.
 • Posterior portion inserts into the perineal body throughout its length.

 • Bulbomembranous urethra: the 2 cm within the urogenital diaphragm and striated sphincter plus the first

PENIS

Anatomy
Gross Structure
 • Paired corpora cavernosa (erectile bodies) extend proximally as the crura, attached to the pubic arch.
 • The urethra runs in the corpus spongiosum, whose proximal part is the bulb; the glans is the distal
   expansion of the spongiosum.
 • Dorsum = superior surface in erection; ventrum = inferior surface, containing the urethra.
 • Corpora join beneath the pubis (penile hilum) and form most of the shaft.

   physiologically and pharmacologically as a single unit.
Tunica albuginea
 • Tough, mainly collagenous envelope of the corpora with an elastic-fibre lattice; mean thickness 2–3 mm.
 • Ventrally it is modified to form a groove accommodating the spongiosum, where the outer layer is thin.
