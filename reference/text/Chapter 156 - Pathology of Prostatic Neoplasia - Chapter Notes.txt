PROSTATIC INTRAEPITHELIAL NEOPLASIA
 • Prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN) = architecturally benign acini or ducts lined by cytologically
   atypical cells; subclassified into low-grade PIN (LGPIN) and high-grade PIN (HGPIN).
 • LGPIN is not reported: pathologists cannot reproducibly separate it from benign tissue, and it carries no
   excess cancer risk on repeat biopsy compared with a benign biopsy.
 • HGPIN as precursor — evidence:
      ◦ More and larger HGPIN foci in prostates with cancer; more HGPIN → more multifocal carcinoma.
      ◦ Shared biomarkers and molecular changes: about 20% of HGPIN carries the TMPRSS2:ERG fusion
        (transmembrane protease serine 2 : ETS-related gene), present in about 50% of prostate cancers.
      ◦ Direct antecedent of adenocarcinoma, but additional genomic alterations are needed for invasion;
        carcinoma glands may be seen budding off HGPIN.
 • Incidence on biopsy about 4–5%; reported range 0–25%, the spread reflecting interobserver threshold
   between LGPIN and HGPIN.
 • Unifocal HGPIN: repeat-biopsy cancer risk not significantly higher than after a benign biopsy.
 • Multifocal HGPIN: older series advised repeat biopsy; modern multiparametric magnetic resonance
   imaging (mpMRI)-guided re-sampling shows very low risk of unsampled significant cancer once digital
   rectal examination (DRE) and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) are considered.
 • Repeat biopsy is not indicated solely for isolated HGPIN, unifocal or multifocal.
 • HGPIN is less worrisome now because cribriform HGPIN is no longer diagnosed — it is reclassified as
   intraductal carcinoma or atypical intraductal proliferation (AIP).
 • HGPIN on transurethral resection (TUR): significance unclear (few, conflicting studies); on current data it
   should not prompt further sampling either.
 • PINATYP: HGPIN with adjacent small atypical glands — outpouchings or tangential sections cannot be
   separated from infiltrating carcinoma (management: see table).

Intraductal Carcinoma and Atypical Intraductal Proliferation
 • Intraductal carcinoma of the prostate (IDC-P): architectural or cytological atypia clearly exceeding HGPIN;
   a more important lesion than HGPIN.
 • Prevalence rises with risk: from about 2% in low-risk to over half in metastatic or recurrent cohorts; about
   60% after androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) or chemotherapy.
 • IDC-P without invasive carcinoma on biopsy is very rare (<0.06% of specimens).
 • Nature:
      ◦ Usually = retrograde extension of high-grade invasive carcinoma into ducts and acini, clonally related
        to adjacent invasive tumour; almost always accompanied by invasive, typically high-grade, carcinoma.
      ◦ Rarely (radical prostatectomy without adjacent high-grade carcinoma) = a high-grade precursor lesion.
 • Prognosis: on biopsy, independently predicts early biochemical recurrence and metastatic failure after
   radiotherapy (intermediate/high risk); enriched in radio-recurrent cancer (with cribriform); adverse even
   when metastatic.
 • IDC-P alone on biopsy: some clinicians re-biopsy first to document invasion. With grade group 1, radical
   prostatectomy upgrades in >80%.

 • AIP: the significant proportion of intraductal lesions short of these criteria; with a benign or grade group

Location
 • Peripheral zone, posterior: the main tumour mass in clinical T2 and in 85% of T1c tumours.

 • Anterior tumours (transition zone or anterior horn of peripheral zone) in about 15% of radical
   prostatectomies: same biochemical recurrence risk as posterior tumours, but with EPE more likely to
   have positive margins.
 • Multifocal in >85%.
 • Bilateral cancer: contralateral tumour usually small, yet about 20% show contralateral adverse size, EPE,
   grade or margins.

Spread of Tumour
 • No discrete histological capsule → "extraprostatic extension", not "capsular penetration".
 • Peripheral tumours exit via perineural space invasion; EPE is posterior and posterolateral, mirroring
   tumour location.
 • Seminal vesicle invasion = tumour in the muscular wall of the seminal vesicle. Routes, commonest first:
      ◦ Commonest: exit at the base → periseminal vesicle soft tissue → seminal vesicle.
      ◦ Less common: along ejaculatory ducts; direct extension from the base into the vesicle wall.
      ◦ Almost never: discontinuous metastasis to the seminal vesicle.
 • Rectal involvement is rare and may mimic a rectal primary.
 • Metastases: commonest sites lymph nodes and bone; may present in left supraclavicular nodes.
 • Lung: very common at autopsy, almost always with bone, usually asymptomatic; small nodules or
   lymphatic spread, not large deposits. Then liver and adrenal.

Tumour Volume
 • Size correlates with stage: EPE uncommon <0.5 cm³; nodes or seminal vesicle invasion uncommon <4
   cm³. Volume tracks grade; location and grade modulate its effect.
 • Transition zone tumours exit at larger volumes than peripheral zone tumours — lower grade and farther
   from the edge of the gland.

Grade
 • Gleason system (late 1960s–early 1970s): glandular architecture at low magnification, patterns 1 (most
   differentiated) to 5 (undifferentiated); substantially modified since (2005 and later).
 • Scoring rule: original system = commonest + second commonest; 2005 update, on biopsy = commonest +
   highest pattern in a core. Single pattern → doubled (e.g. 3+3).
 • Lowest score in practice is 3+3=6, not 2 — so patients wrongly read "6 of 10" as mid-range
   aggressiveness.
 • Expanded pattern 4 in the modified system reclassified many former score 6 tumours to 7, so modern
   score 6 is more homogeneous and more favourable:
      ◦ Virtually no progression of organ-confined score 6 after radical prostatectomy.
      ◦ Pure score 6 has no capacity for pelvic nodal metastasis.
      ◦ EPE uncommon with 3+3=6 at radical prostatectomy.
 • Grade group system: five tiers, proposed at Johns Hopkins, validated multi-institutionally; reflects biology
   better than Gleason score. Validated for biochemical recurrence after radiotherapy, distant metastasis
   and cancer-specific mortality; endorsed by WHO 2016 (World Health Organization), CAP (College of
   American Pathologists) and AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer).

Differential Diagnosis
 • Under-diagnosis of limited cancer is among the commonest problems in prostate pathology; benign
   mimickers abound.
 • Basal cell markers — high-molecular-weight cytokeratin (HMWCK) and p63 — label benign glands;
   absence supports cancer. Pitfall: partial atrophy, adenosis and HGPIN may also lack basal cells.
 • α-methylacyl-CoA racemase (AMACR) preferentially labels carcinoma and HGPIN — an adjunct only, with
   common false positives and negatives.
 • "Atypical, suspicious for carcinoma": about 5% of biopsies.
      ◦ Cancer on follow-up about 40%; PSA does not stratify the risk.
      ◦ About 80% of subsequent cancers are grade group 1; about 5% grade groups 4–5.
      ◦ Follow-up warranted: serum or urine tests, imaging, or re-biopsy with extra sampling of the atypical
        site; only about 40% are re-biopsied.
      ◦ Atypical is the diagnosis most often changed on expert review → seek expert consultation before re-
        biopsy.

Prognosis
 • Adverse biopsy findings predict adverse prostatectomy findings; favourable ones do not (sampling
   error).
 • Extent measures: number of positive cores, total mm of cancer, % per core, total % of specimen — none
   convincingly superior. Report number of positive cores plus one other measure.
 • Biopsy grade + clinical stage + PSA together predict extent of disease more accurately.
 • Perineural invasion on biopsy (report it): higher EPE; adverse on active surveillance; prognostic with
   external-beam radiotherapy, less so brachytherapy; predicts bone metastasis after radiotherapy plus ADT
   in locally advanced disease.
 • Atrophy (all types): linked to carcinogenesis with inflammation, but common on biopsy and does not raise
   cancer risk on re-biopsy.

 • Gleason score is probably the most powerful biopsy predictor: pathological stage; side-specific EPE and
   EPE into the neurovascular bundle; progression after radical prostatectomy; brachytherapy candidacy;
   radiotherapy prognosis; active surveillance entry and intervention; outcome after cryotherapy or high-
   intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU); focal therapy candidacy.
 • Percent pattern 4 within score 7 correlates with adverse pathology and biochemical recurrence → report
   % pattern 4 for grade groups 2–3:
      ◦ Grade group 2 with limited pattern 4 → may suit active surveillance (with age, comorbidity, imaging,
        extent, preference).
      ◦ Grade group 3 at 60% pattern 4 (near grade group 2) → neoadjuvant hormone therapy with
        radiotherapy possibly avoidable; at 90% (near grade group 4) → recommended.
Concept — Every biopsy rule follows from sampling error being one-directional: a bad biopsy is believed, a
good biopsy is provisional, and an atypical biopsy is re-examined before it is repeated.

Assessment of Transurethral Resection Specimens
Processing
 • Staged by percentage of tissue involved (5% cut-off; see staging table).
 • 6–8 cassettes detect all T1b; 8–10 cassettes detect >90% of T1a.
 • Some institutions embed all TUR tissue in men <65 years, in whom aggressive therapy for T1a might be
   pursued.

Differential Diagnosis
 • Adenosis — the chief mimic of low-grade adenocarcinoma: about 1.6% of benign TURs, 0.8% of needle
   biopsies; transition zone, often multifocal, incidental in TURs for obstruction.
 • Adenosis confers no increased risk of harbouring or developing cancer.

Assessment of Radical Prostatectomy Specimens
Processing
 • Partial sampling protocols give accurate staging where specimens are not totally embedded.
 • Whole-mount sections look better for teaching and publication, but give identical information to routine
   sections.

Prognosis
Gleason score.
 • Grade each major nodule separately; the largest (dominant nodule) usually has the highest stage and
   grade. If a smaller nodule is higher grade or stage, label nodules #1, #2 and grade each.
 • Radical prostatectomy grade correlates well with prognosis (5-year biochemical recurrence-free survival
   by grade group: see Box 156.1 table).
 • Minor tertiary pattern 5 (<5% of a 3+4 or 4+3 nodule): raises biochemical recurrence risk (magnitude
   disputed — intermediate between grade groups 2 and 3, or equal to 3), but stays in its grade group
   because it does not raise nodal risk.
 • Percent pattern 4 within score 7 also adds prognostic information at prostatectomy.
Lymph node metastases.
 • Incidence has fallen with screening; predicted by clinical stage, PSA and biopsy grade, so some surgeons
   omit lymphadenectomy at low risk.
 • Intraductal or cribriform carcinoma at prostatectomy → higher risk of nodal and distant metastasis.

 • Uncommon with Gleason score <8 (rates in Box 156.1 table).
 • Node-positive disease almost always progresses (occult distant metastasis). At 15 years: biochemical
   recurrence-free about 7%, metastasis-free about 42%, cancer-specific survival about 58%.
 • Predictors after node-positive prostatectomy: prostatectomy Gleason score and number and percentage
   of positive nodes.
Extraprostatic extension and seminal vesicle invasion.
 • Capsule poorly defined, especially apex, base and anteriorly → "EPE", not capsular invasion or
   penetration.
 • Hard to recognise: vague boundaries, desmoplasia in periprostatic fat.
 • Focal EPE (a few glands) vs nonfocal EPE (more extensive): degree correlates with progression; objective
   measures agree with it, none preferred.
 • Seminal vesicle invasion is far more ominous: 65% 5-year progression after surgery.
Margins.
 • Positive margins average about 15% in robotic series; only about 50% progress — re-excised tissue often
   shows no tumour.
 • Neurovascular-bundle frozen section (resect the bundle if positive): described, not routine — time,
   staffing, false results, and other parameters suffice.
 • Distance from tumour to ink does not affect recurrence risk.
 • Report margin extent and grade at the margin — these guide adjuvant radiotherapy. Focal vs nonfocal
   positive margin cut-off: 3 mm.
 • Intraprostatic incision (not "capsular incision" — there is no capsule): surgical transection of tumour;
   progression risk equals focal EPE with a positive margin.
 • Independent predictors of biochemical progression: Gleason grade, EPE (focal vs nonfocal), margins
   (extent and grade), seminal vesicle invasion, nodal metastasis.
Tumour volume.
 • Not independently prognostic after grade, stage and margins; reported only to match other organs →
   rough estimate (% of gland or maximum dimension) suffices.
Perineural and vascular invasion.

 • Vascular invasion at prostatectomy is uncommon (7% of tumours <4 cm³; most modern tumours are <2

Concept — At prostatectomy the report is built from features that independently predict progression —
grade, EPE extent, margin extent and grade, seminal vesicles and nodes; features that merely travel with
these (volume, perineural invasion) are demoted or dropped.

SUBTYPES OF PROSTATE ADENOCARCINOMA
Mucinous adenocarcinoma.
 • Not more aggressive than acinar cancer after radical prostatectomy.
Neuroendocrine differentiation and small cell carcinoma.
 • Immunohistochemical neuroendocrine differentiation in acinar cancer risks misdiagnosis as small cell
   carcinoma.
 • Small cell carcinoma: often identical to lung small cell; overlaps high-grade acinar carcinoma through
   transdifferentiation.
Ductal adenocarcinoma.
 • Large periurethral ducts → exophytic urethral lesion near the verumontanum → obstruction or
   haematuria, mimicking papillary urothelial carcinoma. Peripheral ducts → presents like acinar cancer on
   needle biopsy.
 • Clinically underestimated: DRE and PSA may be normal.
 • Pattern 4 because cribriform, with prognosis like acinar Gleason 8; whether graded ductal equals
   comparably graded acinar cancer is disputed.
 • After definitive therapy, systemic agents effective in high-risk acinar disease have limited use.
Squamous and sarcomatoid carcinoma.
 • Squamous differentiation is commoner in hormone-treated adenocarcinoma than as a pure primary.
   Sarcomatoid carcinoma = carcinosarcoma.

Urothelial Carcinoma
 • Prostatic duct and acinar involvement in patients with bladder carcinoma in situ (CIS) treated for months
   to years with intravesical therapy; 35–45% of cystoprostatectomies for urothelial carcinoma show
   prostatic involvement.
 • Intraductal urothelial carcinoma only → does not worsen prognosis; bladder stage decides.

Mesenchymal Tumours
 • Sarcomas = 0.1–0.2% of prostatic malignancy.
 • Rhabdomyosarcoma: commonest in childhood (rare in adults). Leiomyosarcoma: commonest adult
   sarcoma.
 • Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour: any age, may follow TUR (or not); mimics leiomyosarcoma.
 • Specialised prostatic stromal tumours: range from stromal tumour of uncertain malignant potential
   (STUMP) to stromal sarcoma; one variant resembles breast phyllodes tumour.
      ◦ STUMP: usually indolent, but may recur rapidly, coexist with or progress to stromal sarcoma → close
        follow-up; consider definitive resection in younger patients.
      ◦ Stromal sarcoma: can metastasise.

Miscellaneous Malignant Tumours
 • Secondary lymphomatous infiltration is far commoner than primary prostatic lymphoma.
 • Commonest leukaemic involvement: chronic lymphocytic leukaemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma;
   monocytic, granulocytic and lymphoblastic forms also occur.

 KEY POINTS
   • LGPIN not reported (irreproducible, clinically insignificant); isolated HGPIN, even multifocal, does not
     justify repeat biopsy once DRE and PSA are considered.
   • IDC-P: usually spread of high-grade invasive carcinoma; only a minority are high-grade precursors.
   • Atypical biopsy → about 40% cancer regardless of PSA; 80% grade group 1 favours markers, imaging
     and selective targeted re-biopsy, while the 20% of higher grade lead some to re-biopsy routinely.
   • Atypical cases change most often on expert review → consult before re-biopsy.
   • Prostatectomy tumour volume is not independently prognostic after grade, stage and margins.
   • Gleason grade, on any specimen, remains among the most influential prognostic factors; grade groups
     are simpler, more intuitive and more prognostic.
