Chapter 20 — Complications of Urologic Surgery                                                                  Introduction

Chapter 20 — Complications of Urologic Surgery
 • Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) lowered morbidity but introduced new complications. Index
   oncological and reconstructive operations are now largely laparoscopic or robotic; trainee exposure to
   open surgery has fallen, but the principles of managing major complications are unchanged.
 • Learning curves have shortened (open radical prostatectomy 100–1000 cases; robotic about 40), yet
   complication rates have not fallen in step, partly because many young surgeons are still on the curve.
 • Laparoscopic risk factors: complex procedures (partial nephrectomy, cystectomy), operating time >4 h,
   serum creatinine >1.5 mg/dL.
 • Complexity versus experience: complications rise up to ninefold with complexity and fall fourfold with
   experience. Yet absolute numbers can rise with experience, because harder cases are taken on;
   complication and conversion rates climb from easy to very difficult operations.
 • Laparoscopy carries every open complication plus its own (pneumoperitoneum, access), so a high index
   of suspicion is needed during and after surgery.
 • Technology also treats complications (interventional radiology, MIS repair). Recent graduates may meet
   complications never seen in training, with no specific guidelines; simulators and animal models may
   bridge the gap.
 • Scope: positioning, access, vascular, urinary, bowel and visceral complications; procedure-specific
   endourological complications are covered in their own chapters.
Concept — complication burden reflects complexity multiplied by inexperience: experience lowers risk per
case but moves surgeons to harder cases, so absolute numbers need not fall.

CLASSIFICATION OF COMPLICATIONS

The Clavien-Dindo Scale
 • The recommended uniform reporting system. It grades by invasiveness of the treatment needed, not by
   severity of the event.
 • Not designed for urology but validated as simple, reproducible and applicable; urological use roughly
   doubled between 2010 and 2012.
 • Limitations: management-based rather than morbidity-based; no early/late distinction; omits
   intraoperative events; may be unsuitable in paediatric urology.
 • High interobserver agreement, even in ambiguous cases; about 80% trainee–attending concordance. The
   most widely used tool.
 • Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL): good concordance for higher grades, poor at grade 3 or below,
   because perceived risk and required management diverge. Since applied to radical nephrectomy and
   robotic prostatectomy.
 • Standardisation is the cornerstone of error reduction; future procedural guidelines may standardise steps
   and define surgical errors.

Treatment modality is the cornerstone. Seven grades; subdivisions allow rare, severe complications to be pooled.

PHYSIOLOGIC COMPLICATIONS
 • Pneumoperitoneum: bradycardia from peritoneal gas distension; hypercarbia and acidosis from carbon
   dioxide (CO2); reduced respiratory reserve with raised airway pressure (detailed in the laparoscopy
      chapters).

POSITIONAL AND NEUROMUSCULAR COMPLICATIONS
 • Mechanism: direct surgical trauma or positioning, mostly a nerve compressed against the patient's body
   or the table.
 • Positions: supine, lateral decubitus (flank), lithotomy, and robotic steep Trendelenburg, which
   compromises all four limbs.
 • Obesity raises risk twice over: weight on pressure points and longer operations.
 • Incidence about 1% (large laparoscopic series), <3% in urology generally, up to about 6% in robotic series.
   Permanent deficit is very rare; most recover spontaneously.

Positional Nerve Injury
 • Prolonged compression, with or without padding; presents immediately after surgery with sensory loss,
   paraesthesia or weakness in the nerve's territory.

Risk Factors and Diagnosis
 • Delayed diagnosis is disastrous; the whole team should know the risk factors, for prevention and early
   recognition.
Concept — positional injury is a product of pressure and time: every preventive measure lowers one
(padding, limb angles) or the other (lowering the legs, shorter operations), and obesity worsens both.

URINARY COMPLICATIONS
 • Urine leaks are common after upper and lower tract, extirpative and reconstructive surgery, adding
   morbidity and stay; management is similar open or MIS. Intraoperative detection and repair reduce
   morbidity and avoid secondary procedures, but many leaks appear days to weeks later.

URINE LEAKS AFTER UPPER TRACT UROLOGIC SURGERY
 • Follow partial nephrectomy and pyeloplasty; the ureter is also injured in gynaecological and general
   surgical retroperitoneal operations. Same principles throughout.

Urine Leaks After Partial Nephrectomy
 • Second only to haemorrhage as a complication of partial nephrectomy.

 • Incidence falling: historically about 1–17% open and laparoscopic, below 1% in expert robotic series.
   Early low laparoscopic rates reflected selection (large, endophytic, hilar tumours went open). No review
   shows a significant difference between techniques; robotic surgery may improve ischaemia time, margins
   and renal recovery over laparoscopy.
 • Risk factors: tumour size, endophytic growth, warm ischaemia time, operating time, hilar location.
 • Presentation: after transperitoneal MIS, ileus, fever, abdominal pain; after open surgery, contained
   retroperitoneally (ileus rare). Mean about day 13 (3–32); most are readmitted.
 • Drains: benefit uncertain; many omit one after MIS because urinomas are rare.
 • Diagnosis: computed tomography (CT) urography with delayed images is the mainstay (extent and site).
   Drain creatinine >48 h after surgery confirms a fistula but not its site or size.
 • Prevention: find collecting-system entry with retrograde dilute methylene blue via a pre-placed ureteric
   catheter; close watertight with absorbable suture before renorrhaphy.
Management
 • Conservative management is the mainstay after any approach: most leaks heal with drainage and no
   infection, sometimes needing a drain long term.
 • Trap: high early drain output after MIS may be pooled blood and irrigant redistributing once the patient is
   supine or mobile, not urine; check drain creatinine.
 • Prolonged leak (>30 days) is linked to variant pelvic anatomy and age; a minority need secondary
   intervention; healing can take 80 days or more.
Escalation: drain creatinine → keep or reinsert the Foley (bladder pressure reaches the upper tract) →
ureteric stent with retrograde pyelogram → drain off suction and withdrawn in stages, since a drain against
the leak acts as a siphon → interventional radiology (IR) drainage of undrained or growing collections →
second stent, nephrostomy, or ureteroscopic calyceal fulguration with fibrin sealant (desmopressin rarely) →
refractory infected leak: nephrectomy, last resort.
 • Skin: urostomy appliance over a leaking drain site; the patient logs output to track resolution.
Concept — an upper-tract leak heals once the path of least resistance runs down the ureter: each step
lowers downstream pressure or removes a competing low-pressure exit.

URETERAL INJURIES

Incidence and Anatomic Landmarks
 • Possibly the commonest surgical complication urologists manage, often as an intraoperative consult.
 • Hysterectomy: under 1% despite sealing devices, and most unrecognised at operation; laparoscopic
   hysterectomy injuries lie near the uterine artery and uterosacral ligament. Laparoscopic colorectal
   surgery about 1%.
 • Delayed recognition → urinoma, ileus, sepsis, shock; nephrostomy need about tenfold that of early
   recognition.
 • Risk factors: pelvic radiotherapy, malignancy, previous surgery, adhesions, extensive retroperitoneal
   lymph node dissection (RPLND).
 • Prophylactic ureteric catheters: no robust evidence; may cause oedema, obstruction and acute kidney
   injury (AKI).
 • Gynaecological anatomy: ureter runs with the ovarian vessels, beneath the uterine artery, beside the
   uterosacral ligament; vulnerable at the pelvic inlet and infundibulopelvic ligament.

Mechanisms of Ureteral Injury
 • Spectrum: pinpoint thermal (usually silent) → ligation → partial or complete transection. Transections are
   most often identified and repaired at once; ligation and thermal injury present late; devascularisation →
   delayed stricture or necrosis.
 • Rare in urological laparoscopy. Identify the ureter early, before ligating any critical pedicle.
 • Danger sites and landmarks: gonadal vessels and lower renal pole; pelvic brim, where it crosses the
   common iliac artery (best place to find and trace it); under the uterine vessels; posterior seminal vesicle
   dissection in robotic prostatectomy. Most useful in retroperitoneal fibrosis and complex partial
   nephrectomy.

Strictures
 • Endoscopy first (laser, balloon) for short strictures; surgery if it fails or the stricture is >2 cm.
   Retroperitoneal fibrosis makes repair hard.
 • By level: short upper → ureteroureterostomy; lower → reimplantation with psoas hitch; mid → Boari
   flap; long upper → ileal ureter; long gaps → robotic buccal mucosa graft; right side → appendix.
 • Indocyanine green (ICG) with robotic Firefly imaging checks perfusion of trimmed ends. Open repair
   when adhesions or multiple prior operations preclude MIS.
 • Prevention: preoperative stents; lighted stents to offset absent haptic feedback.
Concept — level and length choose the repair; inflammation chooses the timing. The lower the defect, the
more bladder can be brought up (reimplant → psoas hitch → Boari flap); higher or longer gaps need
ureteroureterostomy or interposition. Repair within 7 days or after 6 weeks, with drainage bridging the
interval.

LOWER TRACT URINARY COMPLICATIONS

Bladder Injuries
 • Rare in urological MIS; commoner in gynaecological (raising the bladder flap at hysterectomy) and
   colorectal surgery.
 • At operation: two-layer absorbable closure; catheter 7–10 days to heal and prevent VVF.
 • Postoperatively it mimics ureteric injury (distension, ileus, fever, rigors, leucocytosis, rising drain output);
   diagnose by CT cystography.
 • Intraperitoneal → two-layer repair (robotic or open); small contained extraperitoneal → catheter alone;
   extensive post-hysterectomy injury → definitive repair (fistula risk).
 • Commonest urological bladder injury: posterior bladder-neck “buttonhole” in robotic radical
   prostatectomy (RARP) at the vesicoprostatic junction. Keep the plane without stripping the bladder neck;
   identify the ureteric orifices when difficult (prior transurethral resection, microwave therapy, large
   median lobe); 5-Fr feeding tubes or preoperative stents help the less experienced.
 • Bladder cuff leak (distal ureterectomy, nephroureterectomy) resolves with 7–10 days of drainage.

Urine Leaks After Radical Prostatectomy
Overview
 • Urethrovesical anastomotic leak is among the commonest early complications (days 7–10), up to about
   15%.
 • Definition: rising drain output with proportionally falling catheter output, confirmed by extravasation
   on cystography, usually posterior (weakest point).
 • Removal on day 2 after posterior reconstruction did not increase leaks, but most keep the catheter 7–10
   days.
 • Graded by Clavien-Dindo, in practice by the intervention needed.

 • Significant leaks collect extraperitoneally (open) or intraperitoneally (robotic) → ileus, longer stay,
   intravenous fluids; fever or sepsis → antibiotics or percutaneous drainage. Reintervention about 0.5%
   open to 2.5% in early MIS series, falling with a watertight running anastomosis.
 • Sequelae: bladder neck contracture; incontinence if scar involves the external sphincter.
Contributing Factors
 • Postoperative bleeding: haematocrit fall >6 points marks risk. Also obesity, operating time, bladder neck
   size, absent bladder neck reconstruction.
 • Barbed versus monofilament suture: no difference.
 • Salvage prostatectomy: poor anastomosis in treated tissue; biograft scaffolding reduced leaks. Anterior
   or total reconstruction leaks less than conventional apposition.
Diagnosis
 • Large leaks are obvious; raised drain creatinine confirms. Retrograde cystography is valuable; CT
   cystography if fever and leucocytosis suggest a collection.
 • Ensure the catheter is in and draining. Never apply traction: it disrupts the anastomosis and risks
   contracture.
 • Healing is time-dependent: most leaks found on day 8 close by 2–3 weeks. Conservative measures buy
   time if there is no intraperitoneal leak, ileus or sepsis.
Management
 • Most leaks are small and settle with prolonged catheter drainage; adequate bladder drainage is the key.
Escalation: confirm catheter patency; change to a side-fenestrated catheter (radiological guidance if
needed) → drain against the anastomosis on CT: reposition (siphon), off bulb suction → IR drainage of large
collections, especially once the drain is out → Foley on low intermittent wall suction (vented 18-G
angiocatheter) or underwater seal (Pleur-evac); continuous wall suction is counterproductive (mucosa
blocks holes) → nephroureteral stents on suction or nephrostomy (divert but do not keep the anastomosis
patent; rarely needed) → cystoscopic open-ended ureteric catheters exteriorised through the urethral
catheter (only for partial disruption with easily seen orifices; risks total disruption) → formal reconstruction
(robotic after RARP).
 • Early formal repair of major disruption reduces ileus, infection, stay, incontinence and contracture. After
   day 7 repair is rarely attempted (friable tissue): CT cystogram, percutaneous drain, parenteral antibiotics
   if febrile, maximal drainage; recovery is slow but follows.

VASCULAR COMPLICATIONS

Overview
 • The most dreaded complications: among the commonest major MIS complications and the commonest
   intraoperative complication. Audit showed serious complications falling; recent reports do not, reflecting
   new surgeons and more complex MIS.
 • Occur at access, dissection or vessel isolation. Most major injuries are intraoperative; most
   postoperative bleeding is from unrecognised small vessels.
 • MIS handicaps: no manual compression; instruments limited by ports; sponges hard to introduce.
 • Early recognition, calm response. Major vessel injury → immediate open conversion with manual
   compression and emergency vascular consultation.

Intraoperative Vascular Complications
 • 2–3% of urological MIS (non-urological: about 0.5% minor to 20% major, e.g. hepatic resection).
 • Upper tract most prone (renal vessels off the aorta and IVC; many vessels in partial nephrectomy); lower
   tract mainly with extended iliac node dissection.
 • Haemorrhage is the commonest intraoperative complication (about 2%; about 40% of perioperative
   complications), especially near the great vessels (RPLND).
 • Vascular injury is the commonest reason for open conversion; emergency conversion is more frequent in
   laparoscopic live-donor nephrectomy. Conversion about 1.5%, death about 0.2%.
 • Risk factors: previous surgery, concomitant abdominal disease, obesity, aberrant anatomy, inexperience
   → careful early case selection.
Management
 • Cognitive reaction first: control your stress response, alert the team to a possible massive haemorrhage
   protocol; after temporary control, pause and state the contingency plan.
 • Benchmark: react about 22 s, control <1 min, repair about 3 min (about 170 s); longer if only temporarily
   controlled.
Consensus algorithm (Passerotti): raise pneumoperitoneum cautiously (gas embolus risk with venous injury)
→ aspirate, expose → working suction, laparoscopic sponges → add ports promptly, away from other trocars
→ identify every injury → clips judiciously, never randomly (failed clips obstruct suturing and stapling) →
suture repair, the most reliable rescue → robotic: assistant presses with the suction tip; selective vascular
clamps → low threshold for conversion and vascular consult.
Vascular Injury During Abdominal Access
 • About 0.1% (about 0.04% with blind insufflation); Veress needle or trocar.
 • Blood aspirated through the Veress needle → stop insufflating (CO2 embolism).

 • Gas embolism: sudden hypoxia, hypercarbia, hypotension, arrhythmia, head and upper-limb cyanosis,
   “mill-wheel” murmur, falling end-tidal CO2, raised right heart pressure. Gas in the right heart or
   pulmonary artery → reduced venous return and cardiac output → pulmonary hypertension → right heart
   failure. Mortality is high once blood pressure or heart rate falls; transoesophageal echocardiography
   detects 0.02 mL.

Treatment: desufflate → 100% oxygen (FiO2) → Trendelenburg and left lateral decubitus (trap gas in the
right atrium) → central line aspiration.
 • Leave the offending needle or trocar in place; alternative access locates the injury, and the trocar may
   tamponade it.
 • Veress versus trocar: a Veress puncture of the aorta or inferior vena cava (IVC) may self-limit if the
   haematoma is not expanding (still inspect directly); trocar injuries almost always need repair, usually
   with conversion; through-and-through injuries are reliably repaired only open. Multidisciplinary:
   resuscitation, vascular surgery; experienced surgeons may repair by MIS.
 • Pressures: 15–18 mmHg for trocar insertion → 12–15 mmHg thereafter.
 • AirSeal: valveless trocar; stable pneumoperitoneum; smoke evacuation; less wall and diaphragm tension
   (easier ventilation in steep Trendelenburg or flank). The laxer wall means all trocars go in under standard
   insufflation before AirSeal mode.
Mechanisms of Injury
 • Umbilicus lies from 5 cm cephalad to 3 cm caudal of the iliac bifurcation, so it is unreliable; the anterior
   superior iliac spine (ASIS) has been proposed instead.
 • Veress angle: 45° at normal body mass index (BMI), 90° in obesity, as the umbilicus moves caudally with
   rising BMI.
 • Vessel most often injured at the umbilicus: left common iliac vein.
 • Skin to retroperitoneum about 6 cm, as little as 2 cm when thin; Trendelenburg shortens it.
 • Atherosclerotic aorta or abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA): rigid wall, no spontaneous tamponade,
   “mini-fracture”, plaque embolism to the legs → enter at the left upper quadrant; review or obtain
   imaging.
 • Flank position: upper quadrants are classic safe sites (right: liver over upper pole; left: spleen only if
   enlarged). Robotic kidney: Veress at the future camera port (two fingerbreadths above the umbilicus,
   lateral rectus line), perpendicular to the posterior peritoneum (bowel falls medially). Extensive previous
   surgery → Hasson or optical trocar.
Comparison of Different Access Techniques
 • Under-reporting hampers comparison. In U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) data, 81% of trocar
   deaths were from major vessel injury (aorta, IVC, iliacs; iliac rarely fatal). No consensus on the safest
   technique.

Management of Vascular Incidents
 • Suction, irrigation, direct vision; open control above and below; extra suction; vascular help as needed.
 • Transient pneumoperitoneum 20–25 mmHg (embolism caution) defines and temporises even medium–
   large venous defects, for no more than about 10 minutes (CO2 absorption, splanchnic hypoperfusion).

 • Pressure (gauze or rolled Surgicel on the assistant's suction) → accessory trocars → continue by MIS if
   controlled → convert if the injury cannot be defined or controlled; transfuse early, before instability.
Injury to Epigastric Vessels
 • 0.2–2%; often presents late because the trocar tamponades it.
 • Vessels run in the lateral rectus sheath; injured when a trocar angles medially. Prevent: transilluminate;
   lateral ports ≥5.5–6 cm from the midline; inspect sites as trocars come out.
 • Repair: Foley balloon traction temporises (rarely needed); best is a fascial closure device (Carter-
   Thomason) placing a figure-of-eight under vision without enlarging the incision.
Injury to Major Vessels
 • The most feared (taught by simulation). Most cannot be ligated (no collaterals) → repair, usually after
   conversion.
 • Suspected trocar injury: leave it in place (tamponade), add ports if stable, convert through a midline
   incision.
 • Robotic rescue: ProGrasp temporises; other arms and suction assess; rescue stitch of polyglactin on a
   CT-1 needle with a Hem-o-lok at the tail tents the wall between throws; the large needle is findable in
   blood.
 • Ligatable: internal iliac and inferior mesenteric (good collaterals).
 • Superior mesenteric artery (SMA): in the lateral position the left renal vein no longer marks the SMA–
   aorta relation; the SMA is taken for the left renal artery when tumour distorts anatomy or retraction is
   poor. Suspect it if the artery is medial to the aorta, anterior to the renal vein, or atypical → mobilise
   bowel, medialise mesentery, expose the aorta if in doubt. Variant or multiple renal arteries (sometimes
   missed on imaging) occur in over half of patients.
 • SMA stump or proximal trunk injury: mortality up to 75%. Backflow via the marginal artery of
   Drummond and arc of Riolan may help; no distal flow → repair (re-anastomosis, splenic or left renal
   stump transposition, graft). Vascular consult and conversion are mandatory.
 • Retroperitoneal haematoma: stable, non-expanding → observe, do not explore, continue or abandon by
   urgency; unstable or expanding → open repair (aorta, IVC, external iliac artery).
 • Venous tears: insufflate to 20 mmHg, suture quickly, return to 12–15 mmHg promptly. External iliac vein:
   also turn off sequential compression devices to decompress it. The robotic fourth arm gives clamping
   and tension; use sealing devices near major structures.
Bleeding During Tumorectomy in Partial Nephrectomy
 • Robotic partial nephrectomy (RAPN) is the commonest approach; routine artery-and-vein clamping is
   declining. Right-sided tumours bleed more.
 • Artery-only clamping: more blood loss than full hilar clamping, no more transfusions. Add a venous
   clamp if bleeding halts progress; parenchymal venous backflow, by contrast, slows towards the crater.

Stapler Malfunction
 • About 1% accepted. FDA reviews: deaths from misfiring and failed or malformed staple lines; recalls for
   broken parts or sterility; under-reported (most programme directors had seen one; a quarter changed the
   operation).
 • Commonest urological use: renal hilum. Commonest cause: tissue too thick.
Prevention: inspect on unpacking → load precisely → dissect the vessel → see the whole jaw and tip before

 • Precipitous haematocrit fall is the usual clue; haematuria adds alarm. Up to 10% are asymptomatic;
   routine early CT finds some, nearly all silent.

 • <2–4 mm pseudoaneurysms resolved spontaneously. May mimic recurrence (arterial-phase

 • Causes: trocar technique, Veress needle, over-retraction, handling, electrocautery. About a fifth of trocar

Duodenal and Pancreatic Injuries During Nephrectomy
 • Duodenum: right renal surgery (Kocherisation). Shear or puncture; even <5 mm injuries perforate →
   delayed fever, pain, sepsis. Intramural haematoma → prolonged obstructive ileus, bowel rest, TPN.
 • Pancreatic tail: left nephrectomy, near the upper pole and adrenal → fistula, pseudocyst, abscess.
 • Octreotide reduces morbidity after pancreatic resection and in severe pancreatitis, but empirical use
   after injury is questioned (more fistulae, longer drainage and stay).
 • Obvious injury → distal pancreatectomy. Delayed: clear drain fluid with high amylase → CT for
   collections → extra drains; no drain → percutaneous drainage, somatostatin, bowel rest, nasogastric
   suction, nutrition; persistent → distal pancreatectomy.

Rectal Injury
 • Commonest bowel complication of radical prostatectomy; a learning-curve concern.
 • Laparoscopic highest (about 1%); open and robotic similar (under 0.5%); salvage about 7%. Risk factors:
   salvage, stage, Gleason grade, nodal status, experience, not approach.
 • Recto-anastomotic fistula under 0.1%; predictors: laceration extent, prior radiotherapy, rectal infiltration.
 • Also difficult after TURP, microwave thermotherapy, recurrent prostatitis (fibrosis).
 • Commonest RARP site: near the apex in posterior dissection. No cautery on the anterior rectal wall.
   Rectal EEA dilator identifies the rectum in fibrotic cases; modified bowel preparation before salvage
   surgery.
Management
 • No gross spillage → primary closure: debrided, vascularised, tension-free; multilayer non-overlapping;
   mucosa and serosa absorbable, perirectal bolster non-absorbable; usually two layers.

Port Site Hernia
 • 0.3–5.4%; risk: older age, previous hernia, operating time, trocar size.
 • Painless bulge, mostly at extraumbilical ports, within weeks (mean about 10 days); CT confirms.
 • 12-mm ports far exceed 10-mm; use 5-mm where possible. Bladeless trocars leave smaller defects,
   though 12-mm bladeless hernias occur. Inspect robotic 8-mm off-midline ports (enlarged by arm torque).
 • Single-site surgery (SILS, single-incision laparoscopic surgery; LESS, laparoendoscopic single-site surgery):
   better cosmesis, more incisional and umbilical hernias (larger incision); about 5% with da Vinci Xi single-
   site, better with da Vinci SP (smaller incision, semi-flexible instruments); more data needed.
 • Close large fascial defects: single figure-of-eight absorbable suture, usually with a closure device.
   Symptomatic hernia → laparoscopic or robotic mesh repair.

Venous Thromboembolism
 • Venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk: pelvic surgery, malignancy, obesity, long operations (MIS is longer
   on the learning curve). Pneumoperitoneum and reverse Trendelenburg might cause stasis, but there is no
   evidence of more DVT after laparoscopy; true DVT and pulmonary embolism (PE) incidence is unknown.
 • MIS prostatectomy: symptomatic VTE about 0.5%, mostly DVT alone, occasional fatal PE; associated with
   smoking, large prostate, re-exploration, long operation and stay.
 • By procedure (American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program, ACS
   NSQIP): about 1% for nephrectomy, partial nephrectomy, nephroureterectomy and prostatectomy; lower
   for MIS nephrectomy; about 5% for radical cystectomy. Most VTEs occur after discharge.
 • Preventer trial (Brady Institute, randomised): perioperative subcutaneous heparin after prostatectomy
   did not reduce symptomatic VTE.
 • Cystectomy: about half of VTEs after discharge; discharge on low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH;
   enoxaparin) roughly halved VTE; oral anticoagulants appear similarly safe and effective.
 • No consensus guidelines; practice varies (about a third of prostatectomy patients get none) → follow
   local policy.
 • American Urological Association (AUA) best practice statement 2009: pharmacological prophylaxis
   mandatory at high risk (age >60, prior VTE, cancer, hypercoagulable state).
 • Risk factors: age, personal or family VTE, malignancy, obesity, thrombophilia, and transfusion (after
   cystectomy, each unit raises VTE risk by about 7%); restrictive transfusion lowers VTE without raising
   mortality or cardiac events.
 • Caprini score (modified by the American College of Chest Physicians, ACCP): most widely used, validated
   in urology; summed weighted points: very low 0; low 1–2; moderate 3–4; high ≥5.

Lymphoceles
 • The commonest complication of lymphatic disruption after pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND): lymph
   collections without epithelial lining, usually along the iliac vessels. First described about 50 years ago
   (Basinger and Gittes).
 • Transperitoneal RARP was expected to reduce them (peritoneal reabsorption), but loculation and
   retroperitonealisation occur within 48–72 h; in the robotic era, approach makes no difference to
   symptomatic lymphoceles.
 • Rates vary with template (most are silent); extended dissection roughly doubles them.
 • Lymphostasis: clips versus bipolar (no robust data; some favour clips); robotic vessel sealer; fibrin
   sealants; peritoneal flap fixed to the bladder. Inspect and control visible lymphatics after PLND or radical
   upper tract surgery; drain most major nodal dissections.
 • Management: asymptomatic and <5 cm → conservative; large symptomatic (iliac compression → leg
   oedema; bladder compression → frequency, urgency) → percutaneous drainage; thick-walled or
   recurrent after drainage → laparoscopic marsupialisation into a peritoneal window. Aspiration is not
   advised (always reaccumulates; infection). Sclerotherapy (povidone-iodine, doxycycline) may hinder later
   marsupialisation. Lymphangiography with embolisation: high success, faster fall in output than
   sclerotherapy, occasional reintervention; long-term data awaited.
 • Infected lymphocele: fever, rigors, sepsis → intravenous fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, percutaneous
   drainage; always consider a urine leak.
Concept — lymphoceles are treated by symptoms and recurrence, not size alone; the definitive cure is
internal drainage into the peritoneum, which is why aspiration and sclerotherapy, which compromise it, are
avoided.

SECOND VICTIM SYNDROME
 • The surgeon's own distress after a serious complication. In surgery responsibility rests on “the hands of
   the surgeon” rather than diffusing through the system. Almost half of healthcare providers experience it.
 • Risk factors: lapse in judgement or concentration, inexperience, burnout, personal problems.

 • Course: adrenaline surge → failure, sadness, depression, isolation, unworthiness, medicolegal fear;
   cognitive impairment for months (concentration, decisions, dexterity). The detachment that lets surgeons
   work in chaos breaks down.
 • Culture is harsh; training covers managing complications, not supporting peers.
 • Recovery: confidential forums beyond morbidity and mortality meetings, trusted seniors, mindfulness
   and meditation. Only a minority (experienced, resilient) report a positive career effect. “The only surgeon
   without complications is one who does not operate” ignores the influence of the relationship with the
   first victim.
Concept — a complication has two victims; the surgeon's impaired cognition afterwards is itself a safety risk,
so peer support belongs to complication management.

CONCLUSION

 KEY POINTS
   • Physiological, urinary, vascular, bowel and visceral complications occur with every form of urological
     surgery.
   • Laparoscopic and robotic surgery bring a distinct set of complications and demand new operative,
     diagnostic and therapeutic skills.
   • More complex MIS operations mean complication rates can rise.
   • MIS complications can present atypically and late.
   • A high index of suspicion enables prompt diagnosis. Many complications are managed conservatively
     or medically; diagnostic and interventional radiology is central; some need operation.
   • Prevention matters most: patient selection, positioning, planning, meticulous technique.
