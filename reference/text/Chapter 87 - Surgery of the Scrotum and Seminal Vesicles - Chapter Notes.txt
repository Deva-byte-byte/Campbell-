SURGICAL ANATOMY OF THE SCROTUM
Scrotal Wall
 • Anatomy governs technique: safe entry, recognition of planes at debridement, multilayer closure.
 • Skin is rugated because it is attached to the underlying dartos muscle; it is pigmented and hair-bearing.
 • Thin and highly elastic, so the scrotum changes size with temperature to support spermatogenesis.
 • Loose tissue is forgiving to handle, but the same distensibility gives poor tamponade: bleeding is not
   contained, haematomas enlarge and may need re-intervention. Meticulous haemostasis is therefore
   mandatory.

Scrotal Contents
 • Held in place by the spermatic cord and the gubernacular attachments.
 • Coverings, testis outwards: tunica albuginea → tunica vaginalis → internal spermatic fascia → cremaster
   muscle → external spermatic fascia → dartos → skin.
 • The dartos fascia is a barrier to spread of necrotising fasciitis to the deeper scrotal structures.
 • Epididymis: arises from the efferent ducts of the rete testis in the mediastinum testis and lies
   posterolaterally — caput superior, corpus along the long axis, cauda at the lower pole.
 • Vas deferens: continuous with the cauda → spermatic cord → pelvis → ampulla → joins the seminal
   vesicle (SV) → proximal ejaculatory duct.
 • Two vestigial remnants: the appendix testis on the upper pole of the testis and the appendix epididymis
   on the head of the epididymis.

 • Venous drainage follows the arteries: external pudendal and posterior scrotal veins → great saphenous
   and internal iliac veins.
 • What the table tests: the testis has three sources from three different parent trunks (aorta, internal iliac,
   external iliac).
 • Anastomoses: cranially, fine testicular–deferential anastomoses in the cord about 10 cm above the testis;
   caudally, testicular–deferential–cremasteric anastomoses at the cauda epididymis.
 • Consequence: the testis survives injury or ligation of one or two arteries — hence the testicular artery can
   be ligated for extra length in the Fowler–Stephens orchidopexy for undescended testis, with low risk.
 • Superior epididymal artery carries most epididymal flow, yet can be sacrificed to mobilise the testis in
   vasectomy reversal because it anastomoses with the inferior epididymal artery.
 • Transdermal absorption: thin, vascular scrotal skin absorbs up to about 40 times more than other skin —
   exploited by scrotal testosterone patches, since withdrawn because frequent scrotal hair clipping was
   impractical.
 • Scrotal skin is rich in 5α-reductase, so scrotal testosterone produced high dihydrotestosterone (DHT)
   levels.

Concept — The scrotal wall is supplied anteriorly from the groin (external pudendal vessels, ilioinguinal and

ligation for mobilisation safe and cord denervation possible without devascularisation.

PREOPERATIVE CONSIDERATIONS
 • Hair removal improves exposure, reduces surgical site infection, and eases suturing and dressing.
 • Razor rather than clippers for the scrotum: a comparative study found less skin trauma and a better
   shave with razors, although hospitals generally favour clippers → disposable razor on the day of surgery.
 • Antisepsis: iodine solutions or chlorhexidine gluconate. A Cochrane review favoured chlorhexidine scrub
   over alcohol-based povidone-iodine, but on small, poor-quality trials → choose on cost and allergy
   profile.
 • Antibiotic prophylaxis — American Urological Association (AUA) Best Practice Statement: for genital
   surgery, a single dose of intravenous cephalosporin or clindamycin only in patients with risk factors —
   advanced age, anatomical urinary tract abnormality, poor nutrition, smoking, chronic corticosteroid use,
   immunodeficiency, externalised catheters, colonised endogenous or exogenous material, distant
   coexistent infection, prolonged hospitalisation.
 • Anaesthesia: local, regional, spinal or general; subcutaneous infiltration plus spermatic cord block allows
   many scrotal procedures in the office.
 • Preincisional 0.5% lidocaine without adrenaline is recommended in theatre or office to control
   postprocedural pain.

 • Scrotal hypothermia may be used once the cord is clamped; however, animal models show worse
   testicular function and histology after cold than after warm ischaemia.
 • Incision: transverse or median raphe. The median raphe is optimal because the superficial dartos forms
   an avascular median plane between the hemiscrota.
 • Common to all techniques: secure haemostasis as the tunica vaginalis is bluntly freed from the scrotal
   wall, and avoid injuring testis, epididymis and cord when incising the tunica.
 • Obstructive azoospermia can follow epididymal or vasal injury during hydrocelectomy — take care when
   opening and excising the sac.
 • Closure: always close the dartos (running or interrupted) — most haematomas arise from inadequate
   dartos closure.
Concept — Approach follows pathology: the inguinal route controls the cord before a possible tumour is
touched, and the scrotal route is used only once malignancy is excluded; because the scrotum cannot
tamponade, closure of the dartos is the haemostatic step that prevents the commonest complication.

SURGERIES OF THE SCROTUM
Scrotal Wall
Cyst/Tumour Excision
 • Sebaceous and epidermal inclusion cysts are managed conservatively or excised if not infected; standard
   ellipsoid excision gives excellent outcomes and cosmesis.
 • Infected cyst: antibiotics or incision and drainage (I&D) with systemic therapy, depending on size and
   extent — possible in clinic or emergency department with minimal analgesia.
 • Excise once infection resolves, because recurrence is relatively high.
Partial/Total Scrotectomy
 • Partial scrotectomy is uncommon; the main indication is Fournier gangrene (FG).
 • FG mainstay: intravenous fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics and urgent surgical debridement —
   gangrene can spread 2–3 cm per hour, and delay significantly increases mortality.
 • Mortality about 20% despite aggressive treatment, unchanged over 25 years.
 • Repeat debridement every 24–48 hours until all visibly necrotic tissue is removed (Fig. 87.3A); on average
   up to four procedures.
 • Multidisciplinary care: trauma, general and colorectal surgery, intensive care, infectious disease.
 • Anorectal extension may require faecal diversion by colostomy — an essential planning consideration.
 • Scrotal violation in non-seminomatous germ cell tumour (NSGCT): after trans-scrotal aspiration, biopsy,
   exploration or orchidectomy, partial scrotectomy is required to prevent contamination and local
   recurrence; a systematic review found residual primary tumour in a minority of scar excisions.
 • Other indications for extensive excision: hidradenitis suppurativa, post-radiation lymphoedema,
   penoscrotal Paget disease, primary lymphangitis; also cosmetic reduction of scrotal descent.
 • Total scrotectomy is rarer: extensive FG, malignancy, extensive trauma.
 • If more than 50–60% of scrotal skin is removed, no immediate reconstruction: the testes go into medial
   thigh pockets with loose closure until reconstruction, or are wrapped in saline gauze or included in the
   vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) dressing.
Scrotal Reconstruction
 • Depends on the extent of the defect and availability of suitable local tissue.
 • Minor defects: wet-to-dry dressings and healing by secondary intention.
 • Significant defects (loss of more than half the scrotal skin): negative-pressure wound therapy (VAC) —
   removes bacteria and oedema, improves angiogenesis, promotes granulation → faster recovery, smaller
   wound, better graft take.

Aspiration and Sclerotherapy
 • History: William of Saliceto instilled sugar and ginger in the 13th century; sclerotherapy re-emerged in
   1975 as a cost-effective office alternative to hydrocelectomy and spermatocelectomy.
 • Aspiration and sclerotherapy (AS) remains a viable option for patients who are not surgical candidates or
   wish to avoid surgery.
 • Aspiration alone recurs in most cases → a sclerosing agent should always be added.
 • Agents: doxycycline, tetracycline, alcohol, polidocanol, ethanolamine oleate, sodium tetradecyl sulphate
   (STDS). Single-procedure success varies widely; the text names sterile alcohol, tetracycline and
   polidocanol as best.

Aspiration and sclerotherapy technique.
 • Sequence: prepare hemiscrotum with chlorhexidine → local anaesthetic at the aspiration site plus cord
   block (1% lidocaine with 0.25% bupivacaine) → 16–19-gauge angiocatheter into the lateral or
   anterosuperior sac (plastic cannula preferred — less likely to slip out) → pre-connected via tubing to a 60-
   mL Luer-Lok syringe (closed system) → drain completely and record volume → microscopy for sperm;
   culture or cytology if infection or malignancy is suspected → inject sclerosant into the collapsed sac and
   massage.
 • Recurrence in the following weeks → repeat AS or proceed to hydrocelectomy.
 • Limitations: pain, multiple treatments needed, poor efficacy in multiloculated and large (>750 mL) sacs.
 • AS versus hydrocelectomy: AS has lower success and satisfaction but fewer complications and lower
   cost; repeat treatment brings success close to that of surgery. It is a safe, effective alternative for men
   wishing to avoid surgery.
Simple Orchiectomy
 • Bilateral simple orchidectomy remains an option in hormone-sensitive metastatic prostate cancer for
   patients wishing to avoid medical androgen deprivation therapy (ADT).
 • 1990s trend: orchidectomy declined as gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist use rose.
 • Resurgence is driven by the rising cost of medical castration, falling reimbursement and a reported
   survival advantage.
 • Evidence: a retrospective comparison with luteinising hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH) agonists
   reported less prostate-specific antigen (PSA) rebound and better progression-free and overall survival
   after surgical castration; orchidectomy also caused less gynaecomastia and gave better quality of life (less
   physical discomfort and cancer-related anxiety). A much larger later analysis did not confirm the survival
   benefit.
 • Counsel on the potential negative effect on self-image.
 • Preserving appearance: testicular prosthesis or epididymis-sparing (subepididymal) orchidectomy —
   higher satisfaction than conventional orchidectomy, most patients satisfied cosmetically, and cost saving
   against medical castration.
 • Unilateral simple orchidectomy: severe trauma, or prolonged ischaemia from torsion when salvage is
   impossible.
 • Chronic orchialgia is a rare indication, without level-one evidence — only after all conservative treatment
   has failed.
Technique.
 • For orchialgia, the inguinal approach gives better pain outcomes, probably because more proximal
   segments of the ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric and genitofemoral nerves are removed with the cord (Fig.
   87.8); a small retrospective series found higher cure rates with inguinal than with scrotal orchidectomy.
 • Persistent pain after orchidectomy: resection of the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve gave
   excellent relief in most patients of a small series.
 • Scrotal orchidectomy sequence: single vertical median raphe incision or bilateral transverse incisions →
   open all fascial and tunical layers → divide the cord into two bundles → double-clamp each proximally
   and distally → divide between clamps → suture-ligate the proximal stump with 2-0 Vicryl (polyglactin).
 • Epididymis-sparing sequence: instead of dividing the cord, sharply dissect the testis off the epididymis
   under the operating microscope → find and suture-ligate the superior, middle and inferior epididymal
   arteries that perforate the testis → approximate the longitudinal epididymal edges with 4-0 chromic, ends
   left untied → tie the free ends to join caput and cauda.

Testicular Prosthesis Placement
 • Consider for every male lacking a testis (congenital, surgical, traumatic) — psychological distress from an
   empty scrotum occurs at any age; offer before orchidectomy for testicular cancer.
 • History: Vitallium (1941), Lucite (1943) and glass (1953) prostheses.
 • Only Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved device: Coloplast Torosa — silicone, four sizes (extra
   small to large), filled with saline through an injection port opposite the suture tab (Fig. 87.9).
 • Port rule: pierce no more than five times intraoperatively; never access postoperatively.
 • Strict asepsis, as for any prosthetic implantation.

EPIDIDYMIS
Excision of Epididymal Cysts and Spermatoceles
 • Origin: cystic accumulations of semen in the epididymal tubules, rete testis or efferent ductules; mostly
   idiopathic, some after infective or traumatic inflammation.
 • Common benign ultrasound finding, becoming more frequent with age.
 • Operate only for discomfort, infection, impaired quality of life, disability from size, or infertility — as for
   hydrocele.
 • Counsel men of reproductive age about obstructive azoospermia, particularly when the cyst lies in the
   corpus or cauda.
 • Sequence: trans-scrotal incision → dissect to tunica vaginalis → sharply open tunica → dissect cyst down
   to its stalk → ligate stalk with 5-0 or 6-0 absorbable suture. If it cannot be freed entirely → unroof part of
   the wall → reapproximate the epididymal tunic with 4-0 absorbable suture.

 • Possible malignancy → inguinal approach, to avoid disrupting natural lymphatic drainage; cord clamped

 KEY POINTS: SURGERY OF THE SCROTAL CONTENTS
   • High-resolution ultrasonography evaluates scrotal fluid collections; it may identify intratesticular
     malignancy and so guides the surgical approach.
   • Excisional hydrocelectomy techniques have the lowest recurrence rates.

Concept — Epididymal surgery is judged by its fertility cost: benign cysts are operated on only for symptoms
because the price is obstructive azoospermia, and tumours follow the same inguinal-first oncological rule as
the testis.

VAS DEFERENS
Vasectomy
 • Safe, effective, permanent male contraception; used by more than 10% of men in several countries
   (Australia, Bhutan, Canada, the Netherlands, New Zealand, the Republic of Korea, Great Britain, the
   United States).
 • Typical practice: the great majority are performed on men aged 25–44, in the office, by urologists.
 • Sex gap: in the United States, tubal ligation remains more prevalent than vasectomy (Centers for Disease
   Control and Prevention (CDC) National Survey of Family Growth, 2017–2019) — attributed to family-
   planning priorities, lower Medicaid coverage of vasectomy, and marital status (more unmarried women
   undergo tubal ligation than unmarried men undergo vasectomy).
 • Plan as for theatre surgery, even in the office.
 • Counselling points: not immediately sterile — other contraception until postvasectomy semen analysis
   (PVSA) confirms occlusion; repeat vasectomy needed in <1%; pregnancy about 1 in 2000 even after an
   azoospermic PVSA, from recanalisation; chronic scrotal pain about 1–2%; alternatives; signed consent.
 • No prophylactic antibiotics for routine vasectomy.
 • Anaesthesia: local with or without oral sedation; intravenous sedation or general anaesthesia if local is
   declined or judged inadequate.
 • Local agents: 1% or 2% lidocaine without adrenaline, alone or as a 50:50 mix with bupivacaine; long-
   acting liposomal bupivacaine (EXPAREL) gives good short- and long-term relief but may be cost-
   prohibitive.
 • AUA panel: use the smallest needle (25–32 gauge) to reduce injection pain; topical anaesthetic cream
   must not be the sole anaesthetic.
 • Two key steps — isolation and occlusion. Use the technique one is trained in, and change it if failures are
   significantly higher than expected.

Conventional Vasectomy
 • Single versus bilateral incision: no study shows superiority; a single incision seems to cause fewer side
   effects and is quicker, whereas bilateral incisions avoid grasping and dividing the same vas twice. With a
   single incision, a gentle tug on the vas that moves the ipsilateral testis confirms the side.
 • Level of opening: single midline incision at the penoscrotal junction or between it and the top of the
   testis; bilateral incisions at or above the penoscrotal junction to reach the straight vas. Lower openings
   reach the narrow convoluted vas, harder to handle now and at any later reversal.
 • Sequence: three-finger grasp (thumb, index, middle) traps the vas; the same hand isolates both vasa, a
   few drops of fluid lubricating the skin → further anaesthetic precisely at the planned site → test with an
   Adson forceps (tugging and touch are expected, sharp pain is not; top up subcutaneously if needed) →
   longitudinal incision over the vasal sheath down to the vas → grasp with towel clamp or Allis forceps;
   overlapping clamps stop the vas slipping back → avoid adjacent structures, occlude only the vas →
   remove a short segment → occlude the free ends → check haemostasis → drop the ends back → repeat
   on the other side → close skin with absorbable suture (edges bleed and alarm patients).
Minimally Invasive Vasectomy
 • Definition: any technique with a small (≤10 mm) skin opening and minimal vasal dissection, using special
   instruments — the sharp curved mosquito haemostat (Fig. 87.10) and the ring-tipped vas fixation clamp,
   whose cantilevered design prevents injury (Fig. 87.11).
 • Isolation: the vas is raised to the anterior scrotal skin by the three-finger grasp.
 • Open versus closed access: open — scalpel skin incision, minimal perivasal dissection, then clamp the vas;
   closed — clamp the vas through the skin first, transfer the clamp to the non-dominant hand, then incise.
   The remainder is common to all approaches.
 • Incisions may be left open — they are small and bleed little.
No-Scalpel Vasectomy
 • Origin: the first MIV technique, developed in China in 1974 by Li Shunqiang.
 • NSV is strictly a vas isolation technique — it does not specify the occlusion method.

SURGICAL MANAGEMENT OF CHRONIC SCROTAL PAIN
 • Pain is a subjective sensation arising when noxious stimuli activate nociceptors that transmit from the
   peripheral to the central nervous system.
 • Chronic scrotal pain: constant or intermittent pain lasting more than 3 months and interfering with daily
   activities; multifactorial, cause often undetermined; a common reason for clinic attendance, and patients
   typically consult several urologists.
 • Possible sources: testis, epididymis, pampiniform plexus, post-vasectomy sperm extravasation, post-
   surgical haematoma, proximal injury to the ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric, genitofemoral, pudendal or
   sympathetic nerves.
 • Referred pain from pelvic floor, prostate or other viscera sharing those nerve pathways.
 • Selection for surgery rests on knowing the causative diseases, the scrotal nerve anatomy and a systematic
   approach.

Surgical Management of Intrascrotal Pain
Acute and Chronic Epididymitis
 • Acute epididymitis usually resolves with rest, scrotal support, elevation, ice, antibiotics or NSAIDs.
 • Local softening (malacia) of the epididymis suggests abscess; an abscess not responding to antibiotics is
   drained or surgically debrided.
 • Chronic epididymitis: lasting more than 6 weeks despite adequate therapy.
 • Epididymectomy for epididymitis gives poor results — pain often persists → last resort; pain-free rates
   are low and highly variable.
 • Indications for partial or total epididymectomy after failed medical therapy: post-vasectomy epididymal
   engorgement, intractable epididymal pain, severe acute or chronic epididymitis, symptomatic epididymal
   cysts.
 • A palpable abnormality (mass, cyst, granuloma) significantly improves the chance of pain control.
 • Counsel on the low likelihood of complete resolution and the possible later need for orchidectomy.
Epididymectomy for Palpable Abnormalities of the Epididymis
 • Results are more favourable than for epididymitis when there is a palpable abnormality such as a cyst.
 • Sequence (cystic dilatation, granuloma, tender swollen epididymis): ligate the efferent tubules draining
   into the caput → sharply dissect corpus and cauda off the testis → in partial epididymectomy, suture-
   ligate the proximal and distal stumps to limit sperm extravasation.
Microsurgical Spermatic Cord Denervation
 • Indication: non-specific chronic pain localising to the testis and/or epididymis, or post-vasectomy pain.
 • A spermatic cord block selects responders to microsurgical denervation of the spermatic cord (MDSC),
   and the degree of relief after block correlates with the degree of relief after MDSC. In a prospective
   study, most patients were pain-free at 2 years.
 • Subinguinal sequence: 4-cm incision over the external inguinal ring → isolate the cord → divide the
   ilioinguinal nerve (electrocautery) or ligate it (4-0 silk) → identify and ligate the genital branch of the
   genitofemoral nerve → operating microscope (4×–20×) with micro-Doppler to find arterial pulsation →
   preserve the testicular and cremasteric arteries and the lymphatics (the latter to prevent hydrocele) →
   divide the remaining cord structures — pampiniform veins, cremaster fibres and the perivasal tissue.
 • Preserve the vas and vasal vessels while dividing the nerves in its sheath; the goal is to transect all
   genitofemoral branches (Fig. 87.18).
 • Scrotal alternative: vertical paramedian scrotal incision to isolate the cord, with the same preservation of
   arteries and lymphatics.
 • Trifecta nerve complex (Fig. 87.17) — a reproducible distribution of cord nerves, in decreasing density:
   cremasteric muscle fibres → perivasal tissue and vasal sheath → posterior periarterial tissue/cord
   lipoma. Immunohistochemistry confirms sensory and sympathetic nerves in the perivasal sheath and
   spermatic fascia.
 • Therefore, via the scrotal approach, the posterior periarterial tissue/cord lipoma must also be
   transected to maximise pain control.

 • Varicocele (clinical or subclinical) is found in about 15% of men.
 • Keep it in the differential of orchialgia, especially a dull ache worse on standing and exertion.

 • First described by Gabriele Fallopius in 1561; detailed study continued in the late 19th century.
 • Paired glands producing about 60% of the seminal fluid.

Perineal
 • Mostly used at perineal radical prostatectomy.
 • Limited incision makes dissection of the ampullae and SVs difficult; poor visibility risks neurovascular

 • Stay as close to the midline as possible to protect the bundles; laparoscopic SV dissection has been

 • Zinner syndrome: SV cyst + ipsilateral renal agenesis + ejaculatory duct obstruction (often with an ectopic

Transurethral Endoscopic Treatments
 • Preferred approach to SV cysts, persistent haematospermia, and ejaculatory duct obstruction and/or
   stones — less invasive, faster, fewer complications.
 • Transurethral unroofing with a standard resectoscope: resect an area about 1 cm long × 1 cm wide × 1
   cm deep between bladder neck and verumontanum → once unroofed, rinse the cyst with saline and
   cauterise with the loop; easy access, little trauma or bleeding, few complications, rapid recovery.
 • Transurethral seminal vesiculoscopy: semirigid ureteroscope after serial ureteric dilatation; the holmium
   laser is safe and effective for incising obstructed ducts, coagulating haemorrhagic mucosa and
   fragmenting duct or SV stones.
 • In infertility: sperm appeared initially in most men but fell after 6 months — re-obstruction is common,
   from the underlying disease or the procedure.
Transrectal Ultrasound–Guided Aspiration
 • TRUS suggests obstruction when the SV width is >1.5 cm and the ejaculatory duct diameter >2.3 mm.
 • Interpret cautiously: a dilated SV does not prove obstruction, and obstructed ducts may have undilated
   SVs.
 • Confirm by SV aspiration showing sperm.

 • Primary SV tumours are extremely rare; benign and malignant both appear as complex solid–cystic
   retrovesical masses, often asymptomatic, with symptoms overlapping cysts, stones, inflammation and

 • Work-up: abdominal and pelvic imaging first; MRI is the most accurate tool (multiplanar, excellent tissue
