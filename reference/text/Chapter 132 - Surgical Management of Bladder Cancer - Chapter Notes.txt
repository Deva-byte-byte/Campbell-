Chapter 132 — Surgical Management of Bladder Cancer:
Transurethral, Open and Robotic

INTRODUCTION

Epidemiology and Framing
 • Bladder cancer is one of the commonest non-cutaneous malignancies in the United States; the
   American Cancer Society projected roughly 82,000 new cases and 16,700 deaths for 2023.
 • Without appropriate surgery the disease is lethal and carries high health-care cost; surgery for both
   non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) demands
   attention to technique, oncological principle and disease pathogenesis.
 • Transurethral resection (TUR) establishes the pathological diagnosis and local extent, and is itself a
   complete oncological procedure for low-grade NMIBC.
 • MIBC requires radical cystectomy with regional lymph node dissection for local control and the best
   chance of cure.

 Evolving Systemic Therapy
   • Perioperative trials are testing immune checkpoint inhibitors with or without chemotherapy and

 whereas MIBC needs radical cystectomy with lymphadenectomy, and modern survival gains owe as much
 to cisplatin-based neoadjuvant therapy as to surgical refinement.

 TRANSURETHRAL RESECTION OF BLADDER TUMOURS

 Preoperative Evaluation
   • History directed at risk factors: tobacco; drugs — cyclophosphamide, phenacetin; occupational and
     environmental carcinogens — aromatic amines, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, trihalomethanes.
   • Surgical implants and valvular heart disease alter antibiotic prophylaxis; check against current
     guidelines.
   • Bleeding history and anticoagulants: aspirin is generally continued (no significant bleeding observed);
     caution with warfarin, heparin, rivaroxaban, apixaban, clopidogrel, ticagrelor and prasugrel — stopping
     time set by each drug's half-life, and bridging agreed preoperatively with the relevant team.
   • Examination: suprapubic palpation for a mass; digital rectal examination (DRE) in men if prostatic
     involvement is suspected; bimanual examination in women for anterior vaginal involvement.
   • Laboratory: full blood count, metabolic panel, coagulation panel, urinalysis with culture; active
     infection → defer resection until bacteria are cleared.
   • Upper tract imaging completes the diagnostic work-up: renal ultrasound with retrograde pyelogram,
     computed tomography (CT) urogram (CTU), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and rarely intravenous
     pyelogram.

 Operative Set-up and Inspection
   • Sequence: rigid cystoscope, dorsal lithotomy → antibiotic prophylaxis within 1 hour → 30° lens with
     visual obturator (limits urethral trauma): whole urethra, preliminary bladder survey, ureteric orifices →
     70° lens: complete survey, especially bladder neck, dome and anterior wall → 120° lens if still
     incomplete → record location and extent → blue-light cystoscopy where available for lesions poorly
     seen under white light, such as carcinoma in situ (CIS; see Chapter 130).
   • Antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended by the American Urological Association (AUA) — extrapolated
     from a randomised trial in TUR of the prostate showing less postoperative bacteriuria.
   • Avoid fluoroquinolones where possible: the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) label change of
     July 2016 cautioned against their use in uncomplicated urinary tract infection (no specific statement on
     prophylaxis).
   • AUA and Society of Urologic Oncology (SUO) joint guideline: enhanced cystoscopy — narrow-band
     imaging and blue-light cystoscopy — if available; both increase tumour detection and reduce
     recurrence.

 Resection Principles
   • Goal: visual eradication of all tumour and an adequate depth — loop electrode, cutting current,
     specimen to include muscularis propria (Fig. 132.1).
   • Tumour extends histologically beyond its visible edge → include a margin of approximately 2 cm of
     normal-appearing tissue where possible; wide resection secures completeness for both broad-based
     and tentacular growth patterns (Fig. 132.2).
   • After visual completion: empty the bladder → bimanual examination under anaesthesia; a mass still
     palpable after resection is staged clinical T3b (cT3b).

 PATIENT PREPARATION

 Staging
   • CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis if renal function is adequate and there is no iodinated contrast
     allergy; MRI substitutes when renal function is impaired.
   • Contrast cross-sectional imaging assesses the upper tracts and regional and distant spread, and guides
     perioperative chemotherapy.
   • MRI, with better soft-tissue resolution, assesses depth of invasion and can help distinguish NMIBC
     from MIBC before TUR.
   • Nodal staging: a systematic review suggests MRI and positron emission tomography–CT (PET/CT) may
     be more sensitive than CT, yet CT remains the commonest modality (cost, availability, speed); CT
     nodal accuracy varies widely with the node-size threshold.
   • Bone and brain imaging only when clinically indicated.

 Medical Optimisation and Stoma Marking
   • Expect coronary artery disease, smoking-related lung disease and peripheral vascular disease.
   • Enterostomal therapist stoma marking for every patient, whatever the planned diversion —
     conversion to an ileal conduit during a planned continent diversion is rare but must be consented.
   • Mark standing and seated to avoid folds and maximise appliance fit; without a stomal therapist the
     surgeon marks, patient awake and in usual clothing.

 Bowel Preparation
   • Mechanical preparation was intended to reduce anastomotic leak and wound and abdominal infection;
     randomised trials in colorectal surgery showed no benefit — complications, leak, wound infection and
     abscess were similar without it.
   • In radical cystectomy, pooled randomised trials show no or limited preparation → faster return of
     bowel activity and flatus, less fever and fewer wound complications, without other harm.
   • Routine mechanical bowel preparation is not advised, especially when only ileum is used; no routine
     oral antibiotic adjunct.

 Alvimopan
   • Peripherally acting μ-opioid receptor antagonist, given 30–90 minutes before surgery and twice daily
     thereafter.
   • Phase III trial (bowel resection): less postoperative ileus, earlier discharge; phase IV study (radical
     cystectomy): shorter stay, less total parenteral nutrition, cost saving.

 Antibiotic Prophylaxis
   • Intravenous, within 1 hour of incision, tailored to local susceptibility; cover gram-positive skin flora
     and gram-negative aerobes and anaerobes of distal small and large bowel — a broad-spectrum
     cephalosporin such as cefoxitin is usually adequate.

 Venous Thromboembolism Prophylaxis
   • High-risk patients without significant bleeding: mechanical (pneumatic compression) plus
     pharmacological prophylaxis, started before induction of general or spinal anaesthesia.
   • Extended prophylaxis for 4 weeks: a randomised trial of 28 vs 8 days after abdominal or pelvic surgery
     showed a marked reduction in major venous thromboembolism (VTE) without more bleeding.

 Positioning and Skin Preparation
   • Men: supine, table flexion point at the anterior superior iliac spine, flexed about 15° (less after spinal
     fusion or lumbar injury) (Fig. 132.4A).
   • Women: low lithotomy (stirrups or spreader bars) for vaginal access; table flexion generally not
     possible.
   • Field: xiphoid to upper thighs, including genitalia, vagina and perineum.
   • 10% povidone-iodine; avoid chlorhexidine gluconate on genital skin.
 Concept — Preparation is enhanced recovery by subtraction and addition: remove what does not help
 (bowel preparation, oral antibiotics) and add what protects bowel recovery and prevents clot (alvimopan,
 a timed intravenous antibiotic, four weeks of thromboprophylaxis).

 SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

 Exposure and Bladder Mobilisation
   • Sequence: lower midline incision, pubic symphysis → umbilicus (infraumbilical often suffices; extend
     cephalad as needed) (Fig. 132.4B) → fascia divided exactly in the midline (aids closure, avoids
     detaching rectus abdominis from its pubic insertion); upward umbilical traction reveals the linea alba →
     space of Retzius entered; blunt release of bladder from pelvic sidewalls cephalad to vas deferens
     (men) / round ligament (women) → peritoneotomy lateral to a medial umbilical ligament; urachus
     controlled and divided (Fig. 132.5) → peritoneum incised lateral to both medial umbilical ligaments to
     the internal inguinal rings; vasa deferentia or round ligaments divided.

   • Right: white line of Toldt incised around the caecum, posterior peritoneum opened to mobilise the

   • Bookwalter-type retractor, bowel packed cephalad; confirm with the anaesthetist that the vena cava is
     not compressed; moist packs behind retractor blades.

 Ureteric Dissection
   • Sequence: free each ureter from a few centimetres above the iliac crossing to the detrusor hiatus →
     preserve adventitia → ligate and divide the obliterated umbilical artery first (maximises ureteric
     length) → ligate (tie or suture ligature) and divide.
   • Distal ureteric margin frozen section is controversial: a positive margin correlates with later upper
     tract recurrence, but a survival effect is unproven, and sequential resection to a negative margin does
     not eliminate upper tract recurrence.
   • Urine management by preference: temporary ureteric catheters led off the field, or temporary ligation
     to avoid spillage.
 Concept — Ureteric length and blood supply are the currency of the later diversion: adventitia is preserved,
 the obliterated umbilical artery divided early, and the left ureter given a ready-made retrosigmoid tunnel.

 RADICAL CYSTECTOMY: MALE

 Posterior Dissection
   • Sequence: anterior pedicles ligated → rectal cul-de-sac identified; peritoneum over the seminal
     vesicles incised (Fig. 132.9) — ensure margin if tumour at bladder base → rectum freed in midline
     (blunt or sharp) to the prostate → Denonvilliers fascia incised → dissection carried laterally; posterior
     pedicles identified (Fig. 132.10A) and controlled with clips, ties, staplers or sealers (Fig. 132.10B).
   • Advanced disease, prior pelvic radiation, or fibrosis after resection or intravesical chemotherapy →
     no blunt dissection; sharp dissection under vision.
   • Rectal injury → primary repair, with or without flap coverage and/or faecal diversion.
   • Sealing devices transmit heat to the rectum → shield the rectum with a gloved finger from the
     instrument tips.

 Anterior and Apical Dissection
   • Sequence: urethra now palpable → endopelvic fascia incised over levators → urethra–dorsal venous
     complex confluence identified → dorsal venous complex ligated and divided (Fig. 132.11) → anterior
     urethra incised.

 Urethral Margin and Neobladder
   • Planned orthotopic neobladder → preserve urethral length and send a urethral margin frozen
     section.
   • Urethral recurrence occurs in a minority, usually within two years; risk is lower after orthotopic than
     cutaneous diversion (reason unclear).
   • Negative intraoperative frozen section → no urethral recurrence at 10 years in one series;
     preoperative prostatic urethral biopsy correlates poorly with final margin, whereas a negative frozen
     section has very high negative predictive value.
   • Positive urethral margin → no orthotopic neobladder; warn the patient preoperatively.

 Nerve Sparing
   • Controversial, unlike in radical prostatectomy; technique is analogous (Fig. 132.12).
   • Functional results are significantly worse than after prostatectomy and not clearly better than non-
     nerve-sparing cystectomy; only a minority regain erections adequate for intercourse at one year, more
     after neobladder than ileal conduit.
   • Age is a strong predictor of recovery, as after prostatectomy.
   • Local recurrence after nerve sparing is uncommon but has occurred even in pT2 disease.
   • Decide by preoperative sexual function and disease burden; non-nerve-sparing (Fig. 132.13) is
     preferred in advanced disease and pre-existing erectile dysfunction.

 Prostate Sparing (Subtotal Prostatic Resection)
   • Variants: whole prostate left, or prostatic capsule and/or seminal vesicles spared; studied mainly with
     orthotopic neobladder.
   • Early series: excellent erectile function and no local recurrence, but short follow-up; meta-analysis:
     better potency and better day- and night-time continence.
   • Concern: high rate of occult prostate cancer in cystoprostatectomy specimens; in carefully selected
     men (no prostate cancer preoperatively, negative frozen section) local control is good, but distant
     metastasis remains frequent.
   • Work-up: DRE, prostate-specific antigen (PSA), transurethral prostatic resection, intraoperative frozen
     section; abnormal PSA or DRE → prostate biopsy before surgery.
 Concept — Every functional preservation in men is paid for at an oncological margin: the urethra for a
 neobladder (the frozen section decides), the nerves for potency (a disappointing yield), and the prostate for
 erection and continence (the occult prostate cancer risk).

 RADICAL CYSTECTOMY: FEMALE

 Rationale and Counselling
   • Traditional operation: anterior pelvic exenteration — bladder, urethra, anterior vagina, uterus, cervix;
     essential with a positive examination under anaesthesia or suspected anterior vaginal wall invasion
     (cT4a).
   • Women present with more advanced disease, and female sex is an independent risk factor for death
     from disease → anterior exenteration remains the gold standard.
   • Sexual morbidity: limited recovery of sexual function by two years; dyspareunia, reduced libido and
     vaginal dryness common; more than half receive no preoperative counselling → counsel thoroughly.
   • Pelvic organ sparing can be considered in highly selected women prioritising sexual function:
     gynaecological organ involvement is uncommon in MIBC and ovarian involvement was absent in one
     series.
   • The ovaries continue to produce testosterone after menopause; bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy
     impairs sexual function → discuss ovarian sparing when disease is not locally advanced.
   • cT1–cT2 with orthotopic neobladder planned → vaginal and urethral sparing are necessary.

 Anterior Exenteration Technique
   • Sequence: bowel and anterior bladder mobilisation and ureteric dissection as in men, except ovarian
     vessels ligated (2-0 silk distally; 2-0 silk suture ligature plus tie proximally) and divided → identify
     posterior cervical fornix (Fig. 132.14A) → incise vaginal cuff there (Fig. 132.14B) → control lateral and
     posterior pedicles (staplers, sealers or clips) → specimen: uterus, cervix, anterior vaginal cuff, bladder
     → urethral meatus incised antegrade or from the introitus; specimen removed (Fig. 132.15A–B) →
     keep enough vaginal mucosa above the meatus for closure → release posterior vaginal wall from
     rectum if needed (Fig. 132.15C) → clamshell closure of posterior flap to introitus with 2-0 polyglactin
     910 (preserves girth at the cost of length) → watertight, interrupted closure → vaginal pack, removed
     on day 2.
   • The female pelvis is vascular and the pedicles are sinusoidal over the lateral vaginal wall → meticulous
     haemostasis.
   • Non-watertight closure → troublesome drainage of peritoneal fluid; the pack distends the vagina,
     tamponades wall bleeding (particularly after vaginal sparing) and reveals defects.

 Vaginal- and Urethral-Sparing Cystectomy for Neobladder
   • Orthotopic neobladder if the bladder neck is uninvolved and disease is ≤cT2.
   • Requires urethral length proximal to the striated sphincter and anterior vaginal wall sparing to
     support the neobladder.
   • Sequence: separate lateral pedicles from lateral vaginal wall before ligation — after removing cervix
     and uterus at the fornix (Fig. 132.16A) or with them in place → vaginal pack or end-to-end
     anastomosis (EEA) sizer defines the midline bladder–vagina plane → extend laterally → do not divide
     the pedicles until the midpoint of the lateral vaginal wall is reached (preserves bladder margin) →
     continue to bladder neck (Foley balloon as guide) → preserve striated sphincter; transect → urethral
     margin frozen section; malignancy → orthotopic diversion contraindicated → vaginal apex closed with
     2-0 polyglactin 910; urethral anastomotic sutures placed (Fig. 132.16B–C).

 PARTIAL CYSTECTOMY

 Selection and Outcomes
   • Solitary, small MIBC without concurrent CIS → results similar to radical cystectomy; population-based
     cohort matched for node count and clinicopathology: equivalent overall and cancer-specific survival.
   • Recurrence pathways: NMIBC recurrence → intravesical therapy; muscle-invasive recurrence →
     salvage radical cystectomy, but survival after salvage is far worse if disease is extravesical or node-
     positive than if organ-confined.
   • Preoperative: counsel about possible radical cystectomy and diversion (margin may be unattainable or
     extravesical disease found); cisplatin-eligible → offer neoadjuvant chemotherapy.

 Technique
   • Sequence: pelvic lymphadenectomy and anterior bladder mobilisation → cystotomy away from the
     tumour → excise tumour with full-thickness wall and perivesical fat, 1–2 cm mucosal margin,
     confirmed by frozen section → excise ureteric orifice or intramural ureter and reimplant if needed →
     close with 2-0 polyglactin 910 in 2–3 layers; fill via catheter to confirm watertight → copious warm

 ROBOTIC RADICAL CYSTECTOMY

 Background
   • Patients are difficult: pulmonary and cardiac comorbidity, frailty, poor performance status, advanced
     age, prior abdominal surgery or pelvic radiation.
   • Enhanced recovery shortened stay and hastened bowel recovery, but complications and transfusion
     rates remained stubbornly high — the drive toward minimally invasive surgery.
   • Evolution: laparoscopic simple cystectomy → laparoscopic radical cystectomy with extracorporeal
     diversion → robotic radical cystectomy and lymphadenectomy → intracorporeal diversion (first
     reported 2003; uptake rising per the International Robotic Cystectomy Consortium).
   • Early resistance concerned oncological equipoise (no tactile feedback). Unlike prostate cancer, bladder
     cancer has no effective salvage: urine spillage, inadequate lymphadenectomy or a positive soft-tissue
     margin → recurrence nearly always fatal.

 Patient Selection
   • Indications and staging are identical to open surgery.
   • Steep Trendelenburg and pneumoperitoneum → raised pulmonary pressures, ventilation mismatch,
     raised intraocular pressure, reduced cardiac output — address with anaesthesia before the day of
     surgery.
   • Prior abdominal or pelvic surgery is not a strict contraindication (though excluded from trials); avoid
     early in the learning curve after prior urological surgery (partial cystectomy, ureteric reimplantation)
     — positive-margin risk.
   • Obesity: positioning difficulty and worse ventilation in Trendelenburg; team decision; when in doubt,
     open or convert.
   • Technique described for the da Vinci system (Intuitive Surgical); principles transfer to new platforms.

 Patient Positioning
   • Tailored case by case: prior radiation, rectal or bowel risk, sexual and endocrine preservation, limited
     limb movement, existing prosthetic reservoirs.

     (large Weck clips).

 Extended Pelvic Lymphadenectomy
   • Why lymphadenectomy before cystectomy (ureters in continuity): avoids prolonged ureteric occlusion
     (renal function), keeps urine output as a resuscitation guide for anaesthesia; occlusion-induced
     dilatation offers no reliable anastomotic advantage; ureters do not obstruct the field.
   • Landmarks are those of open surgery; the camera angle changes the visual cues.
   • Keep each packet whole and bag it immediately; the 15-mm port avoids fragmenting packets and
     transecting nodes.

 Female Radical Cystectomy
   • En bloc with uterus and ovaries as in open surgery. An EEA sizer placed vaginally identifies the
     posterior cervical fornix (or vaginal vault after hysterectomy), gives countertraction and distends the
     wall for the sparing plane.
   • En bloc anterior vaginectomy: posterior fornix opened → vessel sealer along anterolateral vaginal wall
     to introitus → at meatus, catheter detached from bag, clipped by assistant and pulled
     intracorporeally to retract the meatus → complete urethral and meatal excision (Fig. 132.32E–F).
   • Vaginal sparing (uterus in situ; Fig. 132.32): fourth arm lifts bladder, EEA countertraction (A) →
     peritoneum incised, anterior vaginal wall exposed (B) → midline dissection toward introitus in a near-
     avascular plane → lateral posterior pedicles defined as in men (C) → clips or sealer to the meatus (D)
     → neobladder: apical dissection after urachal release; no urethral sparing: open vagina just behind
     bladder neck with the catheter manoeuvre.

 Postoperative Care
   • Genitourinary and gastrointestinal surgery in elderly, comorbid patients: major complications are
     infrequent but most patients have some complication — commonest gastrointestinal, then
     infectious.
   • Monitor blood count, electrolytes, renal function and fluid balance; often intensive care or step-down
     initially.
   • No routine nasogastric tube — only for impaired mentation or airway-protection concerns.
   • Continue VTE prophylaxis unless bleeding; early ambulation; incentive spirometry, deep breathing,
     coughing.
   • Delayed bowel function prolongs stay: alvimopan; neostigmine (with telemetry); promotility
     suppositories; stress ulcer prophylaxis; antiemetics; early enteral feeding if no nausea or vomiting;
     opioid avoidance — ketorolac and paracetamol unless contraindicated.
 Concept — Robotic cystectomy is judged on oncology first because bladder cancer has no salvage;
 randomised evidence shows equivalent progression-free survival with recovery gains, and each technical
 rule (whole node packets, never grasp the ureter, lymphadenectomy before ureteric division) protects the
 margin, the node count or the ureter.


   KEY POINTS
    • Bladder cancer is common, costly and frequently fatal.
    • Radical cystectomy offers excellent survival for organ-confined disease.
    • TUR is diagnostic and therapeutic, especially for low-grade tumours.
    • Perioperative intravesical chemotherapy reduces recurrence by about 13%.
    • Standard template: genitofemoral nerve laterally, internal iliac artery medially, Cooper ligament
      distally, ureter crossing the common iliac artery proximally.
    • Ureteric margin frozen section predicts upper tract recurrence, but sequential resection to a
      negative margin may have no impact.
    • Nerve-sparing radical cystectomy aims to preserve sexual function and mirrors radical
      prostatectomy technique.
    • Subtotal prostatic resection may further aid sexual function, only where occult prostate cancer risk is
      low.
    • Vaginal sparing is appropriate without locally advanced disease and permits orthotopic diversion.
    • Partial cystectomy suits rare, solitary urothelial tumours without concomitant CIS.
    • Urachal adenocarcinoma: en bloc resection of umbilicus and bladder dome.
    • Robotic radical cystectomy gives similar oncological outcomes to open and can speed recovery.
    • Enhanced recovery protocols restore physiology sooner and shorten stay.
