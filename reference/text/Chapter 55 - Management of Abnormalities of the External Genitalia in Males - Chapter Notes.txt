Chapter 55 — Abnormalities of the External Genitalia in Males

Chapter 55 — Management of Abnormalities of the
External Genitalia in Males
 • The normal penis comprises the prepuce (foreskin) and frenulum, glans, urethral meatus, coronal sulcus,
   and a shaft of two corpora cavernosa with the urethra. The ventral median raphe is continuous with the
   scrotal raphe and ends at the edge of the outer prepuce.
 • Penile and scrotal anomalies are common and may be congenital, acquired or iatrogenic. Congenital
   anomalies arise from disorders of sexual differentiation, genital differentiation or genital growth, and
   may be part of a syndrome or involve other organ systems — up to half of children with anorectal
   malformations have an associated urological malformation.
 • Fetal sex determination by ultrasound is not accurate enough before 12 weeks' gestation; after 13
   weeks it is accurate in at least 99% of fetuses without malformed genitalia.
 • Antenatal ultrasound has identified ambiguous genitalia and penoscrotal transposition. Three-
   dimensional (3D) ultrasound is being used to examine the fetal genitalia, but there is no evidence yet
   that it improves diagnosis.
 • Most neonatal genital abnormalities do not produce ambiguity; far commoner are inconspicuous penis
   and abnormal penile orientation. Recognising them depends on knowing normal anatomy and
   embryology. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) published a guide (2000) to normal and altered
   genital embryology and to the evaluation and management of ambiguous genitalia.

NORMAL MALE EXTERNAL GENITALIA

Embryology
 • The external genitalia of the two sexes are identical until the ninth week of gestation.
 • Male differentiation requires an intact chain: testosterone synthesis by fetal Leydig cells → conversion to
   dihydrotestosterone (DHT) by 5α-reductase → androgen receptors able to recognise the hormone.
 • DHT acting on androgen receptors during weeks 9–13 converts the undifferentiated primordia into male
   structures; without androgen action they passively become female structures.

 • The phallus develops proximal to distal. The long-held theory that the glanular urethra forms by

Concept — Male genital development is active and androgen-dependent, whereas female development is the
passive default; a break anywhere in the testosterone → DHT → receptor chain therefore produces
undervirilisation rather than a new structure.

Penile Length and Tanner Classification
 • Penile length rises with gestational age, from about 6 mm at 16 weeks to about 26 mm at 38 weeks, and
   prenatal measurements correlate with postnatal ones.
 • A second rapid phase occurs in the first 3 months of life (minipuberty): at birth the inhibitory effect of
   maternal oestrogen on the fetal pituitary is lost, producing a gonadotrophin surge and increased Leydig-
   cell testosterone. Growth then slows through childhood and accelerates again at puberty until adult size is
   reached.
 • The normal term neonate has a stretched length of 3.5 ± 0.7 cm and diameter of 1.1 ± 0.2 cm; the adult
   stretched length is 13.3 ± 1.6 cm. Stretched penile length (SPL) is measured from the penopubic junction
   (pubic symphysis) to the meatus / tip of the glans.

PENILE ANOMALIES

Prepuce (Foreskin)
Phimosis and Paraphimosis
 • At birth there is a physiological phimosis — partial or complete inability to retract the prepuce — caused
   by natural adhesions between the glans and inner preputial skin and/or a preputial ring.
 • Two factors separate the prepuce from the glans: (1) accumulation of smegma (epithelial debris)
   beneath the prepuce during the first 3–4 years, and (2) intermittent erections.
 • Retractability increases with age: about 90% of uncircumcised boys have a fully retractable prepuce by 3
   years, and fewer than 1% are phimotic at 17 years. Primary phimosis therefore usually resolves during
   childhood.
 • Secondary phimosis results from scarring — for example after forceful retraction with subsequent
   scarring of the prepuce to the glans — or from balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO). Forceful retraction
   should be discouraged because it causes cicatrix.
 • Conditions associated with the uncircumcised penis include paraphimosis, infection, urinary tract
   infection (UTI) and cancer.
 • Paraphimosis is entrapment of the retracted prepuce behind the glans. Severe oedema develops within
   hours, faster the tighter the preputial tip, making reduction harder; untreated, it can progress to
   gangrene. Reduction must therefore be timely, by manipulation, dorsal slit or circumcision.
 • Management in escalating order: manual compression of the glans with distal traction on the
   oedematous prepuce (successful in most) → adjuncts to reduce oedema: an iced glove for 5 minutes,
   granulated sugar for 1–2 hours, or multiple punctures of the oedematous skin → dorsal slit →
   circumcision.

 • Indications to improve retractability are persistent primary phimosis, secondary phimosis, balanitis,
   posthitis (inflammation of the prepuce), BXO and UTI.
 • Topical corticosteroid is effective with few side-effects. Betamethasone 0.05% twice daily for 30 days and
   three times daily for 21 days are equally effective, response may be seen within 2 weeks, and roughly
   one in six fails — attributed to increased collagen and reduced elastic-fibre density in the prepuce.
Concept — Non-retractility is normal physiology that time, smegma and erections resolve; treatment is
aimed at scarring (secondary phimosis, BXO) or its consequences (infection, paraphimosis), and forceful
retraction converts the physiological problem into the pathological one.

Circumcision
 • Circumcision dates back more than 6000 years; the oldest documented evidence is thought to be
   Egyptian sixth-dynasty tomb art (2345–2181 BCE). Proposed origins include religious sacrifice, a rite of
   passage, hygiene, differentiation of cultural groups and discouragement of masturbation.
 • Elective neonatal circumcision remains controversial, and the AAP position has evolved:

 • Benefits of circumcision are prevention of penile cancer, UTI, sexually transmitted diseases including
   HIV, and phimosis, and reduced balanitis. The allegation that neonatal circumcision causes sexual
   dysfunction is not supported by long-term studies, and neonatal circumcision is more cost-effective
   than postneonatal circumcision (procedure charges plus future health-care costs).
 • Penile cancer: carcinoma of the penis develops almost exclusively in men not circumcised at birth, and
   phimosis is a significant risk factor. Neonatal circumcision protects much more strongly against invasive
   cancer than against carcinoma in situ.
 • UTI: classic studies found that febrile infant UTIs occur overwhelmingly in uncircumcised boys, confirmed
   in larger cohorts; the excess risk lasts at least to 5 years of age and is attributed to colonisation of the
   prepuce by uropathogens. A retractile prepuce halves the risk of recurrent febrile UTI compared with a
   persistently non-retractile one; significant phimosis raises infant infection risk; and topical steroid
   treatment of phimosis after a UTI (with normal ultrasound) significantly reduces recurrence. It is
   calculated that 111 neonatal circumcisions are needed to prevent one UTI, yet neonatal circumcision is
   cheaper than treating UTIs in uncircumcised boys. Epididymitis is also less common after circumcision.
 • HIV: large randomised series show about 60% protection against HIV acquisition in men. However, a
   randomised trial of circumcising HIV-infected men showed no reduction in transmission to their female
   partners. Circumcision may reduce ulceration, bacterial vaginosis and trichomoniasis in female partners.
 • Human papillomavirus (HPV) and herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2): randomised trials support a
   protective effect, with lower HSV-2 seroconversion and lower prevalence of high-risk (carcinogenic) HPV
   genotypes after circumcision.

 KEY POINTS: CIRCUMCISION
   • Primary phimosis resolves spontaneously in the vast majority; fewer than 1% of 17-year-olds are
     phimotic.
   • Indications to improve retractability: persistent primary phimosis, secondary phimosis, balanitis,
     posthitis, BXO and UTI.
   • Topical corticosteroid relaxes the phimotic ring in the large majority of cases.
   • The AAP acknowledges health benefits sufficient to justify access for families who choose it and third-
     party payment.
   • Local or topical anaesthesia should be given for neonatal circumcision.
   • Defer circumcision when another penile abnormality detected in the newborn may require surgery.
   • Health benefits: reduced penile cancer, sexually transmitted diseases including HIV, UTI, balanitis and
     phimosis.

Concept — The benefits of circumcision all follow from removing a moist, colonisable and potentially
phimotic preputial sac; the AAP's position is a cost–benefit judgement that supports access without
mandating the procedure, and the prepuce is preserved whenever it may be needed for reconstruction.
Glanular adhesions and skin bridges.
 • Both are attachments of the glans to the shaft, common after circumcision and usually noticed by a carer.
   They occur regardless of how complete the circumcision was, and result from physiological retraction of
   the penis into the suprapubic fat pad, diaper irritation, or incomplete lysis of adhesions at circumcision.

 • A glanular adhesion joins the inner prepuce or circumcision line to the glans. Its incidence falls steadily
   with age through epithelial separation — common in infancy, rare after 9 years. Persistent adhesions can
   be lysed in the office after a topical analgesic (EMLA or a vapocoolant spray). Low-dose corticosteroids
   are not supported.
 • A skin bridge is an epithelialised adhesion that can cause chordee and torsion. It is excised in the office
   with analgesia, or in theatre when broad or thick and requiring haemostasis or suturing of the glanular
   and shaft defects.
 • The commonest acquired penile lesion is entrapped smegma beneath a non-retractile prepuce or
   glanular adhesions — a yellowish mass that is released as the adhesions resolve spontaneously or are
   lysed.
Meatal stenosis.
 • In children meatal stenosis occurs almost only after circumcision in infancy. It may be congenital, mainly
   in neonates with hypospadias, or acquired.
 • Normal meatal calibre by age (calibrate with a bougie à boule or urethral sound):

 • Proposed causes of secondary stenosis: severe meatal inflammation and cicatrix after lysis of normal
   adhesions and circumcision, frenular devascularisation, and chronic meatitis from diaper irritation of
   the exposed meatus. BXO is another cause. Only a small proportion of circumcised boys become
   symptomatic.
 • The three symptoms: (1) upward deviation of the stream from a meatal baffle or ventral web at the
   inferior meatus; (2) a narrow, high-velocity stream with a sense of incomplete emptying, straining or
   frequency; (3) pain at the start of micturition.
 • Urethrography (voiding or retrograde) may show proximal urethral dilatation but is not needed for
   diagnosis; reserve it for UTI, incontinence or suspected more proximal urethral abnormality.
 • Meatotomy or meatoplasty — in the office with topical EMLA, or under general anaesthesia — uses a
   ventral incision long enough to restore normal calibre. Suturing the urethral mucosa to the glans with
   fine, rapidly absorbable sutures reduces recurrence; clamp tailoring without sutures is equally effective.
Penile trauma.
 • The most serious circumcision complications are urethral injury, excision of the glans and/or shaft, and
   penile necrosis.
 • Urethral injury requires urethroplasty matched to the severity of excision. An excised glans can be
   sutured back, often without microscopic repair, with good results if done within 8 hours.
 • Penile necrosis follows thermal injury — for example cautery touching the metal ring or clamp, or
   inappropriate use of lasers. The options are all imperfect: penile reconstruction, or female gender
   reassignment with bilateral orchidectomy; the latter is problematic because many such children grow up
   with a male identity, attributed to prenatal and neonatal androgen imprinting.

Circumcision complications.
 • The overall complication rate is 0.2–5%. It is low when circumcision is performed in the first year of life
   but rises 10- to 20-fold after infancy. Complications may present immediately or months to years later.
 • Bleeding is the commonest complication (about 0.1%), more frequent in older children. It usually arises
   from the frenulum, less often from a shaft vessel or a skin edge between sutures, and is managed with
   compression, ophthalmic cautery, a silver nitrate stick or a suture; occasionally exploration is needed to
   evacuate a haematoma.
 • Wound infection is rare; antibiotic ointment (e.g., bacitracin) usually prevents it. Rare cases of Fournier
   gangrene are reported.
 • Penile degloving — from excessive skin removal or failure of the skin edges to adhere to the mucosal
   collar — re-epithelialises without intervention, using antibiotic ointment and warm baths to prevent
   eschar. Immediate suturing and skin grafting are not recommended.
Penile skin complications.
 • Insufficient or asymmetrical excision causes health, cosmetic and social problems as the child grows.
   Revision requires general anaesthesia, unlike neonatal circumcision.
 • Excessive excision can cause chordee, torsion and lateral deviation, repaired if necessary with penile skin
   flaps, Z-plasty or split-thickness grafts, and a trapped penis from a cicatricial scar.
 • A trapped penis is managed with topical betamethasone 0.05% plus manual retraction, which softens
   the cicatrix in most children, exposing the glans or leaving a mild ring amenable to a vertical relaxing
   incision; otherwise formal repair with penile skin flaps or Z-plasty.
Balanitis xerotica obliterans.
 • BXO — lichen sclerosus et atrophicus — is a chronic infiltrative and cicatrising skin condition that causes
   pathological phimosis. It can affect the glans, meatus and urethra.
 • The usual presentation is inability to retract the prepuce at puberty; BXO is rare before 5 years. Other
   symptoms are local infection, irritation, discomfort after micturition, bleeding, and occasionally acute
   retention or incontinence. Older patients and those with meatal involvement have a more severe
   course.
 • The cause is unknown — no viral, bacterial or familial cause has been identified — and the association
   with penile cancer in children is not established.
 • Topical corticosteroids help only mild preputial disease with minimal scarring. Circumcision is the
   preferred treatment, with meatotomy or meatoplasty when the meatus is involved. Children with
   meatal disease must be followed because about one-fifth need further meatal surgery. Buccal mucosal
   urethroplasty is promising in severe disease.
 • A prepuce-sparing approach — triradiate preputial incisions with intralesional triamcinolone — left
   most boys with a fully retractile prepuce free of visible disease, with significantly less meatal stenosis
   than primary circumcision; a minority developed recurrence requiring circumcision or repeat
   preputioplasty.

   • Circumcision revision requires general anaesthesia.
   • Meatal stenosis occurs almost exclusively in the circumcised penis.
   • Topical corticosteroids do not resolve penile adhesions.

Abnormal Penile Number
Aphallia
 • Penile agenesis results from failure of development of the genital tubercle. It is very rare — about 1 in
   10–30 million births.
 • The karyotype is almost always 46,XY. The usual appearance is a well-developed scrotum with
   descended testes and no penile shaft; the anus is usually displaced anteriorly, and the urethra opens at
   the anal verge beside a small skin tag, or into the rectum.
 • About one in three cases ends in stillbirth or neonatal death.
 • Associated malformations are common: cryptorchidism, vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), horseshoe kidney,
   renal agenesis, imperforate anus, and musculoskeletal and cardiopulmonary anomalies. The
   genitourinary–gastrointestinal connection varies, and its level predicts outcome:

 • Aphallia should be considered in apparent females with severe urinary tract dysplasia and genital

   from in utero or postnatal sex-steroid imprinting. Reassignment involves neonatal orchidectomy and
   feminising genitoplasty, with a neovagina later; combined urinary tract reconstruction with an intestinal
   neovagina through a posterior sagittal and abdominal approach has been described.

   children may lose the fat pad simply with growth.

   tissue to the ventral base of the shaft (as for buried penis); transverse incision of the web, separation of

Penile Torsion
 • Penile torsion is a rotational deformity of the shaft, usually counter-clockwise (to the left). Penile size is
   usually normal, and the condition is often unrecognised until circumcision or preputial retraction. It may
   accompany hypospadias, chordee or dorsal hood deformity without urethral abnormality.
 • The median raphe usually spirals obliquely in the direction of the torsion and inserts atypically rather
   than at the base of the glans in line with the meatus. The cause is probably an anomalous arrangement of
   penile shaft skin.
 • Most torsions are less than 60°. If repair is being considered, neonatal circumcision is discouraged and is
   done at the time of detorsion. Even when the glans points more than 90° from the midline, the corpora
   and corpus spongiosum are usually normally oriented at the base.
 • Mild torsion: deglove → rotate the glans opposite to the defect (clockwise for a counter-clockwise
   torsion) → suture the glans to the penile skin. A serpentine raphe that cannot be restored is acceptable
   provided the meatus is normal.
 • Torsion of 90° or more: skin rearrangement alone is insufficient → mobilise the base and incise dysgenic
   fibrous bands → if still rotated, place non-absorbable anchoring sutures at the base of the corpora
   cavernosa, possibly rotating a dorsal dartos flap or mobilising the urethral plate and urethra.
Concept — Torsion is a skin-level deformity on normally oriented corpora in all but severe cases, so degloving
and redraping suffice below 90°, and only beyond that must the base be mobilised and anchored.

Accessory Urethral Openings
Congenital Urethral Fistula
 • The urethra and meatus are normal, with a urethrocutaneous fistula, typically coronal or subcoronal. It
   is usually isolated but may accompany imperforate anus or ventral chordee; distal hypospadias and
   chordee occur in a minority.
 • The cause is unknown — possibly a focal urethral-plate defect preventing fusion of the urethral folds.
   Some cases are diagnosed after circumcision, raising the possibility of an iatrogenic injury.
 • Repair: circumscribe the fistula and close in multiple layers, as for a post-hypospadias fistula; or, if the
   glans bridge is thin, open the ventral glans through the distal urethra and repair by Thiersch–Duplay
   tubularisation with urethral-plate incision as needed.

 • Rare (about 200 reported cases). Duplication is most commonly sagittal, one urethra ventral and one
   dorsal (about 94%); collateral duplication in the same horizontal plane is extremely rare.

   sphincter, verumontanum); the dorsal urethra is the accessory, with or without a stream.

 • Transposition is partial or complete; lesser forms are the doughnut, prepenile and shawl scrotum. The

Congenital Haemangiomas
 • About 1% of haemangiomas affect the genitalia; their origin and the control of growth and involution are

   malformations. They appear at birth or later and, unlike cutaneous haemangiomas, enlarge gradually.
 • They feel like a "bag of worms", as a varicocele does, but are firm and do not decompress when
   recumbent.
 • Because examination underestimates their extent, define them with Doppler ultrasound, computed
   tomography (CT) or MRI. Definitive treatment is en bloc resection; preoperative angioembolisation
   reduces size and bleeding.

Klippel–Trénaunay–Weber Syndrome
 • A triad of cutaneous vascular malformation (most commonly naevus flammeus) with soft-tissue and
   bone hypertrophy, present at birth, usually in a lower limb, sometimes trunk or face. The lesions tend to
   bleed.
 • Genitourinary cutaneous or visceral involvement occurs in about 30%; cutaneous genital lesions
   frequently cause intractable bleeding, and excision is associated with significant blood loss.

Vascular Malformations
 • Penile vascular malformations are congenital but usually diagnosed in the teens or young adulthood, as a
   faint blue patch or soft blue mass. Careful excision is effective; for glans lesions the neodymium:yttrium-
   aluminium-garnet (Nd:YAG) laser may give a better result.
Concept — Haemangiomas involute and malformations do not; that single difference in natural history is why
haemangiomas are observed unless they ulcerate, whereas malformations are imaged and resected.

HERNIA AND HYDROCELES

Embryology
 • The processus vaginalis forms in the third month of gestation as the peritoneum bulges into the
   inguinal canal just before testicular descent. After descent it obliterates, and the portion around the
   testis becomes the tunica vaginalis.
 • Only about 18% are closed in term infants; obliteration continues postnatally, and failure to obliterate
   accounts for nearly all inguinoscrotal abnormalities in children.
 • In boys having unilateral hernia repair, a patent contralateral processus is found in about two-thirds
   under 2 months and about 40% at 1–2 years, persisting at similar frequency to 16 years; incidental
   patency in older children and adults is about 20%.

Epidemiology and Pathogenesis
Inguinal Hernia and Communicating Hydrocele
 • Inguinal hernias occur in 1–5% of children, are 5–10 times commoner in boys, and are much commoner
   in premature infants (about 13% of babies born before 32 weeks and nearly 30% of those under 1 kg).
 • Right-sided predominance (3:1) is attributed to later descent of the right testis. Risk factors for
   bilaterality: female sex, prematurity, age under 1 year and cryptorchidism. Breastfeeding may be
   protective.
 • Hernias present at any age, but the mean age is 3–4 years, with nearly one-third before 6 months.
   Concomitant hydroceles are frequent, most scrotal, some of the cord.
 • Most new hydroceles after birth and before puberty are communicating, although a proportion of
   apparently non-communicating and cord hydroceles resolve spontaneously. The processus is found
   completely obliterated in only a minority at hydrocele repair.

Noncommunicating Hydroceles
 • Hydroceles that resolve in infancy, or appear at or after puberty, are usually non-communicating. They
   arise from delayed absorption or abnormal fluid dynamics within the tunica vaginalis or, less commonly,
   in response to tumour, trauma or inflammation.

 • Simple scrotal hydroceles occur in at least 5% of male neonates, are typically bilateral, and resolve by
   reabsorption.

Genetics and Associated Conditions
 • There is a genetic predisposition: relatives of probands are at increased risk, highest in first-degree
   relatives and especially sisters of affected girls; transmission appears autosomal dominant with reduced
   penetrance.
 • Associations: bladder exstrophy, epididymal anomalies, connective tissue disorders, cystic fibrosis and
   posterior urethral valves; inguinal hernia is a feature of more than 200 syndromes. Findings at repair may
   include polyorchidism, transverse testicular ectopia (± persistent Müllerian ducts) and splenogonadal
   fusion. Abnormal epididymal–testicular attachment correlates with the degree of processus closure.
   Ascites or a ventriculoperitoneal shunt increases hernia risk.
Concept — Hernia risk tracks anything that keeps the processus open (prematurity, later right-sided descent,
cryptorchidism) or raises intra-abdominal fluid and pressure (ascites, shunts).

Diagnosis
Signs and Symptoms
 • Hernias and communicating hydroceles present as a painless groin bulge, sometimes extending along the
   cord to the scrotum, often only with raised intra-abdominal pressure (crying, straining) and reducing
   when supine. An intermittent bulge distinguishes a reducible hernia or communicating hydrocele from a
   scrotal or cord hydrocele.
 • Incarceration: an irritable or inconsolable child with a persistent or larger bulge that does not reduce,
   poor feeding, and signs of bowel obstruction (distension, vomiting, no flatus or stool).
 • A scrotal hydrocele may appear chronically or acutely after inflammation, infection or trauma; its size is
   stable or decreases. A cord hydrocele is painless, variable in size, and may be mistaken for the testis
   because of its oval shape.

Physical Examination
 • Examine standing if age-appropriate, otherwise supine; inspect from the lower abdominal crease along
   the canal to the scrotum, watching a crying child's bulge appear and subside. Provoke it by restraining an
   infant to induce crying, or by jumping, coughing, laughing, blowing bubbles or balloons in older
   children. If no bulge is elicited, parents' photographs are diagnostically reliable.
 • Palpate craniocaudally from superolateral to the pubic tubercle down to the scrotum to define proximal
   and distal extent: hernias and communicating hydroceles start at the internal ring. The silk-stocking sign
   — the sensation of rubbing silk when the cord is rolled over the pubic tubercle — indicates thickened
   cord structures.
 • A cord hydrocele can mimic a testis or large epididymal cyst, but normal cord is palpable above and
   below it and the testis is felt separately. A scrotal hydrocele may show a blue hue; if it reaches or crosses
   the internal ring (abdominoscrotal), abdominal examination reveals a ballotable mass.
 • Hydrocele fluid transilluminates — but so may neonatal bowel. The testis is palpable in a soft hydrocele
   but may be impalpable in a tense one.

Radiologic Imaging
 • Imaging is not usually needed. Ultrasound helps in the obese child (an elongated echolucent track from
   the groin along the cord), to confirm bowel or omentum in an incarcerated hernia, to distinguish a tense

   hydrocele.
Concept — Fluctuation is the key discriminator: a bulge that comes and goes means a patent processus
(hernia or communicating hydrocele) and therefore surgery, whereas a fixed collection means a closed
processus and permits observation.

Surgical Repair
Inguinal Hernia
 • Inguinal hernias need repair shortly after diagnosis because of the risk of complications. Easily reducible
   hernias and communicating hydroceles are repaired as outpatients within a few weeks; difficult reduction
   → more urgent repair; an irreducible hernia needs immediate exploration. Counsel parents on the signs
   of incarceration.
 • Premature infants can be repaired before discharge, but repair may be delayed in extremely low-birth-
   weight infants (under 1500 g) or those with congenital heart disease, pulmonary disease, sepsis or
   metabolic disease because of anaesthetic risk; some advise waiting until 50–52 weeks postconception.

Hydroceles
 • Infant hydroceles can resolve spontaneously — most by 1 year, some by 2 years. Persistence,
   enlargement or new fluctuation (implying a communicating hydrocele/hernia) is an indication for
   surgery. Cord hydroceles rarely resolve but rarely need urgent surgery, and can be corrected after 1 year.

Standard Inguinal Hydrocele/Hernia Repair
 • High ligation of the sac at the internal ring has a high success rate, low morbidity, little pain and good
   cosmesis.
 • Sequence: skin-crease incision in Langer's lines superolateral to the pubic tubercle → incise Scarpa's
   fascia → clear the external oblique laterally and caudally to the external ring → open the external oblique
   in the line of its fibres, sparing the ilioinguinal nerve (in young infants the rings nearly coincide, so the
   external oblique need not be opened — Mitchell-Banks technique) → separate cremaster fibres
   perpendicular to the cord → find the sac anteromedially → separate it atraumatically and divide
   between clamps → dissect the proximal limb to the internal ring and ligate there to prevent recurrence
   (open the sac first if contents are present or suspected, or if it is large or thick, to reduce bowel,
   omentum or a sliding component) → tighten a widened internal ring medially without compressing the
   cord → leave a short distal sac; excise the anterior wall of a long one → excise redundant tunica vaginalis
   without encroaching on the cord, and remove appendages.
 • Peripubertal hydrocele: use an inguinal approach if the hydrocele is clinically communicating; otherwise
   a trans-scrotal repair as in adults, converting to inguinal if a proximal communication is found. Hydroceles
   are non-communicating in the large majority of boys over 10–12 years, which supports a scrotal
   approach from that age.

Complications
 • Early bleeding and infection are rare.

Scrotal Approach to Inguinal Hernia Repair
 • Paralleling scrotal orchidopexy, a scrotal approach (a median raphe incision for bilateral hernias) ligates
   the processus at the external ring. Early data show similar success and morbidity with a faster operation
   and cosmetic benefit, but the level of ligation and the effect of age need long-term follow-up.

Laparoscopic Inguinal Hernia Repair
 • Two techniques: intraperitoneal peritoneal closure of the defect and an extraperitoneal approach (of
   growing interest). Success and complication rates now approach or equal open repair, the convergence
   explained by experience and technical refinement.
 • Pooled comparisons against open repair: earlier meta-analysis — a trend to higher recurrence, longer
   unilateral and shorter bilateral operating times, and significantly fewer contralateral metachronous
   hernias; later — similar recurrence, fewer ascended testes and metachronous hernias, but more wound
   infection; the most recent — shorter operative times for unilateral and bilateral hernias (longer for
   unilateral hernias in girls), similar recurrence and fewer complications.
 • Surgeon preference remains open repair: in a European survey, mostly of senior and experienced
   laparoscopic surgeons, most favoured open repair for its lower recurrence and lower risk of injury to
   adjacent structures; the minority preferring laparoscopy cited fewer metachronous contralateral hernias,
   cosmesis, ease, less vas/vessel injury and less pain, and few would use it for incarceration.
 • Miniaturisation: two-port and single-port techniques give similar outcomes; in infants of 5 kg or less
   microlaparoscopic repair (2-mm instruments) had low recurrence (higher with American Society of
   Anesthesiologists (ASA) score ≥ 3), no atrophy, but some high testes needing surgery. Division of the sac
   distal to the ring with a non-absorbable purse-string works in infants under 1 year, whose recurrence rate
   is equal to or lower than older children's.

Assessment of the Contralateral Internal Ring
 • Whether to assess the contralateral processus in unilateral hernia remains controversial. The aim is to
   avoid metachronous hernia and its risks and costs. Routine contralateral exploration gave way to
   selective exploration in high-risk groups (young age, prematurity, sex, associated disease); surveys show
   many surgeons still explore premature infants and those under 2 years.
 • Laparoscopy — via the umbilical port when a laparoscopic repair is planned, or transinguinally through
   the sac at open repair — gives a rapid, direct and accurate view of the contralateral ring, with sensitivity
   and specificity above 99% on meta-analysis. Bubbles of air escaping through the ring indicate patency.
 • Contralateral patency is inversely related to age — about 40% in infants, 30% in toddlers and 20% after 10
   years, similar to the 20% found at adult autopsy — and is found more often at open exploration than at
   laparoscopy.
 • Metachronous hernia is uncommon: about 7% when contralateral status is unknown (90% of them
   within 5 years), and lower still when laparoscopy shows a closed ring; closing a patent contralateral
   processus significantly reduces metachronous hernia.
 • These data are indirect: the natural history of a known open ring remains unknown until a prospective

Abdominoscrotal Hydrocele
 • Uncommon (about 1% of hydroceles) and bilateral in about 30%. A ballotable scrotal mass extending
   into the abdomen, palpable there when large; ultrasound defines the proximal extent. It presents in
   infancy, either as such or as a scrotal hydrocele that enlarges, improves or resolves. Associated:
   cryptorchidism, contralateral hernia or hydrocele, vanishing testis.
 • Mechanism: a scrotal hydrocele that enlarges into the retroperitoneal or properitoneal space after
   closure of the processus; the fluid is exudative, and rising size and pressure may cause lymphatic
   obstruction and progression. Massive extension can cause hydroureteronephrosis, lower-limb oedema
   or appendicitis. Dysmorphic elongation of the testis occurs but is usually reversible.
 • Surgery: traditionally inguinal, dissecting the sac from its abdominal attachments and mobilising it
   distally, ± orchidopexy to prevent iatrogenic cryptorchidism; aspirating the scrotal component eases
   proximal dissection. Alternatives: midline abdominal approach for large bilateral cases, or laparoscopic
   decompression followed by inguinal excision. A strip of sac may be left on the cord to protect cord and
   vas. Tightening the internal ring is unnecessary — the processus is invariably closed. A primary scrotal
   approach (drainage, excision, extensive plication with limited dissection) had no recurrences and fewer
   complications than inguinal repair in a comparative series.

 KEY POINTS: HERNIAS AND HYDROCELES
   • Inguinal hernias occur in 1–5% of children, commoner in boys and premature infants; right-sided (3:1)
     from later right descent; bilaterality risk with female sex, prematurity, age under 1 year and
     cryptorchidism.
   • An intermittent bulge distinguishes reducible hernia and communicating hydrocele from scrotal or
     cord hydrocele; home photographs are reliable.
   • Repair hernias soon after diagnosis; observe hydroceles, which often resolve.
   • Repair may be inguinal, scrotal or laparoscopic; the inguinal approach currently has the highest
     success.
   • Recurrence (0.5–1%) follows unidentified or unligated sac, torn sac, damaged floor or missed direct
     hernia.
   • Diagnostic laparoscopy gives rapid, direct and accurate inspection of the contralateral ring.

Concept — The abdominoscrotal hydrocele is a closed-processus hydrocele that has expanded upwards under
pressure: being non-communicating, it needs excision or drainage rather than high ligation, and its mass
effect explains its complications.

ACUTE SCROTUM
 • Acute scrotum is new-onset pain, swelling and/or tenderness of the intrascrotal contents. The
   differential is limited but signs overlap; some clinical features are reliable, and scrotal imaging helps.
 • Frequency: torsion of the appendix testis is commonest (40–60%), then spermatic cord torsion (20–30%,
   excluding neonates), epididymitis (5–15%), and other or no pathology (about 10%).
 • Age pattern: appendage torsion is typical after infancy and before puberty; epididymitis and cord torsion
   peak in the perinatal and pubertal periods.
 • Appendage torsion and epididymitis are managed conservatively with limited consequence; testicular
   torsion needs prompt exploration because the gonad is at risk of ischaemic loss, especially with delay in
   presentation, evaluation or treatment.
 • Delay in presentation stems from embarrassment or denial and from poor public awareness: adolescents
   and parents are often unaware of torsion, adolescents fear raising false alarms and families fear
   burdening services; most parents have never discussed it with their child, and few learned of it from their
   paediatrician. A short electronic module for clinicians improved confidence, and a public educational
   video was produced.

Spermatic Cord Torsion
Acute Intravaginal Spermatic Cord Torsion
Predisposing factors.
 • Intravaginal torsion is attributed to excess testicular mobility in a "bell-clapper" deformity, in which the
   tunica vaginalis attaches abnormally high on the cord. The deformity is found in about 12% of males at
   autopsy, yet torsion is far rarer (about 8.6 per 100,000 males aged 10–19 per year).
 • There is a familial predisposition (mode of transmission unknown), including multigenerational families.
 • The trigger is unknown; suggested are cold or a change in temperature activating the cremasteric reflex
   (even in tropical countries) and rapid pubertal testicular growth — yet torsion also occurs at rest or
   during sleep.
 • Cryptorchid testes are at increased risk and are hard to assess because of their position. Torsion after
   orchidopexy is attributed to failure of the fixing suture, absorbable or non-absorbable.
Clinical presentation.
 • Torsion can occur at any age, but most cases are after 10 years, peaking at 12–16 years. Its prevalence is
   1 in 4000, with left-sided predominance and rare bilaterality.
 • Classically there is acute, severe scrotal pain at rest (even during sleep), with activity or after trauma,
   sometimes with previous episodes. Pain may instead be milder, less acute, absent, or felt in the groin or


                                                                page 26 of 38
Chapter 55 — Abnormalities of the External Genitalia in Males                                       ACUTE SCROTUM

   abdomen. Nausea and vomiting occur in 10–60%. Oedema and erythema depend on duration and
   degree; dysuria and fever are uncommon.
 • Commonest findings: generalised testicular tenderness, abnormal testicular orientation (high-riding
   from a shortened cord; horizontal lie) and an absent cremasteric reflex.
 • The cremasteric (genitofemoral) reflex, normally present after age 2 years, is elicited by stroking the
   inner thigh. It is absent in torsion in some series but intact in up to 10% of proven torsions in others: its
   presence correlates with intact flow but does not exclude torsion, particularly when the presentation is
   otherwise suggestive.
 • Anterior epididymis, cord thickening, testicular induration, loss of the testis–epididymis boundary,
   oedema and erythema may be present, but landmarks disappear and examination becomes less reliable
   as duration increases.
 • Clinical tools: on multivariate analysis absent cremasteric reflex, nausea/vomiting and scrotal skin
   changes predict torsion. A decision tool found no torsion in patients with acute pain (under 72 hours)
   who had a normal testicular lie, no nausea or vomiting, and age 0–10 years (complete sensitivity and
   negative predictive value). The TWIST score stratifies risk using these factors and has been validated, and
   a standardised scoring pathway shortened the time from emergency department or ultrasound to
   theatre.
Diagnostic studies.
 • Urinalysis has limited value in torsion but identifies pyuria/bacteriuria (epididymitis) or haematuria
   (calculus).
 • Before rapid imaging, immediate exploration was routine. Radionuclide imaging (about 90% sensitive
   and specific) is slow, not readily available and uses ionising radiation — now rarely used. Ultrasound is
   rapid, available and safe, showing architecture, parenchymal flow and details such as hydrocele and wall
   thickening.
 • CDUS findings of torsion: reduced or absent colour flow or waveforms, and parenchymal heterogeneity
   compared with the other testis. Heterogeneous echotexture indicates necrosis, whereas homogeneous
   echotexture predicts a lower risk of orchidectomy.
 • Absent intratesticular flow is highly specific for torsion (classic series: sensitive, 100% specific, 97%
   accurate). In a blinded prospective study CDUS had 100% negative predictive value, leading its authors to
   conclude routine exploration is unnecessary when perfusion is normal — but other series report
   sensitivity of only 63–90%, because newer equipment detects residual flow in partial torsion and because
   the test is operator-dependent.
 • Supportive Doppler signs: subtle waveform abnormalities — increased or decreased amplitude and
   reversal of diastolic flow — and an enlarged, altered-echogenicity epididymis with absent, reduced or
   occasionally increased flow.
 • High-resolution ultrasonography (HRUS, 10–20 MHz) of the whole cord: the "snail" and "whirlpool"
   signs of a twisted cord are reliable; a cord twist was seen in almost all surgically proven torsions, and a
   linear cord was highly specific against torsion — but partial or intermittent torsion can show normal
   flow and no visible twist.
 • Emerging modalities: contrast-enhanced pulse-inversion ultrasonography (CEUS) — superior to CDUS for
   indeterminate testicular lesions; infrared thermography (scrotal cooling after torsion in animals); trans-

Prognosis
 • The effect on fertility is poorly understood, but subtle semen abnormalities are common. Sperm density
   is often normal, and correlates with shorter torsion and less atrophy. The inverse relation of duration to
   semen quality, and contralateral biopsy data, suggest global testicular dysfunction after torsion.
 • The autoimmune (antisperm antibody) hypothesis has been dispelled; animal and human data support
   ischaemia–reperfusion injury. Years after torsion, FSH, LH and testosterone are normal but inhibin B is
   reduced in both detorsed and orchidectomised patients, with no difference between them. More long-
   term data are needed, including on adjunctive treatments.

Intermittent Intravaginal Spermatic Cord Torsion
 • Self-limited episodes of acute scrotal pain precede acute torsion in 30–50% of patients. Episodes, single
   or multiple, begin and resolve abruptly over minutes to hours, and their frequency correlates with
   eventual torsion and testicular loss.
 • Nausea, vomiting or swelling may be absent. Findings depend on whether the testis is twisted at the time;
   a normal vertical lie is commonest (horizontal lie possible), and in series the cremasteric reflex was
   usually present and CDUS flow usually normal. A whirlpool sign, an abnormal boggy cord, or a
   pseudomass below the twist may indicate intermittent torsion.
 • Diagnosis needs a high index of suspicion unless the testis is seen to untwist, or CDUS shows absent/
   reduced flow before and normal/increased flow after marked relief of symptoms.
 • Once confirmed or highly suspected, elective bilateral orchidopexy is indicated to avert torsion and loss.
   Patients and parents should know that absolute confirmation may be impossible and symptoms may
   persist.

Extravaginal Spermatic Cord Torsion (Perinatal Testicular Torsion)
 • Perinatal torsion applies to infants whether the event is prenatal (hours to months before birth), during
   delivery or postpartum. The whole cord twists before the tunica vaginalis and dartos are fixed within
   the scrotum (extravaginal).
 • It usually occurs well before delivery, producing a "vanishing" testis or a haemosiderin-containing
   nubbin in the scrotum or, less often, the canal. Loss near delivery gives a hard, painless testis fixed to
   erythematous or dark scrotal skin, ± oedema; a coexisting hydrocele complicates examination.
 • Incidence about 6.1 per 100,000 births; familial cases occur. High birth weight and difficult delivery are
   suggested but unconfirmed. In a minority, symptoms follow a documented normal examination. Rarely,
   neonatal intravaginal torsion or infarction without torsion occurs.
 • Bilateral torsion occurs in a minority (5–22%), synchronous or metachronous — even when the first
   event was prenatal.

Epididymitis
 • A broad category, infectious or non-infectious; its incidence is poorly defined, partly because a torsed
   appendage may be misdiagnosed as epididymitis. Prevalence peaks in infancy and at puberty; in a long
   clinic series most cases were pubertal (11–14 years), few under 2 years, and about a quarter had a
   second episode.
 • Onset is classically more insidious than cord or appendage torsion but may be rapid. Fever and dysuria
   are variable; nausea is rare. Look for a history of UTI, urethral discharge, sexual activity, intermittent
   catheterisation, dysfunctional voiding, urethral abnormality or ejaculatory duct anomaly.
 • Examination ranges from localised epididymal enlargement and tenderness to a massively swollen,
   erythematous hemiscrotum without landmarks. The cremasteric reflex should be intact. Pyuria and/or
   bacteriuria in 20–40%. CDUS shows increased epididymal size and flow, sometimes testicular too.
 • Management aims to relieve inflammation and treat infection: ice, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory
   drugs (NSAIDs), scrotal elevation and rest. Without UTI, symptoms resolve without antibiotics. With
   pyuria, give broad-spectrum antibiotics with Gram-negative cover, intravenously with admission if
   needed; consider sexually transmitted disease treatment in adolescents.
 • Imaging is now selective. Early series found many anatomical anomalies (urethral anomalies, ureteral
   ectopia) in prepubertal boys; later series found a low risk except in infants, with anomalies commoner on
   VCUG in culture-positive patients. Some advise renal ultrasound only, or imaging only for recurrence. In a
   prepubertal boy with a positive urine culture, renal ultrasound and VCUG are indicated. Findings such as
   pyelectasis or low-grade VUR may be incidental, but urethral or ureteral obstruction and ejaculatory duct
   or vasal anomalies matter.
 • Recurrent epididymitis: endoscopy may reveal ejaculatory duct anomalies missed on VCUG; recurrent
   epididymo-orchitis may need excision of a utricle or ejaculatory duct cyst with vasovasostomy or
   vasectomy.

VARICOCELE
 • Varicocele — abnormal dilatation and tortuosity of the internal spermatic veins of the pampiniform
   plexus — is common in adolescents and may contribute significantly to adult subfertility. Its effect on
   paternity is less clear: most men with varicocele in population studies have fathered children.

Epidemiology and Pathogenesis
 • Prevalence is 8–16% in adolescents, similar to about 15% in adults; since varicoceles very rarely resolve
   and rarely appear in older adults, the adolescent and adult groups are presumed to be the same
   population.
 • Most appear after 10 years, progress through puberty, and peak at Tanner stage 3. Historically left-sided,
   concurrent right-sided varicoceles are increasingly recognised: bilateral repairs were done in a third of
   one young series, and CDUS shows subclinical right or bilateral reflux in many boys with left varicoceles —
   of unclear clinical significance. In adults, bilaterality is 15–50%.
 • A palpable isolated right varicocele warrants abdominal and pelvic ultrasound for a mass obstructing
   drainage to the vena cava.



                                                                page 32 of 38
Chapter 55 — Abnormalities of the External Genitalia in Males                                                              VARICOCELE

 • Pathophysiology is multifactorial: the primary factors are raised pressure in the left renal vein, collateral
   venous anastomoses, and incompetent valves at the junction of the left internal spermatic vein with
   the left renal vein. Progression may relate to continuous or spontaneous (not Valsalva-induced) reflux.
   The "nutcracker phenomenon" — compression of the left renal vein between the aorta and superior
   mesenteric artery — explains some cases.
 • A generalised venous abnormality is suggested by abnormal prostatic plexus flow in bilateral varicoceles
   and by associations with saphenofemoral incompetence and varicose veins. Venography shows that
   adolescents more often have congenital abnormalities (fewer competent valves, nutcracker
   phenomenon), whereas adult reflux arises through collateral pathways.
 • Genetics: varicocele is 4–8 times commoner in first-degree relatives, especially brothers.
 • A tall, thin habitus (low body mass index, BMI) is associated internationally — perhaps from a longer
   spermatic vein and higher hydrostatic pressure, or simply easier detection on examination.

Diagnosis and Classification
 • Most are found incidentally by a primary care practitioner or on self-examination; pain is an uncommon
   presentation (2–11%).
 • Examine supine and standing; inspect for visible swelling, then palpate the cord at rest and with Valsalva.

Associated Pathological Processes
 • There is abundant evidence that varicocele can impair testicular growth, spermatogenesis and fertility
   potential. The injury is presumed to be thermal (raised scrotal temperature), but the pathogenesis is
   poorly understood.

Testicular Hypotrophy
 • Without semen analysis in most adolescents, testicular hypotrophy is used as the surrogate of pathology
   and marker of surgical success. Reduced volume is long associated with varicocele and rises with grade in
   some studies but not others.
 • Ipsilateral volume loss may improve or resolve after repair, taken to mean that hypotrophy reflects real
   injury. The effect may be bilateral: the right testis is smaller in boys with grade 3 left varicoceles and
   grows after left repair, and bilaterally small testes are associated with lower sperm density.
 • "Significant hypotrophy" is variably defined — a 10%, 15% or 20%, or 2–3 mL, difference — and its
   reported frequency varies with measurement method, referral pattern and formula. Pubertal growth can
   be rapid and asynchronous (age 10–19, by Tanner stage), so serial measurements over years define
   hypotrophy better than a single one, especially in early puberty.
 • "Catch-up" growth — normalisation of left relative to right size — follows repair in a third to four-fifths
   of boys, but the inference that this reflects improved function is challenged: the differential may improve
   spontaneously without treatment, while in others it worsens. Catch-up does not depend on lymphatic or
   arterial preservation; repair enlarges small but not normal testes. Postoperative left hypertrophy (a left
   testis over 10% larger) is attributed to non-lymphatic-sparing repair or a rebound in spermatogenesis.
 • A significant left–right discrepancy remains the main indication for repair when semen analysis is not
   obtained — but many boys without varicocele have a smaller left testis, and the link between
   discrepancy and function is unproven; randomised prospective studies are needed.

Testicular Histology
 • In adults histology ranges from normal (even with severe hypospermatogenesis) to degenerative tubular
   changes, altered Leydig-cell number and vascular proliferation. Adolescent data are scant; abnormal
   tubular maturation and Leydig-cell numbers at biopsy did not predict adult sperm quality.

Hormonal Function
 • Hormonal profiles are inconclusive. Basal LH and FSH are not consistently different from controls;
   stimulated levels do not improve after surgery; an exaggerated GnRH response in some patients does not
   correlate with hypotrophy; inhibin B does not consistently correlate with size or semen.
 • More recently, total motile sperm count (TMC; normal above 9 million per ejaculate) rose with total
   testicular volume and inhibin B and fell with FSH, and cut-offs were proposed as surrogates for TMC in
   boys deferring semen analysis; a higher reflux grade was associated with hypotrophy and higher FSH.

Semen Quality
 • Semen quality is the most useful indicator of fertility potential, despite collection and interpretation
   difficulties. Quality improves with testicular growth and maturation, so analysis is best obtained once
   testicular growth stabilises. Ejaculation begins at a bone age of about 13 years and normospermia is
   reached by a bone age of about 17; LH and FSH fall as normospermia is attained, though some Tanner 5
   boys remain asthenozoospermic.
 • No age- or Tanner-based norms exist, so studies use adult World Health Organization criteria, usually on
   at least two samples; a single analysis may suffice because TMC is reproducible.
 • In randomised studies of treated versus untreated boys, semen quality was similar except for a
   postoperative rise in sperm concentration (a presumed rebound). Total testicular volume predicts TMC;
   in one series the differential did not, but in another a differential above 20% doubled, and a total volume
   under 30 mL quadrupled, the odds of a low TMC.
 • Grade and postoperative catch-up growth do not reliably predict ultimate semen quality; poorer sperm
   quality may be confined to a subset of boys with varicocele.

Intratesticular Varicocele
 • In 1–2% of adolescents the varicocele has an intratesticular component on CDUS: anechoic structures
   over 2 mm near the mediastinum testis with venous flow that increases on Valsalva. Asymmetry
   worsened without treatment and responded to repair, so surgery is indicated when an intratesticular
   varicocele coexists with testicular asymmetry.
Concept — Every adolescent varicocele marker is a surrogate: volume, grade, hormones and histology are
used because semen is unavailable, and none reliably predicts adult semen quality — which is why
observation, not repair, is the default.

Treatment
 • Observation is the approach for most adolescents until a surgical indication appears.
 • Indications for surgery: significant left (> 20%) or bilateral testicular hypotrophy, pain, or abnormal
   semen analysis — the last most reliable at Tanner 5 and/or age 18 or older. Pain is a rare indication (2–
   10%) and is relieved in most (68–88%).
 • Practice varies widely in indications, approach, follow-up and use of semen analysis; few urologists order
   semen analysis routinely, and many are uncomfortable raising it.

Surgical Repair of Varicocele
 • Options: inguinal or subinguinal, retroperitoneal (open or laparoscopic), or venographic. The decision
   turns on (1) whether to spare the testicular artery and/or lymphatics, and (2) the resulting rates of
   recurrence and hydrocele.
 • Long-term data are lacking: follow-up is often too short to capture recurrence or hydrocele, which may
   take 2 years or more to appear; many hydroceles resolve, but up to half need scrotal repair or
   aspiration. Artery-sparing and artery-ligating laparoscopic techniques improve semen quality equally.

Subinguinal or Inguinal Microsurgical Varicocelectomy
 • The subinguinal microscopic approach makes artery and lymphatic sparing easier, with high success and
   low hydrocele risk, but is time-consuming and needs microsurgical skill; the technique mirrors the adult
   procedure (Chapter 70). Loupe magnification gives inferior results to the operating microscope. It is
   associated, rarely, with testicular atrophy (necrosis), not reported after suprainguinal repair.

Retroperitoneal and Laparoscopic Varicocelectomy
 • Ligation of the internal spermatic vessels above the internal ring, open or laparoscopic, is simple and
   highly successful but has higher hydrocele rates. Mass ligation (Palomo) is highly successful and efficient.
 • Lymphatic- and/or artery-sparing techniques, though harder, reduce hydrocele but may lower success,
   and do not improve catch-up growth; artery sparing was associated with better semen quality in one
   series, but no randomised data define its effect on testicular function.
 • Atrophy risk: in theory after artery ligation in a boy with prior inguinal surgery (though not seen after
   laparoscopic Palomo in a small series), and after a non-artery-sparing repair followed by surgery that
   compromises the vasal artery or vein (hernia repair, vasectomy).

   the peritoneum medially → expose and isolate the spermatic vessel bundle → clip or ligate, ± divide. A
   microscopic lymphatic-sparing modification through this incision had no recurrences and was faster and
   more reliable than a subinguinal approach.

 KEY POINTS: VARICOCELE
   • Prevalence is similar in adolescents and adults — about 15%.
   • Tall, thin boys more often have varicoceles, and a genetic predisposition is implicated.
   • Varicoceles are commonly found on examination and least commonly because of pain.
   • Testicular volume is best measured by ultrasound.
   • Hypotrophy of 15–20% may accompany an ipsilateral varicocele; it commonly resolves after repair but
     can resolve spontaneously.
   • Poorer sperm quality may be limited to a subset; grade and catch-up growth do not reliably predict
     semen quality.
   • Success rates are similar for retroperitoneal (open or laparoscopic) and subinguinal approaches;
     lymphatic-sparing techniques have the lowest hydrocele rates, and most hydroceles that form need
     surgery.

Concept — Every varicocele technique trades hydrocele against recurrence: mass ligation buys certainty at
the cost of the lymphatics, sparing techniques protect the lymphatics at the risk of missed veins, and
microscopic magnification is how the subinguinal approach achieves both.

 • Congenital anomalies of the accessory testicular structures often accompany disorders of testicular
   descent and genital development, but may be isolated or part of syndromes that spare the genitalia.
 • Epididymal cysts are simple cysts, palpable or found incidentally on ultrasound; they are common and
   rise in frequency with age (about a third of scanned boys over 15). They are clinically indistinguishable
   from spermatoceles, except that spermatoceles are postpubertal and contain sperm. Their cause is
   unknown but may be hormonal — they are linked to diethylstilbestrol (DES) exposure. They differ on
   ultrasound and pathology from the multicystic solid epididymal cystadenomas of von Hippel–Lindau
   disease. Many resolve spontaneously; surgery is rarely needed.
