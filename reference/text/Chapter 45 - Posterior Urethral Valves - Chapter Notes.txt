Chapter 45 — Posterior Urethral Valves
Aseem Ravindra Shukla and Arun K. Srinivasan

Posterior urethral valves (PUV) are the commonest cause of bladder outlet obstruction in infant males. The
condition is readily diagnosed and treated by a straightforward initial surgical intervention, yet confers
profound long-term consequences. Obstruction is suspected antenatally in most, and pathognomonic
postnatal imaging confirms it. Endoscopic ablation or urinary diversion relieves the obstruction, but the
embryological insult to bladder and kidney is not relieved, and these infants require extended joint care
from urologists and nephrologists.

Description
 • Morgagni recognised a valve-like posterior urethral obstruction in 1769, confirmed by Langenbeck in
   1802 — both from post-mortem dissection.
 • Hugh Hampton Young gave the first endoscopic diagnosis and coined the term "posterior urethral
   valves" (1919).
 • Randall performed the first endoscopic resection of valves in 1920 (published 1921). His description still
   matches the modern endoscopic view: markedly dilated prostatic urethra, raised vesical neck, relaxed
   bladder orifice, deep pittings in either lateral prostatic urethral wall, and a fine frenulum extending
   distally from the verumontanum for about 1 cm, dividing into a valve on either side that rises from floor
   to side wall.

Genetic Basis of Posterior Urethral Valves
 • The majority of cases are sporadic, but sibling occurrence means inheritance may be autosomal
   recessive, X-linked recessive, or multifactorial.
 • Chromosome 11 is the recurring locus — affected siblings shared a partial duplication of the short arm of
   chromosome 11; chromosome 11 with chromosome 1 implicated elsewhere.
 • Copy number variants (CNVs) identify candidate gene regions in the majority of isolated PUV cases
   examined, and CNV burden carries phenotypic weight — a higher nadir creatinine and greater likelihood
   of progression to stage V chronic kidney disease (CKD).
 • Whole-genome sequencing maps PUV loci to TBX5 (transcription factor) and PTK7 (planar cell polarity
   gene) — both encoding proteins detectable in the developing human urinary tract.
Concept — the genetics remain descriptive rather than diagnostic, but the copy number variant work matters
clinically because it links genotype to renal prognosis rather than to the presence of the valve.

EPIDEMIOLOGY
 • Congenital urinary tract anomalies affect up to 1 in 500 pregnancies, and obstructive uropathy accounts
   for most of them.
 • Lower urinary tract obstruction (LUTO) has an incidence of 2.2 per 10,000 births, of which PUV is the
   commonest pathology at 1.4 per 10,000, followed by urethral atresia, then prune-belly syndrome.
 • Prevalence ratio of PUV is 2.10 per 10,000 births — defined as affected births divided by total live births
   plus stillbirths, a denominator chosen because birth incidence depends on the unreliable counting of
   early spontaneous pregnancy loss.
 • Prevalence is significantly higher in Black and minority ethnic groups than in white Europeans.
Concept — incidence and prevalence are not interchangeable here: the prevalence ratio exists precisely
because antenatal demise makes true incidence uncountable, and quoting either figure without its
denominator is the error.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
Bladder
 • Detrusor contractility is reduced in PUV bladders, on both electrical field stimulation and carbachol
   stimulation of bladder strips, attributed to replacement of detrusor smooth muscle by fibrosis.
 • The obstruction produces the triad seen on voiding cystourethrography (VCUG): dilatation of the
   posterior urethra, hypertrophy of the bladder neck, and flattening of the verumontanum with dilated
   ejaculatory ducts — pathognomonic, and these changes largely regress after valve ablation.

Valve Bladder Syndrome — the vicious cycle
 • Mitchell coined the term in 1982, observing that bladder filling and emptying were intricately related to
   the degree of renal pelviectasis and to overall renal function.
 • The cycle, as a sequence: fetal obstruction → bladder hypertrophy → high voiding pressures maintain
   emptying (compensated phase) → progressive wall remodelling and fibrosis with less efficient
   contraction → falling voiding pressure and rising post-void residual (PVR) → urine volume rises (normal
   growth, latent polyuria of dysplasia, raised glomerular pressure from poor emptying) → urine stored in
   the upper tracts → that bolus refills the partially emptied bladder → the bladder is never empty for any
   sustained period → decompensation → greater PVR, overflow incontinence, further renal damage.
 • Incontinence in a boy with PUV after a period of normal voiding is a red flag, not a continence problem
   — One series found a higher incidence of renal deterioration in boys presenting with incontinence after
   age 5 even when infant renal function had been normal.
Concept — the valve bladder is a compensation failure, not a persistent obstruction: the same high-pressure
hypertrophy that protects emptying in infancy is what fibroses the detrusor and drives decompensation later,
so relief of the outlet does not close the cycle.

Upper Urinary Tract
 • Sustained elevation of intravesical storage pressure over prolonged intervals is transmitted to the
   ureter, renal pelvis and ultimately the glomerulus, producing architectural and functional change at
   every ascending level.
 • Severe hydroureteronephrosis is the clinical marker of that transmission; increased echogenicity,
   parenchymal thinning with cortical cysts, and loss of corticomedullary differentiation imply significant
   renal dysplasia.
 • Ureteric dilatation has three contributors: direct pressure transmission from the dysfunctional bladder,
   vesicoureteric reflux (VUR) — present in up to 70% of PUV patients — and polyuria from progressive
   renal damage and congenital dysplasia.
 • Chronic ureteric dilatation causes wall thickening, loss of peristalsis and loss of mucosal coaptation,
   raising the risk of urinary stasis, infection and further pressure rise in the renal units.

Renal Dysfunction
 • Two separate but interrelated aetiologies: obstructive uropathy from the valves, and pre-existing renal
   dysplasia.

Evidence for the obstructive component
 • Fetal sheep ureteric obstruction models: hydronephrosis develops rapidly, but irreversible dysplastic
   architectural change is present by term.
 • Apoptosis and increased oxidative stress in murine obstructed kidneys may mediate dysplasia in the face
   of outlet obstruction.
 • Raised pressure damages renal tubular luminal cells → poorly concentrated urine; obstruction
   additionally reduces medullary blood flow → loss of the medullary concentration gradient → significant
   polyuria and the post-obstructive diuresis that follows catheter placement in infants with PUV.
Evidence for the dysplastic and renin–angiotensin component
 • Severe dysplasia correlates with decreased renin–angiotensin system activity and with angiotensin II
   type 1 receptor polymorphisms.
 • Plasma renin activity rises before the conventional markers of renal damage — before creatinine rise,
   renal scarring or reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR).
 • Angiotensin II damages the kidney twice over: haemodynamically, by altering glomerular flow, and
   profibrogenically, by inducing transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) and tumour necrosis factor-α
   (TNF-α).
 • TGF-β1 and TNF-α are candidate biomarkers: they fall as renal function improves after valve ablation
   and rise with worsening outcome.
 • Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) reduce fibrosis experimentally and reduce TGF-β1 and
   TNF-α expression clinically — a potential role in selected children with PUV.
 • ACE2 gene expression is significantly higher, and angiotensin II type 1 receptor gene expression lower,
   in PUV than in other renal dysplasias and in controls — supporting a congenital dysplastic, not purely
   obstructive, mechanism.
 • Countervailing finding: fetal sheep with partial bladder outlet obstruction show increased renin and
   angiotensin messenger RNA with upregulated AT2 receptor expression — the opposite direction.
Concept — the governing asymmetry is dysplasia versus obstruction: obstruction is relievable and its renal
injury partly reversible, dysplasia is fixed at birth and sets the ceiling on long-term function, and the
contradictory renin–angiotensin data exist because the two processes are being measured together in the
same kidney.

Vesicoureteral Reflux and Dysplasia
 • Hoover and Duckett observed that high-grade VUR in PUV is usually ipsilateral to a poorly functioning
   renal unit, with preserved function contralaterally, and hypothesised a "pop-off" mechanism — the
   dysplastic refluxing kidney acting as a pressure reservoir protecting the opposite kidney. They coined
   vesicoureteral reflux and dysplasia (VURD).
 • Longer follow-up has refuted the protective claim. In longer-term follow-up, most VURD children with a
   normal serum creatinine in the second year of life had lost that normal value by 8 to 10 years, and GFR
   was already abnormal in the majority within the first 2 years — creatinine had simply not yet declared it.
 • Children with valve bladder and a pop-off mechanism were more likely to have unsafe initial
   urodynamics, higher voiding pressures, voiding dysfunction and a requirement for clean intermittent
   catheterisation (CIC) than those without.
 • The contralateral, non-refluxing unit itself carries a high risk of congenital scarring.

 • VURD must never generate a false sense of security — follow-up is as close as for any other PUV cohort.
Concept — VURD is the classic false reassurance: the observation is real, the inference of contralateral
protection is wrong, and the reason is that a normal creatinine in a growing infant conceals an already
abnormal glomerular filtration rate.

 KEY POINTS
  • The renal dysfunction, vesicoureteric reflux and voiding dysfunction seen in children with PUV are
    mediated by a dysfunctional bladder.
  • Renal impairment in PUV is caused by coexisting renal dysplasia and obstructive uropathy.
  • VURD syndrome confers no protective benefit on long-term renal prognosis.


DIAGNOSIS
Antenatal Diagnosis
 • Antenatal detection continues to improve with better fetal ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging
   (MRI).
 • Detection prepares the team and the parents, but there is scant evidence that it improves outcomes for
   the child.
 • A meta-analysis found no demonstrable improvement in progression to CKD or renal replacement
   therapy attributable to antenatal diagnosis, the literature being limited by heterogeneous cohorts and
   significant bias.

Chapter 45 — Posterior Urethral Valves                                                                   Diagnosis



 • The counterintuitive result underscores the limits of current technology in both diagnosis and prediction
   of kidney outcome, and the need for standardised, multimodal antenatal care pathways.
Concept — antenatal diagnosis changes when the disease is known about, not what the kidney has already
become: the dysplastic injury is established in utero, so detection cannot be expected to alter renal destiny.

Ultrasonography
 • PUV is detected in approximately 1 in 1250 ultrasound screenings, accounts for 10% of significant
   antenatally detected genitourinary disease, and afflicts one third of surviving infants with bilateral renal
   disease.
 • Corroborating signs: oligohydramnios, and the dilated posterior urethra producing the "keyhole sign".
 • Increased renal echogenicity is a reliable indicator of renal damage, not merely of obstruction.
 • The mandate is nonetheless fixed: a thickened, enlarged bladder with bilateral ureterectasis and
   pelvicaliectasis, with or without oligohydramnios, in a male fetus requires early postnatal ultrasound
   and VCUG before discharge from the postpartum unit.
 • About half of PUV births are diagnosed antenatally; of the remainder, half present in the neonatal
   period and half with urinary tract infection (UTI).
Concept — antenatal ultrasound reliably detects lower urinary tract obstruction but poorly identifies its
cause, which is why the antenatal finding triggers a postnatal protocol rather than an antenatal diagnosis.

Fetal Magnetic Resonance Imaging
 • An adjunct, increasingly available at major centres, not a replacement.
 • Assesses the same three parameters as ultrasound: degree of urethral dilatation, bladder distension with
   wall thickening, and reduced amniotic fluid.
 • In early gestation it altered the initial sonographic diagnosis in a substantial minority of cases.

Postnatal Diagnosis
Ultrasonography
 • In the newborn, free fluid around one or both kidneys implies a urinoma.
 • Bladder wall is regarded as thickened at more than 3 mm when distended, or more than 5 mm when
   empty.
Voiding Cystourethrography
 • Timing sequence: renal and bladder sonography first → VCUG as soon as the infant is
   haemodynamically stabilised and able to undergo a contrast study → before discharge.
 • High-grade VUR is present in approximately half of patients at diagnosis.
 • The voiding-phase appearance is the pathognomonic sequence: contrast crosses a hypertrophied,
   elevated bladder neck → enters a grossly dilated posterior urethra → funnels abruptly at a transverse
   membrane or cusp, representing the obstructing leaflets seen at cystoscopy.
 • Technique, as a sequence: insert a 5- to 8-Fr feeding tube → if it curls in the capacious posterior urethra
   or at the hypertrophied bladder neck, use a coudé catheter to advance into the bladder → during the
   voiding phase, withdraw the catheter gradually to a point distal to the posterior urethra so that
   segment is seen unobstructed. The same withdrawal rule applies where a feeding tube is already in situ.
Concept — the whole diagnostic yield of the voiding cystourethrogram sits in the voiding phase at the point
of abrupt calibre change, which is why a catheter left across the posterior urethra produces a technically
complete but diagnostically worthless study.

Voiding Urosonography
 • Second-generation ultrasound contrast permits dynamic, radiation-free imaging of the lower urinary
   tract.
 • Evidence now supports ceVUS for urethral imaging — reliable diagnosis of PUV and of other urethral
   pathology such as urethral diverticulum.
 • Increasingly used as part of a concerted effort to reduce radiation exposure in children, and useful
   when performed by experienced radiologists.
Radionuclide Renal Scan
 • Its purpose is quantification of differential renal function, not diagnosis of the valve.
 • Cortical deficits on the scan may imply renal dysplasia.
 • Timing is dictated by tubular maturity: the study is performed at about 2 to 3 months of age, so the
   tubules can process the radionuclide accurately.
 • Mercaptoacetyltriglycine (MAG3) is the agent of choice for assessing functional contribution.
 • The trap is interpretive: delayed clearance of tracer from a dilated collecting system is readily misread
   as ureterovesical junction obstruction.
Laboratory Evaluation
 • Neonatal values reflect maternal biochemistry and must be interpreted with caution.
 • After 48 hours the placentally transmitted maternal contribution clears and the infant's own baseline
   renal function becomes apparent; subsequent values and trends are what infer effective renal function.
 • Nadir creatinine at 1 month, 6 months and 1 year is the key prognostic instrument, and may be plotted
   to assess the immediate response to treatment in the neonatal period.
 • The caveat is that the serum creatinine plateau may not be reached even in unaffected children until
   well into the first year — between roughly day 65 and day 220 of life.
Concept — every biochemical number in the newborn with valves is a moving target: the first 48 hours
belong to the mother, the plateau belongs to the second half of the first year, and only the nadir creatinine
trend carries prognostic weight.

  • Antenatal diagnosis is primarily by fetal ultrasound, sometimes aided by fetal MRI; a thickened dilated

CLINICAL PRESENTATION AND INITIAL MANAGEMENT
Antenatal Presentation and Management
 • A significant proportion of children with PUV now reach the urologist antenatally, for prenatal advice
   and management.
 • The three considerations that govern antenatal decision-making are the presence of oligohydramnios,
   the degree and laterality of hydronephrosis, and the gestational age.
Selection for antenatal intervention
 • Antenatal intervention was popularised in the mid-1980s, enabled by advances in fibreoptics and
   endoscopic miniaturisation.
 • Ultrasound remains the mainstay of antenatal diagnosis, but fetoscopy offers better sensitivity — at the
   cost of the considerable risks of intervention.
 • Historical criteria for offering intervention: oligohydramnios, a dilated bladder, and severe
   hydroureteronephrosis, without renal cortical cystic lesions, in a fetus with a normal karyotype.
 • The Society of Fetal Urology convened a multidisciplinary panel in 2016, proposing a standardised
   severity classification of LUTO.
 • A published stratification names the four features predicting the most favourable outcome from
   intervention: oligohydramnios but not anhydramnios; severe hydronephrosis; favourable fetal urine
   biochemistry; absence of renal cortical cysts.
 • The counterargument is for selective, cautious intervention: some children do well despite significant
   obstruction, and others are carried through the intrauterine period by intervention only to fare badly
   afterwards.

 • A single sample is inferior to serial sampling: triple serial sampling at 48- to 72-hour intervals is the
   more accurate proxy for irreversible renal damage, with sequential rises across samples portending an
   unfavourable prognosis.
 • Fetal urine biochemistry before 23 weeks significantly predicts renal outcome — β2-microglobulin the
   most sensitive marker; sodium and calcium the most specific but less sensitive.
 • Contrary evidence: a systematic review found that fetal urine indices did not accurately predict poor renal
   function. The results were not universally replicated.

Concept — the fetal urine argument rests on the dysplastic tubule's inability to reabsorb: low solute means a
kidney still working, and it is the trajectory across serial samples, not any single threshold, that carries the
prognosis.

Vesicoamniotic shunting
 • Shunting treats oligohydramnios, not the kidney — the potential benefit is ameliorative for pulmonary
   function, and it represents the first stage of fetal intervention; several hundred procedures are reported.
 • Fetal sheep models show that restoration of amniotic fluid volume prevents lung hypoplasia, though
   absence of controlled studies constrains conclusions.
 • A systematic review showed a survival advantage after shunting, but without randomised trials.
PLUTO — Percutaneous vesicoamniotic shunting versus conservative management for Lower Urinary Tract
Obstruction (2013)
 • Design: randomised comparison of vesicoamniotic shunting against conservative management in fetal
   LUTO.
 • Limited by poor recruitment and pregnancy terminations — only 12 live births in each arm.
 • Result: a trend toward improved survival at 28 days in the shunted group, but overall survival was very
   poor in both arms — only two infants survived to 2 years of age with normal renal function.
 • High mortality from pulmonary hypoplasia, and greater pregnancy loss in the shunt arm from
   procedure-related complications and early rupture of membranes.

Fetal cystoscopy and fetal surgery
 • No PLUTO-equivalent randomised evidence exists for the more complex interventions.
 • Technique, as a sequence: 1.0-mm fetoscope → antegrade transuterine percutaneous incision into the
   fetal bladder → entry into the posterior urethra through the bladder neck → perforation of the
   obstructing membrane with an Nd:YAG laser or cauterising wire.
 • A series of 14 fetal procedures — antenatal valve ablation, vesicoamniotic shunting, cutaneous
   ureterostomy and vesicostomy: fetal mortality 43%, six infants dying of premature delivery and
   respiratory failure, and most surviving children in renal failure at a mean of 11.6 years.

Parental counselling
 • A defined role for the paediatric urologist: educate parents on the putative diagnosis, possible
   outcomes, alternative diagnoses and the expected course of the remainder of the pregnancy.
 • Direct delivery to an institution equipped for immediate neonatal surgical management.
 • Frame the prenatal visit as the start of a lifelong relationship — continuity of appropriate expertise from
   infancy into adulthood.

 KEY POINTS
  • Up to 50% of infants with PUV are diagnosed antenatally.
  • Severity of obstruction is objectively assessed by amniotic fluid volume, degree of renal dysplasia,
    and fetal urinary markers.
  • Vesicoamniotic shunting may confer a survival benefit in selected cases, but no clear benefit in the
    risk of renal failure.

Postnatal Management
 • Serious comorbidities may dominate the presentation: pulmonary hypoplasia and the physical stigmata
   of oligohydramnios — Potter facies, club feet, deformed hands, poor visceral muscle tone — and may
   demand intensive initial management.
 • Clinically the infant may void with difficulty, with a weak or intermittent stream.
 • Technique for drainage, as a sequence: pass a 5- or 7-Fr feeding tube (or similar-calibre catheter) per
   urethra → if it curls in the dilated posterior urethra or is impeded by the hypertrophied, elevated
   bladder neck, use a coudé-tip catheter, or curl the feeding-tube tip dorsally over a stylet → apply
   posterior digital rectal pressure to guide the tube anteriorly through the dilated prostatic urethra and
   high bladder neck → confirm intravesical placement by bladder sonography or, in some cases, a one-
   shot cystogram.

 • Minimise balloon filling: in a small, hypertrophic bladder a filled balloon induces bladder spasm and may
   occlude the ureteric orifices.
 • Hyponatraemia may be present, sometimes triggered by UTI; it is readily corrected with sodium
   supplementation and is attributed to transient pseudohypoaldosteronism.
Concept — the first catheter is the whole of initial management, and every technical detail exists because the
anatomy that obstructs the valve also obstructs the catheter: the dilated posterior urethra that curls the tube
and the elevated bladder neck that deflects it.

Pulmonary Hypoplasia
 • Despite the urological focus on bladder and kidney, pulmonary hypoplasia is the most profound
   complication and the commonest cause of perinatal mortality in severe LUTO.
 • The cyanotic neonate requires complex ventilatory support, and this is typically the factor delaying
   definitive surgical treatment of the valves.
 • Aetiology is unclear and likely multifactorial, the proposed mechanism being a sequence:
   oligohydramnios plus grossly hydronephrotic kidneys and a dilated bladder → increased uterine
   pressure on the fetus → reduced diaphragmatic expansion → reduced lung volume and growth →
   impaired development of the fetal pulmonary tree, which requires intraluminal pressure, volume and
   flow, together with cellular signalling to the developing alveoli.
Urinomas
 • A urinoma complicates 3% to nearly 10% of PUV cases; detection rose to around 15% after the
   introduction of renal ultrasonography in 1979.
 • Two distinct extravasation patterns: forniceal rupture → fluid trapped within the renal capsule,
   appearing on ultrasound as distorted renal parenchyma; and transperitoneal transudation of fluid, or
   bladder rupture → neonatal ascites.
 • VCUG or radionuclide cystography may delineate the site of leakage in ascites, but the cause is often
   not determinable.

SURGICAL INTERVENTION
Valve Ablation
 • Cystoscopy with valve ablation is the preferred initial surgical option in any neonate diagnosed with
   PUV.
 • The treatment goal is to restore urethral urine flow and enable normal cyclic filling and emptying of the
   bladder — superior to urinary diversion with passive drainage.
 • Experimental support for cycling: a diversion-and-undiversion model showed the changes occurring in a
   bladder prevented from cycling, and a fetal sheep model developed increased expression of extracellular
   matrix elements and apoptosis after high diversion.
 • Where the infant is small, premature or of low birth weight, urethral calibre may preclude cystoscopy
   — primary vesicostomy is then an option; alternatively, sequential urethral dilatation to accommodate a
   cystoscope over a mean of about a fortnight has been described.

 • A urethral catheter is typically left for at least 24 hours and need not be kept longer, though longer
   catheterisation is common while renal or respiratory parameters are monitored in intensive care.
 • A VCUG must be repeated within 1 month of ablation to confirm that the valves are no longer visible.
 • Markers of adequate ablation: improvement in bladder wall thickness, reduced upper tract dilatation,
   and demonstrably reduced posterior urethral dilatation. Immediate signs of diminished bladder
   pressure, with some improvement in renal dilatation and in the volume of reflux, are common.
Concept — the operation is an incision, not a resection: the valve is an avascular leaflet, so the endpoint is

Bladder neck procedures
 • The rationale was anatomical: bladder neck hypertrophy with elevation of the bladder neck proximal to
   the posterior urethra, and the incomplete emptying persisting on imaging after ablation in some males,
   prompted interest in transurethral incision of the bladder neck during or after primary ablation.
 • Earliest reports were Y-V plasty and bladder neck incision; after Mitchell's elaboration of the valve
   bladder, bladder neck obstruction was widely accepted as a plausible explanation for persistent
   dysfunction.
 • Bladder neck incision is still offered by some, but adoption is limited by concerns of retrograde
   ejaculation and by lack of improvement against controls even in short-term pilot studies; the benefit
   demonstrated in neurogenic bladder has not transferred, and there is a dearth of studies confirming
   extended durability.
 • Botulinum toxin injection into the hypertrophied bladder neck failed to improve voiding dynamics.

Vesicostomy
 • It reduces bladder storage pressures and may optimise GFR.
 • The old objection — that vesicostomy defunctionalises the bladder and causes long-term loss of
   compliance — has been refuted: a properly created vesicostomy allows bladder filling and preserves
   contractile function, because urine must still be expelled through the stoma, albeit at a reduced leak
   point pressure.
 • It is a temporary diversion: it does not alter ultimate clinical outcomes compared with primary ablation,
   and does not prevent the bladder from serving as an adequate reservoir for renal transplantation.

 • Technique, as a sequence: 2-cm midline transverse incision midway between the pubic symphysis and

 • The key operative step is to keep the posterior bladder wall taut, achieved by bringing the dome to the

Upper Tract Diversion
 • The rationale: direct decompression of the kidney by cutaneous ureterostomy or pyelostomy gives low-
   pressure drainage, and was thought to protect the upper tract from ureterovesical junction obstruction
   caused by a tortuous intramural ureter traversing a thickened, valve-affected bladder. Loop diversion of
   ureter or renal pelvis still permits bladder filling and cycling, mitigating the defunctionalisation concern.
 • The refutation: long-term follow-up failed to detect any renal-protective benefit, and diversion often
   requires a complicated undiversion as the child matures.
 • The decisive observation: renal biopsies taken at the time of high diversion uniformly show renal
   dysplasia, while the diversion does not protect from renal failure — implying that dysplasia occurring in
   early fetal development, not neonatal surgical strategy, is the primary mediator of renal outcome.
 • Residual indication: a rare undertaking in an infant with complete lower tract decompression yet
   worsening renal function, increasing upper tract dilatation and possibly sepsis, undertaken after
   multispecialty consultation with paediatric nephrology and radiology, and with the explicit
   understanding that improved renal outcome is not supported by evidence.

 • Historical indication: the non-functioning refluxing renal unit of VURD syndrome, regarded as a source
   of infection and sepsis, warranting prophylactic excision.
 • Contemporary position: a focus on bladder emptying and circumcision has reduced UTI incidence
   sufficiently that nephroureterectomy is rarely considered.
 • Renal preservation is prioritised even for poorly functioning units contributing moderate polyuria —
   polyuria is preferable to anuria.
 • Where frequent UTIs localise to the non-functioning unit and mandate nephrectomy, preserve the
   ureter for potential later reconstruction such as ureteral augmentation.
Concept — the ureter of a dead kidney is a graft and its urine is a safety valve against anuria, which is why
removing the VURD unit costs more than it saves once infection is controlled by emptying and circumcision.

  • Cystoscopy with valve ablation is the preferred initial treatment for PUV.
  • Vesicostomy does not inhibit bladder cycling — the bladder continues to contract — but is reserved

  • High urinary diversion offers no renal protective benefit and requires a complex secondary procedure

  • Circumcision should be encouraged prophylactically in any male with PUV, and especially any male

MANAGEMENT OF VESICOURETERAL REFLUX
 • VUR is found on VCUG in 50–80% of infants worked up for PUV.
 • The governing principle is that reflux here is secondary — a consequence of obstruction and secondarily
   elevated bladder pressures — which is precisely why it should dissuade ureteral reimplantation in
   nearly all cases.
 • Ablation or vesicostomy alone resolves reflux in 25–40% of those refluxing beforehand.
 • Reflux resolves more readily at lower grades and when the associated kidney has adequate function.
 • The presence of reflux did not correlate with renal outcomes — reflux alone is not an indication for
   intervention.
 • The management target is the bladder, not the ureter: address persistent bladder outlet obstruction,
   reduce intravesical pressure with anticholinergic treatment, and treat the underlying bladder
   dysfunction; urodynamic evaluation may guide this.
 • Where recurrent UTI genuinely mandates intervention, bladder management must precede surgery.

 • Surgical risks are elevated in this population: higher rates of stricture and persistent reflux when dilated
   ureters are reimplanted into thick-walled bladders that have not been properly rehabilitated

 • Any treatment of reflux in a child with profound bowel dysfunction risks further deterioration of an

 • Endoscopic correction carries fewer risks of myogenic disruption and renal deterioration than

Concept — reflux in valves is a pressure gauge, not a lesion: correcting it surgically without first rehabilitating
the bladder treats the reading rather than the pressure, which is why reimplantation is rare and, when
unavoidable, follows bladder management rather than preceding it.

BLADDER DYSFUNCTION AND VALVE BLADDER SYNDROME
 • The bladder is the lifelong focus of management, having been exposed to obstruction from its earliest
   development.
 • The bladder initiates the cascade: voiding dysfunction, urinary reflux, and worsening of renal dysplasia
   and obstructive uropathy — with valve bladder syndrome as the ultimate manifestation.
 • Three sequential contractility patterns through childhood: detrusor hyperreflexia in infancy and early
   childhood → decreasing intravesical pressures and improved compliance in childhood → increased
   bladder capacity with hypocontractility and atony in adolescence and beyond.
 • An essential qualification: the patterns overlap in most children and are not arbitrary or destined —
   they are guideposts for monitoring, for choosing management, and for assessing the effect of
   intervention over the long term.
 • Continence is reached late: males with PUV achieved daytime and night-time continence at around 5.5
   years against 2.9 years in peers.
 • Clinical history grossly underestimates the problem: after endoscopic ablation, self-reported
   incontinence averaged about a fifth of patients, while urodynamically demonstrated bladder
   dysfunction was present in over half.
 • Uroflowmetry and post-void residual measurement should therefore be routine in follow-up of any
   toilet-trained child with a history of PUV, rather than relying on examination or questionnaires.

Bladder Management
 • Follow-up rests on observation, clinical history and urodynamics, with parent and child education as the
   critical determinant of success for any behavioural prescription.
 • Counsel families not to push toilet training aggressively, and to expect a lag behind the normal
   population.
 • Expected continence outcomes: daytime incontinence in 7–35%; nocturnal enuresis in about one in four
   children after valve ablation.
 • Once toilet training is achieved, the behavioural package is: adequate fluid intake, timed voiding, and
   double voiding; biofeedback therapy and home pelvic floor exercises are also useful.

Role of Urodynamics
 • Bladder contractility is a dynamic condition changing with age, so serial monitoring rather than a single
   study is the point.
 • Videourodynamics — fluoroscopy combined with standard urodynamics — is especially useful for
   assessing storage and emptying under dynamic conditions and for understanding the contribution of
   VUR to bladder function.
 • Urodynamics is most useful when the clinical scenario changes — worsening upper tract dilatation or
   the appearance of UTIs.
 • The counterweight is the known disadvantage of radiation exposure and infection.

Valve Bladder Syndrome
 • Mitchell coined the term in 1982 after reviewing 11 patients in whom hydroureteronephrosis and renal
   function continued to worsen despite no clinical evidence of residual bladder outlet obstruction.
 • The mechanism, as a sequence: initial compensation by high voiding pressures → rising urine volumes
   as the child grows, augmented by the polyuria of nephrogenic diabetes insipidus from evolving renal
   impairment → a bladder increasingly unable to empty completely → rising PVR → loss of any period of
   complete relaxation, with detrusor fibres continuously in partial or complete stretch → genotypic and
   phenotypic changes that further impair contractility → partial emptying immediately refilled by urine
   stored in the hydronephrotic kidneys → transmission of rising bladder pressure back to the kidneys →
   worsening renal function.

 • The three processes producing the valve bladder: polyuria, bladder insensitivity, and residual urine
   volume — all secondary to impaired bladder compliance creating high wall tension from increased
   collagen and matrix, which together sustain bladder overdistension.
 • Valve bladder syndrome is a continuum of bladder dysfunction, not a discrete endpoint, and devolution
   into it is neither unavoidable nor uniform — effective drainage of the overdistended bladder may alter
   the course and preserve function.
Treatment — the escalating ladder
 • Mainstay: monitoring of PVR, flow rates and voiding pressures, with timed voiding, double voiding, and
   anticholinergics or α-blockers.
 • CIC becomes necessary if residual volume rises despite anticholinergics, to arrest progression toward
   decompensation.
 • Overnight bladder drainage is an increasingly standard adjunct in children with classic evidence of a
   developing valve bladder.
    ◦ Trigger: ureteric dilatation and hydronephrosis unresponsive to behavioural modification, or
      worsening renal function or recurrent UTI.
    ◦ Method: a urinary catheter left in the bladder for a 7- to 10-hour period, giving extended bladder
      decompression so the kidneys drain without meeting the raised voiding or resting pressures they
      face by day.
    ◦ It interrupts the vicious cycle of bladder remodelling and its renal consequences. Significant
      improvement in hydronephrosis, continence and UTIs after overnight drainage, intermittent nocturnal
      catheterisation and/or nocturnal double voiding.
 • Where CIC or overnight drainage is difficult because of an elevated bladder neck or a sensate urethra,
   an appendicovesicostomy on the Mitrofanoff principle (1980) is the option. After Mitrofanoff for a valve
   bladder, pelvicaliectasis decreased and compliance improved, yet renal deterioration still developed in
   35%.
 • Laparoscopic and robot-assisted creation of the catheterisable channel is increasingly adopted.
 • Augmentation cystoplasty is now rarely used for a valve bladder, attributed to better understanding of
   bladder dysfunction, behavioural modification and timely overnight drainage.
    ◦ Indication: a small-capacity, high-pressure, thick-walled valve bladder with worsening upper tract
      anatomy refractory to conservative measures.
    ◦ Ureteral augmentation is preferred over ileal in PUV, avoiding the mucus production, acidosis and
      stones of ileal augmentation.
    ◦ The ideal anatomy is already present in many of these boys — severe ureteric dilatation or unilateral
      VURD — allowing the ureter to be detubularised and patched onto a bisected bladder without bowel
      manipulation.
    ◦ Lasting increases in capacity and reductions in filling pressure are reported at medium-term follow-up
      after ureteral augmentation.
Concept — every rung of valve bladder treatment buys the detrusor a period of genuine emptiness, from
double voiding through nocturnal catheter drainage to a catheterisable channel, because the injurious
variable is not pressure at a moment but the absence of any interval of relaxation.

PROGNOSTIC INDICATORS FOR RENAL FUNCTION
 • Despite advances in antenatal diagnosis and intervention and in rapid postnatal treatment, the lifetime
   prevalence of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in males with PUV is 20–50%.
 • The five recognised risk factors: age at diagnosis, renal dysplasia with or without VUR, nadir creatinine
   during the first year of life, recurrent UTI, and bladder dysfunction.

Nadir Creatinine
 • The 1-year value is more accurate as a predictive tool than the 1-month value.
 • Nadir creatinine and creatinine velocity remain the best predictors of long-term renal function, with
   urinary peptides as a promising future avenue.
 • Creatinine velocity — the rate of change of creatinine over the first 30 days of life — is itself a

Age at Presentation
 • The assumption that antenatal diagnosis would forestall renal injury has not been sustained.
 • Patients diagnosed in the pre-sonography era (before 1982) had a lower ESRD risk (16.8%) than those
   diagnosed in the post-sonography era (36.6%) — the paradox explained by survival bias: critically ill
   infants in the earlier era likely died before the diagnosis was complete, so modern survivors include a
   more severely affected cohort.
 • Poorer long-term renal outcome in those presenting before 1 year of age (41%) than after 1 year (15%)
   follows the same pattern.
 • The countervailing assumption — that late presenters have milder disease — is also not corroborated:
   some reports find delayed presentation associated with significantly higher risk of azotaemia, higher
   serum creatinine and poorer long-term renal outcome.
Concept — the two directions of the age-at-presentation literature cannot both be read at face value because
the denominator changed: earlier eras selected for survivors, and later series select for children whose
disease declared itself late for reasons that may be severity rather than mildness.

Quantifying Dysplasia Without Biopsy
 • Ultrasound features portending poor prognosis: hyperechogenic kidneys, cortical cystic change, and loss
   of corticomedullary differentiation.
 • Renal parenchymal area — the area of the kidney minus the area of the pelvicalyceal system on the first
   postnatal ultrasound — is the quantitative development of this: each 1-cm² increase was associated with
   lower ESRD risk in infants with a 1-month creatinine in the intermediate band of 0.8 to 1.1 mg/dL.
 • Parenchymal area differs significantly between children who progress to renal failure and those who do
   not (21.41 cm² vs 16 cm²), with a difference also in the corticomedullary differentiation index.
 • Deep learning applied to ultrasound anatomic features predicted CKD progression, and was more
   accurate combined with existing clinical models than either alone; machine learning may eventually
   allow office-based prognostic inference.
 • Other emerging predictors: fetal MRI, postnatal ultrasound findings, and genetic markers — all
   highlighting the need for tools incorporating genetic and biochemical markers.
 • The VCUG is being revisited for prognosis: bladder height-to-width ratio and posterior urethra height-
   to-width ratio, with early evidence that a higher posterior urethral height-to-width ratio correlates with
   higher nadir creatinine.

TRANSPLANTATION IN VALVE PATIENTS
 • ESRD prevalence in males with PUV is up to 50%; in the 2006 North American Pediatric Renal Trials and
   Collaborative Studies annual report, obstructive uropathy was the second commonest cause for
   transplantation, accounting for 15.8% of paediatric transplants since 1987.
 • These recipients are a particularly difficult cohort: likely comorbidities include high-grade VUR into
   native non-functioning kidneys and valve bladder syndrome with a thick-walled, poorly contractile or
   hypercontractile bladder.

 • The paediatric urologist must be a critical member of the transplant team and examine the prospective
   recipient as part of pretransplant evaluation, because complication and graft loss rates are significantly
   higher where bladder dysfunction is incompletely managed or the reservoir is not optimised.
Outcomes
 • The thickened PUV bladder wall contributes to a significantly increased incidence of ureteric
   obstruction after transplantation on both univariate and multivariate analysis, but recent studies show
   no increased risk of graft loss or patient death despite ureteric obstruction, stenting or dilatation.
 • Outcomes were similar whether initial management had been valve ablation, vesicostomy or
   supravesical diversion; bladder dysfunction increased graft failure risk but not significantly (8-year
   follow-up).
 • Graft survival in PUV is similar to other CKD groups, with pretransplant urodynamic assessment and
   bladder optimisation the requisite step.
 • Australian data: graft survival equivalent to other diagnoses until 10 years post-transplant, with
   increased graft loss thereafter compared with other congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract
   (CAKUT) — attributed to bladder dysfunction.
Pretransplant preparation
 • Videourodynamics should be obtained in every transplant candidate to determine safe storage
   pressures and contractile function of the future reservoir.
 • Overnight bladder drainage or CIC may be started before transplant to optimise the reservoir and
   establish the bladder management skills that graft success will require.
 • Pretransplant nephrectomy is rarely required — considered only where proteinuria or severe polyuria
   creates haemodynamic challenges.

    hypoplasia but confers no protection from renal impairment.

QUALITY OF LIFE WITH POSTERIOR URETHRAL VALVES
 • PUV is an illness with lifetime repercussions, and understanding long-term risk factors is necessary for
   counselling, preparing and treating these patients into adulthood.
 • Developmental neurocognitive disorders appear more common in children with PUV: a Finnish registry-
   based study found neurodevelopmental disorder in 15% of males with PUV, with nearly 9% carrying an
   attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder–attention-deficit disorder (ADHD-ADD) spectrum diagnosis —
   significantly increased against population controls.
 • Early evaluation and early intervention once cognitive assessment is possible is part of overall
   management.
 • Known associated risks of the valve bladder and of transplantation include erectile dysfunction and
   infertility.
 • Lower urinary tract symptoms affect adults with a valve history two to three times more often than the
   general population.
 • The apparent contradiction in the sexual-function data is one of subgroup: overall rates of erectile
   dysfunction and infertility in adult valve patients were no different from the general population, yet
   subgroup analysis of the same patients with urinary incontinence or renal insufficiency showed the
   greatest impairment of quality of life.
 • The chronicity of bladder dysfunction, UTI risk and the sequelae of renal dysfunction require
   communication between paediatric and adult urologists, and the adult urologist must be conversant
   with PUV pathophysiology to care for these patients after transition.
Concept — the population-level reassurance and the individual-level burden are not in conflict: adults with
valves do as well as anyone unless they carry incontinence or renal insufficiency, which is precisely why
transition of care is targeted at that subgroup rather than at the diagnosis.

ANTERIOR URETHRAL VALVES
 • The commonest congenital obstructive lesion of the anterior urethra, but 25 to 30 times less common
   than PUV.
 • Frequently associated with a large anterior urethral diverticulum, the lesion being variously described as
   obstruction by a wall of the diverticulum or as a semilunar fold draping down from the anterior urethral
   wall.
 • Embryology is unclear. Hypoplastic corpus spongiosum over the affected segment suggests either a
   place on the hypospadias spectrum or a faulty union between urethral mucosa and the epithelium of
   the fossa navicularis; rupture of dilated bulbourethral glands has also been proposed.
 • Presentation varies with severity and hence with age: post-void dribbling and mild incontinence;
   significant bulging of the distal penis; a palpable obstructed bladder; or renal insufficiency and UTI.

Management, by scenario
 • Premature or small infant: vesicostomy until the urethra will accommodate a cystoscope or further
   reconstruction.
 • Majority of cases: cystoscopy with valve ablation using a Bugbee electrode or laser, and when
   successful no further urethral surgery is required.
 • Gross urethral diverticulum: surgical reconstruction over a urethral catheter.
Outcomes
 • As with PUV, long-term renal function is contingent on preoperative creatinine and GFR.
 • Pretreatment azotaemia, VUR and UTI together increased the risk of poor renal outcome 25-fold.

URETHRAL ATRESIA
 • Also termed congenital urethral stricture; rarely described, probably because of its high associated
   mortality.
 • Survival depends on decompression having occurred: the obstruction being incomplete, or
   decompression by an antenatal shunt or a patent urachus. When an infant does survive, the outcome
   can resemble that of obstructing PUV.
 • Anatomy: an obstructing membrane at the distal end of the prostatic urethra, with the urethra distal to
   it often hypoplastic.
 • Part of the LUTO spectrum: confirmed after birth by cystoscopy, and a vesicostomy is usually required.
 • The association with prune-belly syndrome remains controversial, but infants often present with
   oligohydramnios, bilateral hydroureteronephrosis and weak abdominal wall musculature.

URETHRAL DUPLICATION
 • A rare urethral anomaly with several anatomic variants.
 • The duplication may begin at the bladder neck or more distally. One urethra usually terminates on the
   glans near its orthotopic position, while the other may open on the glans, more ventrally along the
   shaft, or, in the most severe cases, as proximally as the anal sphincter.
 • The governing anatomical rule: duplication occurs in the sagittal plane, and the ventral urethra —
   usually the functional meatus — contains the sphincteric complex and the verumontanum.
 • Diagnosis is obvious where two distinct meatal openings are seen on the glans; otherwise it requires a
   high index of suspicion in what appears to be atypical proximal hypospadias with a patent-appearing
   glanular opening.
 • VCUG confirms the diagnosis during the voiding phase in many cases; retrograde injection of the
   distinct urethra is an alternative.

URETHRORRHAGIA
 • Also called benign urethritis or idiopathic urethrorrhagia: spotting of blood on the underwear after
   urination, or voiding of clear urine followed by a few drops of blood.
 • The history localises the lesion: blood in the underwear after voiding clear urine places the
   inflammation distal to the external sphincter.
 • Typically benign and self-limited, though visibly alarming to families; commonest in peripubertal males.
 • Proposed aetiologies: meatal stenosis and dysfunctional elimination syndrome; and, for the voiding-
   dysfunction school, a sequence — incomplete relaxation of the external urethral sphincter → increased
   voiding pressures → turbulent flow → negative intraluminal urethral pressure → engorgement of
   urethral mucosal sinuses → small extravasation of blood. Meatal stenosis is proposed to act by the
   same route.
Evaluation — the paediatric departure from adult practice
 • Adult urologists regard cystoscopy and upper tract evaluation as necessary for haematuria; in
   symptomatic adolescent males, spontaneous resolution occurs in up to 92%, so routine cystoscopy is
   not necessary.
 • The appropriate evaluation: a detailed history of bowel and bladder function, renal and bladder
   ultrasound, and office measurement of urinary flow rate and PVR.
 • Cystoscopy is reserved for atypical urethrorrhagia — accompanied by symptoms of urethral stricture or
   by increased urethral bleeding.
The stricture association
 • Although most urethrorrhagia is idiopathic, urethral stricture is diagnosed during or after evaluation in
   14–28% of patients.
 • This raises the question of whether cystoscopy itself, traversing abnormal inflamed epithelium, initiates
   stricture formation.
 • In atypical cases, cystoscopy showed varying levels of bulbar urethral inflammation; about a quarter
   with a white membranous exudate were subsequently diagnosed with a stricture over a mean of 5
   years, and 12% had a stricture found at cystoscopy with no prior instrumentation.
 • The balanced reading: cystoscopy is benign in most cases, but may exacerbate inflammation in a small
   subgroup that no radiological modality can identify in advance — hence careful analysis of voiding
   habits and baseline uroflow before cystoscopy is considered.
Concept — urethrorrhagia is managed as a voiding disorder rather than a haematuria, and the reason is the
92% spontaneous resolution rate set against a procedure that may itself propagate the stricture it is
performed to exclude.

 • The vast majority of males born with an anorectal malformation have a fistula to the urinary tract.

 • The fistula is usually demonstrated on distal colostogram during workup before anorectoplasty, the
   initial diverting colostomy having been performed in the neonatal period.
 • Associated genitourinary anomalies occur in 25–50%, so routine diagnostic imaging for renal anomalies
   and VUR is recommended.
 • In a series of anorectal malformation patients, 16.3% developed a febrile UTI, of whom about half had
   VUR; on multivariate analysis, the association of genitourinary malformation with UTI did not reach
   statistical significance.
 • Definitive repair is by a posterior sagittal approach, addressing the rectourinary fistula concurrently,
   with care to identify the tract and excise it in its entirety adjacent to urethra and rectum to prevent a
   diverticulum.
Operative sequence, with the urologist present
 • Cystoscopy, attempting to pass an open-ended ureteric catheter through the fistula tract — a guidewire
   may be passed if the fistula is of small calibre → place a urethral catheter into the bladder across the
   urethra → position the child prone and begin posterior sagittal anorectoplasty → mobilise the rectum
   away from the urethra, using the previously placed urethral catheter as a guide to location → excise the
   fistula tract completely and approximate the urethral defect as close to the urethra as possible,
   reducing the risk of a urethral diverticulum → close the rectal defect primarily, or excise the fistulous
   portion of intestine where redundant tissue is available → interpose healthy adjacent tissue → leave
   the catheter for at least 1 week to ensure healing of the urethroplasty.
Concept — the catheter placed at the start of the anorectoplasty is the operation's safety device: it marks the
urethra for the surgeon mobilising the rectum and then stents the repair, which is why the urologist's
contribution comes before the dissection rather than after an injury.

 KEY POINTS
  • Anterior urethral valves and urethral atresia are less common causes of LUTO, and long-term
    outcomes can be similar to those of PUV if the neonate survives.
  • Dysfunctional voiding causing turbulent urinary flow may lead to urethrorrhagia.
  • Treatment for urethrorrhagia should focus on improving voiding habits and conservative
    management, reserving cystoscopy for refractory cases and where there is significant straining during
    voiding.
  • An infant diagnosed with anorectal malformation should undergo renal ultrasound and VCUG because
    of the high risk of associated genitourinary anomalies.
