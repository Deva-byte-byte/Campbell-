Chapter 144 — Treatment of Advanced Renal Cell Carcinoma                                                   Overview

Chapter 144 — Treatment of Advanced Renal Cell
Carcinoma
 • Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a family of cancers, not one disease: histologically, biologically and clinically
   distinct entities arising in the kidney.
 • Burden: approximately 81,800 new cancers of the kidney and renal pelvis and approximately 14,890 RCC-
   related deaths in the United States in 2023; patients with metastatic disease account for the majority of
   RCC deaths.
 • About one-third present with synchronous metastases, and a further 20–40% of clinically localised
   tumours later metastasise.
 • Metastatic RCC is almost always fatal, with a 10-year survival below 5%.
 • Therapy has evolved along three avenues:
     ◦ Targeted agents, designed from the genetic and molecular changes underlying each subtype, are now
       integral to metastatic clear cell RCC (ccRCC); surgery, irradiation and cytokine therapy remain
       appropriate in selected patients.
     ◦ Immune checkpoint inhibitors, which modulate T-cell function, are active in ccRCC both front-line and
       after progression on therapy against the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) axis. Dual
       checkpoint blockade and combinations of programmed death-1/programmed death ligand-1 (PD-1/
       PD-L1) inhibitors with agents against VEGF or its receptors (VEGFRs) are now superior to previous
       standards.
     ◦ Comprehensive genome interrogation has revealed alterations in carbohydrate and amino-acid
       metabolism and in chromatin remodelling in ccRCC; their contribution is not yet defined, but they are a
       hoped-for source of new strategies.
 • Non-clear cell RCC: ccRCC agents are often used, but with scant prospective evidence; defining the
   oncogenic pathways of papillary, chromophobe and other variants is opening subtype-specific targeted
   approaches.

PROGNOSTIC FACTORS
 • Five-year survival in metastatic RCC is approximately 12% (Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results
   [SEER] data); most patients die of their disease.
 • Better outcome: a long interval between diagnosis and metastasis; fewer metastatic sites. Worse
   outcome: poor performance status; lymph node and/or liver metastases.
 • Two models are widely used, especially to stratify clinical trials: the Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer
   Center (MSKCC) model and the International Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma Database Consortium
   (IMDC) model.
 • MSKCC model — cytokine era: derived from pretreatment factors in 670 patients in chemotherapy or
   cytokine trials, most treated with interferon-α (IFN-α). Five factors independently predicted poor outcome
   on multivariate analysis. Later validated in first-line IFN-based therapy and in external cohorts.
 • IMDC model — tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) era: derived from patients receiving first-line VEGF-
   targeted agents at United States and Canadian centres; validated by the same group in an independent
   cohort of 849 patients and comparable to the MSKCC model. It confirmed performance status,
   hypercalcaemia, anaemia and time from diagnosis to treatment, and added neutrophilia and
   thrombocytosis.
 • Validated models guide management decisions and the design and interpretation of trials.

SURGICAL MANAGEMENT OF METASTATIC RENAL CELL CARCINOMA
Cytoreductive Nephrectomy in Patients With Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma
 • Studied extensively in the cytokine era. The impetus was the perception that bulky tumours suppress key
   components of immunity, and that removing large primaries might bring clinical benefit.
 • Arguments of early proponents: (1) rare but documented spontaneous regression of metastases after
   nephrectomy; (2) preclinical evidence that large primary tumours inhibit T-cell function; (3) cytokines
   rarely induce responses in the primary tumour.
 • Counterweight: perioperative morbidity and mortality, and a significant proportion who never proceed to
   systemic therapy, demanded unequivocal evidence of benefit and a way to select patients.
 • Nephrectomy as the sole intervention does not alter outcome in metastatic disease.

 • Retrospective combined-modality experience (nephrectomy → cytokine):
     ◦ In the National Cancer Institute (NCI) series of nephrectomy followed by high-dose interleukin-2 (IL-2),
       responses were modest and only about half of patients went on to receive IL-2; rapid postoperative
       progression and perioperative morbidity were the commonest barriers.
     ◦ A series including Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status 2, multiple sites,
       and brain or liver metastases saw fewer than a quarter reach IL-2.
     ◦ Series restricted to favourable patients (good performance status, minimal comorbidity, no liver or
       brain metastases) saw most reach systemic therapy, with high response rates and good survival.
     ◦ A population-based SEER analysis associated cytoreductive nephrectomy with better overall and
       cancer-specific survival.
     ◦ Their main contribution was to establish feasibility and lay the foundation for randomised trials.
 • Cytokine-era randomised evidence — the most compelling support for cytoreductive nephrectomy:
     ◦ SWOG 8949 (Southwest Oncology Group): IFN-α-2b alone versus after cytoreductive nephrectomy.
       Eligibility: histological diagnosis of any subtype, ECOG 0 or 1, resectable primary, no prior
       chemotherapy, irradiation or immunotherapy, adequate organ function. Response rate did not differ;
       OS improved.
     ◦ A smaller European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) trial of similar design
       reproduced the survival advantage, and a combined analysis was consistent.
     ◦ Conclusion: cytoreductive nephrectomy is supported in carefully selected patients likely to proceed to
       cytokine therapy; its role before systemic therapy in non-clear cell subtypes is unclear.
 • Targeted-therapy era — retrospective support: the practice continued on cytokine-era data. In a
   retrospective series of patients receiving VEGF-pathway antagonists, prior cytoreduction was associated
   with longer survival, and the advantage persisted after adjustment for risk factors. National Cancer
   Database data showed cytoreduction used more often at academic centres and associated with a survival
   advantage. Both are subject to selection bias.
 • CARMENA (Cancer du Rein Metastatique Nephrectomie et Antiangiogéniques; French Association of
   Urology): metastatic ccRCC randomised to cytoreductive nephrectomy followed by sunitinib versus
   sunitinib alone; primary objective noninferiority of sunitinib alone, primary endpoint OS.
     ◦ Planned 576 patients; poor accrual (450 at 79 European centres over 9 years) led to early closure.
     ◦ Result: median OS 13.9 months with nephrectomy–sunitinib versus 18.4 months with sunitinib alone
       (hazard ratio [HR] 0.89) → sunitinib alone noninferior in MSKCC intermediate- and poor-risk patients.
     ◦ Caveat 1 — population: almost 45% were MSKCC poor risk, a group not normally offered
       cytoreduction. About 25% of the nephrectomy arm did not receive the intended treatment (7% no
       nephrectomy; 18% no sunitinib after nephrectomy), and 17% of the sunitinib arm later underwent
       nephrectomy. In the per-protocol analysis, noninferiority was no longer demonstrated.
     ◦ Caveat 2 — selection: investigators could exclude patients with low metastatic burden, so the
       population most likely to benefit may be under-represented.
     ◦ Caveat 3 — power: stopped with far fewer events than planned, and the interim analysis had not met
       the statistical criteria for early stopping.
 • SURTIME (Immediate Surgery or Surgery After Sunitinib Malate in Treating Patients With Metastatic
   Kidney Cancer): metastatic ccRCC randomised to immediate cytoreductive nephrectomy versus sunitinib
   followed by nephrectomy; hampered by low accrual.
     ◦ OS favoured deferred nephrectomy (median 32.4 versus 15.0 months; HR 0.57).
     ◦ Deferred nephrectomy after sunitinib was safe — supports sequencing surgery after systemic therapy.

 KEY POINTS: CYTOREDUCTIVE NEPHRECTOMY IN METASTATIC RENAL CELL CARCINOMA
   • Two randomised trials showed improved survival with cytoreductive nephrectomy followed by IFN-α
     versus IFN-α alone in carefully selected patients.
   • Patients with poor performance status, comorbidity, rapidly progressive disease or brain metastases
     are unlikely to benefit.
   • Retrospective data suggested benefit in the VEGFR-targeted era, but a randomised trial showed no
     advantage in intermediate- and poor-prognosis patients; it does not address low-burden, favourable
     patients.

Concept — Cytoreduction helps only patients fit enough, and with disease slow enough, to reach effective
systemic therapy; when that therapy is active, operating first on poor-risk patients mainly delays it — hence
selection and deferral.

Resection of Metastases
 • Current systemic agents rarely cure; resection of limited metastatic disease is associated with long
   disease-free intervals and OS in some patients.
 • Never evaluated in a prospective randomised trial. Favourable outcomes may reflect selection bias,
   tumour biology, natural history or other confounders.
 • Evidence is from retrospective series. Isolated pulmonary metastases are the lesions most often
   resectable with curative intent. After complete resection of limited disease, 5-year survival is 35–50% in
   many series.
 • Favourable predictors: complete (curative) resection; solitary metastasis; age <60 years; disease-free
   interval >1 year; pulmonary site; smaller size (<4 cm in one series); metachronous presentation.
 • Complete metastasectomy remained feasible and predicted better cancer-specific survival even with
   multiple lesions.
 • Bottom line: without prospective proof of survival benefit, resection of isolated lesions is a reasonable
   and widely used practice in well-selected patients.

 KEY POINTS: METASTASECTOMY
   • Resection of isolated metastases is appropriate in selected patients; complete resection may bring
     long disease-free intervals.
   • Improved outcome is associated with complete resection, solitary lesion, age <60 years, smaller size,
     pulmonary site and metachronous disease.
   • No prospective randomised evidence; benefit may be selection, biology or natural history rather than
     resection.

Concept — Every predictor of success after metastasectomy is also a marker of indolent disease, so the
operation selects good biology as much as it treats it.

Palliative Surgery
 • Palliative cytoreductive nephrectomy — indications: intractable pain, haematuria, constitutional
   symptoms, and paraneoplastic syndromes (hypercalcaemia, erythrocytosis, secondary thrombocytosis,
   hypertension).
 • Non-surgical alternatives are often effective: pain and hypercalcaemia are managed medically;
   haematuria by angioembolisation.
 • Resection does not always relieve symptoms — in one series, only about half of patients with
   hypercalcaemia had a meaningful fall in calcium. Purely palliative nephrectomy is therefore infrequent
   but appropriate in some.
 • Palliative (non-curative) metastasectomy: solitary brain metastasis; lesions in weight-bearing bones or
   joints; vertebral metastases with impending spinal cord or radicular compromise. Usually combined with
   radiation and/or systemic therapy.

 KEY POINTS: PALLIATIVE SURGERY IN ADVANCED RENAL CELL CARCINOMA
   • Cytoreductive nephrectomy may relieve symptoms from the primary (pain, haematuria) or
     paraneoplastic manifestations.
   • Non-surgical options often suffice, so nephrectomy is infrequently done with purely palliative intent.
   • Metastasectomy, often with radiation or systemic therapy, is used to relieve symptoms or prevent life-
     threatening or disabling sequelae.

Cytokine Era
 • Summary: with VEGF and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors available, single-agent IFN is
   no longer used first-line. Newer agents rarely produce durable responses, so high-dose intravenous IL-2
   remains reasonable initial therapy in carefully selected patients.
 • Immunological rationale:
     ◦ Spontaneous regression of metastases after nephrectomy was the earliest evidence of immune
       control; it is thought to reflect T- or B-cell–mediated immunity, is rare (<1%) and often transient, yet
       shaped immunotherapy development.
     ◦ Cytotoxic T lymphocytes within resected tumours, and tumour-associated antigens serving as human
       leukocyte antigen–restricted targets for T-cell killing.
 • Sequence of approaches: nonspecific immune stimulators (cytokines) ± adoptive cellular therapy →
   allogeneic immunotherapy, vaccines and modulators of T-cell function. Almost all were directed at ccRCC.
Interferons
 • Proteins with diverse functions, including immunomodulation. IFN-α was among the first cytokines
   tested: initially leukocyte-derived, then recombinant (early to mid-1980s), which allowed higher doses.
 • Objective response 10–15% in most trials (higher in early reports); durable complete responses <2%.
 • No schedule or route is superior; adding chemotherapy or other cytokines does not improve on single-
   agent IFN.
 • Randomised evidence of modest benefit: IFN-α better than medroxyprogesterone (response and OS);
   vinblastine + IFN-α better than vinblastine alone (response and OS); a meta-analysis of randomised trials
   showed a survival advantage for IFN-based therapy.
 • Given this, and easier administration than IL-2, IFN was the usual initial agent until VEGF-pathway
   antagonists arrived.

Interleukin-2
 • Response rate 15–20% across NCI and Cytokine Working Group trials and meta-analyses; early reports
   exceeded 30%.
 • High-dose IL-2: complete regression in 7–9%, and over 60% of complete responders remain disease-free
   on long-term follow-up. Some partial responders became resectable and achieved long-term remission.
 • FDA approval 1992, based on durable complete responses.
 • Regimen: intravenous bolus 600,000 or 720,000 IU/kg every 8 hours, as tolerated, to a maximum of 15
   doses.
 • Toxicity — vascular leak syndrome → hypotension, third-space fluid retention, respiratory compromise,
   multiorgan damage. Early treatment-related mortality was 2–5%; it fell with careful selection, intensive
   monitoring and early intravenous fluids, vasopressors and antibiotics.
 • Lower-dose regimens (daily subcutaneous, continuous infusion) appeared comparable in single-arm
   studies, but two randomised trials showed lower response rates and fewer durable complete responses
   → the authors recommend only high-dose IL-2.
 • Combinations do not help:
     ◦ IL-2 + IFN: more toxic, no more effective. In a randomised trial of intermediate-dose continuous-
       infusion IL-2 versus IFN-α versus the combination, the combination gave higher response and 1-year
       event-free survival but no survival difference, with more toxicity.
     ◦ IL-2 + chemotherapy (particularly 5-fluorouracil [5-FU]): high early response rates were not
       reproduced.
     ◦ IL-2 + tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes or lymphokine-activated killer cells: no reliable benefit; largely
       abandoned.
 • Predictors of benefit: clear cell histology (non-clear cell patients too few to judge); performance status,
   number and site of metastases, prior nephrectomy and time from nephrectomy to systemic therapy.
   Worst outcome: more than one metastatic site, metastases within 1 year of diagnosis, liver metastases.
 • Carbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX, G250): associated with response retrospectively, but a prospective trial
   found it not predictive.

Targeted Therapy Era
 • VEGF-pathway antagonists are the paradigm of rational targeted therapy: oncogenic pathway → rational
   drug design → structured clinical evaluation.
 • Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease: in the early 1990s, NCI investigators used genetic linkage analysis in
   VHL families to identify germline VHL mutations or deletions. Carriers develop tumours in multiple
   organs, including bilateral, multifocal clear cell kidney cancer.

 • VHL is a classic tumour suppressor gene: a somatic "second hit" inactivating the normal allele is required
   for tumour formation.
 • VHL lies on the short arm of chromosome 3; its protein exists as two alternatively spliced variants.
 • VHL protein function: forms a complex with elongins B and C and cullin-2 (CUL2) that tags proteins for
   ubiquitin-mediated degradation; its targets include the α subunits of the hypoxia-inducible factors
   (HIFs).
 • HIF accumulation upregulates VEGF, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor-α
   (TGF-α), glucose transporter-1 (GLUT-1) and erythropoietin, which drive development and progression of
   ccRCC.
 • Sporadic ccRCC: somatic VHL inactivation, by mutation or promoter hypermethylation, is found in the
   large majority of tumours → VHL loss is the central event in clear cell oncogenesis and the basis for
   targeting its pathway.

Bevacizumab
 • Humanised monoclonal antibody against VEGF-A; the first VEGF-pathway antagonist tested in ccRCC.
 • Randomised phase II after cytokine failure (3 or 10 mg/kg intravenously every 2 weeks versus placebo):
   10 mg/kg prolonged PFS; no OS gain (crossover permitted); modest response. Well tolerated: bleeding,
   hypertension, fatigue, proteinuria.
 • Bevacizumab + erlotinib (an epidermal growth factor receptor [EGFR] tyrosine kinase inhibitor):
   promising single-arm data, but a randomised phase II trial showed no superiority over bevacizumab
   alone.
 • CALGB 90206 (Cancer and Leukemia Group B) and AVOREN: first-line bevacizumab + IFN-α versus IFN-α in
   metastatic ccRCC.
     ◦ Higher response and longer PFS with the combination (CALGB 8.5 versus 5.2 months; AVOREN 10.2
       versus 5.4 months).
     ◦ No OS improvement in either. There was no crossover, but many patients received off-protocol
       therapy, which confounds OS.
     ◦ More grade 3 hypertension, fatigue, anorexia and asthenia with the combination.
 • Limitations: no bevacizumab-alone arm, so IFN's contribution is unknown; the combination's PFS
   resembles that of single-agent bevacizumab in a first-line phase II study; never compared with VEGFR
   TKIs.
 • Current role: not widely used as a single agent; being explored with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors because of its
   favourable toxicity profile.

Sorafenib
 • Oral kinase inhibitor of VEGFR2, PDGFR-β and raf-1. First activity shown in a randomised discontinuation
   phase II trial.
 • TARGET (phase III, after cytokine therapy, versus placebo): PFS 5.5 versus 2.8 months; mature intention-
   to-treat OS not significantly different (17.8 versus 15.3 months).
 • First-line randomised phase II versus IFN-α: more tumour regression, but PFS identical.
 • Toxicity: hypertension, fatigue, rash, hand–foot syndrome, diarrhoea.
 • FDA-approved but now infrequently, if ever, used.
Sunitinib
 • Oral inhibitor of VEGFR2, PDGFR-β, c-KIT and FMS-like tyrosine kinase-3. Dual VEGF + PDGF blockade is
   probably synergistic: VEGF blockade disrupts endothelial proliferation; PDGF blockade disrupts pericyte
   function.
 • Dose: 50 mg daily for 4 weeks of a 6-week cycle (4 on, 2 off).
 • Two single-arm phase II trials, mostly after cytokines, gave high response rates → FDA approval.
 • Landmark phase III versus IFN-α, first-line (750 patients):
     ◦ PFS 11 versus 5 months and response 31% versus 6% (independent review).
     ◦ OS 26.4 versus 21.8 months — a strong trend (P = 0.051).
     ◦ Better quality of life than IFN.
 • Toxicity: diarrhoea, rash and hand–foot syndrome, fatigue and asthenia, hypertension; also bone marrow
   suppression and hypothyroidism.
 • Current role: superseded by PD-1/PD-L1-based combinations, especially in intermediate and poor risk;
   used in favourable-risk patients or those unable to receive checkpoint inhibitors. It is the comparator
   arm of every modern first-line trial.
Pazopanib
 • Rationale for selective agents: sunitinib and sorafenib hit many targets, some irrelevant to ccRCC; dose
   reductions and discontinuations are common. More selective VEGFR inhibitors aim to cut toxicity without
   losing efficacy (Table 144.5).
 • Pazopanib: potent VEGFR inhibitor that retains PDGFR activity.
 • Phase III versus placebo (untreated or one prior cytokine; 2:1 randomisation): PFS and response clearly
   favoured pazopanib, in both treatment-naive and cytokine-pretreated subgroups. Mild toxicity; mature OS
   not significantly better.
 • COMPARZ (phase III, first-line, 1110 patients; pazopanib 800 mg daily versus standard sunitinib): PFS
   noninferior (HR 1.05); OS comparable (28.4 versus 29.3 months).
     ◦ More with sunitinib: fatigue, thrombocytopenia, hand–foot syndrome.
     ◦ More with pazopanib: raised alanine aminotransferase.
     ◦ Quality of life (fatigue; soreness of mouth, throat, hands and feet) favoured pazopanib in the first 6
       months.
 • PISCES (patient-preference crossover, 10 weeks of each drug with a washout): most patients preferred
   pazopanib, though some preferred sunitinib.
 • Bottom line: a reasonable alternative wherever sunitinib is suitable; caution in patients at risk of
   hepatotoxicity. In the checkpoint era, single-agent use is largely limited to good-risk first-line patients.

 • Temsirolimus phase II (three intravenous weekly doses): modest response; response and PFS

IMMUNE CHECKPOINT ERA
 • The antitumour immune response is set by a balance of stimulatory and inhibitory signals. Inhibitory
   receptors on effector cells, such as CD8+ T lymphocytes, tightly regulate it, and responses are
   downregulated through cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated antigen-4 (CTLA-4) and PD-1.
 • Ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4), single-arm phase II: active in ccRCC, including after IL-2. Autoimmune enteritis
   and hypophysitis were notable, and autoimmune events predicted response. Grade 3–4 events in up to a
   third halted single-agent development.
 • PD-1 and its ligands: PD-1 on T cells; PD-L1 and PD-L2 on tumour cells, macrophages and other cells.
   Engagement of PD-1 downregulates antigen-driven immune responses. Tumours with PD-1-positive
   tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes tend to be larger, higher grade, more advanced and more often
   sarcomatoid.
 • Nivolumab (anti-PD-1) phase I, with expansion cohorts across solid tumours including RCC: objective
   responses, several durable, and prolonged stable disease. In the biomarker subset, responses occurred
   only in PD-L1-expressing tumours → PD-L1 proposed as a candidate predictive biomarker. Autoimmune
   toxicity included diarrhoea, hypophysitis and vitiligo.
 • BMS-936559 (anti-PD-L1) phase I: objective responses and prolonged stable disease in some RCC patients.
 • CheckMate 025 (phase III, 821 patients with a clear cell component after 1–2 antiangiogenic agents;
   nivolumab 3 mg/kg every 2 weeks versus everolimus 10 mg daily; primary endpoint OS):
     ◦ OS 25.0 versus 19.6 months (HR 0.73).
     ◦ PFS not different (4.6 versus 4.4 months).
     ◦ Response 25% versus 5%.
     ◦ Grade 3–4 events 19% versus 37% — nivolumab better tolerated; autoimmune events and fatigue.
 • Bottom line: single-agent nivolumab is a reasonable option after VEGF-axis therapy; this trial prompted
   front-line testing of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, mostly in combination.
Concept — Checkpoint inhibitors release a brake on an existing antitumour response rather than stimulating
immunity nonspecifically; their toxicity is autoimmune, and their benefit appears as OS and durability rather
than PFS.

IMMUNE CHECKPOINT INHIBITOR-BASED COMBINATION STRATEGIES
 • Rationale: many patients do not benefit from PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy, and complete responses — a
   surrogate for long-term disease-free survival — occur in fewer than 10%.
 • CTLA-4 is a CD28 homologue on T cells; binding of B7 costimulatory molecules to CTLA-4 suppresses
   antigen-dependent T-cell stimulation. Ipilimumab is approved in melanoma, where PD-1 + CTLA-4
   blockade is synergistic but toxic.
 • CheckMate 016 (phase I dose-finding in RCC): nivolumab 3 mg/kg + ipilimumab 1 mg/kg every 3 weeks ×
   4 doses had the most acceptable toxicity; higher ipilimumab doses gave substantially more grade 3–4
   events.
 • CheckMate 214 (phase III, first-line; 1096 randomised to nivolumab + ipilimumab or sunitinib; primary
   analysis in 847 IMDC intermediate/poor-risk patients; co-primary OS, PFS and response):
     ◦ OS: not reached versus 26.0 months (HR 0.63).
     ◦ Response 42% versus 27%; complete response 9% versus 1%.
     ◦ PFS 11.6 versus 8.4 months (HR 0.82) — not significant against the prespecified threshold.
     ◦ Toxicity: grade 3–4 events 46%; immune-related events needing high-dose glucocorticoids 35%;
       permanent discontinuation 22%.

Other Treatment Options in Patients With Clear Cell Renal Cell Carcinoma
Chemotherapy
 • Conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy is largely ineffective in ccRCC. Single agents (5-FU, platinum
   compounds, gemcitabine, vinblastine, bleomycin) and a wide range of combinations fail.
 • Meta-analyses: overall response 5.5–6.0%.
 • Chemoresistance: overexpression of the multidrug resistance (MDR) gene is proposed, but adding MDR
   inhibitors (toremifene, ciclosporin, verapamil) did not help → other, unrecognised factors.
 • No role in most patients with ccRCC. Possible exception worth study: sarcomatoid component, where
   gemcitabine-based chemotherapy showed promising activity in a small series.
Hormonal Therapy
 • Trialled in the 1970s and 1980s, before cytokines, for lack of alternatives and because the male
   preponderance (about 2:1) suggested a hormonal basis.
 • Medroxyprogesterone and similar agents produce regressions in a small minority; response about 2%.
 • Progestational and other hormonal agents have no current role.
Concept — ccRCC is intrinsically chemoresistant and hormone-insensitive; its exploitable vulnerabilities are
angiogenic (VHL–HIF) and immunological, which is why effective therapy arrived only with those two drug
classes.

SYSTEMIC THERAPY FOR NON–CLEAR CELL VARIANTS OF RENAL CELL CARCINOMA
 • Non-clear cell subtypes make up about 15–25% of kidney cancers. They have been studied in few
   prospective trials and have no agents of proven efficacy, so patients often receive ccRCC agents.
Papillary RCC and VEGF-pathway agents
 • VEGFR inhibitors have only modest efficacy in papillary RCC. Sorafenib and sunitinib showed minimal
   activity retrospectively; prospective phase II sunitinib responses were 5–10%.
 • Erlotinib (oral EGFR inhibitor; Southwest Oncology Group): modest response with poor 6-month PFS.
mTOR inhibitors
 • Temsirolimus: in an exploratory subgroup of the poor-prognosis phase III trial (about 20% non-clear cell,
   mostly papillary), OS and PFS were better than with IFN — limited by being a subgroup analysis.
 • Everolimus: RAPTOR (phase II, first-line papillary) gave modest PFS; about a quarter stopped for adverse
   events (asthenia, fatigue, anaemia). A second phase II in non-clear cell RCC found responders among
   chromophobe, papillary and unclassified tumours, with a trend to longer PFS in chromophobe → modest
   activity.
 • Sunitinib versus everolimus (two randomised phase II trials): both modest; neither clearly superior; trend
   favouring sunitinib.
Hereditary papillary renal carcinoma and the MET pathway
 • Hereditary forms of papillary and chromophobe RCC reveal the critical molecular events in both familial
   and sporadic tumours, as VHL did for ccRCC.
 • Hereditary papillary renal carcinoma (HPRC): bilateral, multifocal papillary type 1 RCC, caused by
   activating germline mutations in the tyrosine kinase domain of the proto-oncogene MET on
   chromosome 7, usually with nonrandom duplication of the chromosome carrying the mutant allele.
 • MET is the cell-surface receptor for hepatocyte growth factor (HGF); kinase-domain mutations make it
   constitutively active. The HGF/MET pathway drives growth, proliferation and motility.
 • Fig. 144.12: MET activation (by HGF binding, autocrine or paracrine, or by activating mutation or
   overexpression) signals through effectors such as Grb2 → motility, matrix remodelling, survival and
   proliferation → invasion, metastasis and tumour progression. Intervention points: HGF-binding
   antagonists, MET tyrosine kinase inhibitors, receptor/effector antagonists.
 • Sporadic papillary RCC: activating somatic MET mutations in a minority (about 13%); trisomy 7 (carrying
   both HGF and MET) in more than two-thirds — an alternative route to MET activation.
 • Foretinib (MET + VEGFR2 inhibitor; intermittent and continuous dosing cohorts): modest overall response;
   primary endpoint (response ≥25%) not met; hypertension commonest adverse event. Germline MET
   mutation carriers: about half responded, versus few without → MET inhibition viable in a subset.
 • Selective MET inhibitors (savolitinib, crizotinib; little VEGFR activity) show activity in MET-activated
   tumours; randomised trials against sunitinib are ongoing.
Hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell cancer and fumarate hydratase
 • HLRCC: germline mutation of fumarate hydratase (FH), the Krebs-cycle enzyme converting fumarate to
   malate → highly aggressive papillary type 2 RCC.
 • Fumarate accumulation causes VHL-independent HIF upregulation and activation of proangiogenic and
   growth factors.
 • Metabolic shift: Krebs-cycle disruption → dependence on aerobic glycolysis → exquisite sensitivity to
   glucose deprivation.
