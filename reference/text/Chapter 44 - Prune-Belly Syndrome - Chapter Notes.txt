Chapter 44 — Prune-Belly Syndrome
Campbell-Walsh-Wein Urology · Part V Paediatric Urology

 • Prune-belly syndrome (PBS): a constellation of anomalies of highly variable severity.
 • Classic triad: deficiency of the abdominal wall musculature; bilateral intra-abdominal testes; anomalous
   urinary tract.
 • Urinary tract: variable hydronephrosis, renal dysplasia, dilated tortuous ureters, enlarged bladder, dilated
   prostatic urethra.
 • Associated systems: respiratory, gastrointestinal, cardiac, musculoskeletal.
 • Spectrum: from death in the newborn period to minimal involvement.
 • Principal determinant of long-term survival: severity of the urinary tract anomaly, above all the degree
   of renal dysplasia.
 • History: Frölich (1839) first described the abdominal wall flaccidity → Parker (1895) described the full
   triad → Osler (1901) gave the name "prune-belly" from his description of the abdominal wall.
 • Synonyms: triad syndrome, Eagle-Barrett syndrome, abdominal muscle deficiency syndrome.
Concept — Survival follows the kidneys, not the belly: the abdominal wall defect that names the syndrome is
the least prognostic element of the triad.

INCIDENCE
 • 1 in 29,000 to 1 in 44,000 live births — similar to bladder exstrophy.
 • 95% male. Females have the abdominal wall deficiency and urinary tract dysmorphism without any
   gonadal anomaly.
 • Heavy disease burden: admissions and hospital days many-fold greater than in the general paediatric
   population.
 • Higher incidence in twins, children of African ancestry, and those born to younger mothers.
 • Declining in developed countries owing to prenatal diagnosis and termination of pregnancy.
 • United States national paediatric inpatient database (newborns 2000–2006): weighted incidence 3.8 per
   100,000 live births.

GENETICS
 • Features suggesting a genetic basis: high male-to-female ratio, occasional occurrence in male siblings and
   cousins, increased occurrence in twins.
 • Against: most cases are sporadic with a normal karyotype.
 • Twins: 1 in 23 children with PBS is from a twin pregnancy, yet most twins are discordant — evidence
   against a genetic cause. Proposed twin mechanism: uneven distribution of mesenchyme at primitive
   streak development, third week.
 • Chromosomal associations: Turner syndrome, monosomy 16, trisomy 13, trisomy 18; also Beckwith-
   Wiedemann syndrome. Consensus: a chromosomal abnormality is the exception, not the rule.
 • Proposed inheritance patterns (none established): X-linked recessive; two-step autosomal dominant
   mutation; polygenic transmission; sex-influenced autosomal recessive in familial PBS.
 • Strongest evidence: multiplex families (two or more affected). Array copy number variation and next-
   generation sequencing have identified variants causing PBS or megabladder.

 • Gene classes: smooth muscle cell transcription factors; contractile filament proteins or enzymes;
   cytoskeletal regulatory proteins; neural enzymes or receptors.
 • Key novel genes: myocardin (MYOCD) and filamin A (FLNA).
 • Applications: genetic counselling; fetal screening of megabladder for variants. Genotype–phenotype
   correlation, kindred segregation and pregnancy recommendations still require longitudinal data.
Concept — The phenotype suggests heredity but most pedigrees do not; the genetic signal lies in smooth
muscle genes found in rare multiplex families, consistent with the bladder and ureter being the core lesion.

EMBRYOLOGY
 • No experimental model exists, so the mechanism remains elusive; four theories overlap and none is
   universally accepted.

CLINICAL FEATURES

Genitourinary Anomalies
Kidneys
 • Spectrum from normal parenchyma to dysplasia. Dysplasia in about 50%, variable in degree and
   laterality.
 • Most severe dysplasia: bladder outlet obstruction without decompression through a patent urachus.
 • Ultrasound signature of dysplasia: markedly echogenic parenchyma with cortical cysts.

 • Collecting system characteristically dilated, often severely — but degree of dilation does not correlate
   with degree of dysplasia.
 • Calyceal morphology may be well preserved despite massively dilated ureters and pelvis.
 • Asymmetry: one kidney severely dysplastic or dilated, the other mildly abnormal.

 • Pelviureteric junction (PUJ) obstruction may be primary or secondary, but non-obstructive
   hydronephrosis is the rule.
 • Renal infection, not obstruction, poses the greatest risk to renal function.

Ureters
 • Dilated, tortuous and redundant. Proximal (upper) ureter less abnormal than distal, although massive
   dilation and stenosis can occur at any level.
 • Severity of urinary tract abnormality is not proportional to abdominal wall flaccidity.
 • Histology: loss of smooth muscle, increased fibrous connective tissue; more normal smooth muscle
   proximally — critical in ureteral reconstruction.
 • Raised collagen-to-smooth-muscle ratio, especially in refluxing ureters.
 • Ultrastructure: fewer thick and thin myofibrils → poor peristalsis.
 • Vesicoureteric reflux (VUR) in about 75%.
 • Obstruction uncommon but reported at the PUJ and vesicoureteric junction (VUJ); kinking at any level
   may further impair drainage.
 • Mechanism of ineffective peristalsis (as in isolated megaureter): poor wall coaptation → conduction wave
   reaches a sparse, weakly contractile muscle population separated by collagen → bolus passes into more
   dilated, non-propulsive segments → ineffective peristalsis on fluoroscopy → upper tract stasis →
   infection.
Concept — The PBS ureter is a failed pump rather than a blocked pipe: stasis arises from muscle deficiency,
so reconstruction relies on the better-muscled proximal segment and targets stasis and reflux, not
obstruction.

Bladder
 • Massively enlarged, often fixed to the flaccid anterior abdominal wall, with a urachal
   pseudodiverticulum.
 • Urachus patent at birth in 25–30%.
 • Histology: increased collagen-to-muscle ratio in the absence of obstruction; ganglion cell distribution
   normal; reduced α1a-adrenoceptor immunostaining; fetal bladders show fewer smooth muscle fibres,
   less type III collagen and fewer nerves.
 • Trigone splayed, ureteric orifices displaced laterally and superiorly → contributes to the high incidence
   of VUR.
 • Bladder neck opens widely into a very dilated posterior urethra.

Prostate and Accessory Sex Organs
 • Posterior urethral dilation is due to prostatic hypoplasia, probably from abnormal mesenchymal–
   epithelial development.
 • Histology: few prostatic elements; fewer epithelial and smooth muscle cells; more connective tissue.
 • Obstructive lesions of the distal posterior urethra in about 20%: urethral atresia, valves, stenosis,
   membrane, diverticulum.
 • Urethral angulation during voiding from lack of prostatic parenchyma.
 • Prostatic hypoplasia is a factor in ejaculatory failure.
 • Vas deferens and seminal vesicles often atretic, occasionally dilated or thickened; magnetic resonance
   imaging (MRI) in adolescents frequently shows prostatic and seminal vesicle hypoplasia or agenesis.
 • Epididymis poorly attached to the testis (as in abdominal undescended testes); possible discontinuity
   between efferent ductules and rete testis.
 • Retrograde ejaculation from an incompetent bladder neck.

Anterior Urethra
 • Usually normal. Commonest anomalies: urethral atresia or hypoplasia, and megalourethra.
 • Urethral atresia is often lethal unless associated with a patent urachus.
 • Atresia or hypoplasia postulated to reflect an unused rather than malformed urethra.
 • Spontaneous bladder rupture with fistula formation reported.
 • Megalourethra is more common in PBS than in any other syndrome. Proposed cause: transient in utero
   obstruction at the junction of glanular and penile urethra.

Testes
 • Bilateral intra-abdominal testes over the iliac vessels, adjacent to the dilated ureters — the most typical
   finding.
 • Mechanical theory (distended bladder, intra-abdominal pressure) is doubted because pseudo-prune
   patients with the urinary and abdominal anomalies may have descended testes.
 • Fetal gubernaculum: altered collagen and elastic fibres — a possible further factor in cryptorchidism.

 • Infertility: testicular histology + ductal structural defects + prostatic abnormality + retrograde
   ejaculation.
 • Paternity with normal live births achieved by sperm retrieval and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI).
   Normal pregnancy with assisted vaginal delivery reported in affected females.
 • Early childhood orchidopexy followed to adulthood: normal follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH),
   luteinising hormone (LH) and testosterone; some produce semen (antegrade or retrograde) with
   spermatozoa in reduced numbers, some motile.
 • Testicular tumour: few reported cases; patterns resembling intratubular germ cell neoplasia in infants.
   Risk probably low (scant germinal epithelium), but scrotal placement and long-term follow-up are
   needed to reduce risk and aid detection.
Concept — If the abdomen, not the testis, destroys germ cells, then time in the abdomen is the modifiable
variable — the basis of early orchidopexy.

 KEY POINTS: CLINICAL FEATURES
   • Kidney dysplasia occurs in half of patients.
   • Hydroureteronephrosis is often severe, yet calyceal morphology may be well preserved.
   • The proximal ureter has more normal muscle than the distal ureter.
   • VUR is present in most patients.
   • The bladder is large, with a urachal pseudodiverticulum and a wide bladder neck opening into a dilated
     prostatic urethra.


Extragenitourinary Abnormalities
 • About 75% have non–urinary tract abnormalities. After the abdominal wall: cardiac, pulmonary,
   orthopaedic.
 • Nearly half are born premature — a major contributor to comorbidity.

Abdominal Wall Defect
 • The most characteristic feature of the newborn. Musculature may be totally absent, but usually uneven:
   medial and inferior segments most deficient.
 • At birth: wrinkled, redundant skin; flank bulging; viscera visible through the thin wall. Worst areas: skin,
   subcutaneous fat and a single fibrous layer on the peritoneum.
 • Electromyographic mapping: inferior and medial segments most consistently affected.
 • Electron microscopy: non-specific myofilament disarray, Z-line disorganisation, mitochondrial
   proliferation.
 • Normal spinal anterior horn cells → not neuropathic.
 • Deficiency is patchy and may be disproportionate to urinary tract severity.
 • With growth: some improve (tonus, fewer wrinkles); others develop a "potbelly" with lower rib deformity.
 • Gait usually unaffected though may be delayed; children roll to the side and push up with the arms to sit.
 • Flaring of the costal margin from poor lower chest support.
 • Ineffective cough → vulnerability to respiratory illness.
 • Wound healing good; no tendency to infection or incisional hernia.

Cardiac Anomalies
 • About 10%: patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), atrial and ventricular septal defects (ASD, VSD), tetralogy of
   Fallot.
 • Cardiac problems at birth may take precedence over urological issues.

Pulmonary Abnormalities
 • Occur at any age.
 • Pulmonary hypoplasia from severe oligohydramnios (renal dysplasia or severe outlet obstruction) → may
   cause newborn death.
 • Pneumothorax and pneumomediastinum, with or without hypoplasia.
 • Significant pulmonary difficulty in over half of survivors; nearly half of newborns need intubation and
   ventilation.
 • Inability to raise intra-abdominal pressure → recurrent pneumonia and lobar atelectasis.
 • Acute illness or anaesthesia readily precipitates respiratory infection and insufficiency, often on a
   background of chronic bronchitis.
 • Restrictive lung disease from scoliosis and rib cage deformity, commonest pectus excavatum.

Gastrointestinal Abnormalities
 • At least 30%.
 • Majority from incomplete midgut rotation → wide mesentery → excess bowel mobility → malrotation,
   volvulus, atresia, stenosis.
 • Splenic torsion from abnormal mesenteric fixation.
 • Also: omphalocele, gastroschisis, situs inversus abdominis, VACTERL association (vertebral defects, anal
   atresia, cardiac defects, tracheo-oesophageal fistula, renal anomalies, limb abnormalities), anorectal
   malformation including imperforate anus.
 • Poor intra-abdominal pressure → lifelong constipation, acquired megacolon.

Orthopedic Abnormalities
 • Up to 65% in contemporary series; second in frequency only to the genitourinary tract and abdominal
   wall.

 • Oligohydramnios-related: talipes equinovarus (commonest), hip dysplasia, congenital scoliosis.
 • Dimpling of the lateral knees: oligohydramnios sign, frequent in PBS.
 • Distended bladder compressing the external iliac vessels → lower limb hypoplasia, absence or

 • Manifest or worsen with growth: renal osteodystrophy, hip dislocation, scoliosis, pectus excavatum or

Oral Abnormalities
 • Dental and bone abnormalities reported.
Concept — Two mechanisms generate most extragenitourinary morbidity: oligohydramnios (pulmonary
hypoplasia, limb deformity, knee dimples) and absent abdominal wall pressure (ineffective cough,
atelectasis, constipation).

 KEY POINTS: ASSOCIATED ABNORMALITIES
   • Megalourethra is more common in PBS than in any other syndrome; in the scaphoid variety the ventral
     urethra dilates on voiding, in the fusiform the entire phallus.
   • Infertility reflects testicular histology, ductal defects, and prostatic and vesicular abnormalities with
     retrograde ejaculation.
   • Pulmonary hypoplasia from severe oligohydramnios (renal dysplasia or severe outlet obstruction) may
     cause newborn death.
   • Abdominal flaccidity has no direct relationship with urological severity and does not impair short- or
     long-term survival; with orthopaedic abnormalities, if significant and untreated, it may reduce quality
     of life in adolescents and adults.

PRESENTATION

Prenatal Diagnosis
 • Fetal hydronephrosis is diagnosed accurately in the second trimester and is present in about 1% of
   pregnancies; its cause cannot always be determined (accuracy estimated 30–85%).
 • PBS mimics other causes of bladder outlet obstruction — posterior urethral valves, megacystis–
   megaureter syndrome.
 • Diagnosed as early as 11–14 weeks, yet the classic findings — hydroureteronephrosis, distended bladder,
   irregular abdominal circumference — are not consistently seen at 30 weeks.
 • Early fetal ascites has been suggested to correlate with PBS.
 • Most have no demonstrable obstruction; degree of prenatal hydroureteronephrosis does not correlate
   with postnatal renal function.
 • Sonographic appearances: massively dilated bladder filling the abdomen with absent amniotic fluid;
   bladder reaching the kidneys; urachal diverticulum; elongated, dilated posterior urethra; echogenic
   hydronephrotic kidneys. A dilated prostatic urethra indicates outlet obstruction from urethral atresia.

Neonatal Presentation
 • Flaccid, wrinkled abdominal wall at birth is diagnostic on sight, whether or not suspected prenatally.
 • Reinforcing signs: bilateral impalpable testes, knee dimples.
 • Pulmonary and cardiac problems take precedence: without true outlet obstruction (as in urethral
   atresia), hydroureteronephrosis is not immediately life-threatening.

Incomplete Syndrome
 • Males lacking the full triad but sharing other features.
 • Delayed diagnosis (absent obvious abdominal signs) → recurrent UTI → late renal failure in a high
   proportion; hence close observation, monitoring and selective intervention.
 • Those without abdominal wall signs may present as late as adulthood with renal failure and
   hypertension; most adult presenters eventually develop UTI from chronic stasis.

Female Syndrome
 • 5% of PBS; most have abdominal wall deficiency and an abnormal urinary tract; some have the wall defect
   with a normal urinary tract. No gonadal anomaly.
 • In the largest series: outlet obstruction common, anorectal anomalies frequent, and high newborn
   mortality, similar to males.

RUBACE Classification
 • Phenotypic severity score for living patients in Woodard categories II and III.
 • RUBAC = Renal, Ureter, Bladder, Abdominal wall, Cryptorchidism (the triad; maximum 16). E =
   Extragenitourinary, scored by organ system or by an existing congenital syndrome or association ("PBS-

 • RUBAC + E = RUBACE; stated range 5–31. Normal anatomy scores zero in any subcategory.

EVALUATION AND MANAGEMENT

Prenatal Management
 • Some advocate in utero intervention for dilation and oligohydramnios; others termination.
 • Termination is difficult to justify: the cause of prenatal hydronephrosis cannot be diagnosed precisely and
   postnatal renal function cannot be predicted from dilation — except in rare early, severe
   oligohydramnios.
 • Prenatal intervention: no proven benefit for postnatal renal function.
 • Justified only in: urethral atresia with progressive oligohydramnios; decompression to prevent dystocia.

Neonatal Management
 • Team: neonatologist, nephrologist, urologist; cardiologist as indicated; early orthopaedic review for
   significant limb anomalies.
 • First priority: cardiac and respiratory. Immediate chest radiograph for pneumothorax,
   pneumomediastinum and pulmonary hypoplasia.
 • Early urological intervention only for bladder outlet obstruction: bladder catheter or, eventually,
   percutaneous suprapubic tube in the neonatal intensive care unit.
 • Initial creatinine reflects maternal function → the trend over days to weeks better predicts long-term
   function.
 • Serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), electrolytes: detect acidosis and electrolyte disturbance.
 • Baseline creatinine <0.7 mg/dL predicts adequate renal function through childhood, absent repeated
   pyelonephritis.
 • Renal and bladder ultrasonography once stable: parenchymal thickness, echogenicity, cortical cysts,
   degree of dilation.
 • Avoid UTI (stasis plus compromised function): circumcision if no structural penile abnormality; antibiotic
   prophylaxis, especially before instrumentation including the first voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG).
 • VCUG: avoid purposeless instrumentation, but indicated with renal insufficiency to distinguish outlet
   obstruction from upper tract stagnation and to assess emptying. VUR found in up to 70%.
 • VCUG not needed early if renal function is normal and bladder drainage via urethra or patent urachus is
   adequate.
 • Strict sterile technique for any instrumentation — a static system is easily inoculated.

 KEY POINTS: NEONATAL MANAGEMENT
   • A team of neonatology, nephrology, urology and others such as cardiology is necessary.
   • A chest radiograph for pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum and pulmonary hypoplasia is necessary.
   • Baseline renal assessment: renal and bladder ultrasonography, BUN, creatinine and electrolytes.
   • VCUG in the neonatal period only for renal insufficiency or evidence of outlet obstruction, and only
     after antibiotic prophylaxis.
   • Circumcision is advisable in the absence of a structural penile abnormality.
   • Early intervention for outlet obstruction, preferably with a percutaneous suprapubic tube.


Infant Management
 • Neonates are managed by Woodard category (Table 44.2).
 • Category I: supportive care only; urinary tract intervention no more than simple bladder drainage — fate
   usually cannot be altered.
 • Category III: rarely early urinary tract intervention (balanced hydronephrosis, good function). Regular
   monitoring of dilation (ultrasonography), renal function (serum creatinine) and UTI. Cryptorchidism
   corrected in the first year. Persistent VUR: prophylaxis, monitoring, surgery if symptomatic.
   Abdominoplasty if flaccidity persists or worsens.
 • Category II: individualised (variable severity of each facet); much controversy. Renal function and
   drainage assessment required in those with renal insufficiency.

Surgical Treatment
 • Origin: historical compilation (1950–1970) showed that most category II infants died, with or without
   surgery → a more aggressive approach was adopted.
 • Rationale: infection and progressive renal insufficiency are the chief threats → reconstruct to normalise
   anatomy and function.
 • Components: ureteral shortening, tailoring or infolding, ureteric reimplantation, reduction cystoplasty;
   rarely, removal of symptomatic poorly functioning dysplastic or hydronephrotic kidneys.
 • Delay reconstruction until at least 3 months of age for pulmonary maturation.
 • Outcome: stable imaging, stable creatinine, fewer infections.
 • Comprehensive single-stage surgery — urinary tract reconstruction with orchidopexy, abdominoplasty
   and circumcision — does not increase morbidity; in long follow-up most maintain normal creatinine, a
   minority develop insufficiency or need transplantation.
 • Comparisons are hampered by scant long-term data, inconsistent severity assignment and variable natural
   history; individualisation of care is emphasised; controversy over category II will persist until stratification
   such as RUBACE allows accurate selection.
 • Surgery falls into three groups: urinary tract, orchidopexy, abdominal wall.

 • Indications for reconstruction: progressive or severe hydroureteronephrosis; high-grade VUR; recurrent

Upper Urinary Tract Reconstruction
 • Ureteroplasty and ureteroneocystostomy: remodelling remains controversial. Best for repeated non-
   suppressible upper tract infection or progressive anatomical or functional deterioration from dilation
   and tortuosity, often with VUR. Goal: reduce stasis and correct massive reflux.
 • Keys: meticulous technique; preserve the upper few centimetres of proximal ureter (less dilated);
   tailoring or infolding is often still needed; submucosal tunnelling in the abnormal bladder is difficult.
 • Pyeloplasty: secondary PUJ obstruction → proximal ureterolysis preserving ureteral vascularity may
   decompress the pelvis; true mechanical obstruction → non-dismembered pyeloureteral anastomosis
   between dilated pelvis and a normal upper ureteral segment.
 • Timing: excellent results reported neonatally and in older children, but extensive reconstruction is no
   longer recommended before 3–6 months. Comprehensive surgery series spanning decades report
   functional stabilisation of the urinary tract in up to 90%.
Lower Urinary Tract Reconstruction
 • Reduction Cystoplasty: poor contractility → incomplete, infrequent emptying → stasis and VUR → reduce
   and remodel into a spherical shape to direct contraction. Techniques range from urachal diverticulum
   excision to mucosal excision with overlapping muscle flaps. Capacity and residuals recur → justified only
   to excise a large urachal diverticulum or as part of wider reconstruction. Clean intermittent
   catheterisation (CIC) per urethra or via appendicovesicostomy gives better long-term emptying until
   voiding pressure improves with age or abdominoplasty.
 • Internal Urethrotomy: normal sphincteric resistance implicated in "unbalanced" voiding; urethrotomy
   improved flow, residual and upper tract appearance. Considered for high residuals, worsening
   hydroureteronephrosis or VUR with recurrent infection, but no sustained long-term success. Techniques:
   Otis urethrotome to 24–30 Fr, one or two anterior/anterolateral incisions; or direct-vision urethrotomy at
   the distal prostatic urethra. No incontinence resulted. No further experience published; not a procedure
   for infants today.
 • Anterior Urethral Dilation or Reconstruction: atresia or hypoplasia; some survive without intervention
   but often need treatment to improve emptying. Progressive gentle dilation, in situ or through-and-
   through via a vesicostomy; not uniformly successful. Hydrodistension (one to three sessions) improves
   calibre in hypoplasia. No formal graft urethroplasty reported in PBS.
 • Megalourethra repair (fusiform or scaphoid): circumferential subcoronal incision → penile degloving →
   expose megalourethra → tailor by excision or infold → reconstruct over an appropriately sized catheter →
   excise redundant prepuce → approximate skin.
 • Circumcision: as in other boys at UTI risk (posterior urethral valves, VUR), reduces recurrent UTI;
   incorporated in all reconstructions, orchidopexies and abdominoplasties in one large series.

Orchidopexy
 • Timing set by the need for early treatment of the undescended testis and by each child's need for
   temporary or definitive reconstruction.
 • Justification for early surgery: germ cells present in infancy; motile spermatozoa found in semen and post-
   ejaculatory urine of adults after infant orchidopexy; excellent prognosis for pubertal hormonal function;
   testicular malignancy risk.
 • Testes lie intra-abdominally on a broad mesorchium over the iliac vessels or dilated ureters → standard
   inguinal approaches usually fail.
 • Transabdominal Orchidopexy: neonatally and up to 6 months, transabdominal mobilisation of spermatic
   vessels usually allows scrotal placement. Bilateral transabdominal orchidopexy at about 6 months is the
   approach of choice, often combined with vesicostomy, urinary reconstruction or abdominoplasty. Vascular
   pedicle may or may not be preserved.
 • When not achievable in the first months: (1) Fowler-Stephens orchidopexy; (2) staged Fowler-Stephens
   orchidopexy; (3) microvascular autotransplantation. Meta-analysis: staged Fowler-Stephens more
   successful than single-stage. After first-stage Fowler-Stephens, vasal collateral vascularity increases by
   the second stage.
 • Laparoscopic Orchidopexy: when no other abdominal surgery is required — primary (vessels preserved)
   or Fowler-Stephens in one or two stages; satisfactory long-term outcomes. Technical adjustment: port
   placement — little resistance to cannula insertion in the lax wall.

 KEY POINTS: SURGICAL RECONSTRUCTION
   • Upper urinary tract reconstruction is controversial but clearly indicated for declining renal function
     with recurrent upper tract infection or progressive hydroureteronephrosis.
   • Orchidopexy is best performed early, giving the best prospect of a successful single-stage procedure
     and of germ cell preservation.
   • Abdominal wall reconstruction, besides cosmetic and psychosocial benefit, has shown improved
     bladder emptying, more effective cough and improved defecation.
   • Comprehensive surgery including all of the above, with circumcision, is feasible in most patients.


LONG-TERM OUTLOOK

Quality of Life
 • Survival and quality of life much improved through medical, surgical and urodynamic advances; perinatal
   mortality remains high in category I (prematurity, dysplasia, pulmonary hypoplasia).
 • Health-related quality of life reduced across physical, emotional, social and school functioning;
   caregivers also report lower quality of life.
 • Orthopaedic, gastrointestinal and cardiopulmonary conditions worsen quality of life; treating non-
   genitourinary conditions improves it.

Chapter 44 — Prune-Belly Syndrome                                                          LONG-TERM OUTLOOK
 • The paediatric urologist, as principal physician, must recognise and manage non-genitourinary
   comorbidity through puberty into adulthood.

Renal Function
 • Nadir creatinine <0.7 mg/dL at 1 year → stable renal function through childhood, unless further insult
   from pyelonephritis or obstruction.
 • Reconstruction maintains stable function in most, but periodic imaging, urine cultures and prompt UTI
   treatment are essential — infection risk is constant (dilation, stasis, CIC).
 • Up to 30% develop chronic renal failure in childhood or adolescence, generally those with impaired
   function at first evaluation.
 • Dysplasia and polyuria also harm long-term function.
 • Transplantation ensures normal growth and development; results equal age-matched groups; recipients
   can retain their native urinary tracts.
 • Individualisation: some need major reconstruction, others almost none; lifelong surveillance because
   dynamics change.

Bladder Function
 • Normal compliance, decreased sensation and contractility → large retained volumes, progressive
   capacity, significant residual.
 • Aggravated by lack of abdominal support, polyuria and poor adherence to timed voiding.
 • All patients, even after reconstruction and abdominoplasty, are trained early: timed voiding, double or
   triple voiding, Valsalva or Credé manoeuvres.
 • Progressive unbalanced voiding with failed training → intermittent or nocturnal catheterisation is
   mandatory (urethral or Mitrofanoff).
 • Effective voiding eventually follows growth, improved proprioception and abdominal support; a minority
   after reconstruction needed CIC, and some regain normal voiding as they grow.

Pulmonary and Gastrointestinal Function
 • Infant and early-childhood pulmonary problems decline with age as thoracic and abdominal tone
   improves, but asthma and reactive airway disease are more prevalent in older patients.
 • Constipation in more than half; constant management required.

Growth and Musculoskeletal Development
 • Normal growth expected with preserved renal function, although growth retardation without renal
   compromise occurred in a third in one series.
 • Musculoskeletal problems may improve or persist into adulthood.
 • Abdominal flaccidity may improve with age, but limitations persist; corsets; swimming strengthens
   musculature without strain; adults with lateral bulging: robotic synthetic mesh.
 • Pectus excavatum rarely compromises lung function in older children; thoracoplasty for cosmesis in early
   adulthood.
 • Significant scoliosis and lordosis: early orthopaedic management.
 • Lower limb anomalies detected in infancy, some best corrected then; hip and ankle deformities may
   appear later and need surgery.
 • Families report that correcting musculoskeletal problems (abdominoplasty or orthopaedic surgery) is
   the commonest way care improved quality of life.

Sexual Function and Fertility
 • Normal secondary sexual development — testicular hormone production preserved.
 • Normal libido and erections when penile anatomy is preserved.
 • Primary infertility assumed (poor sperm quality, retrograde ejaculation); no spontaneous paternity
   reported.
 • Early orchidopexy preserves motile spermatozoa in limited numbers → assisted reproduction feasible.
Concept — Long-term care is a contest against stasis: whatever reduces residual urine and infection (timed
voiding, catheterisation, reconstruction) protects the kidneys, while dysplasia and polyuria set the ceiling
surgery cannot raise.

 KEY POINTS: LONG-TERM FOLLOW-UP
   • Up to one-third may develop chronic renal insufficiency during childhood or adolescence.
   • Recurrent pyelonephritis, renal dysplasia and polyuria are the major factors in renal decline.
   • Adequate bladder emptying is necessary to avoid recurrent infection and prevent upper tract
     deterioration associated with polyuria.
   • Pulmonary and gastrointestinal abnormalities require constant care in a significant proportion of older
     patients.
   • Abdominal tone improves spontaneously with growth in most, but skeletal abnormalities often need
     surgical correction.
   • Normal sexual development is expected after orchidopexy, but with reduced sperm quality and
     retrograde ejaculation no spontaneous paternity has been reported.
