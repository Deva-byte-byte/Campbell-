Chapter 93 — Management of Upper Urinary Tract
Obstruction
 • Obstruction may be intrinsic, extrinsic, congenital or iatrogenic; in many patients the cause is not
   immediately evident.
 • Treatment spans ureteral stent placement to ileal interposition; endourology, laparoscopy and robotics
   are prominent, so the urologist must understand the technical considerations and risks of every option.
 • The chapter is organised by anatomical level of obstruction: ureteropelvic junction, retrocaval ureter,
   ureteral stricture, ureteroenteric anastomosis, retroperitoneal fibrosis.

PATIENT PRESENTATION AND DIAGNOSTIC STUDIES
 • Ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO) is usually congenital but can present at any age.
 • Neonates and infants: historically a palpable flank mass; now most often asymptomatic hydronephrosis
   on maternal prenatal ultrasonography, a large share of which proves to be UPJO.
 • Other routes to diagnosis: azotaemia (bilateral obstruction or obstruction of a functionally or
   anatomically solitary kidney); incidental finding while imaging unrelated anomalies (e.g. congenital heart
   disease).
 • Older children and adults: intermittent abdominal or flank pain, often with nausea or vomiting;
   haematuria (spontaneous or after minor trauma); microhaematuria, pyuria or frank urinary tract infection
   (UTI); rarely hypertension.

Imaging
 • Goal of imaging: anatomical location and functional significance of an apparent obstruction.
 • Diuretic renography gives an accurate diagnosis in most patients: patient well hydrated → furosemide
   0.3–0.5 mg/kg → diuretic given 20 minutes into the study, allowing the collecting system to fill.
 • Technetium-99m mercaptoacetyltriglycine (99mTc-MAG3) is the preferred isotope over 99mTc-
   diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA) or radio-iodinated Hippuran, because of imaging quality and
   dosimetry.
 • Renography quantifies differential renal function and degree of obstruction even in hydronephrotic
   units, and helps localise the level of obstruction; it is non-invasive, widely available, and best for serial
   follow-up when a standard protocol is used.
 • P40 (percentage of maximal tracer remaining at 40 minutes) raises the sensitivity of the renal scan (from
   about half to about three-quarters).
 • Renography helps exclude concomitant UPJO in children with high-grade reflux; MAG3 renography is the
   most accurate study after therapeutic intervention.
 • Obstructed MAG3 curve: T½ (drainage half-time) >40 minutes despite furosemide; after successful
   pyeloplasty the kidney drains spontaneously, even before furosemide.

 • Best practice combines an anatomical and a functional study (e.g. retrograde pyelogram + diuretic

Whitaker Test (Pressure-Perfusion Study)
 • Sequence: nephrostomy access → bladder drained by catheter (prevents transmission of intravesical
   pressure) → renal pelvis perfused at 10 mL/min with saline or dilute contrast under fluoroscopy → pelvic
   pressure and trans-UPJ gradient measured.
 • First described by Whitaker in 1973.
WHITAKER TEST INTERPRETATION

 • Equivocal results are uncommon (well under one in twenty).
 • Sources of inaccuracy: variation in pelvic anatomy and compliance; patient position.
 • Invasive → reserve for cases non-diagnostic on less invasive tests. The final decision collates
   presentation and all studies.
Concept — Dilation is not obstruction. Every test in this section tries to separate an anatomical finding from
functional impairment. Anatomical imaging (ultrasonography, CT, retrograde) shows where the problem is,
and functional testing (renography, Whitaker) shows whether it matters.

Indications and Options for Intervention
 • Indications: symptoms; impaired overall function or progressive loss of ipsilateral function; stones or
   infection; rarely causal hypertension.
 • Goals: relieve symptoms and preserve renal function, traditionally by reconstruction restoring
   unobstructed flow.
 • Children: early repair gives the best chance of functional recovery, but timing in neonates is controversial
   because kidneys truly at risk are hard to identify.
 • Selective non-operative management of neonatal hydronephrosis is supported: in a prospective series
   only a small minority needed pyeloplasty. Surgery was triggered by progressive hydronephrosis or a 10%
   fall in differential function, and all treated kidneys recovered to baseline.
 • Adults: UPJO may not appear until middle age; if asymptomatic and physiologically indeterminate →
   observation with serial renography. A minority come to surgery over several years, and some lose a small
   amount of function. Most affected patients ultimately benefit from reconstruction.

 • Procedure of choice: historically open dismembered pyeloplasty. Robotic pyeloplasty is now primary
   therapy at most major centres. Endourological incision remains an alternative, particularly for secondary
   UPJO.
 • Utilisation trend: over a nine-year period minimally invasive pyeloplasty rose about tenfold, open fell by
   about 40% and endopyelotomy was stable. A comparative-effectiveness study showed higher failure with
   endopyelotomy, with open and minimally invasive pyeloplasty equivalent.

 • What the table tests: patient selection. Crossing vessels and severe hydronephrosis are the two factors
   that drag endopyelotomy below pyeloplasty.
 • Trap: other series found these factors less important, so crossing vessels are a prognostic factor, not a
   contraindication.

Primary versus Secondary UPJO
 • Because crossing vessels predict endourological failure, primary UPJO is increasingly repaired open,
   laparoscopically or robotically.
 • Secondary UPJO — cross over: failed endourology → pyeloplasty (open, laparoscopic, robotic); failed
   pyeloplasty → endourology, which still succeeds often enough to warrant an attempt.
 • Robotic redo pyeloplasty is technically demanding but gives excellent results with adjuncts such as buccal
   mucosa graft and near-infrared fluorescence.

Nephrectomy
 • Primary nephrectomy: poorly functioning or non-functioning moiety + normal contralateral kidney, often
   with UTI or pain; imaging shows a thin parenchymal shell.
 • Adults: differential function <15% = non-salvageable.
 • If salvageability is unclear → temporary stent or nephrostomy, then reassess function.
 • Children: pyeloplasty and nephrectomy carry similar morbidity for kidneys <20% function. One group
   recommends nephrectomy below 10%, while others report stable function after pyeloplasty in kidneys
   <15%. No agreed cut-off.
 • Other indications: extensive stones with chronic infection and poor function; multiple failed repairs. Only
   if the contralateral kidney is adequate.
Concept — The choice of intervention follows the anatomy. Pyeloplasty handles almost any configuration.
Endourology depends on selection (degree of hydronephrosis, function, calculi, crossing vessels).
Nephrectomy is reserved for the kidney not worth saving when a good kidney exists on the other side.

Options for Intervention
Endourological Management — Principles
 • History: Ramsay 1984 “percutaneous pyelolysis” → Badlani 1986 coined “endopyelotomy”. The concept
   derives from Davis (1943) “intubated ureterotomy.”

 • Core concept: full-thickness lateral incision of the proximal ureter from lumen to peripelvic/periureteral
   fat → stent across → heal.
 • Retrograde ureteroscopic holmium laser incision under direct vision is the commonest approach today.
   Efficacy differs little between techniques; they differ in technical detail and complications.
 • Pyeloplasty success ≈95%: it gives a wide, dependent, funnelled UPJ and permits pelvic reduction.
 • Endourology offers a shorter stay and quicker recovery, but lower success.

Percutaneous Antegrade Endopyelotomy
Indications and contraindications.
 • Indications as for any UPJO. Historically it was offered only with concomitant stones or after failed
   pyeloplasty, then briefly as primary therapy for most patients.
 • Remains appropriate for UPJO + pyelocalyceal stones needing percutaneous access, since both are
   treated at one sitting.
 • Contraindications (any endourological approach): obstructed segment >2 cm, active infection, untreated
   coagulopathy. Such cases can often be managed robotically.
 • Crossing vessels alone are not a contraindication; marked vessel entanglement of the UPJ may defeat
   any endourological approach and is identified by three-dimensional (3D) helical CT.
Patient preparation.
 • Work-up as for any renal operation; anaesthetic risk assessment; sterile urine at definitive intervention. If
   infection cannot clear because of obstruction → temporise with stent or nephrostomy.
 • Counselling: success lower than formal reconstruction; bleeding needing transfusion, urinary leak, drain
   problems, hydropneumothorax with upper-pole access.
Technique.
 • No incision until the UPJ is crossed. Retrograde: hydrophilic wire through an open-ended catheter →
   catheter advanced into pelvis → contrast to guide puncture.
 • Access: prone; mid-posterior or superolateral calyx. Not lower pole, whose acute infundibular angle
   limits access across the UPJ.
 • UPJ intubated antegrade under fluoroscopy, or a retrograde wire is grasped from above (through-and-
   through) → working + safety wire.
 • Incising tools: cold knife (original), electrocautery, holmium:YAG (holmium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet)
   laser, balloon endodilators, and more recently the thulium fibre laser.
 • Incise laterally: anatomical studies show the lateral aspect is devoid of crossing vessels. Direct vision lets
   any vessel be seen and avoided.
 • High insertion: extend the incision to the dependent pelvis, bridging lateral ureteric wall to medial pelvic
   wall. Where the ureter inserts on the anterior pelvis, the marsupialising incision must run truly
   posteriorly.
 • Stent: no consensus on size or duration. 14/7-Fr endopyelotomy stent (wide end across UPJ); if not pre-
   stented and passage is difficult, 10/7-Fr or a standard 8-Fr stent without compromising outcome.
   Evidence on large stents (up to 27 Fr) is conflicting.
Postoperative care and follow-up.
 • No strenuous activity for 8–10 days.
 • Stent duration: the traditional 6 weeks (Davis) is still used; 2 versus 4 weeks showed no difference. Daily
   suppressive antibiotics are common but not evidence-based.

 • Follow-up: 1 month after stent removal (history, examination, urinalysis, diuretic renogram) → 6 months
   → 12-monthly.
 • Most failures occur in year 1, but late failures occur, so follow adults for 2–3 years: some failures still
   appear at 36 months, few by 60 months.
Results.
 • Better than open pyeloplasty for pain, stay and return to activity. Retrograde endopyelotomy and
   laparoscopic/robotic pyeloplasty now offer similar convalescence.
 • Success: pooled reviews ≈ 73.5% (range 57–100%); experienced centres 85–90%, with primary ≈
   secondary UPJO; long-term cold knife/holmium series about two-thirds; also feasible in transplant
   kidneys.

 • What it tests: both decline with time, but the gap widens. Endopyelotomy remains viable only in selected
   patients.
 • After failure: retrograde or repeat percutaneous endopyelotomy, or laparoscopic/robotic/open
   pyeloplasty (outcomes not compromised, though operating time is longer). CT angiography to exclude a
   crossing vessel; if one is significant → do not repeat endopyelotomy.
Complications.
 • Analogous to percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), but bleeding risk may be higher because the UPJO
   parenchyma is thin and atrophic.
 • Bleeding — stepwise: bed rest, hydration, transfusion if needed → do not irrigate the nephrostomy (let
   the system tamponade) → persistent bleeding → selective angiographic embolisation. Keep a low
   threshold for angiography, since successful embolisation usually avoids exploration and possible
   nephrectomy.
 • Infection: sterilise urine first. The American Urological Association (AUA) recommends first- or second-
   generation cephalosporin prophylaxis, adjusted to local antibiograms. Consider prophylaxis while the
   stent is in (especially in women).
 • Early persistent obstruction is rare (stent in place). Clot obstruction of the stent → a few more days of
   nephrostomy drainage.
Special considerations.
 • UPJO + stones: remove stones first, then incise; otherwise fragments migrate into periureteral tissue and
   cause fibrosis or granuloma.
 • Pelvic stone: the “UPJO” may be stone-induced oedema → clear stone, then reassess the UPJ; a retained
   nephrostomy makes a Whitaker test straightforward. Solitary lower-pole stones pose no such dilemma
   → combined percutaneous treatment.
 • Laparoscopic/robotic pyeloplasty with stone removal is equally effective; the choice rests on stone burden
   and surgeon experience.
 • Percutaneous endopyeloplasty: uncommon hybrid, an endoscopic Heineke–Mikulicz repair through a
   percutaneous tract. Good short-term results, feasible with laparoscopic needle-holders and a
   nephroscope; may fail in secondary UPJO because scar limits endoscopic reconstruction.

Retrograde Ureteroscopic Endopyelotomy
 • History: combined percutaneous and flexible ureteroscopic management of an obliterated UPJ (1985) →
   ureteroscopic “pyelolysis” (1986) → cutting electrode through large ureteroscopes, still with a
   nephrostomy (a combined approach). Good early success, but distal ureteral strictures from large rigid
   instruments.
 • Now a single-stage retrograde procedure and the standard. Advantage: direct vision of a correctly sited
   full-thickness incision without percutaneous access.
 • Cost: retrograde ureteroscopic endopyelotomy is the most cost-effective when failures are counted.
   Endopyelotomy is cheaper than pyeloplasty, while open and minimally invasive pyeloplasty are cost-
   neutral, so minimally invasive pyeloplasty is the best option where available.
 • Indication: functionally significant obstruction.
 • Contraindications: long obstruction; upper-tract stones, better treated simultaneously by another route.
   With marked hydronephrosis the antegrade route may be more effective.
Technique.
 • Instrument: women, sometimes a 6.9-Fr semirigid ureteroscope; usually, and in men, flexible
   ureteroscope via access sheath. General anaesthesia, to prevent movement.
 • Sequence: retrograde pyelogram → hydrophilic wire coiled in collecting system → access sheath with
   safety wire across UPJ → flexible ureteroscope to UPJ.
 • Narrow ureter: dilate the intramural ureter (5-mm balloon or 9–10-Fr introducer). If still narrow → stent
   and defer 5–10 days for passive dilation.
 • Incision: drain the pelvis → 200-µm holmium fibre (200 or 365 µm through semirigid scope) → 0.8–1.2 J,
   10–15 Hz → incise laterally while withdrawing across the UPJ → repeat, deepening into peripelvic/
   periureteral fat → extend cranially and caudally into normal ureter until widely patent.
 • Contrast extravasation confirms depth but is usually unnecessary under direct vision. Optional balloon
   dilation to 24 Fr completes the incision.
 • Bleeding: haemostatic laser settings, or reinflate the balloon for 10 minutes of tamponade.
 • Balloon dilation alone (36 Fr creates linear UPJ splits experimentally) → success falls with time, to about
   42%.
 • Finish: stent over wire → Foley catheter (prevents reflux/extravasation, reveals bleeding) → renogram 4
   weeks after stent removal → review every 6–12 months for 24–36 months.
Results.
 • Holmium series: 70–80% success out to 5 years; larger series 60–73%. A literature review gives 44–89%.
 • Excluding crossing vessels >4 mm on preoperative ultrasonography raises success to about 90%.
   Endoluminal ultrasonography can detect vessels but is rarely used, since less invasive imaging gives the
   same data.
 • Best retrograde results still lag behind pyeloplasty.
Complications.
 • Fewer with small instruments and fibres: ureteral stricture rare, embolisation and nephrectomy rare.
   Mostly urinary leak, stent migration, infection.
 • Ureteroarterial fistula has been reported 2 weeks after laser endopyelotomy (managed
   ureteroscopically).

 KEY POINTS: ENDOUROLOGICAL MANAGEMENT OF UPJO
   • Indications: symptoms, overall or progressive ipsilateral functional loss, stones or infection, rarely
     causal hypertension.
   • Endoscopy avoids the abdomen but does not match pyeloplasty success.
   • Incise under direct vision.
   • Low threshold for angiography after bleeding from percutaneous endopyelotomy; embolisation
     obviates exploration (which risks nephrectomy).

Concept — Endopyelotomy works by controlled full-thickness incision and healing over a stent. It fails where
healing cannot open the lumen: a crossing vessel that the incision cannot address, a capacious non-draining
pelvis, or a long segment. Direct vision and a lateral incision exist to avoid the vessel you cannot see.

 • Minimally invasive reconstruction has risen dramatically, yet open surgery remained common (about 41%
   of pyeloplasties in a 2002–2010 claims analysis).
 • Universal principles: widely patent, watertight, tension-free anastomosis; funnel-shaped pelvis-to-
   ureter transition in a dependent position. Minimally invasive surgery mimics open surgery, so the
   principles are shared.
 • Pre-repair drainage only in select cases: infection; azotaemia from a solitary kidney or bilateral disease;
   rarely severe, unrelenting pain. Use a stent or nephrostomy.

Dismembered Pyeloplasty
Indications.
 • Preferred by most urologists: almost universally applicable. Works with high or dependent insertion;
   allows pelvic reduction, straightening of a tortuous ureter and anterior/posterior transposition over
   crossing lower-pole vessels.
 • Only the dismembered technique excises the abnormal UPJ completely (flaps do not).
 • Poorly suited to: long or multiple proximal ureteral strictures; small, inaccessible intrarenal pelvis.
Technique (open and minimally invasive identical).
 • Sequence: identify proximal ureter → dissect cephalad with generous periureteral tissue (blood supply)
   → lateral marking stitch below the obstruction for orientation → excise UPJ → spatulate ureter laterally
   → apex of spatulation to inferior border of pelvis, medial side to superior aspect → full-thickness,
   watertight anastomosis with fine interrupted or running absorbable suture.
 • Reduction pyeloplasty for a very redundant pelvis (often unnecessary): excise redundancy → close
   cephalad pelvis down to the dependent part → anastomose.
 • Crossing vessels: dismemberment allows the UPJ to be transposed to the other side of the vessel.
 • Stent: most place a soft, self-retaining internal stent, removed at 4–6 weeks under local anaesthesia. It
   reduces extravasation and so secondary fibrosis, and allows earlier drain removal.
 • Nephrostomy + stent in uncomplicated adult repair gives no advantage (longer stay, more infection).
   Reserve for secondary UPJO or active inflammation; keep a pre-existing nephrostomy for diversion and
   antegrade studies.
 • External drain: closed suction near but not on the suture line, via a separate stab incision, to prevent
   urinoma, disruption, scarring or sepsis.

Laparoscopic and robotic intervention.
 • Laparoscopic pyeloplasty first 1993; greater complexity and steeper learning curve than open or
   endopyelotomy. It gives less morbidity, shorter stay and faster convalescence with success ≥90%,
   matching open.
 • Meta-analysis: open and minimally invasive have similar success and complications; stay about 2.7 days
   shorter with minimally invasive. Pyeloplasty beats endopyelotomy by about 10–30%; large laparoscopic
   series report 94–100% (short follow-up).
 • Robot (first clinical case 2002): shorter learning curve, wristed suturing → drove the rise in minimally
   invasive pyeloplasty. US use rose from a few per cent (1998) to over half (2009), with robotics accounting
   for nearly half of all pyeloplasties by 2009.
 • Indications: as for open/endourological repair. Crossing-vessel transposition and pelvic reduction are
   both feasible.
 • Absolute contraindications: uncorrected coagulopathy; untreated active UTI; cardiopulmonary
   compromise precluding surgery.
 • Objective: tension-free, watertight, funnel-shaped repair.
 • Dismembered Anderson–Hynes is preferred laparoscopically too; Y-V and flap (Culp) methods are also
   possible.

Transperitoneal technique (template for all routes).
 • Sequence: optional cystoscopy, retrograde pyelogram, stent and Foley → position and
   pneumoperitoneum → mobilise colon medially (or transmesenteric window) → dissect ureter cephalad to
   UPJ and pelvis (avoid extensive dissection and diathermy near the ureter) → define cause → transect
   pelvis above UPJ, spatulate ureter laterally → transpose over any crossing vessel → intracorporeal
   anastomosis (4-0 absorbable, running or interrupted; freehand or EndoStitch) → optional reduction
   pelvioplasty → drain through a trocar site.
 • Antegrade stent option: wire down the ureter via an upper-quadrant port or a 14-gauge angiocatheter
   through the subcostal region → stent over wire. Clamp the Foley so the bladder fills: urine issuing from
   stent side-holes confirms the distal coil lies in the bladder.
Vascular transposition (“vascular hitch”).
 • Lower-pole crossing vessels are mobilised and fixed cranially over the renal pelvis without dismembering
   the UPJ.
 • Used mainly in children, with high success (about 90–95%). Pitfall: uncertain whether the vessel is the
   sole cause of obstruction, since intrinsic UPJ disease is left untreated.
Postoperative care and complications.
 • Sequence: clear fluids day 0 → antibiotic prophylaxis → Foley out at 24–36 hours → drain out before
   discharge if output negligible. If drain output rises after Foley removal → replace Foley for 7 days (reflux
   along stent) → stent out at 4–6 weeks → renography as after open repair.
 • Complications: as for laparoscopic abdominal surgery: colonic injury, haemorrhage, ileus, pneumonia,
   heart failure, thrombophlebitis, urinoma. Roughly 2% intraoperative and 13–16% postoperative.

Results
 • Open dismembered pyeloplasty: very durable, almost never ends in nephrectomy. It resolves symptoms
   in about 95% and decompresses the pelvis in about 91%, arrests functional decline in almost every case
   and improves function in most.
 • Laparoscopic (mostly Anderson–Hynes, primary): operating time 2–4 hours (under 3.5 hours when
   experienced); complications 2–16%; conversion 0–5.5%; transfusion anecdotal; stay ≈2.7 days vs 4.2
   open; success 87–99%, mostly >95%. Safe in infants under 1 year.
 • Failures: mostly within 2 years, but up to 30% occur later. Salvage: open ≈86%, endopyelotomy ≈70%,
   and endoscopy handles most. Paediatric: about 1 in 9 need a secondary procedure, so failure is not rare.
 • Robotic: operating time 100–299 minutes; complications 3–24%; conversion 0–6.8%; stay 2.2–2.8 days;
   success 94.7–100%, similar to laparoscopy; feasible in children.
 • Late failure matters: in one long series success fell from 85% at 3 years to 75% at 10 years. A robotic
   series reported about 92% failure-free survival at 8 years, but largely on telephone follow-up.
 • Renal anomalies (horseshoe, pelvic): laparoscopic pyeloplasty is safe and effective.
 • Secondary UPJO: laparoscopic repair after failed endopyelotomy succeeds in about 83%, with much
   longer operating time; after failed open repair success is high (about 89%).

Foley Y-V-plasty technique.
 • Sequence: outline wide V-flap with methylene blue or stay sutures → ureterotomy through stenosis
   several millimetres into normal ureter → raise flap (scalpel, then Potts or fine Metzenbaum scissors) →

 • Interrupted sutures preferred, as they minimise pursing, buckling and ischaemia.
Spiral and vertical flap technique.
 • Flap length is set by the length of ureter to be bridged: the farther the apex from the base, the longer the
   flap. Length:width ≤3:1 to keep it vascularised.
 • Outline longer than seems necessary (the flap shrinks once the pelvis is opened); trim excess from the
   apex, never the base.
 • Medial incision carried down the ureter through the stenosis → rotate apex to lowest point of
   ureterotomy → close over stent with fine absorbable suture.

Intubated ureterotomy technique.
 • Flap outlined (spiral preferred: longer flap, less ureter left to heal by secondary intention). In this setting
   any flap beats dismembered repair for blood supply.
 • Ureterotomy through the whole stricture(s); minimal ureteral dissection; routine nephrostomy (prevents

 • Original: a stenting catheter exits through the cortex beside the nephrostomy. Now: soft internal stent
   (pelvis or lower infundibulum to bladder); flap apex brought as low as possible; distal ureterotomy left

 • At 6 weeks: antegrade nephrostogram → no leak → stent out cystoscopically → repeat antegrade study
   → clamp → remove nephrostomy.
Ureterocalicostomy technique.
 • Sequence: ureter dissected with generous periureteral tissue → kidney mobilised to expose lower pole →
   resect lower-pole parenchyma (partial lower-pole nephrectomy) to widely expose a dilated calyx →
   spatulate ureter laterally → stent (± nephrostomy) → first stitch at spatulation apex to lateral calyx wall,
   second at 180° → “open” interrupted technique: all sutures placed, then tied → capsule closed over cut
   parenchyma but not near the anastomosis → cover anastomosis with perinephric fat, peritoneal or
   omental flap → urogram about 1 month after stent removal.
 • Why resect, not incise: simple nephrotomy leads to secondary stricture.

Salvage Procedures
 • Failed open pyeloplasty → endourology first. If unsuitable → redo flap or dismembered repair, aided by
   an intraoperative ureteral catheter; expect a long proximal stenosis needing wide mobilisation of kidney
   and ureter for a tension-free repair. Minimally invasive redo is feasible in skilled hands.
 • Other options: ileal ureter; autotransplantation with Boari flap pyelovesicostomy; nephrectomy if the
   kidney is poor and the contralateral kidney normal.

Postoperative Care and Management of Complications
 • External drain out 24–48 hours after urinary drainage stops; stent out at 4–6 weeks.
 • Nephrostomy: nephrostogram no sooner than 7–10 days (later if complex) → patent without leak →
   clamp 12–24 hours → remove if no pain, fever or leakage.
Concept — Every UPJ reconstruction answers one question: what tissue bridges the gap while preserving
blood supply? Dismemberment excises the disease but devascularises. Flaps keep the blood supply but carry
the disease forward. The calyx is used when the pelvis is too small. The choice between them is anatomical.

Aetiology and Diagnosis
 • Rare congenital anomaly from persistence of the posterior cardinal veins; always right-sided.
 • Suspect on a characteristic S-shaped deformity of the right ureter on intravenous or retrograde
   pyelography. The ureter courses lateral to medial behind the inferior vena cava (IVC).
 • Definitive non-invasive diagnosis: 3D CT.
 • Intervene only for functionally significant obstruction causing pain or functional deterioration.

 • Successful in case series; transperitoneal (larger space) or retroperitoneal.

 KEY POINTS: RETROCAVAL URETER
   • Persistent posterior cardinal veins; always right-sided.
   • Diagnosed by retrograde pyelography or CT.
   • Intervene for functionally significant obstruction; open, laparoscopic and robotic repair all succeed.

Concept — The anomaly is venous, not ureteric. The repair therefore moves the ureter, not the cava: divide

 • Adult injury or obstruction of the distal 3–4 cm; add psoas hitch or Boari flap to avoid tension.
 • Laparoscopic: usually transperitoneal (space) with intracorporeal suturing and stent; outcomes

 • Open vs laparoscopic vs robotic: similar major complications (10–20%) and failure (6–16%), though

Psoas Hitch
 • Lower-third defects; adds up to 5 cm. Defect above the pelvic brim usually needs more than a hitch.
 • Indications: distal stricture, injury, failed reimplantation; can combine with TUU.
 • Contraindication: small, contracted, immobile bladder. Urodynamics for capacity/compliance; treat

 • Success >85% in adults and children. Rare complications: fistula, obstruction, nerve, bowel or iliac vein

 • When the diseased segment is too long or mobility too limited. First described in dogs, 1894. Bridges 10–

 • Described in 1964. Sequence: enter Gerota’s fascia → mobilise kidney fully → rotate inferiorly and

 • Limited by the renal vein; dividing and reimplanting it lower on the IVC is possible but rarely done.

Transureteroureterostomy
 • First clinical use 1934. For ureteral length insufficient to reach the bladder.
 • Absolute contraindications: donor ureter too short to reach the recipient without tension; diseased
   recipient ureter.
 • Relative contraindications (any process that can affect both ureters): nephrolithiasis, retroperitoneal
   fibrosis (RPF), urothelial malignancy, chronic pyelonephritis, abdominopelvic radiation.
 • Recipient reflux must be corrected at the same time → preoperative voiding cystogram.
 • Sequence: midline transperitoneal → mobilise colon, then donor ureter with adventitia, and divide just
   above obstruction → expose only about 5 cm of recipient → tunnel under sigmoid mesentery above the
   inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) (prevents tethering and kinking) → minimal recipient mobilisation →
   anteromedial ureterotomy → spatulated end-to-side anastomosis → double-J from donor pelvis to

 • Results: large paediatric series without loss of a single recipient kidney. Revisions were for IMA kinking. In

   with patency about 96% long term. Laparoscopic TUU is feasible without long-term data.

Ileal Ureteral Substitution
 • Long defects, especially proximal. Urothelium-lined tissue is preferred (non-absorptive, resists urine’s
   inflammatory and carcinogenic effects), so bowel is used only when nothing else bridges or the bladder is
   unsuitable.
 • Appendix and fallopian tube are unreliable substitutes. First ileal ureter 1909 (tuberculosis).
 • Physiology: isoperistaltic ileum to bladder → reflux and pelvic pressure rise only during voiding,
   depending on segment length and voiding pressure. Segments >15 cm showed no reflux into the pelvis.
   Tapering does not alter perfusion pressure or metabolism.
 • Metabolic risk is set by baseline renal function: normal function → about 1 in 8 have significant
   problems. Creatinine >2 mg/dL → nearly half develop hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis needing
   conversion to a conduit. Bladder dysfunction adds complications.
 • No proven advantage of tapering, a non-refluxing anastomosis or segmental replacement.
 • Contraindications: creatinine >2 mg/dL; bladder dysfunction or outlet obstruction; inflammatory bowel
   disease; radiation enteritis.
 • Sequence: bowel preparation (often) → long midline → mobilise colon, dissect ureter to healthy tissue
   (pelvis if whole upper ureter diseased) → measure defect → distal ileum ≥15 cm from the ileocaecal
   valve, confirm blood supply, divide mesentery generously → mark distal end, restore continuity → deliver
   through colonic mesenteric window (or reflect caecum/ascending colon on the right) → check

 • Complications: early leak or urinoma; obstruction (oedema, mucus plug, kink); ischaemic necrosis
   (suspect if acute abdomen). Progressive dilation + worsening metabolic state → look for bladder outlet

Minimally Invasive Ileal Ureteral Substitution
 • Laparoscopic (transperitoneal three-port) and robotic series: less analgesia and faster convalescence
   than open, with short follow-up.
 • Complications in fully intracorporeal robotic series include volvulus with segment ischaemia. Longer
   follow-up needed.

Buccal Mucosa Grafting
 • Borrowed from urethroplasty; first human report 1999. Usually an onlay, open or minimally invasive.
 • Robotic onlay with omental flap wrap for blood supply: most patients stricture-free at about 2 years;
   median stricture length about 3–4 cm; about half redo cases.
 • CORRUS (Collaborative of Reconstructive Robotic Ureteral Surgery): recurrent strictures after failed prior
   repair. Buccal onlay at UPJ, proximal and mid-ureter, with failure roughly 15–25% by site.
 • Best niche: ureter encased in dense scar, where circumferential dissection risks injury or
   devascularisation.

Autotransplantation
 • First performed 1963 for proximal ureteral injury.
 • Consider when the contralateral kidney is absent or poor, or other repairs are not feasible.
 • Harvest as for live donation (maximal vessel length) → iliac vascular anastomoses → proximal ureter or
   renal pelvis to bladder.
 • Laparoscopic harvest (usually transperitoneal; retroperitoneal possible) + Gibson-incision implantation →
   less analgesia, faster recovery.
 • Success about 90% long term; in benign series (many for loin pain haematuria syndrome) about one-fifth
   had Clavien–Dindo ≥3a complications, with occasional graft loss.

   • Only short defects suit end-to-end ureteroureterostomy.

   • TUU contraindicated by disease that may affect both ureters or by a donor ureter too short to reach
     tension-free.
   • Ileal ureter for extensive loss; contraindicated by creatinine >2 mg/dL, bladder dysfunction or outlet
     obstruction, inflammatory bowel disease, or radiation injury (enteritis).
   • Autotransplantation when the contralateral kidney is absent or poor, or other repairs are not feasible.

Concept — Ureteral reconstruction is a length ladder. Bring the bladder up (hitch, Boari flap), bring the
kidney down (descensus, autotransplant), borrow the other ureter (TUU), or substitute tissue (ileum, buccal
mucosa). Each rung trades more length for more morbidity. Tension and ischaemia cause failure at every
level.

URETEROENTERIC ANASTOMOTIC STRICTURE

Incidence and Aetiology
 • Conduits: stricture 4–9%, more common on the left. Continent diversions: 3–25%, mostly within 2 years;
   about 5% of strictures appear after 10 years, so long-term upper-tract surveillance is needed.
 • Ureteral ischaemia is central. Blood supply runs in the adventitia, so stripping it causes stricture. Mobilise
   enough for a tension-free join, no more.
 • Why the left: it needs extra length and dissection to pass under the sigmoid mesentery over the aorta,
   and may angulate around the IMA.
 • Most strictures are benign; carcinoma in situ at the ureteral margin is a risk factor.
 • Technique modifiers: shield-shaped rather than slit ileotomy lowers stricture; intraluminal visualisation
   (conduit opened on antimesenteric border) is associated with low rates; routine stenting has advantages;
   indocyanine green fluorescence in robotic cystectomy may reduce benign strictures.
 • Other risk factors: higher body mass index; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score >2; nodal
   involvement; Clavien–Dindo grade 3–4 complications within 30 days; prior abdominal surgery; male sex.
   Leads to ultrasound upper-tract surveillance for the first decade.

Initial Management and Intervention
 • Not all hydronephrosis in a diversion is obstruction. Long-term conduits commonly show chronic non-

 • Triggers for renography: falling function or loss of reflux on routine loopogram.
 • Intervene for pain, infection or renal insufficiency with functional obstruction.
 • Irregular mass at the stricture + rapid progression → suspect recurrent transitional cell carcinoma.
 • Pelvic exenteration (mostly irradiated): high early and late anastomotic complication rates, but most are
   managed conservatively (e.g. nephrostomy).
 • Open revision remains the gold standard (about two-thirds success vs about one-quarter endoscopic),

 • Evolved from balloon plus stent → electrocautery incision → small flexible ureteroscope + holmium laser

RETROPERITONEAL FIBROSIS

Presentation and Aetiology
 • Rare inflammatory fibrotic process compressing retroperitoneal structures, including the ureters.
 • Age 40–60 (over 30 paediatric cases reported); male:female 2–3:1 (one recent series found no sex
   difference); incidence about 1 per 200,000–500,000 per year.
 • Mass centres on the distal aorta at L4–L5 and wraps the ureters → hydronephrosis by extrinsic
   compression or interference with peristalsis.
 • Symptoms: lower back/flank pain (commonest); weight loss, anorexia, nausea, malaise, fever,
   hypertension, oliguria/anuria. Duration 4–6 months before diagnosis; about half present with significant
   obstruction and uraemic symptoms.
 • Extension: IVC → deep venous thrombosis and leg oedema. Renal hilum and vein → renal venous
   hypertension and gross haematuria. Iliac bifurcation, spermatic cord and scrotum. Aortic obstruction and
   mediastinal, biliary, mesenteric or renal involvement are rare.

Evaluation
 • Symptoms non-specific; examination usually unrevealing.

Management
Initial Management
 • Hydronephrosis + uraemia → emergent decompression by nephrostomy or stents. Critically ill with
   electrolyte derangement and little or no urine → nephrostomy favoured.
 • Then watch for post-obstructive diuresis; replace fluid and electrolytes.
 • Then find the cause: stop methysergide or any culprit drug; search for occult malignancy; biopsy
   percutaneously or at ureterolysis.
 • Biopsy may be omitted before medical therapy if there is no malignancy history, no atypical MRI/CT
   features and no lymphadenopathy, a position strengthened by PET.

Outcomes of Ureterolysis
 • Open: about 94% stent-free at 12 months. Ureteral reconstruction or ileal ureter is also successful when
   needed.
 • Laparoscopic (first 1992): occasional conversion (iliac vein injury, dense fibrosis); long operation (bilateral
   much longer than unilateral); obstruction-free in about 90% at about 2.5 years; multi-centre success
   about 83%. Tends to be done where a fellowship-trained laparoscopist is available.
 • Laparoscopic vs open: shorter stay with equal success and complications.
 • Robotic ureterolysis + omental wrap: about 86% patent at about 20 months. One enterocutaneous fistula
   came from unrecognised thermal bowel injury.

 KEY POINTS: RETROPERITONEAL FIBROSIS
   • Mass centres on the distal aorta at L4–L5 and wraps the ureters (compression or impaired peristalsis).
     Mostly idiopathic, associated with chronic periaortitis.
   • Non-specific features; ESR, CRP, leucocytosis, anaemia, variable renal insufficiency.
   • Hydronephrosis + uraemia → emergent nephrostomy or stents, then watch for post-obstructive
     diuresis.
   • Steroids first line; immunosuppressants second.
   • Bilateral ureterolysis with intraperitonealisation or omental wrap; open, laparoscopic or robotic;
     autotransplantation possible.

Concept — RPF is an inflammatory periaortitis that obstructs by encasement. Management follows the
disease activity: decompress, exclude secondary causes, suppress the active inflammation (steroids work
while it is cellular), and operate on the burnt-out, acellular fibrosis, isolating the ureter so it cannot be re-
encased.
