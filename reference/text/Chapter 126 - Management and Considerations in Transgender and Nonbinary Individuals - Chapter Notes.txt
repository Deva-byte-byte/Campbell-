Chapter 126 — Management and Considerations in
Transgender and Nonbinary Individuals

INTRODUCTION
 • Transgender and gender-diverse (TGD) is an umbrella term for a person whose innate sense of being
   male or female (gender identity) differs from the sex assigned at birth. Evidence of transgender people
   in society dates back to at least the 5th century BC across multiple cultures.
 • Gender expression is how gender presents externally — clothing, hairstyle, behaviour, name, voice —
   which society may read as concordant or discordant with identity. Pronouns may align with neither
   identity nor expression.
 • Gender dysphoria is the profound distress caused by the discrepancy between assigned sex and gender
   identity. It may be experienced by people identifying as men, women, transgender, agender, genderqueer,
   nonbinary or gender fluid.
 • Not all TGD individuals experience dysphoria, and the need for medical intervention varies: social
   transition alone suffices for some, while others seek hormone treatment and/or gender-affirming
   surgery (GAS). Transition is individual and may greatly alleviate dysphoria.
 • TGD people face marginalisation both in society and within health care. Unmanaged dysphoria plus
   stigma produces clinically significant distress; reported stigma, prejudice, discrimination, harassment,
   abuse and violence generate social, economic and legal marginalisation, poor mental and physical
   health outcomes, and death.
 • Rising visibility has produced rising demand for high-quality gender-affirming care, which general
   urologists will encounter.

TERMINOLOGY
 • Language in transgender health is in constant evolution. Use of culturally relevant, respectful and non-
   pathologising terminology is critical to a safe and affirming relationship, building rapport and reducing
   the mistrust many TGD people hold toward health systems.

EPIDEMIOLOGY
 • Approximately 25 million people worldwide identify as TGD, including over 1 million in the United States.
 • Prevalence rises steeply with how the question is asked: lowest from health-systems studies (0.01–0.1%),
   higher from adult surveys (0.3–4.5%), highest from child and adolescent surveys (2.5–8.4%), with a
   predominance of transmasculine individuals.
 • All figures are probable underestimates — heterogeneous terminology, absence of large population-
   based studies, and under-reporting from fear of stigmatisation.
Concept — Prevalence here is a function of who is asking and how. The gradient from health-systems data to
adolescent surveys measures disclosure, not biology, so every figure is a floor rather than an estimate.

International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision (ICD-11), released 2019
 • "Gender incongruence" replaced "gender identity disorder" of ICD-10.
 • It is reclassified out of the mental disorders chapter into Conditions Related to Sexual Health — the
   central examinable point, made to reduce stigma and discrimination while preserving access to care.
 • Defined as marked and persistent incongruence between experienced gender and gender assigned at
   birth. Features: strong desire to be of a different gender; incongruence with primary and/or secondary sex
   characteristics; strong desire to possess the characteristics of the experienced gender; strong desire to be
   treated as a different gender.

Clinical Assessment
 • Undertaken by a qualified mental health professional, or a provider competent in the assessment of
   TGD individuals and gender-affirming care.
 • Content: detailed gender history (identity, expression, past and present distress, goals and desires);
   medical and mental health history; psychological evaluation screening for depression, anxiety and
   conditions that may be associated with, exacerbate or mimic dysphoria.
 • Liaison with primary care or endocrinology excludes underlying medical contributors and establishes
   appropriateness for medical intervention.
 • Marked and sustained incongruence must be present, but severe distress is not a prerequisite for
   initiating gender-affirming therapy.
Concept — The diagnostic trend is from pathology to incongruence. ICD-11 retained a code so care remains
accessible but moved it out of mental disorders; the threshold is marked and sustained incongruence, not the
severity of suffering.

WORLD PROFESSIONAL ASSOCIATION FOR TRANSGENDER HEALTH
 • The World Professional Association for Transgender Health (WPATH) is an international, multidisciplinary
   body promoting evidence-based care, education and advocacy for TGD individuals.
 • It publishes the Standards of Care (SOC), covering psychological, medical, surgical and social aspects, used
   worldwide to inform practice. Version 8, published September 2022, is current.
 • TGD individuals treated in accordance with WPATH SOC show improved quality of life and overall well-
   being — the bottom-line justification for the framework.

HORMONE TREATMENT
 • Gender-affirming hormone therapy (GAHT) may be medically necessary to achieve changes consistent
   with gender identity or embodiment goals, as endorsed by WPATH and the Endocrine Society. Under
   medical supervision it is safe and effective in reducing gender dysphoria.
 • Dosing principle: target serum sex steroid levels aligned with gender identity, at physiologic levels, by
   suppressing and/or supplementing hormones.
 • Eligibility (WPATH): persistent well-documented gender dysphoria; capacity to make an informed decision
   and consent; attainment of the age of majority in the person's country; controlled concomitant medical
   or psychiatric conditions.
 • Fertility goals and preservation options are discussed before initiation.

Typical Regimens for Transfeminine Individuals
 • Goals: breast development, body fat redistribution, reduction of facial and body hair. Achieved with
   oestrogen plus an androgen-lowering agent. Antiandrogens act by three mechanisms — suppression of
   gonadotrophin secretion, inhibition of androgen biosynthetic enzymes, or direct androgen receptor
   antagonism.
Oestrogen
 • Routes: oral conjugated oestrogens, oral 17β-oestradiol, transdermal 17β-oestradiol, or intramuscular
   oestradiol.
 • Ethinyl oestradiol is not recommended by the Endocrine Society: roughly twentyfold increase in
   thromboembolic risk, and it cannot be directly monitored on conjugated oestrogen assays.
 • Commonest United States regimen: oral 17β-oestradiol 2–6 mg daily, titrated to premenopausal
   cisgender female serum oestradiol of 100–200 pg/mL.
 • Transdermal oestrogen where venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk is higher — age >45 years, or
   previous VTE.
Antiandrogen
 • Required because physiologic-dose oestrogen monotherapy does not suppress total testosterone into
   the normal cisgender female range.
 • Spironolactone is the commonest antiandrogen in the United States — a potassium-sparing diuretic
   acting as a moderate androgen receptor antagonist and a partial inhibitor of 17α-hydroxylase/17,20-
   lyase, so it also reduces testosterone synthesis.
 • Cyproterone acetate is standard of care in Europe, per the European Network for Investigation of Gender
   Incongruence protocol.
 • Alternatives: GnRH agonists goserelin and leuprolide, and the nonsteroidal androgen receptor
   antagonist bicalutamide.
5-Alpha Reductase Inhibitors
 • Finasteride and dutasteride block conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone (DHT).
 • Less effective than spironolactone or cyproterone, because they neither reduce testosterone production
   nor block testosterone binding.

Progesterone

Expected Changes, Risks and Monitoring
 • Physical changes within 3–12 months: decreased sexual desire, decreased spontaneous erections,
   decreased facial and body hair, decreased sebum, increased skin softness, breast tissue growth, fat
   redistribution. Long term: prostatic and testicular atrophy.
 • Psychological: decreased dysphoria, anxiety, depression and perceived stress, with improved quality of
   life. Physiologic: decreased systolic blood pressure, decreased haematocrit, increased triglycerides,
   increased sex hormone-binding globulin, decreased spermatogenesis.
 • Irreversibility is the examinable point: some breast development, and decline or absence of fertility, are
   irreversible. Most other effects are at least partly reversible.
 • Risks parallel hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in cisgender people: thromboembolic disease,
   cardiovascular disease, hypertriglyceridaemia, liver dysfunction, osteoporosis, hormone-related
   cancers. Risk arises from supraphysiologic dosing, or from inadequate dosing failing to maintain normal
   physiology — both ends of the dosing error.
 • Monitoring: clinical review of weight, blood pressure and adverse effects; sex steroid levels every 3
   months during year 1, then once or twice yearly. Where complete feminisation with suppression of male
   characteristics is sought, testosterone <50 ng/dL and oestradiol 100–200 pg/mL, checked 3-monthly.
 • On spironolactone: electrolytes and creatinine every 3 months in year 1, then annually.
 • Prolactin at baseline, at least annually during transition, then every 2 years — because of prolactinoma
   risk after long-term, high-dose oestrogen.
 • Bone density testing where osteoporotic risk factors exist: previous fracture, family history,
   glucocorticoid use, prolonged hypogonadism. Smoking cessation strongly encouraged for the additive
   VTE risk.
Hormone Therapy Adjustment After Gender-Affirming Surgery
 • An endocrinologist or experienced clinician oversees postoperative care. After gonadectomy, hormone
   replacement and surveillance are necessary to prevent the effects of chronic hormone deficiency, per
   WPATH SOC.
 • Oestrogen dose is reduced by 50–75% after gonadectomy, to a dose adequate to maintain bone density.
 • Adequacy of the reduced dose is judged by luteinising hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone
   (FSH) levels.
 • Antiandrogen therapy can be discontinued at this stage.

Typical Regimens for Transmasculine Individuals
 • Principles follow HRT protocols with testosterone for hypogonadal cisgender males.
 • Screen before initiation for contraindications: pregnancy, unstable coronary artery disease, untreated
   polycythaemia.
 • History of oestrogen-sensitive malignancy warrants oncology referral — testosterone aromatises to
   oestrogen.

 • Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) may require additional evaluation, given an elevated risk of side
   effects.
 • Effective contraception is essential in those at risk of pregnancy, because of testosterone teratogenicity.
Testosterone and Adjuncts
 • Parenteral or transdermal routes, targeting serum testosterone in the typical cisgender male range of
   300–1000 ng/dL. Oral testosterone, axillary solutions, patches, nasal sprays, buccal tablets and pellets
   are less commonly used. Testosterone undecanoate is rarely employed in the United States because of
   oil pulmonary embolism risk.
 • GnRH analogues may be used to cease menses before initiating testosterone, but are less common in
   GAHT because of cost.
 • Progesterone: where menses do not cease within a few months of starting testosterone, consider a
   progestational agent or endometrial ablation.
Expected Changes, Risks and Monitoring
 • Early, from 1–2 months: fat redistribution, cessation of menses, decreased fertility, increased sebum,
   acne, clitoromegaly, vaginal atrophy, increased sexual desire. Later: facial and body hair growth,
   increased muscularity, deepening of the voice. Dysphoria, anxiety, depression and perceived stress
   commonly decrease.
 • Irreversible: clitoral growth, facial hair growth, voice change, male-pattern baldness.
 • Reversible with stated timelines: cessation of menses reverses within 2–6 months, vaginal atrophy within
   3–6 months, increased muscle mass within 6–12 months. Body hair growth and fat redistribution are
   variably reversible.
 • Risks extrapolated from hypogonadal cisgender men: erythrocytosis, liver dysfunction, cardiovascular
   disease, sleep apnoea, excessive weight gain, cystic acne, hormone-related malignancy. Long-term data
   in transmasculine individuals have been reassuring when therapy is used appropriately.
 • Monitoring: evaluation every 3 months during year 1, then one to two times yearly, for virilisation and
   adverse reactions; serum testosterone every 3 months until within the normal physiologic range;
   haematocrit at baseline and every 3 months in year 1, then one to two times yearly, because of
   polycythaemia risk; routine weight, blood pressure and lipids.
 • Osteoporosis screening is targeted, not universal: those who discontinue testosterone, are non-
   adherent, or develop risk factors for bone loss.
Hormone Therapy Adjustment After Gender-Affirming Surgery
 • After gonadectomy or hysterectomy, progesterone may be discontinued.
 • No reduction in testosterone dosing is required after gonadectomy — the direct contrast with the 50–
   75% oestrogen reduction.

Considerations in Nonbinary Individuals
 • The principle is individualised GAHT tailored to unique physical and functional goals, not a binary
   endpoint: defeminisation with or without masculinisation for those assigned female at birth, or
   demasculinisation with or without feminisation for those assigned male at birth.
 • Assigned female at birth, seeking cessation of menses with partial masculinisation: intrauterine device
   (IUD), progestins, GnRH analogues, endometrial ablation or hysterectomy, combined with testosterone
   supplementation and/or nandrolone, an anabolic steroid that increases muscle mass without
   significantly increasing facial and body hair growth.

 • Assigned male at birth, seeking full feminisation while preserving erectile function: oestrogen-only

withdrawal of antiandrogen, and the separate monitoring targets.

UROLOGIC CONSIDERATIONS
 • Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) — frequency, urgency, nocturia, weak or hesitant stream,
   incomplete emptying, incontinence — are prevalent generally and also affect TGD patients.
 • In TGD individuals LUTS arise from a defined set of contributors: natal anatomy; hormonal therapy;
   gender-affirming surgery; pelvic floor dysfunction; and social, psychological and behavioural factors.
   This list is the examinable framework.

Effect of Hormone Therapy
 • Testosterone in transmasculine individuals suppresses circulating oestrogen, producing vaginal epithelial
   change resembling that of postmenopausal cisgender women.
 • Vaginal atrophy follows: dryness, irritation, bleeding on penetration, dyspareunia, urinary urgency and
   frequency, with significant effect on quality of life.
 • Management: topical oestrogen to the vaginal mucosa as cream, ring or tablets.

Effect of Genitourinary Surgery
 • After penile inversion vaginoplasty, roughly a third of patients report altered voiding, a minority of whom
   are worse; nocturia is the commonest complaint.
 • Possible mechanisms: shortened urethra, prostatic urethral colonisation, vaginal dilation, underlying
   pelvic floor dysfunction. Damage to neural innervation of bladder or sphincter is unlikely but possible.
 • Urinary stream divergence 5.6–33%, attributed to asymmetric labia, scarring, adhesive bands or residual
   corporal tissue. Incontinence 4–16%, usually self-limiting. Urethral stricture 0–4%; meatal stenosis 1–
   6%; urethroneovaginal fistula 0–4%, the last arising from unrecognised urethral injury.
 • After masculinising genitoplasty, roughly a third of phalloplasty patients report altered voiding, a third of
   those worse. Post-phalloplasty dribbling arises from pooling of urine at the junction between the native
   urethra and the pars fixa of the reconstructed urethra; compression at the base of the reconstructed
   scrotum has been suggested to empty this space between voids.
 • Urethral stricture 2–63% after metoidioplasty with urethral lengthening; 7–58% after phalloplasty.
   Urethral fistula 5–50% after metoidioplasty with urethral lengthening; 18–78% after phalloplasty.

Pelvic Floor, Behavioural Factors and Tucking
 • Stress, anxiety, depression, trauma and dysphoria-related behaviour contribute to pelvic floor dysfunction
   and bothersome urinary symptoms.
 • Evaluation for pre-existing pelvic floor dysfunction before gender-affirming surgery is essential, with
   timely treatment where identified.
 • Preoperative pelvic floor physical therapy before full-depth vaginoplasty improves the postoperative
   experience of neovaginal dilation, by teaching relaxation of the pelvic floor musculature.
 • Tucking is manual displacement of the testes upward into the inguinal canals, with penis and scrotal skin
   positioned between the legs and rearward toward the anus, maintained with tight underwear, tape, or a
   garment called a gaff; some continue during sleep.

FERTILITY PRESERVATION
 • Gender-affirming treatment carries consequences ranging from impaired fertility to complete elimination
   of reproductive capacity.
 • Counselling occurs before initiation of hormone therapy and/or surgery, as recommended by WPATH,
   the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), the American Society for
   Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) and the Endocrine Society.
 • Bottom line of the survey data: a substantial minority of TGD individuals wish to have genetically related
   children and actively consider preservation — relevant because many initiate GAHT in adolescence, when
   future fertility is not a salient concern.
 • Barriers: cost, compounded by lack of insurance coverage; urgency to commence transition; difficulty
   with future-oriented decision making; inadequate provider knowledge and bias limiting the offer; and
   access difficulties. Oocyte harvest and freezing costs several-fold more than sperm banking, and use of
   preserved gametes always requires assisted reproductive technology (ART), adding further expense. The
   process itself may exacerbate dysphoria.

Options for Transfeminine Individuals
 • Sperm cryopreservation is the most pursued option — a well-established protocol, simple and
   affordable.
 • Barrier: ejaculation may itself evoke distress, compounded by antiandrogen effects on erection and
   ejaculation. Alternatives: vibratory stimulation, electro-ejaculation, or surgical retrieval from testis or
   epididymis.
 • Prolonged GAHT progressively degrades semen quality — increasing oligozoospermia,
   asthenozoospermia, teratozoospermia and azoospermia. GAHT should be held for at least 3 months
   before sperm collection.
 • Cryopreserved sperm may be used for in vitro fertilisation (IVF), intrauterine insemination (IUI), or
   intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) with IVF and embryo transfer; alternatively, embryo
   cryopreservation using fresh sperm and a donor oocyte.
 • Testicular tissue cryopreservation is experimental but the sole option in prepubertal transfeminine
   individuals. Tissue is obtained by surgical biopsy, which may be performed concomitantly with gender-
   affirming surgery, then autotransplanted to testis, scrotum or ectopic subdermal sites to allow
   maturation, followed by ART.

Options for Transmasculine Individuals
 • Embryo and/or oocyte cryopreservation are the most viable options. Both require controlled ovarian
   hyperstimulation (COH) then oocyte retrieval under sedation, and entail considerable cost.

 • The examinable contrast: embryo cryopreservation requires fertilisation with donor or partner sperm at
   the time of freezing; oocyte cryopreservation preserves fertility without committing to a male partner or
   sperm donor.
 • Ideally performed before commencing GAHT, although ovarian function may recover after testosterone
   exposure, with recovery varying by age and duration of testosterone use.
 • Obstacles: temporary suspension of testosterone causes undesired physical change and resumption of
   endometrial bleeding; and pelvic examination, transvaginal ultrasonography and oocyte retrieval may
   themselves trigger emotional instability and distress.
 • Ovarian tissue cryopreservation is the only option requiring neither cessation of GAHT nor controlled
   ovarian hyperstimulation, and is the sole option in prepubertal transmasculine individuals. Tissue is
   obtained by surgical biopsy, possibly concurrent with gender-affirming surgery; on reimplantation
   primordial follicles mature, at which point GAHT must be suspended.
 • Fertility generally resumes soon after cessation of testosterone: menstruation returns within 3 to 6
   months, and the majority conceive within 6 months. Conception on testosterone is possible, but
   testosterone must be discontinued when attempting to conceive, because of teratogenicity.
Concept — Gametes must be secured before hormones, because hormones degrade what surgery then
removes. The only options surviving established GAHT are the experimental tissue-based ones; everything
else needs a hormone holiday or a pre-treatment window that closes permanently at gonadectomy.

GENITOURINARY CANCER SURVEILLANCE
 • The field is understudied, limited by data availability, and the long-term effect of GAHT on cancer risk
   remains largely unknown.
 • TGD individuals who retain their natal reproductive organs remain at risk for the corresponding cancers
   — the governing principle.
 • Risk drivers: stigma-associated smoking and excessive alcohol use; higher prevalence of human
   immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis and human papillomavirus (HPV) associated with high-risk
   sexual behaviours including commercial sex work and unprotected intercourse; and discrimination,
   economic marginalisation and unmet care needs, producing increased AIDS-related, liver and anal
   cancers.
 • Barriers to cancer care: lack of clinician training, insufficient cultural competence, limited TGD-specific
   screening guidelines, discrimination, and disparities in employment and insurance coverage, with the
   net effect of later-stage diagnosis and decreased survival. TGD patients with cancer tend to be younger,
   less likely to be non-Hispanic White, and less likely to hold private insurance than cisgender patients.

Prostate Cancer Screening in Transfeminine Individuals
 • Incidence is rare — a reported occurrence rate of 0.04%.
 • Despite lower incidence than in cisgender males, disease may exhibit more aggressive characteristics,
   potentially contributing to increased mortality.
 • Most cases occur in those who initiated hormone therapy after the age of 50, suggesting occult disease.
 • No established guidelines exist for this population. Current United States Preventive Services Task Force
   (USPSTF) and American Urological Association (AUA) advice is shared decision making on prostate-
   specific antigen (PSA) testing for ages 55 to 69 years.
 • PSA interpretation on GAHT is the key adjustment: an upper limit of normal of 1 ng/mL, or an increase
   from nadir of 0.3 ng/mL or greater.

 • Examination follows the anatomy: natal anatomy or vulvoplasty → digital rectal examination; full-depth

 • Biopsy may be transrectal, perineal or transneovaginal, depending on anatomy. Treatment selection
   weighs pathology, staging, comorbidity, and prior or future desires for gender-affirming surgery.

Testicular Cancer Screening in Transfeminine Individuals
 • Sparsely documented — a comprehensive literature review found only three case reports.
 • Routine screening beyond patient self-examination is not currently recommended.

Cervical Cancer Screening in Transmasculine Individuals
 • All individuals with a cervix undergo routine screening, per USPSTF and the American Society for
   Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology (ASCCP).
 • Ages 21–29: cervical cytology alone every 3 years. Ages 30–65: cytology alone every 3 years, or high-risk
   HPV (hrHPV) testing alone every 5 years, or hrHPV with cytology (cotesting) every 5 years.
 • Continue until the patient no longer has a cervix, or until over 66 years with two consecutive negative
   tests.
 • Added risk: active HIV infection is twice as prevalent as in cisgender males and ten times as prevalent as
   in cisgender females in the transgender population, and HIV increases the likelihood of persistent HPV
   infection, which drives cervical cancer.
 • Making the examination tolerable is part of the clinical task: offer screening in a primary care setting not
   predominantly serving people assigned female at birth; use the smallest available speculum; give the
   patient agency over speculum insertion; offer a support person.
 • In those who have taken testosterone, oestradiol 1% cream vaginally for 1 to 2 weeks beforehand
   reduces pain from testosterone-induced vaginal atrophy.
Concept — Screen the organ, not the gender. What is retained determines what is screened; what is
exogenous determines how the result is interpreted, and the shifted PSA threshold is the model for that
second half.

LONG-TERM FOLLOW-UP OF TRANSGENDER AND GENDER-DIVERSE INDIVIDUALS
 • Routine care: age-, risk-factor- and organ-appropriate screening. TGD individuals face elevated risk of
   cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis and certain malignancies. Patients must be educated about their
   own anatomy and the recommended screenings, particularly after surgery, because comprehensive
   records may not be readily available to health care providers.
 • Monitoring hormone levels and therapy: routine measurement per society guidance, to ensure
   therapeutic dosing while mitigating adverse events.
 • Monitoring for complications after gender-affirming surgery: close early follow-up for functional and
   cosmetic outcomes; in the longer term, patient education on recognising signs and symptoms of
   complications and encouragement to seek follow-up.
 • Mental health considerations: persistent medical and mental health needs tied to gender identity may
   require further medical intervention, mental health support or advocacy; regular follow-up is the vehicle
   for addressing them.

   • When provided under medical supervision, GAHT has been shown to be safe and effective in reducing
     gender dysphoria.

Trauma-Informed Care
 • Minority groups, including those with diverse sexual orientations, gender identities and expressions, face
   heightened risk of bias, discrimination, harassment, violence, anxiety, depression, self-harm, suicide
   attempts, substance abuse and poorer overall health than heterosexual and cisgender populations.
 • The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) advocates a trauma-
   informed approach for marginalised communities.
 • Trauma-informed care is holistic, acknowledging the effect of trauma and adverse experience — physical,
   sexual or emotional abuse, neglect, or exposure to violence — on physical, emotional and mental well-
   being.
 • Key principle: a safe, affirming and supportive environment, attuned to potential triggers and
   vulnerabilities.
 • Safety — a physical and emotional environment free from further harm: affirming language, respect for
   chosen name and pronouns, assured confidentiality.
 • SAMHSA's "Four Rs": Realise the commonality and impact of trauma; Recognise the manifestations of
   trauma-related behaviours; Respond by incorporating trauma knowledge into policies and practice; Resist
   retraumatisation through conscious effort and appropriate language.
 • Trust — transparent, honest and respectful interaction, with opportunity to share and affirm identity;
   peer support from LGBTQ+ groups encouraged for mutual aid and external validation.
 • Collaboration and empowerment — partnership in all aspects of care, respect for autonomy and
   decisions, and provision of information, choices and control.
 • Cultural sensitivity — understanding and respect for cultural and individual difference, with inclusive and
   affirming care.

Creating an Inclusive Practice
 • Gender-affirming care requires a multidisciplinary team: primary care, mental health, endocrinology,
   plastic surgery, urology, otolaryngology, gynaecology, reproductive endocrinology and infertility,
   dermatology, aesthetics, speech therapy and social work.
 • It begins with acknowledging biases and avoiding assumptions about gender or sexual identity. TGD
   individuals report being denied care, verbal harassment, and delaying or avoiding preventive care
   because of discrimination.
 • The first point of contact in the health care system is crucial. All health care workers and frontline staff
   require education in terminology, preferred pronouns and cultural competence.
