NORMAL BLADDER AND BOWEL FUNCTION
Lower Urinary Tract Function and Development
 • Mature lower urinary tract (LUT) function is two processes: storage — filling with normal compliance, no
   detrusor overactivity, outlet closed at rest; and voluntary emptying — coordinated detrusor contraction
   with lowered resistance of the smooth and striated sphincters.
 • Both depend on integration with the pelvic floor striated muscle, peripheral nervous system and central
   nervous system (CNS); developmental physiology frames childhood bladder and bowel dysfunction
   (BBD).
Prenatal development
Bladder arises from the urogenital sinus (a cloacal derivative), weeks 4–6 → scattered muscle cells by week
9 → wall thickens through gestation → fetal urine production from the end of the first trimester → bladder
visible on ultrasound at 14 weeks (storage function has begun).
Animal fetal cystometry
 • Sheep: contractility develops first; functional innervation continues after muscle development is
   complete.
 • Male fetal pigs: mid-second trimester constant dribbling → early third trimester staccato flow with raised
   voiding pressure (detrusor–sphincter discoordination) → detrusor pressure at maximum flow falls
   towards term.
Infancy
 • Evidence comes from longitudinal voiding observation and natural-filling cystometry via suprapubic
   catheters placed at pyeloplasty or nephrectomy. Interrupted voiding is common; cystometry shows a
   rapid rise in detrusor pressure with abrupt cessation of flow.
 • Voiding detrusor pressure frequently exceeds 100 cm H2O, more often in boys than girls.

 • Several voids within a 10-minute interval; these frequent voids almost disappear by 2 years →
   coordinated voiding is part of normal maturation.
 • Capacity variable; postvoid residual (PVR) may be high in an individual void, but healthy infants almost
   always empty efficiently.
 • With age frequency falls and capacity rises; the capacity increase is most marked after 2 years.
Voiding and sleep
 • Voiding during sleep is uncommon; with polysomnography no infant voided asleep.
 • Sequence: electroencephalogram (EEG) change → infant wakes → voids → returns to sleep → higher
   CNS centres control voiding before toilet training.
Concept — Infant dyscoordination is a transient maturational phase, and cortical arousal gates voiding from
birth; dyscoordination that persists beyond toddlerhood is therefore dysfunction, not immaturity.

Lower Gastrointestinal Function
 • Fetal defecation occurs in healthy pregnancies (high-resolution ultrasound); it is no longer regarded as a
   sequela of hypoxia or infection.

Embryology
Primitive gut tube (foregut, midgut, hindgut) forms in week 4 → blind hindgut ends at the cloacal membrane;
distal hindgut expands as the cloaca → cloaca partitioned into urogenital sinus and anorectal canal, weeks
4–6 → cloacal membrane ruptures at week 8 → distal third of the anorectal canal develops from the
external anal pit.
 • Meconium: water, intestinal epithelial cells, exfoliated skin cells and hair, gastrointestinal secretions.
Postnatal stool pattern
 • Defecation depends on age, diet, intrinsic motility, innervation and comorbidity.
 • Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC): stool frequency declines through early
   childhood.
 • Breastfed infants may pass infrequent but soft stools — normal.
 • Consistency reflects the solid-to-fluid ratio and correlates with whole-gut transit time; most newborns
   pass soft or liquid stool; stools become formed as colonic water conservation matures, and transit time
   lengthens from infancy to after puberty.
Storage and emptying
 • Colon propels contents by coordinated smooth-muscle contraction and absorbs water and electrolytes →
   stool deposited in the rectum.
 • The rectum, like the bladder, is a cloacal derivative and a storage–elimination organ: highly compliant,
   smooth muscle quiescent until capacity.
Pre-expulsive phase: rectal distension → conscious desire via rectal mechanoreceptors and pelvic floor
sensory receptors → recto-anal inhibitory reflex (RAIR; anal sampling reflex): internal sphincter relaxes so
anal-canal receptors sample contents → external sphincter contracts simultaneously to preserve
continence.
 • An intact sampling reflex is required for voluntary flatus without faecal incontinence.
INTERNAL VERSUS EXTERNAL ANAL SPHINCTER
                    Internal anal sphincter                                          External anal sphincter
 Muscle             Smooth — thickened extension of colonic smooth muscle            Striated, layered
 Control            Autonomic, not voluntary                                         Spinal and cortical
 At expulsion       Relaxes                                                          Relaxes, with pelvic floor

 • Deferral: external sphincter and pelvic floor contraction; retrograde motility holds rectal contents back;
   rectum stays quiescent until a better opportunity arises or antegrade pressure overcomes it.
 • Expulsive phase: rectal pressure rises (Valsalva plus propulsive rectal contractions) → coordinated
   relaxation of internal sphincter, external sphincter and pelvic floor; squatting or hip flexion straightens
   the anorectal angle and eases expulsion.
Concept — Bladder and rectum are sibling cloacal derivatives running one programme — compliant storage
behind a closed outlet, then voluntary emptying through a relaxed outlet — and the functional disorders in
this chapter are failures of one of those two phases, usually at the outlet.

Toilet Training
 • Timing is affected by anatomical, physiological and behavioural factors; usual order bowel control →
   daytime bladder control → night-time bladder control.
 • BBD can arise during training; no consensus on the best method.

Factors affecting age at completion
 • Intrinsic: larger infant bladder capacity persists into childhood and predicts earlier daytime control.
 • Extrinsic: mothers working outside the home and ready availability of disposable nappies are suggested
   causes of later completion; average age of training has risen worldwide.
 • Delayed initiation is associated with BBD: systematic reviews link it to daytime urinary incontinence and
   nocturnal enuresis; Belgian schoolchildren who started later had more urinary tract infections (UTIs).
 • Bowel: fewer data; later training → more hiding during defecation; hiders are at greater risk of
   constipation and stool withholding.
Concept — Early structured and readiness-based methods reach the same end-point; the harm lies in late
initiation, which tracks with both urinary and bowel dysfunction.

Interaction Between the Bladder and Bowel
Physiology
 • Shared embryology, adjacency and parallel storage–elimination function; confinement in the pelvis
   creates a "zero-sum" volume relationship in which each organ limits the other's capacity.
 • Combined dysfunction in neurological disease (spinal cord injury, spina bifida).
 • Healthy adults: rectal distension alters LUT function and bladder distension alters anorectal function →
   translational research.
 • Cross-organ sensitisation: bladder and bowel afferents run together in the splanchnic, pelvic and
   pudendal nerves; cell bodies in thoracolumbar and lumbosacral dorsal root ganglia (DRG) → neural
   crosstalk lets disease in one organ produce pathology in the other.
Mechanisms in experimental colitis (persist after colitis resolves)
 • Bladder mechanoreceptor hypersensitivity; altered excitability and channel properties of bladder DRG
   neurons.
 • A minority of DRG neurons project to both bladder and colon; separately innervating afferents still
   converge on the same spinal cord regions.
 • Neurogenic inflammation: efferent neuropeptide release maintains colonic afferent hypersensitivity;
   neuropeptide expression rises in bladder-innervating DRG and bladder.
 • More activated bladder mast cells and increased urothelial permeability.
Microbiome
 • Microbiota–gut–brain axis acts via chemical transmitters, neural pathways and immunity — attractive
   given the high neurodevelopmental comorbidity of BBD; may also modulate the LUT.

 • Urine of healthy individuals contains microbiota; adult urobiome differs in recurrent UTI, interstitial
   cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS), urinary incontinence/overactive bladder (UI/OAB) and stones.
 • Children: pilot case–control study found no urobiome difference in BBD versus controls.
Concomitant Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction and Bowel Dysfunction
 • Common and frequently coexistent in healthy children. Dutch population study (validated
   questionnaires, toilet-trained children to 7 years): faecal incontinence common and urinary incontinence
   commoner still, mostly daytime-only; likewise in United States primary care. Faecal incontinence
   associated with urinary incontinence in both cohorts.
 • Functional constipation → more lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS); systematic review: LUTS in a
   substantial proportion of constipated children.
 • Bowel assessment is indispensable in LUTS: in tertiary LUTS referrals not on constipation treatment, the
   majority met criteria for a functional defecation disorder — chiefly functional constipation (commonest
   complaints faecal incontinence and hard or painful stools), plus a further group with functional non-
   retentive faecal incontinence (FNRFI).
 • Children with OAB symptoms have more functional constipation and FNRFI.
 • Conversely, constipated children without LUTS show abnormal pelvic muscle activity during voiding and
   higher PVR; rectal distension unpredictably alters bladder capacity, fullness sensation and detrusor
   overactivity at urodynamics.

 KEY POINTS: NORMAL BLADDER AND BOWEL FUNCTION
   • Bladder and rectum both store waste and expel it efficiently under voluntary control.
   • Physiological and environmental conditions affect toilet-training timing; delayed training may be
     associated with BBD.
   • Translational evidence supports a connection between bladder and bowel function.
   • LUT and bowel dysfunction frequently coexist in healthy children.
   • Untreated bowel dysfunction impairs evaluation and treatment of LUT dysfunction.
   • Effective treatment of constipation improves LUTS.

Concept — Bladder and bowel behave as one functional unit: assess the bowel in every child with LUTS, treat
it first, and expect bladder drugs to recreate the bowel problem that drove the symptoms.

Infrequent Urination/Defecation
Urinary Retention
 • Acute urinary retention (AUR) requires urological consultation and evaluation.
 • Consider mechanical obstruction from malignancy and neurological causes, but the commonest causes
   are minor genital inflammation or trauma and post-instrumentation.
 • Almost half occur between the second and fourth year of life; many non-malignant, non-neurological
   cases have acute constipation and faecal impaction.
 • Severe cause not suspected and child not in significant discomfort → initial approach without
   catheterisation: oral analgesia, warm sitz baths and/or enema — less traumatising.
 • Hypothesis: a noxious stimulus such as bowel distension triggers sympathetic dysreflexia → AUR;
   urethral catheterisation is a further noxious stimulus.

Constipation
 • Functional constipation: infrequent, often hard stools; estimated worldwide prevalence about 14%; like
   AUR, onset peaks at 2–3 years, often around toilet training.
 • Symptoms: infrequent defecation, painful defecation, faecal incontinence, abdominal pain.
 • Organic differential: congenital anorectal disease; coeliac disease; cystic fibrosis; abnormal abdominal
   musculature (prune belly, gastroschisis, Down syndrome); neuropathic causes; systemic disease
   (hypothyroidism, diabetes mellitus); electrolyte disturbance (hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia); drugs and
   toxins.
 • Most have no underlying cause → functional (idiopathic) constipation.
Withholding cycle: painful defecation → voluntary withholding → prolonged retention → more water
absorbed → harder stool → more difficult, painful passage → further withholding.
Concept — Retention in a toddler is usually a response to a pelvic noxious stimulus, most often a loaded
rectum, so the first manoeuvre is to remove that stimulus rather than add another in the form of a catheter.

Pain
 • Pain is especially distressing for parents; education and a management plan, once concerning causes are
   excluded, allow the family to participate fully.
Dysuria
 • Many children referred for "recurrent UTI" have no positive urine culture.
 • Non-infective sources in BBD: genital skin irritation from chronic wetting (topical barrier ointments such
   as Calmoseptine, nappy ointments); pain at the end of voiding suggests a bladder source; bladder
   irritation.
 • Dysuria is non-specific → further evaluation to define the BBD.

Lower Abdominal Pain
 • Chronic intermittent abdominal pain is common and can accompany BBD; functional constipation is a
   common cause of acute and chronic abdominal pain — the primary cause of about half of acute
   presentations in one academic primary-care population.

 • Question: treat BBD alone, or refer to gastroenterology? Differential divides into organic and functional

Genital Pain
 • External genital examination usually normal.
 • Referred pain: in boys, penile pain is often referred from the bladder — milder discomfort shows as
   grabbing the tip of the penis; girls report pain vaguely localised to the vaginal area.
 • Educating families about referred pain helps, because treatment is directed away from the site of
   complaint — nocturnal perineal pain resolved with relaxation biofeedback for an overactive pelvic floor
   plus anticholinergics for detrusor overactivity.
 • BBD can cause recurrent epididymitis; recurrent scrotal pain with bladder outlet dysfunction treated by
   pelvic floor therapy and onabotulinumtoxinA injection of the external urethral sphincter.

 KEY POINTS: ABNORMAL BLADDER AND BOWEL FUNCTION
   • Standardised terminology in BBD is critical for communication and research.
   • Common symptoms: infrequent urination/defecation, incontinence and pain.
   • Usually no organic cause is identified.

Concept — Pain in BBD is usually referred or secondary, so once alarm features are excluded, treatment is
aimed at the dysfunctional organ rather than the painful site.

ASSOCIATED CONDITIONS
Quality of Life
 • Toilet training is developmental and BBD is not life-threatening → treatment delay is common; families
   are told to wait for the child to outgrow it.
 • Consensus across countries: nocturnal enuresis, DUI, constipation, faecal incontinence and BBD impair
   quality of life; impact greater with older age, faecal incontinence and combined BBD.

 • Pharmacological treatment → health-related quality of life improves within 3 months.
 • Simple non-pharmacological, non-procedural measures also improve quality of life: randomised
   controlled trial (RCT) of a group urotherapy session — urinary tract anatomy and physiology, bladder
   retraining, progressive relaxation, psychosocial aspects — improved quality of life without medication; a
   20-page enuresis education booklet with voiding diary improved scores at 1 month versus enuretic
   controls.
Concept — Delay is not neutral: quality of life is already impaired, and even education alone improves it, so
"wait and outgrow" forfeits an easy gain.

The Brain and BBD
 • Neurodevelopmental disorders: early-childhood onset, abnormal brain function, diagnosed through
   delayed milestones and aberrant behaviour; exceedingly common — in a United States cohort of more
   than 3 million children, commoner among publicly than privately insured children before 8 years.
 • Nocturnal enuresis, DUI, faecal incontinence and constipation are all more prevalent in
   neurodevelopmental disorders; three groups carry the highest BBD rates: attention-deficit/hyperactivity
   disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and intellectual disability (ID).
 • ICCS consensus non-systematic review on treating incontinence in these disorders.

Stressful life events in typically developing children
 • Families often ask about psychological causes (death of a pet, moving house, change in family structure,
   trauma, sexual abuse).
 • After sexual abuse or trauma: education on LUT anatomy and physiology and explicit documentation of
   a normal examination reassures caregivers and is often a prerequisite for engaging in urotherapy.
 • United Kingdom community cohort: girls reporting stressful life events at 7–8 years had more new-onset
   urinary incontinence at 9 years; not seen in boys; causation undetermined.
 • Sri Lanka: constipation commoner with stressors such as an alcoholic father or parental unemployment;
   greater BBD severity at presentation associated with unplanned pregnancy, daycare attendance and
   school problems.
Anxiety and depression
 • Common in BBD; direction of causality uncertain.
 • ALSPAC adolescents: depressive symptoms ↔ DUI, faecal incontinence, voiding postponement;
   generalised anxiety disorder ↔ DUI, voiding postponement, enuresis, frequent urination; similar
   associations in North America, South America and Asia.
 • Urologist's role: ask about diagnoses and review medications that imply them; if clinic observation
   suggests an undiagnosed disorder, liaise with primary care; treating the comorbidity helps both BBD and
   overall health.
Concept — Neurodevelopmental and psychological comorbidity is the rule rather than the exception in BBD; it
lowers the ceiling on behavioural therapy, so it is identified and treated alongside the bladder and bowel
rather than after them.

Associated Conditions in Adulthood
 • Mothers (usually the accompanying parent) with postpartum incontinence, IC/BPS or OAB ask whether
   the child's symptoms predict adult disease.
 • Two study designs: children with BBD followed into adulthood, or adults with LUTS recalling childhood
   symptoms.
 • Childhood LUTS predict adolescent and adult LUTS — longitudinal cohort, case–control, cross-sectional
   follow-up; more than a quarter of constipated young adults have been symptomatic since childhood.
Risk factors for persistence or recurrence
 • Women with urge and mixed urinary incontinence more often report childhood BBD.
 • Higher childhood BBD symptom score → adult female urinary incontinence and adult OAB, with greater
   OAB severity and worse quality of life.
 • Prolonged treatment and later symptom recovery: DUI plus enuresis still present at 9 years persists into
   late adolescence; OAB recovery after 12 years predicts adult OAB.
 • A favourable childhood treatment outcome does not lower adult LUTS risk: complete, partial and non-
   responders had equal adult LUTS and incontinence.
 • Dysfunctional voiding in girls (RCT 2000–2003 of outpatient behavioural therapy alone, with home video
   instruction, or with home uroflow biofeedback) → 20 years later uroflow and emptying had improved
   further, but the proportion with DUI was essentially unchanged.
Interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome
 • Women with IC/BPS recall more childhood UTIs and urgency than controls.

 • IC/BPS with IBS-like symptoms → higher pelvic pain and urgency/frequency scores and more recalled

 KEY POINTS: ASSOCIATED CONDITIONS
   • Conservative non-pharmacological management of BBD improves quality of life.
   • BBD is more prevalent in neurodevelopmental disorders, which may alter the treatment plan.
   • Childhood BBD is associated with persistence of LUTS into adulthood.

Concept — BBD behaves as a lifelong phenotype rather than a phase: it tracks into adult OAB, incontinence
and IC/BPS regardless of how well childhood treatment worked, which argues against delay and for planned
transition.

 • Several societies address BBD evaluation and treatment (Table 47.2); the approach below follows them.
 • Diagnosis and planning rest largely on history and examination; simple tests help, but children with BBD
   are often over-investigated.
 • Most LUTS and bowel dysfunction are functional; history and examination exist chiefly to exclude
   anatomical or organic disease.

History
 • Barriers: school-age children toilet independently and spend much of the day away from home → parents
   lack detail; children misunderstand abstract questions.
 • Ask concretely: children cannot say whether they are constipated but can report bowel movements per
   day or week, painful defecation and stool form from Bristol pictures (Fig. 47.1); ask how many times
   something happened in a set period rather than how often.
 • Child and parent provide complementary information; the first history sets the team approach — child,
   parent and clinician; families may already be frustrated by earlier neglect.
 • Families volunteer only the most bothersome symptom (nightly bedwetting eclipses intermittent DUI at
   play; urology clinics hear about LUTS over bowel) → assess bowel function in every child with LUTS, even
   unprompted.
 • Assess bother to the child — an unbothered child is hard to engage in behavioural therapy.

Postvoid Residual/Ultrasound
 • Pelvic ultrasound is a key non-invasive tool; bladder scanners assess completeness of emptying, but PVR
   in healthy children is variable → standardise conditions:
      ◦ bladder not under-distended (<50% expected bladder capacity, EBC) or over-distended (>115% EBC);
      ◦ measure within 5 minutes of voiding;
      ◦ ICCS abnormal values depend on single versus repeated measurement and on age.
 • B-mode ultrasound also measures PVR and shows the bladder and surroundings, but the extra
   information largely does not change management; thickened bladder wall is frequent and is associated
   with hypocontractile bladder function.
 • Renal ultrasound is generally not indicated in BBD; however, DVSS ≥12 plus a history of UTI predicts
   abnormal renal–bladder ultrasound.
Uroflowmetry
 • First-line testing for suspected LUT dysfunction: PVR and uroflowmetry.
 • Normal voiding: outlet relaxes, detrusor contracts against minimal urethral resistance → bell-shaped
   curve.
 • Uroflowmetry alone cannot rule dysfunction in or out — abnormal curves occur in healthy children and
   vary between voids → ideally three flows.
 • Conditions: void as normally as possible; boys aim at a fixed point in the funnel; voided volume >50% EBC
   for age or greater than the maximum voided volume on the diary.
 • ICCS interpretation: curve shape (bell, interrupted, plateau, staccato, tower), maximum flow (Qmax),
   average flow (Qave).
 • Refinements for inter-rater reliability: flow index = actual flow rate / flow rate estimated from a bell curve,
   from equations derived in normally voiding children; Societies for Pediatric Urology (SPU) Voiding
   Dysfunction task force: shape smooth or fractionated — fractionated = pause ≥1 second and/or drop in
   velocity exceeding the square root of the preceding Qmax; clinical correlations need study.
Electromyography
 • Electromyography (EMG) complements curve shape and flow rate; in fractionated curves, pelvic floor and
   abdominal EMG distinguish an intermittently relaxing sphincter from abdominal straining.
 • Active pelvic floor EMG during voiding → dysfunctional voiding.
 • EMG lag time (pelvic floor quieting → onset of flow) <2 seconds → detrusor already contracting as the
   sphincter releases → detrusor overactivity.

Standard Urotherapy
 • Non-pharmacological, non-surgical behavioural treatment, largely built on cognitive behavioural therapy
   techniques; first-line treatment for functional LUT dysfunction.
 • Goal: teach the child how to void, when to void and how often to void; several European countries
   require advanced postgraduate training.
 • Just as "eat less and move more" rarely produces weight loss, urotherapy is not simply telling children to
   void more often and avoid constipation.
Elements (ICCS)
 • Information: normal LUT function and how the child's differs.
 • Instruction: how to resolve the dysfunction.
 • Lifestyle advice: fluid intake, diet, regular bladder and bowel emptying.
 • Registration: bladder diaries or mobile apps for self-awareness; the diary becomes feedback as treatment
   progresses.
 • Support and encouragement: regular follow-up with the caregiver.
Specific instructions
 • Toileting posture: hips and knees at about 90°, with foot stool and/or seat insert as needed → pelvic
   floor relaxation.
 • Deep diaphragmatic inhalation and slow exhalation relax the pelvic floor.
 • Regular voiding at least six to seven times a day; for voiding postponement, anchor voids to events
   (before meals, start of school recess, before reading).
 • School toilet environment is associated with LUTS → agree an achievable school voiding plan.
 • Child owns adherence (to developmental level), family supports; operant conditioning rewards
   collaboration, not dryness — the child controls adherence, not incontinent episodes; the child fills in the
   diaries; a timer watch prompts toilet visits rather than repeated reminders from adults.
Duration and outcome
 • ICCS: standard urotherapy for 4 weeks.
 • Meta-analysis: more than half of children with DUI resolve with standard urotherapy, far above the
   spontaneous cure rate.

Overactive Bladder
 • ICCS definition: urinary urgency, usually with frequency and nocturia, with or without incontinence, in
   the absence of UTI or other obvious pathology.
 • OAB incontinence is usually urgency incontinence, but urgency incontinence does not by itself establish
   OAB: children with voiding postponement also leak with a sudden urge; treating them as OAB helps at
   first and later worsens the LUT dysfunction.
Oral Medications
 • Considered only after standard urotherapy and constipation management, according to individual goals.

URINARY INCONTINENCE: ENURESIS
 • Prevalence about 5–10% at 7 years; falls with age, but children with enuresis persisting beyond 10 years
   have significantly more daytime LUTS.
 • Telephone survey: overall prevalence about 2%, with no decline with age in adulthood.
 • Mechanism: mismatch between nocturnal urine production, nocturnal bladder capacity and arousal
   from sleep.
 • Familial; genome-wide association study: risk loci on chromosomes 6 and 13, with candidate genes in
   sleep, urine production and bladder function.

 KEY POINTS: URINARY INCONTINENCE — ENURESIS
   • Enuresis results from a mismatch between nocturnal urine production, bladder storage capacity and
     sleep arousal.
   • Bothersome daytime LUTS are treated before enuresis.
   • Desmopressin and the enuresis alarm are first-line.
   • Neurodevelopmental disorders, sleep-disordered breathing and constipation affect treatment.

Concept — Enuresis is a three-way mismatch: desmopressin reduces output (immediate, not durable), the
alarm trains arousal (slow, durable) and anticholinergics enlarge functional capacity; resistance usually
signals an unaddressed comorbidity — daytime LUTS, constipation or sleep-disordered breathing.

 • Diagnosis rests largely on history and examination.
 • Combined LUT and bowel dysfunction → EAU/ESPU: treat the bowel first.

Disimpaction
 • Disimpaction is the first step. Faecal impaction = an immobile bulk of dry faeces lodged in the lower
   gastrointestinal tract after chronic constipation.
 • Sometimes, not always, palpable as a hard lower abdominal mass; on DRE or radiograph the sign is a
   dilated rectum containing a large amount of stool.
 • Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is the first-choice laxative for disimpaction: poorly absorbed osmotic laxative
   → softer, more frequent stools; side effects minor — flatulence, bloating, nausea/vomiting, abdominal
   cramps.
 • Two forms: PEG 3350 (molecular weight 3350 g/mol) and PEG 4000 (4000 g/mol). In the US PEG 3350 is
   sold with electrolytes (e.g. GoLYTELY) or without (e.g. MiraLAX).
 • Renal dysfunction or other water/electrolyte disturbance → prescribe PEG with caution and monitor
   serum electrolytes.

 • No bowel movement within 24 hours → dose ineffective; stool is accumulating.

 • After failure of laxatives and retrograde enemas, subsequent therapies are invasive.
 • Antegrade continence enema (ACE): irrigant ± laxative via a Malone appendicostomy or percutaneous

   complications — leakage, tube dislodgement, stomal stenosis, skin infection.

   manometry guide management.

 KEY POINTS: FUNCTIONAL CONSTIPATION
   • Disimpaction with a laxative is the first step.
   • Maintenance for at least 2 months prevents faecal re-accumulation.
   • Myths about increased fluid and fibre should not delay more effective treatment.
