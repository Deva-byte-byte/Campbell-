Chapter 154 — Prostate Cancer Biomarkers
INTRODUCTION
 • Prostate cancer has a serum-based, clinically informative biomarker: prostate-specific antigen (PSA),
   discovered 1979, used for detection, staging and monitoring.
 • Pre-PSA era: diagnosis usually at an incurable stage, after an abnormal digital rectal examination (DRE) or
   symptomatic bone metastases. PSA era: most cancers detected by a modest PSA rise at an early, localised
   stage (T1c).
 • Stage migration to low-risk disease has paralleled the fall in prostate cancer mortality, at least partly
   attributable to population-based PSA testing.
 • PSA is prostate specific, not cancer specific → overlap between cancer and benign disease; even marked
   elevation may reflect inflammation or benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) → false positives → excess
   negative biopsies and overdetection of low-grade cancer.
 • European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer (ERSPC) and Göteborg-1: PSA screening
   significantly reduced cancer-specific mortality, but PSA used as an isolated biopsy trigger produced a high
   negative-biopsy rate and overdiagnosis of Gleason score (GS) 6 / Grade Group (GG) 1 cancer.
 • US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), 2012: Grade D recommendation against screening on
   benefit-to-harm grounds.
 • Two advances have since mitigated screening harm:
     ◦ Active surveillance (AS) proven safe and oncologically effective for most low-risk (GS 6, GG 1) cancers
       → less overtreatment, blunting the harm of overdetection.
     ◦ Guidelines advise secondary testing — imaging or biomarkers — before biopsy when PSA is elevated;
       blood and urine biomarkers reduce unnecessary biopsies (negative, or GG 1 only).
 • Three clinical settings for biomarkers:
     ◦ Early detection of clinically significant cancer — before initial biopsy and before repeat biopsy (a
       particularly challenging group); a positive test selects men for imaging or biopsy.
     ◦ Risk stratification of newly diagnosed favourable-risk cancer — AS versus curative therapy (radical
       prostatectomy or radiation).
     ◦ Post-treatment — adjuvant or salvage decisions in men at high risk of recurrence or metastasis.
 • Molecular substrates: DNA (gene) → messenger RNA (mRNA; transcript) → protein → products such as
   metabolites; epigenetic modifications regulating expression are of growing importance.
 • Guiding principle: cancer cells are biologically and molecularly distinct from benign cells; identifying and
   quantifying these differences in tissue or body fluid is the lynchpin of biomarker discovery.
 • Second-generation, cancer-specific markers — PSA isoforms (proPSA), RNA transcripts (PCA3), gene
   fusions (TMPRSS2:ERG) — improve detection of cancer and of clinically significant cancer (GS ≥7 / GG ≥2)
   relative to PSA. Presumed reason: higher-grade tumours are larger, and larger tumours express more
   cancer-specific molecules.
 • Third generation: markers uniquely associated with aggressive, high-grade disease; only recently
   explored.

cancer rather than any cancer.

BLOOD-BASED BIOMARKERS

Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA or hK3)
 • Developed for post-treatment monitoring; now the guideline first-line screening test — affordable and
   accessible, but poorly cancer specific.
Kallikrein family.
 • PSA = human kallikrein 3 (hK3). Family originally thought to be three — pancreatic/renal kallikrein (hK1),
   human kallikrein 2 (hK2), PSA (hK3); now 15 proteins.
 • Genes on the long arm of chromosome 19 (q13.2–q13.4); all encode serine proteases. Homology with
   PSA: hK1 60%, hK2 about 78–80%.
 • Fig. 154.1 (Yousef and Diamandis, 2001): kallikrein locus map, KLK1 to KLK15, with KLK2 (hK2) and KLK3
   (PSA) adjacent.
Synthesis and function.
 • hK2 and PSA are both released as zymogens (inactive proenzymes) from prostatic epithelium, present in
   semen and serum; both complex with α2-macroglobulin and α1-antichymotrypsin.
 • Processing sequence: pre-pro-PSA with 17–amino acid (aa) leader → cleavage of the "pre" sequence →
   proPSA, 244 aa, inactive, carrying a 7-aa "pro" leader → hK2 cleaves the pro leader → active PSA, 237
   aa.
 • Function: androgen-regulated protease that liquefies semen by cleaving the gel-forming proteins
   semenogelin and fibronectin.
 • Identified in the late 1970s; widespread urological use a decade later.
 • Low-level ectopic expression: breast tissue, breast milk, adrenal and renal carcinomas. Produced primarily
   by prostatic luminal epithelial cells → highly organ specific.
Serum forms.
 • About 70% circulates bound — complexed PSA (cPSA) — to α1-antichymotrypsin (ACT), α2-
   macroglobulin (A2M) or α1-protease inhibitor (API); the remainder is free PSA (fPSA).
 • Fig. 154.2: molecular forms — free PSA (proPSA and clipped forms, benign PSA, intact PSA) versus
   complexed PSA (PSA-ACT, PSA-A2M, PSA-API).

 • Pattern: "total PSA" on a standard assay = free PSA + PSA-ACT + PSA-API; PSA-A2M is invisible to most
   assays.
 • Trap: A2M-bound PSA is the active complex yet the undetectable one; ACT-bound PSA is inactive yet
   detectable.
Androgen dependence.
 • PSA expression is strongly androgen dependent. Immunohistochemical PSA in the prostate peaks
   bimodally — 0–6 months of age and after 10 years — tracking testosterone; serum PSA becomes
   detectable at puberty with rising luteinising hormone and testosterone.
Causes of elevation.
 • Mechanism: disruption of glandular architecture — loss of the basal-cell layer and basement-membrane
   barrier allows PSA to escape into the circulation.
 • Causes: cancer, BPH, inflammation, prostate manipulation (massage, biopsy).
 • Prostate biopsy: rise persists 4 weeks or more.
 • Long-distance cycling (>55 km): rise of about 10%.
 • Ejaculation: most studies show no significant change; in men ≥50, a transient rise, back to baseline
   within 24 hours → ask about sexual activity within 24 hours of a new elevation; if present, abstain before
   repeating.
 • Prostate disease (cancer, BPH, prostatitis) is by far the most important factor; BPH and prostatitis are
   the primary confounders of PSA screening.
 • Per cell, cancer expresses less PSA than benign tissue — but cellular expression ≠ serum level: per cubic
   centimetre, Gleason pattern 3 ≈ benign tissue, whereas Gleason pattern 4 contributes several-fold more
   serum PSA than pattern 3, and more still than benign tissue.
 • Without cancer, PSA varies with age, race and prostate volume.
Medications.
 • Prostate-directed treatment (BPH or cancer) lowers PSA by reducing epithelial volume and PSA
   production per cell.
 • 5α-reductase inhibitors (5ARI) — finasteride, dutasteride — lower PSA by about 50% after 12 months →
   "doubling rule": multiply measured PSA by 2.
 • Doubling overestimates PSA in the first 6 months and underestimates it after several years.
 • Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial (PCPT) and other data: 5ARI therapy substantially improves the
   screening performance of PSA.

Concept — PSA rises when glandular architecture breaks down, not when cells make more of it: this is why
cancer, BPH, inflammation and instrumentation all raise it, why it is organ- rather than cancer-specific, and
why volume-adjusted density outperforms the raw value.

Free PSA
 • 5–45% of serum PSA is free and enzymatically inactive.
 • Cancer-derived PSA escapes proteolytic processing → more complexed to ACT → cancer is associated
   with a lower percentage free PSA (%fPSA).
 • US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval: total PSA 4–10 ng/mL with a negative DRE.
 • Pre-biopsy thresholds: 18% improved discrimination over total PSA; thresholds of 14–28% avoid a
   substantial share of unnecessary biopsies while keeping sensitivity high. In a prospective multi-
   institutional study (men 50–75, PSA 4–10, benign DRE), %fPSA 25% detected 95% of cancers with a higher
   area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC) than total PSA.
 • No single established threshold; proposed values 15–25%.
 • National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN): lower %fPSA → greater risk of high-grade cancer;
   consider %fPSA to improve specificity when evaluating for repeat biopsy.
 • Risk calculators — PCPT and Prostate Biopsy Collaborative Group (PBCG) — incorporate fPSA into
   calibrated estimates for shared decision-making.
 • Fig. 154.4: web-based PCPT risk calculator (University of Texas Health Science Center at San Antonio);
   nomograms output risk of high-grade and of any cancer.
 • 5ARI lowers fPSA in parallel with total PSA → %fPSA is not significantly altered.
 • Commonest clinical use: elevated PSA after a previous negative biopsy. Rebiopsy triggers of 21% (low
   underdiagnosis) and 28% (95% sensitivity) proposed; European repeat-biopsy data conflict — one cohort
   outperformed PSA and PCA3, another performed near chance → need for other markers.
 • Limitations: well-studied inverse association with any cancer, but not validated for GG ≥2 as
   contemporary markers are; reports give any-grade risk, which grossly overestimates clinically significant
   risk → some institutions remove any-grade estimates from reports.
Concept — Free PSA exploits a processing defect: cancer PSA leaks out unprocessed and binds ACT, so the
free fraction falls. Being a ratio, it survives 5ARI; being validated against any cancer, it underperforms for GG
≥2.

ProPSA
 • [–7]proPSA = 237 aa of mature PSA + 7-aa pro-leader; hK2 cleaves the leader to activate PSA. Incomplete
   cleavage → truncated [–5], [–4] and [–2]proPSA, primarily peripheral zone.
 • With cellular disruption these inactive forms circulate as free PSA and may form the majority of
   circulating free PSA in cancer.
 • Truncated forms markedly elevated in cancer tissue; [–2]proPSA (p2PSA), cleaved between leucine 5 and
   serine 6 of the propeptide, is the most promising serum marker.
 • Biology unclear: reduced PSA processing in cancer → relative excess of proPSA, especially p2PSA;
   alternatively, proPSA may be produced by benign tissue adjacent to cancer.
 • Extensively validated before initial and repeat biopsy: %p2PSA an independent predictor of cancer and
   of GS ≥7 (PSA 2–10); in the US National Cancer Institute (NCI) Early Detection Research Network (EDRN)
   validation, it outperformed PSA and %fPSA and correlated with rising Gleason score. Combined with fPSA
   and total PSA (tPSA) as the PHI.
 • Fig. 154.5: proPSA released with 7-aa leader (APLILSR) → hK2 cleavage → active PSA → proteolysis to
   iPSA, or internal nicking to BPSA; partial leader cleavage (ILSR, SR) → [–4] and [–2]proPSA.
 • Fig. 154.6: normal versus cancer — in cancer, loss of tissue architecture permits a relative increase in
   bound PSA and proPSA in serum.

Benign PSA
 • Identical to mature PSA except for internal cleavages at Lys145 and Lys182; largely confined to the
   transition zone, highly expressed in BPH.
 • Tracks prostate volume; no capacity to separate indolent from aggressive disease; alone unlikely to
   distinguish BPH from cancer, possibly useful in combination.

Intact PSA
 • Further fPSA subset; intact-to-free ratio may improve detection. Some assays also capture proPSA,
   confounding its independent value. Used mainly as a component of the 4Kscore.
Concept — The free fraction is not one molecule: where PSA is processed determines what it signals —
peripheral-zone clipped proPSA points to cancer, transition-zone BPSA points to BPH.

IsoPSA
 • Structure-based rather than concentration-based assay: partitions cancer-linked PSA isoforms in an
   aqueous two-phase reagent system.

 • Output K = ratio of complexed PSA in the bottom phase to the top phase; used as binary, or converted
   by logistic regression to KR, the individual probability (0–100%) of GG ≥2 cancer.
 • Higher sensitivity and specificity than total PSA for high-grade versus low-grade or benign disease,
   including with equivocal MRI.
 • Unaffected by 5ARI or α-blockers.
 • One of six commercially available biomarkers in the 2023 NCCN Prostate Cancer Early Detection
   guideline for risk stratification before biopsy.

4Kscore Test
 • Four kallikreins — tPSA, fPSA, iPSA and hK2 — plus age, prior biopsy status and DRE findings →
   probability of GG ≥2 cancer on biopsy (0–100%). hK2 is the unique component; it activates proPSA by
   cleaving the pro-leader.

 • Serum hK2 associated with cancer; hK2 with fPSA:tPSA improved detection in tPSA 2–10; hK2 also

 • Development: Göteborg arm of ERSPC (PSA ≥3.0) — high discrimination. External validation: Rotterdam

   cancers; at prostatectomy, the kallikreins independently predicted aggressive pathology.
 • Caveat: initial cohorts had lower GG ≥2 prevalence than contemporary biomarker-tested populations;
   they nonetheless founded later validation.
 • US biopsy-referral validation: 30–58% fewer biopsies across thresholds of 6–15%, with few GG ≥2 cancers
   missed. Meta-analysis: all four markers contribute; adding the 4Kscore raises clinical-model AUC by about
   0.11.
 • Reported cut-off 7.5%: sensitivity 94%, negative predictive value (NPV) 95% across two large prospective
   validations.
 • Unique: low long-term risk of metastasis or prostate cancer death with a 4Kscore ≤7.5% (two large
   observational studies).
 • One of the six NCCN 2023 biomarkers.

Prostate Health Index
 • PHI = (p2PSA ÷ fPSA) × √tPSA; single score (≥0) associated with likelihood of cancer on biopsy.
 • Multicentre study, PSA 2–10 with normal DRE: PHI outperformed each of its components; subsequent
   literature consistently better than PSA-based models.
 • GG ≥2 (since 2015): better than PSA plus age and than PSA density in initial biopsy. Proposed thresholds:
   24 (high sensitivity, about 30% of unnecessary biopsies avoided) and 28.6 (90% sensitivity, about 30%
   avoided).
 • Limitations: no single validated threshold; reports give value ranges against any-grade cancer, not GG ≥2.

URINE-BASED BIOMARKERS

PCA3
 • Urine is easy to collect and consistently carries shed prostate cells. First urinary marker, identified 1999 by
   differential display on chromosome 9q21–22; named prostate cancer antigen 3 (PCA3) — a long non-
   coding RNA (lncRNA) expressed in cancer far above benign tissue.
 • Assays: reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) → transcription-mediated
   amplification (TMA), with better sensitivity and quantitation.
 • Requires urine collected after an attentive DRE; score = (urine PCA3 mRNA ÷ urine KLK3 mRNA) × 1000.
 • Independent of prostate size — the contrast with PSA.
 • Thresholds most used: 10, 25, 35; above 35, markedly higher biopsy detection than below. The
   appropriate threshold depends on the population, especially previous biopsy status.
 • Nomograms: PCA3 improves a base clinical model (proposed cut-off 17); updated PCPT calculator
   includes PCA3 as a continuous variable, outperforming the base PCPT model (see Fig. 154.4).
 • Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality review: threshold 25 gives moderate sensitivity and
   specificity; PCA3 appears superior to total PSA, but no evidence yet of better health outcomes.
 • FDA approved (United States) and Conformité Européenne (CE) marked (Europe) for men with a
   previous negative biopsy.
 • No longer available as an isolated test; a key component of two NCCN urine assays (EPI and MPS).

SelectMDx
 • RT-PCR of post-DRE urinary mRNA of distal-less homeobox 1 (DLX1) and homeobox C6 (HOXC6),
   combined with age, DRE, PSA and prostate volume → probability of any cancer and of GG ≥2.
 • Derived from three urinary markers (DLX1, HOXC6, Tudor domain-containing protein 1, TDRD1); the mRNA
   added modestly to a strong clinical model. Over a quarter of the validation cohort had PSA ≥10 —
   unrepresentative of the usual biomarker population.
 • Biopsy-naïve validation: high sensitivity (about 93%) and NPV (about 95%) for GG ≥2, maintained for PSA
   ≤10.
 • Limitation: needs prostate volume, available only after transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) or MRI; without it,
   sensitivity, specificity and NPV all fall.
 • One of the six NCCN 2023 biomarkers.

ExoDx Prostate IntelliScore
 • ExoDx Prostate IntelliScore (EPI): exosomal RNA of PCA3, ERG (ETS transcription factor) and SAM pointed
   domain-containing ETS transcription factor (SPDEF) → score for GG ≥2.
 • Exosomes: small vesicles shed into body fluids containing DNA, RNA and protein → urinary markers
   measurable without a DRE. Early work detected PCA3 and TMPRSS2:ERG in urinary exosomes.
 • Predecessor EXO106 (PCA3 + ERG): threshold 10, high sensitivity and NPV in PSA 2–10; adding clinical
   variables improved discrimination.

 • EPI uses no clinical factors. Initial biopsy, PSA 2–20: threshold 15.6 → about 92% sensitivity, NPV about
   91%; about one-third of unnecessary biopsies avoided, about 8% of GG ≥2 missed; replicated in a second
   biopsy-naïve cohort.
 • Repeat biopsy: 15.6 lost sensitivity; threshold 20 kept sensitivity with better specificity and NPV →
   thresholds are population specific.
 • One of the six NCCN 2023 biomarkers.

MyProstateScore
 • MyProstateScore (MPS; formerly Mi-Prostate Score, MiPS): urinary PCA3 + TMPRSS2:ERG (T2:ERG) +
   serum PSA → continuous score 0–100 for GG ≥2.
 • TMPRSS2:ERG (landmark discovery, 2005): fusion of the 5′ untranslated region of the androgen-
   regulated transmembrane protease, serine 2 (TMPRSS2) gene with the E26 transformation-specific (ETS)
   transcription factors ERG or ETS variant 1 (ETV1); nearly 100% specific for prostate cancer.
 • Detectable in post-DRE urine (2008); TMA assay (2011) outperformed PSA; higher urinary score at
   prostatectomy → larger, higher-grade, non-organ-confined tumours.
 • Present in about half of tumours but about 75% of men with cancer (multifocality) → best combined
   with PCA3; the combination roughly doubled specificity for GG ≥2 (17% to 33%) while holding sensitivity
   at 95%.
 • Locked MPS model: better discrimination for GG ≥2 than PSA or the PCPT calculator; across thresholds
   missed ≤2.3% of GG ≥2 cancers.
 • Initial biopsy: threshold 10 → 97% sensitivity, 98% NPV; about one-third of unnecessary biopsies avoided,
   3% of GG ≥2 missed; confirmed in NCCN-eligible men, Black men and men with a suspicious DRE.
 • Repeat biopsy: threshold 15 (set for 90% target sensitivity) → 100% sensitivity and NPV on external
   validation.
 • One of the six NCCN 2023 biomarkers.

miRNA
 • Most genomic DNA does not encode protein. MicroRNA (miRNA): small non-coding RNA (sncRNA),
   usually 19–22 nucleotides, regulating gene expression → potential diagnostic and prognostic markers
   across cancers.
 • miR-141: associated with cancer and aggressive disease. Urine miR-187: independent predictor of cancer
   at biopsy.
 • Multi-sncRNA panels are promising but have no peer-reviewed clinical data yet.

TISSUE-BASED BIOMARKERS

Epigenetic Modifications
 • Epigenetic change alters gene expression without altering DNA sequence — DNA methylation and
   histone acetylation.
 • CpG islands: GC-rich promoter segments; altered methylation changes expression and contributes to
   carcinogenesis. Lifelong exposures (diet, stress) may shift methylation and cancer risk.
 • Hypermethylated genes implicated: glutathione-S-transferase π (GSTP1), adenomatous polyposis coli
   (APC), retinoic acid receptor β2 (RARβ2), RAS association domain family protein isoform A (RASSF1A).
 • Field ("halo") effect: methylation is detectable in histologically normal tissue adjacent to tumour, not in
   distant normal tissue → methylation in a negative biopsy suggests occult cancer.
 • APC methylation in negative biopsies predicted cancer at repeat biopsy with high sensitivity and NPV.
 • ConfirmMDx (MDxHealth): quantitative methylation of GSTP1, APC and RASSF1; independent predictor
   of cancer on repeat biopsy; NPV about 88–90%; in Black men, NPV about 94% for GS ≥7.
Concept — Methylation markers turn a negative biopsy into evidence: because the field effect extends into
benign tissue beside the tumour, a core that missed the cancer can still carry its signature — hence their role
before repeat biopsy.

Genomic Expression Profiles
 • Multigene panels outperform any single gene; run on archival formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE)
   tissue; precedent in chemotherapy-response classifiers for breast and colorectal cancer. Available as
   laboratory-developed tests (LDT).

 • Post-Operative Radiation Therapy Outcomes Score (PORTOS): 24-gene signature derived from the

Germline Genetics and Prostate Cancer
 • Up to 40–50% of prostate cancer risk may be familial or hereditary. Familial cancer (15–20% of cases)
   includes strong family history without a detectable mutation.
 • An affected relative raises risk two- to fourfold.
 • Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs): common variants, not necessarily functional; more than four
   risk SNPs → 4.5-fold risk, tenfold with a family history.
 • STHLM3: the four 4Kscore kallikreins + 232 risk SNPs + clinical variables → composite early-detection test
   for GS ≥7 (AUC 0.74).
 • Most heritable genes are DNA damage repair genes.

Concept — Hereditary prostate cancer is predominantly a failure of DNA damage repair, and the genes that
matter most clinically — BRCA2 above all — are those that raise aggressiveness, not merely incidence.

SUMMARY
 • Early detection while cancer is organ confined improves cure rates and lowers morbidity and mortality.
 • PSA revolutionised detection, but stage migration and a changed natural history have outrun it; PSA
   derivatives improve sensitivity at the risk of specificity.
 • Blood, urine and tissue markers now improve detection and risk stratification; panels will probably
   overcome multifocality and heterogeneity.
 • Translation requires proof of clinical validity and clinical utility — real impact on care.

 KEY POINTS
   • PSA = hK3, human kallikrein family; PSA and hK2 both used in detection.
   • Organ specific, not cancer specific; produced by prostatic epithelium; half-life 2–3 days.
   • Gram for gram, cancer produces less PSA than benign tissue; serum rise reflects disrupted
     architecture; also BPH, prostatitis, recent manipulation.
   • About 70% of serum PSA bound (ACT, A2M, API); cancer → higher bound fraction, lower free PSA.
   • Pre-pro-PSA → [–7]proPSA (237 + 7 aa) → hK2 cleavage → active PSA; incomplete cleavage → [–2], [–
     4], [–5]proPSA, which augment detection.
   • 5ARI reduce serum PSA by 50%.
   • Circulating tumour cells (CTCs) and circulating tumour DNA are promising marker sources, particularly
     in advanced disease.
   • PCA3: urinary non-coding RNA; combined with the highly specific TMPRSS2:ERG fusion for greater
     accuracy.
