 Outcomes
  • Outcomes research has grown over 25 years but is an umbrella term without a consistent definition;
    one view: the effectiveness of public-health interventions and health services on patient outcomes.
  • Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ): research into the end results of healthcare
    practices — effects patients experience and care about (e.g., ability to function); in chronic disease,
    where cure is not always possible, quality of life as well as mortality. Linking care received to outcomes
    makes it the key to monitoring and improving quality of care.
  • Urological end points are many — quality of life, clinical effectiveness, cost, quality of care,
    preferences, appropriateness, access, health status — hence the difficulty of definition.
  • Health services research (HSR), often used interchangeably: multidisciplinary study of how social
    factors, financing, organisational structures and processes, technologies and personal behaviour affect
    access, quality, cost and ultimately health. It misses the clinical side → urological outcomes research =
    HSR + clinical epidemiology + comparative effectiveness research + (partly) clinical trials.

 LONG-TERM DISEASE OUTCOMES THAT ARE COMMONLY ASSESSED IN UROLOGY
  • Patients and urologists focus on outcomes. Surgeons favour morbidity and mortality — the "hardest",
    theoretically easiest end points; patients and other stakeholders add "softer" end points: patient-
    reported outcomes (PROs — symptoms, bother), economics, satisfaction.

  • Expression: count, ratio, proportion or rate; proportion or rate suits clinical studies. A proportion
    ignores time → mortality rate (proportion dying over a specified period) preferred. Example: in a trial
    of second- or third-line therapy for metastatic castration-resistant CaP, all may die during the study →
    compare 1-, 2- and 5-year rates.
  • Time-to-event (survival) analysis — usable for any binary end point; handles varying follow-up and
    loss to follow-up through censoring: a participant known to be event-free up to last follow-up, study
    end, or a competing event that makes the outcome impossible (e.g., fatal unrelated myocardial
    infarction before recurrence) "survives" to that point and is then censored.
  • Kaplan–Meier curves display survival estimates at time points incorporating both events and censoring.
  • Objective ≠ unbiased: design introduces lead-time and length-time bias.

 Disease-Specific Mortality
  • Overall mortality is the hardest but crudest end point: it ignores intercurrent illness and is not germane
    in benign or low-mortality disease → disease-specific mortality.
  • Definition: deaths attributed directly to the disease — harder to define than it appears.
  • Perioperative deaths are instinctively not attributed to the disease, yet arguably are disease- and
    treatment-related. In an early-PSA-era Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) analysis,
    most deaths within a month of CaP surgery were attributed to other causes; reattribution would
    noticeably raise disease-specific mortality in a low-mortality cancer.
  • Medical treatment: ADT raises cardiovascular risk and death — is a cardiac death on ADT a CaP death?
    Pneumonia and sepsis in an immobile metastatic patient — cancer-related or not?
  • Attribution bias — the greatest limitation: a documented cancer is often written on the death
    certificate even when the death is unrelated.
      ◦ CaP: death certificates agree with independent chart review in most cases, but disagree in about
        one in ten; similar across several US regions.
      ◦ Urinary tract tumours (SEER): a substantial share of deaths attributed to non-urological causes;
        more advanced stage → more often attributed to cancer; site matters — renal pelvic tumours far
        less often recorded as cancer deaths than similar-stage kidney tumours.
      ◦ Studies examine in-hospital deaths only; deaths at home or in nursing facilities are harder still.
  • Bottom line: usually reliable, but attribution bias and misclassification can alter conclusions.
  • PSMA-PET: high sensitivity for recurrent or oligometastatic disease even at low PSA, especially after
    biochemical recurrence → changes management in a majority (ADT or systemic therapy, salvage
    prostate-bed radiotherapy, metastasis-directed ablation). Earlier detection than CT and bone
    scintigraphy makes progression-free survival (PFS) appear longer because (1) smaller-volume disease
    has a different natural history and better treatment response, and (2) earlier detection with
    reclassification to a higher stage without changing outcome — stage migration (Will Rogers
    phenomenon) → PFS improves, overall survival does not.

 Other Binary "Survival" Outcomes
  • Any binary outcome can become a survival end point analysed by Kaplan–Meier and proportional
    hazards: metastasis-free, radiological progression-free, symptom-free, biochemical-free survival.
  • Used in benign disease and in CaP, where deaths are rare or late; relevant outcomes differ by disease.
  • Objectivity varies: urodynamics often fails to reproduce stress or urgency incontinence, and no agreed
    leakage threshold defines incontinence; patient-reported alternatives (pad use, symptom scores) are
    less "objective" than radiological or serum measures.

 Proxy End Points
  • Mortality takes years and is almost irrelevant in benign conditions (incontinence, stones) → clinically
    relevant or proxy end points.
  • Prentice criteria (1989) for a valid surrogate: (1) treatment associated with the true end point (overall
    or disease-specific survival); (2) treatment associated with the surrogate; (3) surrogate associated with
    the true end point; (4) the surrogate explains the full effect of treatment on the true end point.
  • Few urological proxies meet all four.

 Disease Progression/Recurrence
  • PFS is a common oncological proxy; research requires standardised radiological definitions of change
    in tumour burden.
  • Evolution: World Health Organization criteria (1981) → study-by-study modifications, confusion and
    conflicting results → European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC), US
    National Cancer Institute (NCI) and National Cancer Institute of Canada working group → Response
    Evaluation Criteria In Solid Tumours (RECIST), 2000: minimum measurable size, up to ten lesions (five
    per organ), unidimensional measurement.
  • Limitations of RECIST: (1) response only, poor measure of progression; (2) no magnetic resonance
    imaging (MRI) or PET; (3) no lymph nodes; (4) cannot assess targeted non-cytotoxic drugs → RECIST 1.1
    (2009).
  • RECIST 1.1 measurable lesion: ≥10 mm on CT, 20 mm on chest radiograph, 10 mm clinically with
    callipers; lymph node ≥15 mm short axis on CT. Not ultrasound; not tumour markers alone — except
    PSA response (Prostate Cancer Clinical Trials Working Group), usable alongside RECIST.
  • Target lesions: one to five, the largest, representative of involved organs and reproducibly measurable;
    their sum of diameters is followed from baseline → radiological PFS, duration of response, overall
    response rate.

  • What it tests: the reference points differ — response is measured against baseline, progression

  • RECIST standardises progression but does not remove detection bias (see bias table).
  • Observer variability: even for stone burden on CT, radiologists disagreed with one another and with

    assumed; hence central review of imaging or pathology in prospective trials.

 Intermediate Clinical End Points in Cancer of the Prostate
  • CaP's long natural history → overall-survival trials can exceed a decade, and many relapsing men never
    die of CaP → a valid intermediate clinical end point (ICE) accelerates drug development and saves
    resources.
  • Intermediate Clinical End Points in Cancer of the Prostate (ICECaP) working group tested PSA-based
    ICEs (time to biochemical failure, PSA doubling time, PSA nadir, end-of-treatment PSA) and metastasis-
    free survival (MFS).

 Receipt of Secondary Therapy
  • Reported in malignant and benign conditions; looks objective but is biased.
  • Adjuvant or secondary? Radiotherapy or hormonal therapy after surgery for high-risk disease is
    ambiguous.
  • Subjective initiation: men with biochemical recurrence after radical prostatectomy often elect
    hormonal therapy or radiotherapy although the "recurrence" may not be clinically meaningful —
    "discretionary" treatment.
 Concept — The harder the end point, the slower and cruder it is; every proxy trades time for validity, the
 Prentice criteria test whether the trade was honest, and in prostate cancer only metastasis-free survival
 has passed.

 Patient-Reported Outcomes Version of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events
  • Clinician-reported adverse events and patient-reported quality of life diverge: patients under-report
    because of time pressure, not wishing to complain, and fear of losing eligibility, dose reduction or
    removal from protocol.
  • Use CTCAE grades for clinical decisions; use PRO-CTCAE to describe aggregate safety and tolerability.

 Assessing Risk for Surgical Complications
  • Beyond procedure-specific factors, three characteristics predict complications across all surgery:
    functional status, comorbidity and frailty.

 Functional Status
  • Definition: ability to perform normal daily activities to meet basic needs, fulfil roles and maintain
    health — effectively the ADLs.
  • Six essential ADLs: eating, dressing, walking or transferring, bathing, toileting, continence.
  • Matters in urological oncology trials for inclusion criteria and outcomes.
  • Karnofsky Performance Scale (KPS, 1949): clinician-scored 100% (normal) to 0% (dead); acceptable
    inter- and intraobserver reliability.
  • Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scale: 0 (fully active) to 5 (dead); performs as well as
    KPS.
  • Future: wearable technology for real-time functional data.

 Comorbidity
  • Term coined by Feinstein (1970): any ailment coexisting with the studied disease.
  • Comorbidity influences treatment choice → confounder; ignoring it causes selection bias.
  • Classic example — TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate): a large administrative-data study
    found higher long-term mortality after TURP than open prostatectomy without controlling for
    comorbidity; a chart-review study adjusting for age and comorbidity found no difference in 5-year
    mortality, later confirmed.
  • Charlson Comorbidity Index — best known; weighted sum; developed to predict 1-year mortality in
    medical inpatients, later 3–5-year mortality after general surgery; short form and an administrative-
    data adaptation exist; other validated scales also used.

 Frailty
  • Definition: clinically recognisable vulnerability from ageing-related decline in reserve across multiple
    systems → more falls, hospitalisation, disability, death.
  • Fried phenotype: frail = ≥3 of 5 — low grip strength, low energy, slow walking, low physical activity,
    unintentional weight loss. Independently predicted falls, functional decline, hospitalisation and death
    over 3 years (Cardiovascular Health Study); later predicted postoperative complications, length of stay
    and discharge to a skilled nursing facility.
  • Correlates with outcome after cystectomy, prostatectomy and prolapse surgery → control for it in non-
    randomised studies.
  • Criticism: Fried's model is essentially sarcopenia; Charlson and the American Society of
    Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification have not reliably predicted postoperative morbidity and mortality.

 PATIENT-REPORTED OUTCOMES
  • Many interventions aim to relieve symptoms, but symptom assessment differs from health-related
    quality of life (HRQoL).
  • Symptoms are the physical manifestations of disease, assessed in ways that suit the condition.
  • Clinicians cannot measure patient-centred outcomes from the consultation: patients minimise to
    please, physicians miss severity. Compared with a validated survey, physicians consistently
    underestimated urinary, sexual and bowel dysfunction, fatigue and bone pain → collect directly from
    patients.

 Concept — A symptom score counts what is wrong; an HRQoL instrument asks how much it matters — and
 only the patient can answer either reliably.

 METHODS OF ASSESSING PATIENT-REPORTED OUTCOMES
  • Questionnaires (surveys, instruments) are preferred to researcher-administered interviews, which are
    time- and labour-intensive; instruments are cheap and practical but need rigorous development.

  • Development sequence: define the PROs → generate and assemble items (physicians, patients,
    epidemiologists, psychologists; literature review for existing validated tools) → pilot for readability and
    redundancy → psychometric analysis.
  • Response formats: Likert (categorical options — used by most PROs, easier to answer and capture)
    versus VAS (continuum).
  • Item rules: below an eighth-grade reading level, no abbreviations or complex terms, one question per
    item.
  • Breadth (domains) versus depth (items per domain): balance for patient burden, favouring breadth
    when the impact of disease or treatment is uncertain.

 Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System
  • PROMIS, from the NIH: standardised, flexible PRO measures; common T-score metric from item
    response theory; rigorous psychometrics with patient and clinician input.
  • Computer adaptive tests (CATs): questions tailored to prior answers → lower burden, higher precision;
    fixed-length short forms where computers are limited.
  • Core domains: fatigue, pain intensity and interference, physical function, sleep disturbance, anxiety,
    depression, social participation; added domains include sexual function, urinary incontinence and
    psychosocial health. The Sexual Function and Satisfaction measure is sensitive to change in men with
    CaP; PROMIS is reliable and responsive for stone pain, sleep disturbance in overactive bladder and
    bladder cancer quality of life.
  • Advantages: free, non-proprietary; customisable; point-of-care administration with instant scoring;
    multilingual (cross-cultural comparison); adult and paediatric norms → growing use, e.g., the CISTO
    study (Comparison of Intravesical Therapy and Surgery as Treatment Options for bladder cancer).

 Specific Symptom Scales
 Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms
  • Lower urinary tract symptom (LUTS) indices were developed for benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) in
    men but are valid in both sexes.
  • International Prostate Symptom Score (I-PSS) — the best LUTS scale; seven AUA Symptom Index items
    (I-PSS adds a single quality-of-life item); measures severity well but not impact on quality of life →
    paired with the BPH Impact Index, which correlates with SF-36 general and mental health indices. Both
    used in large randomised trials.
  • Choice of scale follows the research question.

 Urinary Incontinence
  • Office and urodynamic documentation is often infeasible and artificial: failure to demonstrate leakage
    in clinic does not mean the patient is dry at home or work.
  • Pad weight test: more quantifiable during usual activities, but confounded by how pads are used and

    are more reproducible. Cannot distinguish stress from urgency or capture bother → PROs are
    required.

 Sexual Dysfunction
  • Difficulties: sex differences preclude common end points; function often judged with a partner
    (problem without a regular partner or with chosen inactivity); embarrassment limits honest reporting;
    function is multidimensional — libido, arousal, erection, ejaculation/orgasm.
  • "Objective" tests (nocturnal penile tumescence, duplex Doppler ultrasonography) occur in clinical
    settings unlike home and miss psychogenic dysfunction → PROs are crucial.
  • Men: more than 20 validated instruments, mostly partner-dependent. Erectile Dysfunction Inventory
    of Treatment Satisfaction (EDITS) needs no partner but cannot be used before treatment or without
    treatment. No perfect tool — choose by setting and question.
  • International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) — gold standard, 15 items; its five erection items form
    the Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM), which omits bother and so is not an HRQoL measure.

 Health-Related Quality of Life
  • HRQoL: the parts of life affected by health status — broad, multidimensional, hard to define.
    Descriptions: the patient's appraisal of current functioning against the possible or ideal; the functional,
    psychological, social and economic impact of care. Calman: the gap between expectations and
    experience.
  • Components: health perceptions, function, preferences, satisfaction with care; broader well-being also
    depends on food, shelter, response to illness and work.
  • Must include objective function and bother.

  • Used as an outcome in urological studies; general satisfaction surveys exist, few urology-specific.

   KEY POINTS
    • Quality is measured by structure, process and outcome; structure and process are easier, outcome
      most meaningful.
    • Mortality is hardest but biased: lead- and length-time bias for overall mortality, attribution bias for
      disease-specific mortality.
    • Few proxy end points meet all four surrogate criteria, yet they are used routinely.
    • Standard progression and complication systems should be used, but do not remove design bias.
    • Frailty, functional status and comorbidity are confounders with standard tools.
    • Use validated, reliable patient-centred instruments whenever possible.
