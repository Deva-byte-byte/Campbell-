Chapter 114 — Conservative Management of Urinary Incontinence

Chapter 114 — Conservative Management of Urinary
Incontinence: Behavioural and Pelvic Floor Therapy,
Urethral and Pelvic Devices
 • Conservative treatment of urinary incontinence (UI) and associated lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS)
   is any therapy that does not involve surgery or pharmacotherapy.
 • Behavioural treatments are the mainstay of conservative treatment because they are effective and
   essentially risk free, and several guidelines and consensus panels recommend them as first-line therapy.
 • Definition: behavioural treatments improve UI and LUTS by changing the patient's behaviour or
   environment, or by teaching new skills.
 • Programmes are multicomponent and individualised to the patient's condition, needs and environment.
   Possible components are:
      ◦ patient education and self-monitoring with a bladder diary;
      ◦ pelvic floor muscle (PFM) training (PFMT) and exercise, and active PFM contraction to occlude the
        urethra (stress strategies, "the knack");
      ◦ urge prevention and suppression (urge strategies), and bladder training (BT) with urge control
        techniques (distraction, self-affirmation);
      ◦ biofeedback (BF) and electrical stimulation (E-stim);
      ◦ fixed or incremental voiding schedules and delayed voiding;
      ◦ fluid management, caffeine reduction, dietary change and weight loss;
      ◦ teaching normal voiding technique and other lifestyle change.
 • In most urological and primary care settings, programmes are designed and delivered by registered
   nurses and advanced practice providers (APPs) after a simple non-invasive assessment.
 • The three most established programmes are PFMT, behavioural training with urge suppression, and BT.
   They rest on the idea that patients can be educated about their condition and can learn skills that reduce
   or abolish symptoms. They fall into two fundamental approaches.

 • Lifestyle modifications act indirectly. They change behaviour or remove factors that aggravate symptoms
   or make the bladder harder to control: fluid management, caffeine reduction, avoiding bladder irritants,
   managing constipation, smoking cessation and weight loss.
 • Evidence base. A large body of research supports behavioural treatment. Multiple organisations and
   international guidelines recommend it for UI and non-neurogenic overactive bladder (OAB).
 • The International Consultation on Incontinence (ICI) Committee on Adult Conservative Management
   (7th ICI, 2023) grades every conservative treatment (Tables 114.1 and 114.2) using the Oxford grading
   system (Table 114.3).
 • The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) comparative effectiveness review of non-
   surgical treatments (2018) is summarised in Table 114.4.
 • The chapter also covers mechanical devices that prevent urine loss.

Concept — Conservative therapy is first-line not because it is the most effective option (surgery is more
effective for SUI) but because it is risk free and at least as effective as drugs. Every recommendation in these
tables follows from that asymmetry of harm.

INDICATIONS
 • Behavioural interventions are well established for SUI, UUI, MUI and OAB. They are also appropriate for
   voiding dysfunction, although the evidence there is weaker.
 • Most patients improve to some degree, but outcomes vary widely and responders cannot be predicted.
   Because the treatment is virtually risk free, it should be offered first-line to anyone willing and able to
   participate.
 • Even patients with dementia can benefit, from an appropriate caregiver-guided programme.

Assessment Before Behavioural Treatments
 • The most important step is a focused, detailed history: onset, duration, progression, characteristics and
   situational triggers. These determine how the programme is designed and implemented.
 • A 3- to 7-day bladder diary or frequency–volume chart supplements the history (Fig. 114.2 shows a
   completed example).
      ◦ It records the type and amount of fluid intake, the type and frequency of symptoms (UI episodes),
        voiding frequency and volume, associated urgency, and the circumstances of each leakage.
      ◦ Pad type and number can be added to quantify leakage.

 • The diary is the best non-invasive tool for objectively monitoring voiding habits and treatment effect,
   and it guides which treatment components to use.
 • Voided volumes are more burdensome to record but give a practical estimate of functional bladder
   capacity in daily life.
 • Completing the diary has a self-monitoring effect. The patient becomes aware of drinking and voiding
   habits and sees how leakage relates to activities. This is thought to empower retraining, and it shows the
   times and activities of highest risk, so that skills can be used pre-emptively.
 • The Questionnaire-Based Voiding Diary (QVD) is a validated alternative. It self-assesses fluid type and
   volume and UI type, and is completed at the first office visit in 5–7 minutes (Fig. 114.3).

 • A, beverage intake: ten beverage types (water; caffeinated and decaffeinated coffee, tea and soda; milk;

Concept — Assessment before behavioural therapy is aimed at triggers and habits rather than diagnosis. The
diary is simultaneously the assessment tool, the outcome measure and a treatment in its own right through
self-monitoring.

PATIENT EDUCATION
 • Behavioural treatment rests on principles of learning, so the first step is basic education about the
   condition, the treatment process and its goals.
 • Education covers the anatomy and function of the bladder, urethra, vagina or prostate, PFMs and
   rectum, how they interrelate, and the causes and mechanisms of the patient's own condition.
 • The patient must understand that the programme means changing habits and learning skills, and that
   improvement is gradual. Knowing that results depend on active participation and daily practice improves
   adherence and sets realistic expectations.

Pelvic Floor Muscle Training
 • PFMT and exercise, also called pelvic floor rehabilitation, is a cornerstone of behavioural treatment for
   UI, urgency and frequency.
 • History.
      ◦ Margaret Morris (1936) first described it, teaching women to control and exercise the perivaginal
        muscles to strengthen them and reduce SUI.
      ◦ Arnold Kegel (1948), a gynaecologist, popularised PFM exercises (PFME). He proposed that SUI arose
        from lack of awareness of PFM function and coordination, and showed that repetitive contractions to
        build strength reduced SUI.
      ◦ PFMT has since evolved into a combined nurse-directed behavioural treatment and physical therapy.




                                                          page 8 of 33
Chapter 114 — Conservative Management of Urinary Incontinence                                          Patient Education
 • Proposed mechanism in women (causal chain):
      ◦ a contraction raises the urethra and presses it against the symphysis pubis, preventing urethral
        descent and supporting the pelvic organs;
      ◦ training causes hypertrophy of the striated muscle, increasing external mechanical pressure on the
        urethra;
      ◦ intensive PFMT is thought to reinforce bladder neck support, limiting its descent when abdominal
        pressure rises.
 • PFMT has three elements: learning to contract and relax the PFMs; a regular exercise regimen for
   strength and control; and actively contracting to occlude the urethra during activities that raise
   abdominal pressure. The details of regimens vary.

Assessment of Pelvic Floor Muscle Function
 • Baseline assessment of strength, coordination and control uses visual observation (at rest and with
   Valsalva), transvaginal digital palpation, electromyography (EMG), manometry or ultrasound.
 • Visual contrast in women:
      ◦ correct contraction = perineal elevation: inward (ventrocephalad) movement of the vulva, perineum
        and anus;
      ◦ incorrect contraction = perineal descent: excessive dorsocaudal movement.
 • Digital assessment at rest and during contraction is the technique most used in practice.
 • Manual strength scales:
      ◦ the Oxford Grading System is the most commonly used, and adapted versions have been studied;
      ◦ the Brink score is a four-point scale of contraction pressure, vertical displacement and endurance of
        the squeeze.
 • Digital technique (Fig. 114.4):
     1. Insert one or two fingers into the vagina in the mid-sagittal plane for 6–8 cm.
     2. Palpate the levator ani just above the hymeneal ring, at the midline and at 4 and 8 o'clock, for
        strength and for tenderness.
     3. Assess at rest, during contraction and during relaxation: ability to relax, duration and symmetry of
        contraction, muscle bulk, resting tone and strength.
     4. Ask for a maximal contraction held as long as possible, then short fast contractions ("flicks").
     5. Use tactile feedback (tapping, a slight quick stretch) to help identification when the patient cannot find
        the muscle, does not understand how to contract it, or has very weak PFMs.
 • Trap: when asked to contract, women may use the wrong muscles, strain down (Valsalva), or fail to
   activate all layers. The examiner watches for accessory contraction of the gluteal or abdominal muscles.
 • Brink's three criteria, each recorded:
      ◦ pressure, from imperceptible to a firm squeeze;
      ◦ duration in seconds;
      ◦ alteration in position: a well-supported pelvic floor lifts the base of the examiner's finger.
 • Note any co-contraction (abdominal movement, gluteal lifting) and the degree of any PFM overactivity
   (voluntary relaxation absent or partial).

Teaching Pelvic Floor Muscle Control
 • The first step is for the patient to identify the PFMs and contract and relax them reliably. This is taught
   with verbal feedback during vaginal or anal palpation, with visual or auditory BF, or with E-stim.
 • Typical instructions:
      ◦ "Contract around my fingers; pull up and in."
      ◦ "Without tensing legs, buttocks or stomach, tighten the ring around the anus as if stopping gas or a
        stool; feel closing and lifting."
      ◦ Men: "Move the penis up and down without moving anything else."
      ◦ Women: "Feel the vagina and rectum pull up and in."
 • The commonest problem is muscle isolation, and many women lack confidence that they are contracting
   correctly.
      ◦ Patients recruit the rectus abdominis or gluteals instead of, or as well as, the PFMs (co-contraction).
      ◦ Some women substitute a straining Valsalva, which threatens to worsen the very condition being
        treated.
      ◦ Some abdominal contraction is counterproductive because it raises pressure on the bladder and pelvic
        floor.
 • The clinician therefore teaches selective PFM contraction with ancillary muscles relaxed. Telling the
   patient not to hold her breath, or to count aloud, helps avoid bearing down.
 • Some clinicians add transversus abdominis training, believing it facilitates PFM contraction. Evidence on
   whether abdominal or hip training adds efficacy is conflicting, and a scoping review found little evidence
   for the proposed core-strengthening mechanism.
 • Verify correct contraction before starting the exercise regimen. Inability to identify or exercise the PFMs
   correctly may be the commonest reason for poor outcome. Control is a skill that takes time, but most
   patients succeed with repeated training.
 • The old approach was a pamphlet or brief instruction to "lift the pelvic floor" or interrupt the stream. It
   suffices for some patients but does not ensure the correct muscles are used at home.
Concept — The usual point of failure in PFMT is muscle identification, not exercise dose. Every teaching step
exists to separate a true PFM lift from co-contraction and Valsalva.

Pelvic Floor Muscle Exercise Regimens
 • Once correct contraction and relaxation are shown, daily exercise has two purposes: strength and motor
   skill.
 • The ideal regimen is not established. The ICI could make no robust recommendation on generic versus
   individualised exercise, submaximal versus near-maximal contraction, daily versus three times weekly, or
   adding upright positions. Good results have come from 45–60 paired contractions and relaxations per
   day.
 • The authors use an "exercise prescription" (Box 114.2):
      ◦ quick flicks of 1–2 seconds, then endurance contractions of 5 seconds building to 10 seconds;
      ◦ high concentrated effort on each contraction, because greater intensity is associated with greater
        strength gain.
 • Relax completely between contractions, with a work-to-rest ratio of 1:1 or 1:2, to allow recovery and
   optimise strength building.
 • Spread the exercises over 2–3 sessions a day to avoid fatigue.
 • Start reclining, the least challenging position, then progress to sitting and standing so that the skill works
   in any position.

Use of Pelvic Floor Muscle Contraction to Prevent Stress Urinary Incontinence
 • The goal is to prevent leakage in daily life by volitionally occluding the urethra with a PFM contraction.
   Exercise alone improves urethral pressure and support; this additional motor skill lets the patient close
   the urethra consciously when loss is imminent.
 • The history or diary identifies the circumstances of leakage. The patient then learns to anticipate them
   and contract before and during coughing, sneezing, lifting or any other precipitating activity.
 • This anticipatory contraction is called the stress strategy or "the knack". An anticipatory contraction
   reduces the volume of cough-related leakage immediately.
 • It needs vigilance and conscious effort at first but eventually becomes automatic.

Evidence for Pelvic Floor Muscle Training
 • A very large body of evidence supports PFMT.
      ◦ Cochrane review: effective for SUI and MUI and can reduce urgency; women with pure SUI may do
        best.
      ◦ PFMT beats no treatment, placebo drug or inactive control for SUI, UUI and MUI, on cure, QoL, daily
        leakage episodes and pad test leakage, both immediately and long term.
 • The chapter's pooled figures are cure 16–27% and improvement 48–80.7%.
 • The best approach is unknown, but there is consensus that supervised PFMT is more effective than
   unsupervised. The degree and type of supervision needed are uncertain.
 • The effect is enhanced by sound training principles: specificity, overload progression, confirmation of
   correct contraction before training, and active use of the PFM to occlude the urethra (the knack).
 • Age is not a deterrent. The effect in SUI does not diminish with age; outcomes in older women match
   those in younger women. Therefore supervised PFMT is first-line for women of all ages with SUI, UUI or
   MUI.
 • Pregnancy and postpartum:
      ◦ A 2020 Cochrane review found that a structured PFMT programme offered to continent antenatal
        women early in pregnancy may prevent UI in late pregnancy and postpartum.
      ◦ Postpartum women with UI randomised to professionally taught, supervised PFMT were less likely to
        be incontinent 6–12 months after delivery than those given no treatment or usual care.
      ◦ ICI: PFMT first-line for postpartum UI persisting after delivery. The text gives this as 3 months; Table
        114.2 gives 2–3 months. The source does not explain the difference.
 • Men after RP:
      ◦ SUI complicates RP whatever the technique. PFMT is the primary conservative treatment for PPI,
        taught by digital rectal examination (DRE) with verbal feedback, or with BF or E-stim.
      ◦ Trials in established PPI are mixed but generally show that PFMT reduces UI in the first 3 months;
        persistent long-term UI is less studied.
      ◦ In an RCT of men with UI persisting 1–17 years after RP, 8 weeks of behavioural training (PFMT plus
        bladder control strategies) reduced UI episodes significantly more than control, and the benefit was
        sustained to 12 months.
      ◦ Adding E-stim or BF to PFMT does not improve continence. However, BF is an effective teaching
        approach, and providers may prefer it to teaching by DRE.
 • Perioperative PFMT to prevent PPI is covered under Prevention of Urinary Incontinence.

and skill (timed urethral occlusion, the knack). Supervision and confirmed correct contraction are what

Vaginal Cones for Pelvic Floor Muscle Training
 • Weighted vaginal cones were developed to test PFM function. They are used to help patients identify and
   control the PFMs and to support adherence (Fig. 114.5). They provide progressive muscular overload.
 • Mechanism: with the cone in the vagina and the patient standing, the sensation of the cone slipping
   gives strong sensory feedback that prompts a PFM contraction to retain it.
 • Protocol:
     1. Insert the heaviest cone that can be retained standing.
     2. Then retain it while walking.
     3. Increase cone weight and retention time step by step.
     4. Goal: retain the cone while walking for 2 sessions of 20 minutes a day. Rising weight maintains
        overload.
 • Limitations: discomfort; difficult insertion with a narrow introitus; retention impossible with POP or a
   wide introitus.
 • Trap: the vagina is not vertical, so some women retain the cone without contracting the PFMs. Its value
   as a PFMT aid is therefore questionable.
 • Evidence. A Cochrane review of small trials of uncertain quality found some evidence that cones are
   better than no active treatment for SUI, and possibly similar to PFMT and PFM E-stim. More definitive
   trials are needed. An overview of Cochrane reviews found moderate- or high-certainty evidence that
   cones may beat no treatment or placebo.

Other Pelvic Floor Muscle Training Devices
 • Colpexin sphere: a weighted device for women with POP and UI.
      ◦ It is a smooth medical-grade polycarbonate sphere with a braided string. It is self-inserted, by

Behavioural Training With Urge Suppression
 • A stand-alone treatment that teaches patients to use the PFMs to inhibit detrusor contraction, within a
   larger urge suppression strategy, as a new way of responding to urge. It is used for urgency, frequency and
   UUI.
 • Origin (sequence of discovery):
     1. BF-assisted training used bladder and anal sphincter BF together during retrograde filling, to teach
        cortical inhibition of detrusor contraction.
     2. It was noticed that spontaneous anal sphincter activity altered detrusor pressure.
     3. A well-timed voluntary anal sphincter (PFM) contraction under visual BF was then shown to abort fully
        developed detrusor contractions, deter developing ones and suppress urgency.
     4. Later, the skill was shown to be teachable without BF, using verbal feedback from vaginal palpation. In
        an RCT in older women with UUI this gave outcomes similar to bladder–sphincter BF when built into a
        larger strategy.
 • Consequently PFMT, designed for SUI, is now central to the treatment of UUI and OAB. PFM control is
   taught exactly as for SUI; what differs is how the muscle is used — as part of a broader response to urge,
   not only to occlude the urethra. Urge suppression with incremental interval increases is also integral to
   BT.
 • The "gotta go" response is counterproductive. Rushing raises physical pressure on the bladder, heightens
   fullness, worsens urgency and triggers detrusor contraction. Approaching the toilet adds visual cues that
   trigger urgency and UUI, a conditioned response.
 • Urge suppression strategy (Fig. 114.7):
     1. Do not rush; stay away from the bathroom and its cues.
     2. Pause, sit down if possible, and relax the whole body.
     3. Contract the PFM repeatedly and quickly, without relaxing in between.
     4. Concentrate on inhibiting the urge and let it pass, using distraction if it persists.
     5. When it subsides, walk at a normal pace to the toilet.
 • After mastery, delay voiding by 5 minutes to consolidate the skill. In frequent voiders, lengthen the delay
   gradually until voiding frequency is normal.
 • Efficacy. As stand-alone therapy for UUI, it is established in clinical series and intention-to-treat RCTs, with
   mean reductions in incontinence of 60–80%.
 • It also reduces urgency, frequency and nocturia in both men and women.
 • Nocturia protocol: on waking with fullness or urge, use urge suppression. If the urge subsides, go back to
   sleep. If it has not subsided after a minute or two, get up and void, so as not to disturb sleep
   unnecessarily.
 • Combination with drugs improves outcomes of drug therapy for UUI (see the trial table).
 • The urge suppression strategy is also used in BT and delayed voiding.

BLADDER TRAINING AND SCHEDULED VOIDING REGIMENS
 • Scheduled voiding regimens, or toileting programmes, have for decades been a mainstay of treatment for
   UUI and OAB. They comprise BT, timed voiding (TV), habit training (HT) and prompted voiding (PV)
   (Table 114.5).
 • All share a toileting schedule. They differ in:
      ◦ the patient's role (active versus passive);
      ◦ how the schedule is adjusted;
      ◦ the nature of patient education and the use of reinforcement;
      ◦ the nature of the patient–provider interaction.
 • BT is self-administered: the patient must resist urgency and delay voiding, and it includes cognitive and
   psychological components. TV, HT and PV mostly rely on a caregiver.

Bladder Training
 • BT is a broad term covering bladder retraining, bladder discipline, bladder re-education and bladder drill.
   It was developed for UUI.
 • The cycle BT breaks:
      ◦ urgency leads to frequent voiding, which relieves the urge immediately;
      ◦ frequent voiding becomes a habit;
      ◦ the habit leads to reduced functional bladder capacity, DO and sometimes UUI;
      ◦ DO produces more urgency, perpetuating the cycle.
   It is broken by BT or by progressive delayed voiding.
 • Goal: restore normal bladder function and capacity through education plus a mandatory or self-
   adjustable schedule that progressively lengthens the interval between voids.
 • Originally bladder drill was intensive and often inpatient; it is now implemented gradually in outpatient,
   rehabilitation and other settings.
 • BT has three components: education on lower urinary tract (LUT) function, incremental voiding
   schedules, and urge control techniques to postpone voiding and keep to the schedule.

 • Coping with urgency while waiting:
      ◦ relaxation, or distraction with an activity needing mental but not physical effort — a game on a phone
        or tablet, reading, a to-do list. This reduces anxiety and gives the urge time to subside;
      ◦ self-affirmations: "I am in control of my bladder", "I can wait".
 • Urge suppression within BT: a few quick PFM contractions and/or a sustained contraction.
      ◦ PFM contraction produces immediate detrusor relaxation, shown in patients with UI and in patients
        with and without OAB.
      ◦ Proposed mechanism: the quick contraction compresses the urethra mechanically for a few seconds,
        inhibiting the detrusor contraction and reducing the desire to void.
      ◦ Hence repeated quick flicks, with the patient posturally still, waiting for the sensation to pass, are a
        useful adjunct to BT.
 • Ideally the clinician reviews weekly, adjusts the interval and gives positive reinforcement. The patient's
   voiding diary tracks adherence and progress and shows when to change the schedule.
 • Mechanism is unknown. The central feature is that BT dissociates voiding from urgency: voiding by the
   clock rather than on urge may weaken the urge–void response. Other hypotheses are:
      ◦ improved cortical control of bladder activity and urethral closure;
      ◦ improved central modulation of afferent input;
      ◦ behaviour change from better awareness of LUT function;
      ◦ increased "reserve capacity" as patients learn what provokes leakage.

 • The first RCT of BT was in older women with UI. Several systematic reviews have since graded the
   evidence.
 • ICI (trials of UI of all types and of OAB without UI, mostly in women, few in men):
      ◦ BT protocols vary remarkably, and there is no evidence for a best method or parameter set. The ICI
        recommends the most intensive BT supervision possible.
      ◦ BT is more effective than no treatment in UUI, SUI and MUI.
      ◦ There is insufficient evidence in men.
      ◦ BT may work in motivated frail older women who are cognitively and physically intact.

 • A multicentre RCT in continent, cognitively intact older adults hospitalised with a fall-related hip
   fracture gave BT with prompting in hospital plus a telephone reinforcement call 10 days after discharge. It
   reduced the incidence of functional UI at 6 months.
 • The AHRQ review found no difference between BT and PFMT, nor between BT alone and BT plus PFMT.
Concept — BT works by uncoupling the void from the urge. The schedule is the vehicle and urge suppression
is the engine, which is why the protocol advances the interval only as fast as urgency control allows.

Other Scheduled Voiding Regimens
 • Toileting programmes are the cornerstone of continence care for cognitively impaired patients. Apart
   from BT they are caregiver dependent: a professional or family caregiver must assist with toileting.
 • They benefit toileting-dependent nursing home residents, and people at home who have an available,
   willing caregiver.
 • Toileting disability means difficulty with toileting, or needing human or mechanical help with it. Such
   patients may have mobility or cognitive impairment and need help from at least one person, but can
   cooperate with toileting.
 • The choice among TV, HT and PV depends on cognitive and functional status, variability of the voiding
   pattern, and the need for reinforcement to maintain adherence.
Timed voiding (also called scheduled, routine or fixed toileting)
 • Goal: prevent incontinence by giving regular opportunities to empty the bladder before it reaches
   capacity. This reduces leakage from full-bladder detrusor contraction and from SUI; no other physiological
   mechanism has been identified.
 • Use: patients who cannot toilet independently. It is used mainly in institutions as a passive programme —
   the caregiver takes the patient to void every 2–4 hours, except at night — and in neurogenic bladder
   after SCI.
 • The schedule should ideally rest on an objective measure: a bladder diary, a bladder volume instrument,
   or an electronic monitor of incontinence episodes.
 • It is passive because it runs whether or not the patient feels the need to void, usually during waking
   hours only. The goal is dryness; no attempt is made to teach resisting the urge.
 • Outcomes: about 20% of frail elders become completely dry and 30–50% improve, with fewer and
   smaller episodes; home care is probably similar.
 • TV also suits cognitively intact elders with impaired bladder sensation from neurological disease: voiding
   by the clock, before urgency or spontaneous emptying, prevents or reduces UI.
 • A 2- to 3-day trial of TV shows whether it will succeed. TV also normalises frequency in infrequent
   voiders or those with diminished sensation.
Habit training
 • HT is a variant of TV in which the schedule is matched to the patient's own voiding pattern rather than
   fixed.
 • From the diary, the interval is set shorter than the patient's usual interval and timed to precede
   expected incontinent episodes. The interval can therefore lengthen or shorten through the day, pre-
   empting UI.
 • Traditionally it is caregiver dependent, started and maintained by a professional or family caregiver.
 • It is used mainly in institutions with cognitively and physically impaired adults, and has been tested in the
   homebound elderly.

 • It also suits unimpaired adults whose UI recurs at about the same time each day or is diuretic induced.
   The interval can be fixed permanently or lengthened gradually.
 • Pattern urge-response toileting (PURT): 3 weeks of electronic voiding logs → individual schedules →
   caregivers (mainly spouses) taught when and how to toilet homebound frail elders → fewer incontinent
   episodes and less leaked volume.
Prompted voiding
 • Definition: PV combines scheduled voiding with caregiver prompting and contingent social approval. It
   teaches people, with or without cognitive impairment, to initiate their own toileting by asking for help,
   and reinforces them when they do.
 • Setting: mainly nursing homes with cognitively and physically impaired older adults; also home care.
 • Three components:
     1. Monitoring: regular checks, asking the person to report whether wet or dry.
     2. Prompting: asking whether they need to void, and assisting, on schedule.
     3. Praise: positive feedback when continent and when they try to toilet.
 • Suitable for older adults with UUI, MUI or functional UI, whether cognitively intact or impaired.
 • Requirements: a consistently available caregiver is the main one. The individual must also be able to
   delay voiding and cooperate, or be aware of the need to void.
 • Evidence:
      ◦ A systematic review of RCTs in care homes showed fewer incontinent episodes.
      ◦ PV is effective short term for daytime UI in nursing home residents and home care clients when
        caregivers comply.
      ◦ 25–40% of incontinent nursing home residents respond well, with daytime episodes falling to one or
        none. Over a third cannot toilet successfully even with help.
 • A 3-day trial of PV shows whether it is likely to work.
 • An RCT in nursing homes had staff trained for at least 4 hours prompt residents every 2–2.5 hours. It
   reduced wet episodes and was sustainable for 6 months.
 • Functional incidental training (FIT) combines PV with activities of daily living and restorative care (a
   walking programme and exercise), using a designated nursing assistant role. It repeatedly improves both
   physical function and UI in nursing home residents.
 • Ultrasound-assisted PV may beat conventional PV. In an RCT in Japanese nursing homes, staff scanned
   bladder volume and prompted a void at optimal capacity, rather than every 2–3 hours. This produced a
   greater reduction in daytime urine loss. QoL was unchanged in both groups, and caregiver burden
   worsened with conventional PV.

Challenges of Caregiver-Administered Voiding Schedules
 • Toileting regimens impose a significant burden on caregivers. UI management is rated the third most
   troublesome caregiving task, after lack of time for one's own needs and managing the recipient's
   emotional and behavioural problems.
 • Specific strains are odour, toileting, changing pads and clothing, the cost of pads and briefs, wet clothing,
   and changing bed linen.
 • In dementia at home, prompting can cause irritation and arguments, because the person feels treated
   like a child.

Delayed Voiding
 • Delayed voiding (DV) is another way to lengthen the interval between voids, but without a fixed
   schedule.
 • Protocol:
     1. At the first urge, use urge suppression until the urge subsides.
     2. Do not then go straight to the toilet; wait 5 minutes before voiding.
     3. Once comfortable at 5 minutes, increase the wait step by step towards a normal interval.
 • DV combines with BT, PFMT and urge suppression to reduce excessive frequency.
 • Patients who have had UUI rush at even a mild urge for fear of leaking. Most can be persuaded to try a 5-
   minute delay in a safe setting, such as at home alone. They are often surprised that the urge fades, which
   builds a sense of control and confidence.

BEHAVIOURAL TREATMENT FOR VOIDING AND PELVIC FLOOR DYSFUNCTION
 • PFM overactivity (high-tone PFM dysfunction) is characterised by elevated resting tone (hypertonicity)
   and reduced ability to relax. It presents with:
        ◦ pelvic floor tenderness or pain;
        ◦ bladder pain syndrome;
        ◦ voiding symptoms — hesitancy, straining and incomplete emptying.
 • Assessment: digital palpation for tenderness and pain, resting tone, contraction strength and ability to
   relax. Some women have generally high resting tone; others tense only when attempting to void.
 • Treatment teaches conscious PFM relaxation, especially during voiding. The first step is education on
   anatomy and function:
        ◦ normal voiding is coordinated: pelvic floor relaxation precedes, and can initiate, detrusor contraction
          and urethral relaxation;
        ◦ some women habitually void by Valsalva, believing they must push. This provokes a reflex PFM
          contraction that impedes flow;
        ◦ patients must learn that straining is unnecessary and counterproductive, and that voluntary PFM
          relaxation lets the bladder empty naturally.
 • PFMT here builds awareness of tension as distinct from relaxation. An active contraction shows what
   tension feels like, lets the patient contrast it with relaxation, and leads to a more complete relaxation
   afterwards. BF brings muscle tension to conscious awareness.

LIFESTYLE MODIFICATIONS
 • Lifestyle modifications change habits and lifestyle choices that may contribute to UI and LUTS. They
   involve a conscious choice to change a practice under the patient's control, and are sometimes called self-
   care practices.
 • They can occasionally be primary but are usually adjunctive.
 • They cover fluid management, reducing caffeine and other dietary irritants, managing constipation,
   smoking cessation and weight loss.
 • The evidence is much thinner than for the skill-based treatments, yet these measures substantially affect
   bladder health and are integral to conservative management.

Timing of Fluid Intake
 • Overall restriction is not a good strategy, but restriction timed to limited toilet access (for example,
   before an outing) helps. The patient should make up the missed fluid earlier or later so that the daily total
   stays optimal.
 • Evening restriction for nocturia: stop fluids 3–4 hours before bedtime.
 • Daytime fluid retention mobilised during sleep causes nocturia. Manage the daytime accumulation with
   support stockings, or elevate the legs in the late afternoon to mobilise the fluid well before bed.
 • Loop diuretics:
      ◦ A mid- to late-afternoon dose completes the diuresis before bedtime.
      ◦ Loop diuretics aggravate UI by speeding bladder filling and causing sudden urges. Options are to stop
        the drug, switch to a non-loop diuretic, or change the timing.

Evidence for Fluid Management
 • Fluid management is widely used, but the evidence is limited and mixed. A review found evidence that
   reducing intake improved weekly UI episodes.
 • Two small RCTs:
      ◦ In OAB, reducing intake by a quarter reduced daytime frequency, urgency and nocturia; increasing
        intake worsened frequency but not urgency, nocturia or UUI.
      ◦ In women with urodynamically confirmed idiopathic DO, reducing intake reduced frequency and UUI
        episodes.
 • By contrast, the Nurses' Health Study, a large prospective cohort followed for 4 years, found no
   association between total fluid intake and incident UI of any type.
Concept — The aim of fluid management is a normal range, not restriction. Both excess and deficit worsen
LUTS, and most patients seen in practice are over-restricting. Timing and type are adjusted before volume is
cut.

Caffeine Reduction
 • Caffeine worsens urgency, frequency and UUI partly as a diuretic and partly as a bladder irritant. Intake
   from beverages, foods and medications is easily underestimated. Most US adults and most children
   consume caffeine; in children, coffee and energy drinks are rising while soda is falling.
 • Urodynamic effects:
      ◦ it raises detrusor pressure during filling and is a risk factor for DO in women with UI;
      ◦ it lowers the threshold of bladder sensation in the filling phase, raises flow rate and voided volume,
        and causes diuresis (at 4.5 mg/kg in humans);
      ◦ in mice, daily oral caffeine caused DO and increased bladder sensory signalling.
 • Epidemiology:
      ◦ Caffeine is associated with LUTS in both sexes. In the Nurses' Health Study II, high caffeine intake was
        associated with frequent UI.
      ◦ In BACH, women who increased coffee intake had greater progression of urgency; recent increases in
        soda, especially caffeinated diet soda, were linked to worse scores, urgency and LUTS progression. In
        men, more coffee or caffeine at baseline predicted storage symptom progression.
      ◦ About 2 cups of coffee a day (250 mg) is significantly associated with moderate-to-severe UI in US
        men.

 • Nocturia: coffee is not associated with nocturia in either sex. Tea is, but only slightly, and only in women
   drinking 3 or more cups a day. Women with nocturia were less likely to drink caffeine after 6 pm.
 • Intervention evidence:
      ◦ Reducing caffeine reduces episodes of both SUI and UUI.
      ◦ A scoping review found observational associations with urgency but unclear associations with other
        LUTS.
      ◦ Interventions reduced urgency and nocturnal enuresis; results for UI episodes and frequency were
        inconsistent.
 • The ICI recommends caffeine reduction, but the amount needed is unknown. Sensitivity varies, and only
   a subset of patients with urgency or UUI is caffeine sensitive.
 • Practical approach:
     1. Offer a 3- to 5-day trial of reduction to test sensitivity.
     2. If symptoms improve, patients are usually more willing to cut down or stop.
     3. Reduce gradually, mixing caffeinated with decaffeinated beverages over several weeks, to avoid
        withdrawal headache and irritability.

Other Dietary Irritants
 • Suspected irritants include aspartame, citrus fruit, highly spiced foods and tomato products. Many
   clinical cases improve when they are reduced, but this does not mean every patient with LUTS should
   eliminate them.
 • Use a food and beverage diary to identify the patient's own irritants, then eliminate them one at a time
   to confirm the relationship. In an elimination diet, suspect items are withdrawn separately and in
   succession until the culprit is found.
 • Alcohol:
      ◦ It is thought to worsen urgency, frequency and UI through diuresis, but support is weak. In Japanese
        women the increase in UI risk was not significant.
      ◦ In men, alcohol has been called an avoidable risk factor for LUTS, and high consumption is associated
        with moderate and severe LUTS. However, a prospective cohort found beer protective against OAB.
      ◦ A review consistently found modest drinking associated with fewer symptoms than abstinence, and
        heavy drinking with more UI and LUTS.
      ◦ Caffeinated alcoholic drinks need study.
 • Acidic fruit: no relation to UI in the Nurses' Health Study.
 • Carbonated and artificially sweetened beverages (ASBs):
      ◦ Avoidance is widely advised on clinical experience rather than formal evidence.
      ◦ In rats, artificial sweeteners enhance detrusor contraction.
      ◦ In a prospective cohort of women, carbonated soft drinks were independently associated with onset
        of OAB and SUI.
      ◦ In the Women's Health Initiative Observational Study (WHI-OS), ASB consumption was associated
        with higher odds of UI, rising with intake.
      ◦ Whether reducing ASBs relieves symptoms is untested.

Bowel Function
 • Chronic constipation (fewer than 3 stools a week) and straining at stool are linked to LUTS. The ICI found
   "some evidence" that chronic straining at defecation is a risk factor for UI.

Chapter 114 — Conservative Management of Urinary Incontinence                                  Lifestyle Modifications
 • Why they are linked:
      ◦ the bladder, urethra and rectum lie close together and share innervation, so reciprocal effects are
        likely;
      ◦ excessive straining is associated with denervation of the external anal sphincter and PFMs, and
        lifelong straining may have a cumulative effect;
      ◦ animal and clinical data support bladder–bowel cross-sensitisation.
 • Evidence:
      ◦ In the Epidemiology of LUTS (EpiLUTS) II survey, OAB was more often reported by men and women
        with chronic constipation.
      ◦ In a case-control study, constipation and straining were more common in women with LUTS, including
        DO and urgency.
      ◦ In a prospective cohort, women with UI had worse bowel function (constipation and diarrhoea
        scores), and worse UI of any subtype went with worse faecal incontinence, diarrhoea and
        constipation.
 • Faecal impaction, notably in nursing homes, can irritate the bladder (OAB) or obstruct voiding, causing
   incomplete emptying and overflow incontinence.
 • Management:
      ◦ take a bowel history at the initial assessment;
      ◦ ensure adequate dietary fibre and fluid — fibre does not work without sufficient fluid;
      ◦ encourage regular exercise and use external stimulation;
      ◦ set a regular evacuation time, preferably after a meal, to use postprandial bowel motility.
Concept — Bladder and bowel share space, nerves and pelvic floor, so dysfunction in one travels with the
other. Constipation is therefore treated as part of the bladder programme, not as a separate problem.

Obesity and Weight Reduction
 • Obesity is one of the most clearly established risk factors for UI. Epidemiological and interventional data,
   including several systematic reviews, link body mass index (BMI) to UI and other LUTS. Obese women
   have about double the risk of UI.
 • Proposed mechanisms:
      ◦ chronically raised intra-abdominal pressure on the bladder and pelvic floor;
      ◦ metabolic syndrome predisposing to UUI;
      ◦ obesity-related inflammation and oxidative stress altering collagen metabolism.
 • Interventional evidence:
      ◦ UI improves in morbidly obese women after the dramatic weight loss of bariatric surgery. In national
        claims data, resolution of a UI diagnosis was more frequent after bariatric than after non-bariatric
        surgery, although the incidence of new UI did not differ.
      ◦ Intensive lifestyle therapy with a low-calorie diet reduced UI in prediabetic women.
      ◦ Modest weight reduction reduced SUI episodes but not UUI.
      ◦ Loss of 5–10% of body weight, sustained over 12 months, is enough to reduce incontinence episodes
        substantially.
 • Most definitive study: an RCT of an intensive 6-month group weight-loss programme (diet, exercise and
   behaviour modification) against structured education, both with a self-help behavioural UI booklet. The
   weight-loss group lost more weight and had a significantly greater reduction in UI episodes.

 • Clinicians should recommend weight loss as part of lifestyle treatment for overweight women with UI.
   Because BMI is modifiable, keeping weight within guidelines may also prevent UI.
Concept — Obesity is a modifiable pressure load, and the evidence runs from association through to
randomised intervention. A 5–10% loss is enough, which is why behavioural weight loss is the only grade A
lifestyle recommendation.

ADHERENCE TO CONSERVATIVE TREATMENT
 • Effectiveness depends on an involved, motivated patient. Most behavioural interventions are forms of
   self-management, so the clinician's main challenge is to motivate the patient to take part, practise
   consistently and persist long enough for meaningful change.
 • Progress is gradual, which makes compliance harder for patients expecting quick results.
 • Clinician support:
      ◦ set realistic expectations — improvement takes weeks to months and may be irregular, with good and
        bad days;
      ◦ schedule follow-up to maintain accountability, track and reinforce progress, find and address barriers,
        adjust the regimen and encourage persistence;
      ◦ reassure patients that they are not alone.
 • Measurement of adherence has often been overlooked in research. Few studies report adherence to
   exercise, strategies or schedules, and methods for improving adherence are sparsely studied.
 • A secondary analysis of ATLAS (see Vaginal Devices) found that adherence to PFME and bladder control
   strategies can be high and sustained when delivered by trained interventionists.
      ◦ Commonest barriers: remembering to exercise and finding time.
      ◦ Remedies: reminders (timers, text messages), exercise aids, linking exercise to environmental cues,
        and building it into daily activities.
 • Determinants of PFMT adherence: positive intention to adhere, self-efficacy, attitudes to the exercise
   and its benefits, perceived social pressure, and therapist enthusiasm. Patients and clinicians agree that
   patient-related factors are the main barriers.
 • A review of qualitative studies identified six modifiers of adherence: knowledge; physical skill; feelings
   about PFMT; cognitive analysis, planning and attention; prioritisation; and service provision.
 • Clinicians should assess adherence during and at the end of therapy and use patient-focused strategies.
 • Long term:
      ◦ PFMT adherence declines over time and varies widely; positive intentions and self-efficacy predict
        long-term adherence.
      ◦ Long-term outcome studies are few and inconsistent but promising, since many patients sustain their
        improvement.
      ◦ More work is needed on why patients regress and how to maintain benefit.
Concept — Because behavioural therapy is self-management, its efficacy is capped by adherence. The
clinician's real intervention after teaching the skill is sustaining motivation, which is why supervised and
intensive programmes outperform written instruction.

PREVENTION OF URINARY INCONTINENCE
 • Conservative interventions are non-invasive, which makes them suitable for prevention.



                                                          page 28 of 33

 • Primary prevention with PFMT, behavioural training or lifestyle change (weight loss, fluid moderation,
   diet) has been studied in three at-risk groups: older women, childbearing women, and men undergoing
   prostatectomy.

Older Females
 • Age is perhaps the most established risk factor for UI, yet prevention in older women has been little
   studied.
 • First prevention RCT: a group behaviour modification programme against no intervention in continent
   postmenopausal women aged 55 or over. At 12 months the programme gave better continence status,
   PFM strength (pressure and displacement scores), voiding frequency and intervoid interval.
 • A feasibility study of a 6-week pelvic fitness and education programme in women aged 65 or over in
   independent living improved symptom bother, symptom distress (Urogenital Distress Inventory-6, UDI-6)
   and QoL (Incontinence Impact Questionnaire short form, IIQ-7).
 • An RCT in women aged 55 or over with no or minimal UI compared a 2-hour bladder health class with a
   20-minute video. Neither group progressed in UI or urgency over 12 and 24 months, and the two formats
   were comparable.

Childbearing Females
 • Pregnancy and childbirth are established risk factors, and most prenatal classes teach the pelvic floor
   and PFM exercise.
 • In primiparous women, intensive antenatal PFMT with one-to-one instruction to secure correct
   contraction and continued supervision prevents UI in late pregnancy (35–36 weeks) and in the short-
   term postpartum period (6 weeks to 6 months).
 • A large Cochrane review concluded that antenatal PFMT in continent pregnant women may reduce UI in
   late pregnancy and in the mid-postnatal period (more than 3 to 6 months).
 • The effect on long-term UI and in multiparous women is less clear.
 • ICI recommendations are those in Table 114.2: the 12-week antenatal programme from 16–24 weeks, a
   postnatal programme after forceps or a baby of 4000 g or more, and education for older women.

Males Undergoing Prostatectomy
 • PPI is almost universal immediately after prostatectomy. It usually improves over weeks to months, and
   the literature on behavioural treatment is mixed, but PFMT can speed the return of continence.

FUTURE OF LOWER URINARY TRACT SYMPTOMS PREVENTION AND BLADDER
HEALTH PROMOTION RESEARCH
 • Prevention of UI and LUTS is a growing emphasis. The ICI has examined education-based interventions to
   raise public awareness and promote population bladder health, and delivery models for skill-based
   prevention in at-risk groups (older adults, childbearing women, men undergoing RP).
 • 2010: a multidisciplinary expert panel issued a consensus statement on the healthy bladder. It aimed to
   raise awareness, encourage primary prevention through public education, reduce the personal, societal

 • 2015: the NIDDK formed the Prevention of Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (PLUS) Research Consortium,

VAGINAL DEVICES FOR INCONTINENCE
 • Two types of mechanical device:
      ◦ intravaginal devices that support the bladder neck and urethra;
      ◦ intraurethral or meatal devices that block urine passage.
 • Devices are used mostly for SUI, but also for UUI and MUI.
 • No external meatal or intraurethral device for UI is currently available in the United States.
 • A 2011 Cochrane review found insufficient evidence to recommend any specific device, or to show that
   devices beat other treatments.
 • The ideal device controls leakage adequately, is easy to apply or insert, has few adverse effects and is
   cheap.

Intravaginal Devices
 • Rationale: when pelvic support is weak, the urethra drops to a position where a rise in abdominal
   pressure cannot be transmitted to it to aid closure. An intravaginal device restores the upper urethra
   above the pelvic floor, where abdominal pressure again helps close it. A continence pessary achieves this.
 • The vaginal pessary is one of the oldest medical devices, used for centuries for POP and more recently for
   SUI. The Dutch College of General Practitioners recommends offering women with SUI a choice of PFMT
   or a pessary as initial treatment.
 • How a continence pessary works:
      ◦ it stabilises and supports the urethra and bladder neck;
      ◦ it increases urethral length;
      ◦ it gently compresses the urethra against the pubic bone when abdominal pressure rises.
 • The knob of an incontinence ring sits against the urethrovesical junction (Fig. 114.13) and supports it
   much as a surgically placed vaginal sling does.
 • Women with urgency and frequency caused by pressure from POP may also benefit.
 • Materials: inert plastic or silicone, which prevents odour and absorption of secretions. Designs specific to
   SUI are the Hodge (Fig. 114.14), dish (Fig. 114.15) and ring (Fig. 114.16), each available without or with
   support.
 • Contraindications (few): active pelvic or vaginal infection; severe ulceration; allergy to silicone or latex;
   likely non-compliance with maintenance and follow-up.
 • Difficulty with self-removal and insertion limits wider use.

      ◦ common: vaginal discharge and odour;

MODELS OF DELIVERY
 • Despite the evidence and guideline endorsement, conservative interventions are poorly integrated into
   practice and not widely accessible, chiefly because there are too few qualified providers.

Integration of Continence Specialists in Clinical Practice
 • A successful model is to integrate APPs — continence nurse practitioners and physician assistants — into
   urological practice. APPs have historically embraced behavioural treatment as a specialty for non-surgical
   management of many LUT conditions.
 • Fig. 114.19 gives an algorithm for a behavioural treatment pathway for LUTS; its legend defines post-void
   residuals (PVRs).
 • Urology suits a multidisciplinary Continence and Pelvic Health service that offers comprehensive surgical
   and medical care from experts in UI, voiding and pelvic floor dysfunction.
 • Changes in reimbursement for BF-assisted PFMT, E-stim and posterior tibial nerve stimulation have let
   urologists extend beyond drugs and surgery to multifaceted behavioural programmes.

Group Administration Models
 • PFMT has long been part of prenatal education and fitness classes for pelvic health in pregnancy and
   postpartum. It has also been built into general fitness programmes and specific pelvic health classes to
   treat symptomatic women. These focus on strengthening and need multiple sessions over several weeks.
 • Bladder health classes in a classroom or community use a broader health-class format: anatomy and
   function, voiding habits, toileting technique and bladder control strategies. They are effective both for
   preventing symptoms in older populations and for reducing existing symptoms.
 • Group-Administered Behavioural Treatment (GBT) trial: a multisite RCT in community-dwelling women
   with UI compared a one-time 2-hour bladder health class with a PFME audio CD against no treatment.
      ◦ ICIQ-SF scores were consistently lower with GBT through 12 months, but the difference fell short of
        the projected 3 points.
      ◦ Conclusion: GBT is modestly effective and cost-effective in older women.
 • Such programmes are less effective than individually supervised therapy, but they are risk free, cheap and
   deliverable in medical or non-medical settings. They reach people with mild or preclinical symptoms,
   teaching treatment, prevention and delay of onset, and signposting conventional care.

Self-Administration Models and Mobile Health
 • Pamphlets on PFMT, BT and lifestyle change served as stand-alone instruction or as adjuncts to drugs or
   clinic treatment. Some grew into comprehensive step-by-step programmes, and technology has moved
   them to telehealth devices and websites.
 • Internet versus postal self-training for SUI: an RCT compared an internet programme (email support from
   a urotherapist plus cognitive behavioural assignments) with a postal programme.
      ◦ Both gave large improvements, with no difference in primary outcomes.
      ◦ The internet group did better on several secondary outcomes.

 • Mobile health (mHealth) apps: the commonest teach PFMT for SUI; others target pregnancy and
   postpartum. Free and paid apps are of very variable quality. On the validated Mobile App Rating Scale
   (MARS), many were poor and only one was supported by scientific evidence.
 • Evidence-based apps:
      ◦ The Tät app for SUI (three sets a day of slow, quick and pre-cough contractions) improved ICIQ-SF by
        about the minimal important difference in an RCT. An updated version was effective for UUI and MUI.
      ◦ Comprehensive programmes add urge suppression and lifestyle content:
          ▪ an electronic tutorial with video vignettes on using the PFMs for urge triggers and nighttime
            waking;
          ▪ MyHealtheBladder for female veterans, an 8-week programme of daily modules on PFME, bladder
            control, fluid management, risk-factor reduction and self-monitoring, which reduced ICIQ-SF;
          ▪ a similar programme for perioperative behavioural training in men undergoing RP.
 • Reviews:
      ◦ App-based PFMT for SUI "shows promise" for efficacy and adherence.
      ◦ A scoping review found level 2 evidence of benefit in symptoms, satisfaction, adherence and cost,
        with good fidelity to optimal PFMT parameters.
 • mHealth improves access and may improve motivation and adherence. Useful features are BF linkage,
   audio instruction, video, stories and testimonials, and reminders such as text messages. Research is now
   focused on barriers, facilitators and features that improve uptake, adherence and outcome.
Concept — The constraint on conservative therapy is access, not efficacy. Each delivery model — specialist
APPs, groups, self-administration and apps — trades intensity, and therefore effect size, for reach.

 KEY POINTS
   • Conservative behavioural treatments improve UI and other LUTS by changing behaviour or
     environment, or by teaching new skills.
   • There is strong evidence that conservative interventions are effective first-line treatments for UI and
     other LUTS.
   • Effectiveness relies on the active participation of an involved, motivated patient.
   • Intensive behavioural interventions involving an experienced clinician are more effective.
