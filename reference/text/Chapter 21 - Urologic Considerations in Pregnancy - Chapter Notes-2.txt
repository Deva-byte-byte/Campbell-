Chapter 21 — Urologic Considerations in Pregnancy
PHYSIOLOGIC CHANGES DURING PREGNANCY
 • Pregnancy adapts almost every organ system; knowing normal adaptation lets the urologist separate
   physiology from pathology.

Cardiovascular
 • Fall in systemic vascular resistance (SVR) is the primary driver: low-resistance placenta plus
   progesterone- and prostacyclin-mediated vasodilatation, evident before uteroplacental circulation exists.
 • Mean arterial pressure falls ~10 mmHg below baseline by the second trimester.
 • Sympathetic drive → heart rate rises up to 30%, maximal at 20 weeks.
 • Cardiac output (CO) = stroke volume × heart rate; both rise → CO rises early and peaks 30–50% above
   preconception values.
 • CO is position-dependent: supine CO at term up to 30% below lateral recumbency; reduced venous
   return → stroke volume falls, possibly abruptly.
 • Uterus compresses the inferior vena cava (IVC) ± aorta → supine hypotensive syndrome, worsening with
   gestation; late supine lower-limb return may depend entirely on collaterals.
 • General and regional anaesthesia abolish sympathetic compensation (SVR cannot rise) → further CO fall
   and hypotension; treat maternal hypotension aggressively to protect uteroplacental perfusion.
 • Prevention: avoid supine and lithotomy; right-side elevation (15–20° left tilt) displaces the uterus off the
   IVC.
 • Venous compression also → oedema, varicosities, haemorrhoids and deep venous thrombosis (DVT) risk.
Concept — Falling resistance with rising output defines the pregnant circulation; the gravid uterus turns
supine positioning into a preload problem and anaesthesia removes the compensation — hence left tilt for
every urological procedure.

Respiratory
 • Ligamentous relaxation → chest circumference increases up to 7 cm.
 • Diaphragmatic excursion preserved, but diaphragm displaced cranially up to 4 cm → lung volumes fall
   despite chest-wall expansion.
 • Mechanical restriction: total lung capacity, expiratory reserve volume and residual volume fall;
   functional residual capacity (FRC) falls to ~80% of baseline by term.
 • Progesterone drives chronic hyperventilation: tidal volume up to +50% by 8 weeks → minute ventilation
   ↑ → alveolar O2 ↑, CO2 ↓ → facilitates fetal-to-maternal CO2 transfer.

 • Low maternal carbon dioxide = chronic respiratory alkalosis, partially compensated by renal bicarbonate
   excretion.
 • Oxygen consumption up to +40% + reduced FRC → small oxygen reserve → rapid desaturation at
   intubation.
 • Difficult airway: oropharyngeal oedema, narrower glottic aperture, mucosal friability → higher airway
   classification; obesity and pre-eclampsia compound it; supine positioning further lowers FRC under
   general anaesthesia.
Concept — Pregnancy raises oxygen demand while shrinking the oxygen store (FRC); the anaesthetic danger
is fast desaturation in a harder airway.

Haematologic
 • Blood volume expands 40–50% from ~6 weeks; plasma volume exceeds red blood cell (RBC) mass →
   haematocrit falls: dilutional (physiological) anaemia, nadir 30–34 weeks, partly ameliorated by iron.
 • Mild gestational thrombocytopenia; a platelet count below 116,000/µL is exceedingly rare and
   mandates evaluation, especially perioperatively. Platelet function and haemostasis are intact.
 • Hypercoagulability guards against placental-bed haemorrhage; venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk ~10-
   fold via Virchow triad: stasis (IVC compression), vascular trauma, ↑ procoagulants.
 • Pregnancy-independent VTE risk factors: age >35, obesity, trauma, immobility, infection, smoking,
   nephrotic syndrome, coagulopathy, cancer, orthopaedic surgery, previous VTE.
 • VTE is uncommon but a leading cause of maternal death (~9% of pregnancy-related deaths); pulmonary
   embolism arises predominantly from lower-limb DVT.
 • Hospitalisation independently raises VTE risk ~17-fold; surgery adds arrhythmia, hypoxia, pulmonary
   hypertension and heart failure.
 • Prophylaxis (extrapolated from caesarean data): compression stockings and pneumatic compression —
   favourable risk–benefit; low-dose unfractionated heparin (UFH) for select high risk, with obstetrics and
   anaesthesia.
 • Therapeutic anticoagulation is contraindicated with most regional anaesthesia; UFH is reversible with
   intravenous protamine sulfate. Left lateral decubitus reduces IVC compression and postoperative DVT.
 • Physiological leucocytosis: second/third trimester mean white blood cell (WBC) count 8500/mm³ (range
   5600–12,200), peaking up to 30,000/mm³ in labour → WBC is unreliable for diagnosing infection.
Concept — Pregnancy haematology is dilution plus hypercoagulability: low haematocrit and platelets are
expected, thrombosis is the real threat, and the WBC count loses its value as an infection marker.

Renal and Urinary Tracts
 • Volume expansion and vasodilatation → ↑ renal blood flow → glomerular filtration rate (GFR) +40–65%.
 • Serum creatinine, urea and uric acid fall: mean creatinine ~0.8 mg/dL; blood urea nitrogen (BUN) 13 →
   8–10 mg/dL.
 • Tubular reabsorption balances sodium, but the osmotic threshold for antidiuretic hormone (ADH)
   release and thirst is reset lower → mild hyponatraemia, sodium 4–5 mmol/L below baseline.
 • Kidneys enlarge up to 1 cm, partly from dilatation of the whole collecting system.
 • Pelvicalyceal dilatation: historically up to 90% of pregnancies, reaching 15 mm on the right and 5 mm on
   the left by term; contemporary sonographic reference data show renal pelvis dilatation >10 mm is rare.
 • Dilatation begins very early → not purely mechanical: progesterone, relaxin and endothelin relax smooth
   muscle and increase urinary dead space; it occurs even in ectopic kidneys (pelvic transplant).
 • Mechanical compression by the gravid uterus, predominantly right-sided; dilatation starts by the second
   month, peaks in the second trimester, ureteric diameter up to 2 cm.
 • Obstruction becomes harder to discern; pyelonephritis risk rises with pre-existing bacteriuria.
 • Complete postpartum resolution expected, barring intra-abdominal pathology.
Urine Chemistries During Pregnancy
 • Glycosuria is very common (10–100-fold increase) but intermittent; repeated glycosuria → obstetric
   screening for diabetes.
 • Hyperfiltration ± reduced tubular reabsorption raise protein excretion: upper limits per 24 h —
   proteinuria 300 mg, albuminuria 30 mg; rise is steepest in first and second trimesters.

 • Dipstick proteinuria is inaccurate (ignores urinary concentration); persistent proteinuria → exclude
   diabetes, pre-eclampsia, renal disease, urinary tract infection (UTI).
 • Fetal calcium demand + low albumin → total calcium falls; ionised calcium is unchanged; calciuria
   doubles.
Hematuria in the Pregnant Patient
 • Uterine compression elevates the trigone and increases bladder vascularity → idiopathic microscopic
   haematuria on dipstick in up to 20%.
 • Usually benign; commonest causes: infection, urolithiasis, renal disease, medications, trauma,
   obstruction, tumours.
 • Pregnancy-exclusive causes: pre-eclampsia and placenta percreta.
 • Pre-eclampsia = systemic syndrome of new-onset hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks;
   microscopic haematuria at two to eight times normal.
 • Most literature suggests an association between idiopathic haematuria and later pre-eclampsia, but larger
   cohorts found no association with complications → value of haematuria screening remains unclear.
 • Trap: a nonpregnant-normal creatinine may represent impairment, and a raised WBC is not evidence of
   infection.
Concept — Hydronephrosis of pregnancy is hormonal early and mechanical later, right-predominant and self-
resolving, so dilatation alone never establishes obstruction; rising GFR likewise lowers every filtration marker,
so “normal” values shift downward.

PHYSIOLOGIC HYDRONEPHROSIS OF PREGNANCY
 • The core consult: physiological versus pathological dilatation, especially with colic or suspected
   anatomical obstruction. Structure and function do not correlate; pathological hydronephrosis is rare
   (symptomatic admissions a tiny fraction of deliveries; physiological hydronephrosis common but
   symptomatic in only a small minority).
 • Flank pain correlates poorly with hydronephrosis, and grade does not correlate with pain intensity;
   grade does not affect birth weight.
 • Spontaneous renal fornix rupture (SRFR): rare (first described 1947), usually of unknown cause beyond
   pregnancy; commonest in first pregnancy and third trimester; pain is the only consistent symptom,
   laboratory findings variable, diagnosed on imaging; no long-term urological sequelae, but significantly
   more preterm labour.
Management of symptomatic physiological hydronephrosis (no calculus)
 • Conservative first: analgesia, positioning, intravenous fluids. Failure = evolving infection or deteriorating
   renal function.
 • Randomised stent-versus-conservative trial: stenting gave better relief in moderate–severe
   hydronephrosis, offset by stent discomfort, and few conservative patients crossed over → favour
   conservative care.
 • Double-J stenting under local anaesthesia associated with more caesarean section, preterm labour,
   premature rupture of membranes, lower Apgar scores, postpartum UTI.
 • Where offered a stent, over half chose conservative care with no difference in complications, postpartum
   pain or persistent hydronephrosis → stents reserved for complicated cases or additional obstructing
   pathology; intervention otherwise needed mainly for overt pyelonephritis.
 • Predictors of eventual stenting: maximal anteroposterior renal pelvis diameter, parity, C-reactive protein
   (CRP), WBC count, pain level.

GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS FOR IMAGING OF THE PREGNANT PATIENT
 • Fetal radiation effects: teratogenesis, carcinogenesis, mutagenesis; risk depends on gestational age and
   dose.
 • First trimester (organogenesis, rapid division) = highest teratogenic sensitivity; second/third trimester
   exposure is less teratogenic but raises childhood malignancy risk.
 • American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) guidance (2017): individualise; most
   urological imaging doses are below the threshold for fetal harm; plan with the radiologist to lower dose
   or acoustic output.
ACOG practice recommendations for diagnostic imaging
 • Ultrasonography (US) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) carry no associated risk and are the
   techniques of choice — used prudently, only to answer a relevant clinical question.
 • With few exceptions, radiography, computed tomography (CT) and nuclear medicine deliver doses far
   below those associated with fetal harm; if necessary or more readily available, they should not be
   withheld.
 • Gadolinium limited: only if it significantly improves diagnostic performance and is expected to improve
   maternal or fetal outcome.
 • Nuclear medicine selectively, depending on the radioisotope. Technetium-99m mercaptoacetyltriglycine
   (99mTc-MAG3) — most widely used renal agent; not filtered, extracted by tubules; differential function
   and drainage. 99mTc studies give fetal exposure <5 mGy (safe); half-life 6 h, pure gamma emitter.
 • Intraoperative fluoroscopy: shield the fetus, collimate, low-dose spot films at the lowest feasible
   exposure for retrograde pyelography and stent positioning.
Concept — Fetal risk is the product of gestational age and dose; because diagnostic doses sit below the harm
threshold, necessary imaging is minimised, never withheld.

ACOG practice recommendations for nonobstetric surgery
 • No current anaesthetic agent is teratogenic in humans at standard concentrations at any gestational age.
 • No evidence of fetal brain effect from in-utero anaesthetic or sedative exposure; no animal data for
   exposures <3 hours.
 • Fetal heart rate (FHR) monitoring can guide positioning and cardiorespiratory management and may
   influence a decision to deliver.
 • Never deny or delay medically necessary surgery, regardless of trimester.
 • Postpone elective surgery until after delivery.
 • Antenatal corticosteroids for fetuses at viable preterm gestations; monitor for preterm labour
   perioperatively.
 • Screen for VTE risk and give perioperative prophylaxis.
 • Operate where there are neonatal and paediatric services, an obstetric provider with caesarean
   privileges readily available, and someone qualified to interpret FHR.
 • Ethics: involve all stakeholders, but patient autonomy comes first (ACOG ethics committee opinion);
   counsel risks to mother and fetus honestly; women are not denied procedures because they are
   pregnant.
 • Left lateral decubitus (modified) for open, laparoscopic and endoscopic procedures.
 • Approach by surgeon experience and exposure; no randomised trials; open and laparoscopic outcomes
   broadly equal (laparoscopy associated with earlier delivery and lower birth weight in one series).
 • Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons (SAGES): ports placed under direct vision
   according to fundal height; insufflation 10–15 mmHg is safe; higher pressures cause maternal
   cardiorespiratory change after 20 minutes; use maternal end-tidal capnography.
 • Excess perinatal mortality is due to the disease, not the operation. Third-trimester surgery is associated
   with preterm delivery, and third-trimester ureteroscopy with preterm labour — key for counselling.
 • ACOG now permits necessary surgery in all trimesters, but the second trimester carries the least risk of
   preterm contractions and spontaneous abortion.
Concept — Necessity, not trimester, decides whether to operate; the second trimester is the preferred
window, and untreated disease — not surgery — is what drives perinatal loss.

GENERAL CONCEPTS OF UROLOGIC MEDICATION ADMINISTRATION DURING
PREGNANCY
 • Prescription drug use in pregnancy is almost universal.
 • Physiological change alters pharmacokinetics — absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion.
 • Placental transfer favours uncharged, lipophilic, low-molecular-weight drugs.
 • US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR), 2015: letter
   categories A, B, C, D and X removed from all prescription drug and biological labelling.
 • Label must now carry: a risk summary for pregnancy and lactation, the supporting data, and information
   to guide prescribing and counselling. Consequence: individualised evidence review; rarely a clear “yes”
   or “no”.
 • Use the FDA searchable database and consult the patient's obstetrician.
 • OnabotulinumtoxinA (BoNT-A) (idiopathic overactive bladder, neurogenic lower urinary tract symptoms):
   manufacturer states may cause fetal harm on animal data — reduced fetal weight and ossification;
   repeated daily dosing during organogenesis (extrapolated to the maximum human dose of 400 units)

UROLITHIASIS IN PREGNANCY
 • Incidence 1:200 to 1:1500 pregnancies; renal colic is the leading nonobstetric cause of hospitalisation in
   pregnancy.
 • Stones associated with preterm delivery, pre-eclampsia, placenta praevia, preterm premature rupture of
   membranes, yet no effect on birth weight, Apgar or perinatal mortality.
 • Historically no higher than in age-matched nonpregnant women; recent data: odds rise with gestation,
   peak 0–3 months postpartum, baseline by 1 year.
 • Multiparous women reported to be affected more often (~3:1), but not after adjustment for age;
   population data link prior pregnancy to later nephrolithiasis.
 • Left and right calculi are equally common despite right-predominant hydronephrosis; most present in
   the second or third trimester.

Etiology
 • Recap: dilatation evident by 6–10 weeks, up to 90% by the third trimester, may persist 12 weeks
   postpartum; physiological hydronephrosis is the commonest cause of dilatation and mimics colic.
 • Humoral: progesterone relaxes ureteric smooth muscle, possibly reducing peristalsis. Mechanical
   compression is probably primary — right predominance and rare dilatation below the pelvic brim;
   mechanical mechanism predominates even with altered urinary anatomy.

Evaluation
 • Signs masked, pain localisation altered; many obstructing stones are first misdiagnosed as appendicitis,
   diverticulitis or abruption.
 • Commonest presentation: flank pain with macroscopic or microscopic haematuria, sometimes UTI;
   painless haematuria is unusual with an obstructing stone.
 • Always send urine culture — baseline pyuria lowers its sensitivity for infection.
 • Persistent infection with a urea-splitting organism → consider calculi.
 • Irritative voiding symptoms, chills, nausea and vomiting are nonspecific and shared with other intra-
   abdominal conditions.

Multidisciplinary Guidelines
 • Multi-institutional panel (urology, anaesthesiology, obstetrics, diagnostic and interventional radiology)
   synthesised the literature into point-of-care flowcharts. Selected statements:
 1. Multidisciplinary approach to suspected symptomatic nephrolithiasis.
 2. Initial evaluation: history, obstetric history, examination, urinalysis with reflex culture, basic metabolic
    panel, complete blood count.
 3. Renal–bladder US is first-line imaging.
 4. Diagnosis uncertain and clinical status changes → offer second-line imaging.
 5. Symptoms controllable → trial of passage with hydration and analgesia.
 6. Septic obstructing stone → urgent decompression with ureteric stent.
 7. Exchange ureteric stents every 4 weeks until definitive treatment.
 8. Conservative failure → ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy first-line in noncomplex cases.
 9. Third-trimester ureteroscopy may raise preterm labour but is not excluded.
10. Neuraxial anaesthesia preferred over general where appropriate.
11. Discharge with stent, nephrostomy or residual stone → arranged urology and obstetric follow-up.
 • Narrative reviews reach concordant algorithms and stress that extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy
   (SWL) is contraindicated.
Imaging
 • US is the standard initial study, but has low sensitivity, moderate specificity for calculi (limitations
   tabulated).
 • Colour Doppler ureteric jets are sensitive for obstruction in general, but unpredictable in pregnancy —
   use with caution.
 • Doppler resistive index (RI) rises with acute obstruction; RI is unchanged by pregnancy and by
   physiological hydronephrosis, so it can detect acute obstruction here; a change in RI is more accurate
   than absolute RI.
 • Transvaginal US images the distal ureter and detects far more distal ureteric stones than transabdominal
   US.
 • Some women undergoing intervention for a radiographically “detected” stone prove to have no stone.
 • MRI: calcium not visualised — stone is a filling defect in high-signal urine; small stones missed;
   distinguishes physiological from calculous hydronephrosis; half-Fourier acquisition single-shot turbo spin-
   echo (HASTE) improves utility.
 • Intravenous pyelography and nuclear renography: limited, rarely used.
 • Noncontrast CT is the gold standard in nonpregnant patients (~99% sensitivity and specificity); per
   ACOG, not to be withheld if necessary.
 • Low-dose CT: high positive predictive value, shows nonurological pathology; second line; ultra-low-dose
   protocols promising.
 • American Urological Association (AUA): radiologist and obstetrician estimate exposure so that total
   pregnancy exposure does not exceed the ACOG maximum of 50 mGy.

Conservative management
 • AUA: spontaneous passage rates equal those of nonpregnant patients → observation is initial therapy if
   symptoms controlled; 50–80% pass with hydration and analgesia. Follow closely, since a stone event
   raises maternal and fetal morbidity.
 • Analgesia: traditional regimen is frequent small doses of morphine; coordinate with obstetrics.
 • NSAIDs such as ketorolac are contraindicated — premature closure of the ductus arteriosus and fetal
   pulmonary hypertension if given in the third trimester.
 • Medical expulsive therapy (MET) with α-blockers or calcium channel blockers relaxes ureteric smooth
   muscle; AUA endorses α-blockers for small uncomplicated distal stones in nonpregnant patients. In
   pregnancy: inadequately investigated, off-label — counsel accordingly.
Intervention
  • About one-third require intervention — pain uncontrolled by analgesia, persistent obstruction, infection.
 • Delayed decompression raises maternal and fetal complications; pregnant women with obstructing
   stones and UTI are less likely to be decompressed than nonpregnant women — a care gap.
 • Options: temporising (ureteric stent or PCN) or definitive (ureteroscopy, URS), planned by a
   multidisciplinary team.
 • Stents drain well, but hypercalciuria and hyperuricosuria accelerate encrustation → frequent exchange
   (intervals tabulated below); early gestation therefore means multiple exchanges, with risks of bacteriuria,
   UTI, migration, pain and reduced quality of life.
 • PCN shares the drawbacks — encrustation, colonisation, pain, dislodgement, repeated procedures — and
   is associated with more preterm labour than observation, stenting or URS (possibly confounded by
   acuity). Most tubes need exchange for debris occlusion; many are removed for obstruction, fever or pain.
 • Temporising drainage usually still needs postpartum definitive stone removal → trend toward definitive
   URS in any trimester; complications uncommon and urinary complications no different from
   nonpregnant patients.
 • Radiation at URS: low-dose or pulsed fluoroscopy with collimation, lead shielding placed beneath the
   patient (below-table source), direct vision without fluoroscopy, or intraoperative US guidance.
 • SWL: pregnancy is a contraindication, despite inadvertent cases without fetal harm.
 • Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL): small series only; outside high-volume multidisciplinary centres
   (supine, US-guided), defer until after delivery (anaesthetic and radiation risk).

 KEY POINTS
   • Ultrasonography remains the standard initial imaging study in evaluation of the pregnant patient.
   • A trend toward offering definitive therapy with URS in pregnant patients has evolved with acceptable
     complication rates.
   • Minimise ionising radiation during URS by using a below-table radiograph source and shielding the
     fetus with a lead apron placed below the patient.

Concept — Diagnosis is ultrasound-led with low-dose CT a legitimate backstop; management escalates
observation → temporising drainage → definitive ureteroscopy, and the burden of 3–4-weekly exchanges is
what has pushed practice toward early definitive ureteroscopy.

PREGNANCY IN WOMEN WITH CONGENITAL UROLOGIC CONDITIONS
 • Systematic review of pregnancy after neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD) and urological
   reconstruction (1986–2020): high risk of febrile UTI, premature delivery and delivery complications;
   reassuringly, obstetric outcomes are generally good with multidisciplinary care at a specialised centre.
 • Even with tertiary care, complications are frequent: new or worsening hydronephrosis in most, with
   many needing nephrostomy.
 • Anorectal and cloacal malformations: pregnancy feasible, with special risks.

Myelomeningocele
 • Myelomeningocele (MMC) considerations: age, comorbidity, genetic risk to offspring, pelvic bony and
   muscular anatomy, obesity, diversion status, prior abdominal surgery, renal function, fetal presentation →
   organise multidisciplinary care.
Fertility
 • Fertility normal; ~70% of those who conceive have successful pregnancies.
 • Uterus strains balance, ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt, bowel, bladder, diversion, skin, lungs, heart and
   — foremost — UTI risk.

Pregnancy
 • Reconstruction preceded pregnancy by years and targeted kidneys and continence, not pregnancy.
 • Greatest risk factor for worsening renal function (with or without reconstruction) is pre-existing renal
   insufficiency; pregnancy-related loss of function and new hypertension raise preterm delivery and
   growth restriction.
 • Possible solitary kidney. Measure GFR rather than rely on a single creatinine — altered habitus and low
   muscle mass.
 • Stones after bladder augmentation: 9–50% — incomplete emptying, chronic bacteriuria, foreign bodies,
   mucus, metabolic acidosis, enteric hyperoxaluria, diet.
 • Uterus compresses the ureter against the pelvic brim → worse hydronephrosis and stasis. After lower
   tract reconstruction, hydronephrosis worsens at 20–28 weeks — monitor most closely then.
 • Inhibitors (citrate, magnesium, glycosaminoglycans) mean even known stone formers do not form more
   stones in pregnancy, but impaired sensation can hide upper tract obstruction until systemic illness.
 • Retained defunctioned bladder after diversion without cystectomy: pyocystis may first become overt in
   pregnancy.
 • Other problems: compromised mesenteric perfusion of the diversion segment, intestinal obstruction,
   stomal stenosis or prolapse, channel compression, metabolic complications.
 • Ureterosigmoidostomy: deformation of sigmoid or ureteric anastomoses → disrupted implants or
   incomplete emptying; vaginal delivery can damage the anal sphincter on which it depends → de novo
   urinary ± faecal incontinence.
Mode of Delivery
 • First obtain operative records, urodynamics and upper tract studies; variable paediatric practice and
   revisions make the anatomy the priority.
 • Determinants beyond obstetric factors: type of diversion or prior surgery, disease anatomy, fetal
   presentation.
 • Native urethra or ureterosigmoidostomy → continence may suffer after vaginal delivery; counsel.
 • Augmentation with native bladder neck and urethral continence: vaginal delivery not contraindicated.
   Bladder neck reconstruction risks injury to pelvic support and continence mechanism → most
   recommend caesarean (also with artificial urinary sphincter).
 • Artificial urinary sphincter (AUS): a functioning device does not change pregnancy or delivery
   complications; avoid high forceps; evidence anecdotal.
 • Prior bladder neck sling does not preclude vaginal delivery; after synthetic midurethral sling, only a
   moderate fall in continence after either mode, most delivering vaginally. Continence loss after vaginal
   delivery following any incontinence procedure approaches one in five — discuss.
 • Vaginal: avoid prolonged labour (pelvic floor). Caesarean: urologist familiar with the anatomy protects
   bladder and pedicle. Distance from high-risk care may favour planned caesarean.

Prevention of Neural Tube Defects
 • Polymorphisms in folic acid metabolism are considered the primary deficit → folate supplementation is
   critical.
 • Early-1990s US programme (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 1993, with Health Services
   Administration, FDA, National Institutes of Health), three stages: (1) 0.4 mg folic acid for low-risk, 4 mg
   for high-risk women; (2) improved diet; (3) fortification of the food supply.

   weeks of gestation.
 • Residual risk despite folate: inborn errors of metabolism, variable absorption; genetic, dietary and
   epigenetic factors under study.
 • Prenatal screening: maternal serum α-fetoprotein plus US; fetal MRI as adjunct for prognosis.

Classic Bladder Exstrophy
 • Bladder exstrophy (BE): ~1 in 30,000 live births, male predominance. Most now reach adulthood, but
   adult continuity of care (pelvic floor, sexual function, fertility, pregnancy) is poor.
Genital Anatomy
 • Pubic diastasis; anterior displacement of labia majora, vagina and anus; bifid clitoris; bladder protruding
   through the abdominal wall defect.
 • Bony and muscular defects compromise levator ani anterior support.
 • Vagina: reduced depth, normal calibre; introitus narrow, stenotic, anteriorly displaced; horizontal
   vaginal lie. Diastasis widens with growth → divergent labia, mons and clitoris.

Fertility
 • Normal to mildly impaired; ~68% of those who conceive have successful pregnancies.
 • Adhesions and retroflexion after pelvic surgery; anterior-wall cervix may impair semen delivery.
Pregnancy
 • Preconception counselling and team as for MMC, plus continence.
 • Heritability ~1 in 70 offspring — counsel.

   urethral self-catheterisation. Unlike the general population, no parity–persistent POP association
   detected.

 • Anatomy and postpartum prolapse → planned caesarean with a multidisciplinary team is the accepted

   mesentery; catheterise channels preoperatively.
 • Counsel on preterm delivery — mean gestation ~36 weeks; abnormal presentation in over half →
   extensive planning.

FUNCTIONAL AND ANATOMIC UROLOGIC CONCERNS DURING PREGNANCY
Intermittent Catheterization
 • Uterine growth plus habitus and reduced flexibility make intermittent catheterisation harder — through a
   continent channel and through the native urethra; some women need an indwelling catheter for the
   pregnancy.

Urinary Diversion
 • Heterogeneous population — no global principles, but series show excellent outcomes.
Ileocystoplasty (Bladder Augmentation)
  • Ileal conduit pedicle is displaced cephalad and lateral to the uterus; augmentation pedicle usually stays
    anterior to the uterus.
 • Tension on the mesentery rises slowly → time to adapt; mesenteric adherence to the uterus is rare but
   must be planned for.
 • Literature is small case series, but many augmented women have successful pregnancies and vaginal
   deliveries.
 • High UTI rates and tendency to premature delivery.
 • Without bladder neck closure: de novo incontinence from uterine pressure, usually resolving after
   delivery.
 • Urine pregnancy tests give false positives after contact with bowel mucosa → use serum human
   chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG).
Continent Catheterizable Stoma
 • De novo stomal difficulty, especially a right lower quadrant channel; uterus compresses and stretches
   the channel; resolves after delivery.
 • Options: coudé-tipped catheter; native urethral catheterisation if patent and feasible.
 • Raised intra-abdominal pressure → stomal prolapse or parastomal hernia, likewise expected to resolve.
Neobladder
 • Few reported cases after orthotopic neobladder; caesarean section with urology in the team was the
   preferred mode.
Urinary Tract Infection and Urinary Diversion
 • Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB): ≥1 bacterial species at ≥105 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL (≥108 CFU/
   L), irrespective of pyuria, without signs or symptoms attributable to UTI.
 • Continent diversion or augmentation + intermittent catheterisation → chronic or intermittent bacteriuria
   expected; outside pregnancy it is usually not treated.
 • In pregnancy most experts treat ASB because of fetal risk.
 • Pyelonephritis rises to 16–18% after urinary diversion; UTI/pyelonephritis up to 60% after
   augmentation → preterm labour, fetal loss, small-for-gestational-age infants.

 • ACOG guidance (2020) on spinal cord injury (SCI): UTI and pyelonephritis are the commonest reasons for

UROLOGIC MALIGNANCY DURING PREGNANCY
 • Malignancy complicates ~1 in 1000 pregnancies; commonest is breast (1 in 5000); urological cancers ~13
   per 1,000,000 pregnancies.
 • Balance pregnancy, maternal health and treatment delay → individualised, multidisciplinary care.
 • Many conditions surface incidentally through antenatal screening; haematuria, flank pain and
   hypertension may be attributed to pregnancy.
 • Pregnancy and cancer are both highly antigenic states tolerated by the immune system; pregnancy is
   thought not to alter tumour biology or progression, though data are lacking; little data in
   immunocompromised patients.

Renal Tumors
 • Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the commonest urological tumour in pregnancy. Presentation as usual:
   pain, haematuria, hypertension, mass.
 • Delayed diagnosis (low suspicion, reluctance to image, symptom overlap) → over half >7 cm or advanced
   (International Network on Cancer, Infertility and Pregnancy registry, INCIP).
 • Known risks: tobacco, von Hippel–Lindau disease; no causal role for pregnancy; parity, hormones and
   subtype data inconsistent.
 • Imaging principles as above (ACOG, American College of Radiology and American Institute of Ultrasound
   in Medicine, reaffirmed 2021); AUA: contrast-enhanced CT or MRI to characterise mass, kidney and
   viscera.
 • Iodinated contrast crosses the placenta but is not teratogenic or mutagenic in animals; gadolinium
   remains controversial → contrast CT preferred when enhancement is needed for staging.
 • Renal mass biopsy when haematological, metastatic, inflammatory or infectious lesion suspected. Young
   patient → genetic counselling for familial syndromes (AUA).
 • Treatment as nonpregnant: nephron-sparing or radical nephrectomy, timed by clinical status and
   trimester, including caesarean with simultaneous oncological surgery; team defines fetal risk and advises
   when termination may be appropriate.

 • Laparoscopic surgery feasible with low morbidity in any trimester.
 • Systemic therapy — multikinase inhibitors, mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors, antiangiogenics,

Angiomyolipoma
 • Angiomyolipoma (AML): benign, second commonest renal tumour, usually asymptomatic; many are not
   associated with tuberous sclerosis complex.
 • Rupture → haematuria, flank pain, life-threatening retroperitoneal haemorrhage. Pregnancy
   predisposes: hormonal environment, vascular volume, raised abdominal pressure. Ruptured tumours
   are large (~10 cm).
 • Haemodynamically stable → most reach term; unstable → substantial fetal death.
 • Imaging (including serial imaging during observation) benefits outweigh fetal risk.
 • Outside urgent surgery, embolisation is treatment of choice, despite preterm labour risk from post-
   embolisation syndrome.

Bladder Tumors
 • Exceedingly rare; urothelial carcinoma commonest; adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and
   benign leiomyoma reported.
 • Haematuria is misdiagnosed as vaginal bleeding in about a fifth → catheterised specimen to establish
   the source. Urgency, frequency and nocturia are common in pregnancy and raise little suspicion.
 • Most are non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). SCC (schistosomiasis-associated): aggressive,
   poor prognosis despite radical cystectomy.
 • AUA NMIBC and muscle-invasive guidelines are not always fully applicable in pregnancy.
 • Haematuria without infection → standard evaluation: cystoscopy plus upper tract imaging, primarily CT;
   US diagnoses only about half.
 • Transurethral resection is safe at any gestation; staging imaging often required.
 • NMIBC: defer intravesical adjuvant therapy until after delivery (INCIP). Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG)
   used once without fetal harm but carries systemic risk; mitomycin C likewise usually delayed.
 • Muscle-invasive disease: poor prognosis, no standard; neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radical
   cystectomy timed to trimester, extent and stakeholder wishes.
Concept — Treat as in the nonpregnant patient — definitive surgery for renal tumours, resection for bladder
tumours — but defer what can wait (intravesical and systemic therapy) and time what cannot to the
trimester.

MANAGEMENT OF URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS IN PREGNANCY
 • Up to 26% of women report UTI diagnosis or treatment in pregnancy, with maternal and neonatal risk.
 • ASB in 2–10% of pregnancies (definition above).
 • Moderate-quality evidence: treating ASB likely reduces progression to pyelonephritis.
 • Untreated ASB → pyelonephritis 15–45%; most episodes in the third trimester, when hydronephrosis
   and stasis peak; one-third have prior pyelonephritis.
 • Pyelonephritis → preterm birth (ascending infection may underlie up to half of premature deliveries,
   especially <30 weeks), septicaemia, respiratory distress, low birth weight.
 • Treatment lowers pyelonephritis to 1–4% and reduces preterm birth and low birth weight.
 • Universal screening (US Preventive Services Task Force, USPSTF; Infectious Diseases Society of America,
   IDSA): urine culture at 12–16 weeks or first antenatal visit; positive = midstream clean-catch >100,000

 KEY POINTS: MANAGEMENT OF PLACENTAL ABNORMALITIES
   • US is superior to MRI for routine screening for placenta accreta in at-risk patients, but invasion of
     adjacent pelvic tissues and organs may be better defined by MRI.
   • Optimal timing for planned delivery with suspected placenta percreta is approximately 34 weeks.
   • Antenatally diagnosed percreta → tertiary centre and a team including urologists, general surgeons,
     obstetric anaesthesiologists, interventional radiologists and intensivists.
   • Counsel carefully on complexity and urinary tract injury; consider retrograde catheter placement to
     prevent or identify ureteric injury.
