Chapter 121 — Urinary Tract Fistulae
 • Definition: a urinary tract fistula is an abnormal epithelial- or mesothelial-lined connection between the
   urinary tract (kidney, ureter, bladder, urethra) and another body cavity (gastrointestinal, vascular,
   reproductive) or the skin (cutaneous).
 • Causes are myriad — iatrogenic, malignancy, inflammation, infection, ischaemia, trauma and parturition.
   In the developed world most fistulae are iatrogenic, the sequel of a surgical intervention.
 • Presentation varies with the anatomical connection and the cause, so diagnosis can be difficult; a high
   index of suspicion and a systematic approach are required to confirm the diagnosis and begin
   management.
 • The burden is physical, psychosocial, medicolegal and financial. Diagnosis should be expeditious without
   unnecessary testing, with open and honest dialogue. Until definitive therapy, timely management of
   leakage, infection and skin breakdown improves quality of life (QoL) and preserves the patient–provider
   relationship.
 • The principles of management are virtually universal to all fistula types (Table 121.1), although they rest
   on very limited evidence.
 • Prevention is paramount because most fistulae in industrialised countries are iatrogenic: techniques to
   avoid injury, vigilant intraoperative evaluation for injury, and techniques that enhance detection and
   repair of injury when found.
 • Once a fistula is diagnosed, optimise healing conditions — improve nutrition and eliminate infection
   (thereby minimising inflammation).
 • Malignancy causes fistulae both directly and through the sequelae of treatment (for example, radiation);
   anyone with a history of, or suspicion of, malignancy needs a work-up including biopsy of the fistula
   site.
 • Continuous urinary drainage, diversion of urine away from the tract and relief of obstruction are crucial.
   A continuous flow of urine maintains fistula patency and allows epithelialisation of the tract, which
   reduces the chance of spontaneous closure.
 • A minority heal conservatively; the vast majority need surgery. The surgical principles (Table 121.2) are
   independent of fistula location and surgical route, and are likewise not supported by robust evidence.
 • Factors determining approach and success: size and number of fistulae, complexity, location relative to
   trigone and ureters, need for concurrent procedures, tissue quality and need for interposition,
   accessibility, comorbidity, prior radiation therapy and surgeon experience.
 • The first repair has the highest chance of success — hence optimisation of patient- and surgeon-related
   factors before the first attempt.

 • Pattern: every item removes a reason for the tract to persist — flow of urine, infection, obstruction,
   foreign body, tension, ischaemia, apposed suture lines, or unrecognised tumour.
 • Trap: forgetting biopsy — a fistula may be the only sign of recurrent malignancy.
Concept — a fistula persists because urine keeps flowing through a tract that cannot heal; every
management principle either diverts that flow or restores the tissue's capacity to heal.

Vesicovaginal Fistula
Aetiopathogenesis and Epidemiology
 • Global burden: an estimated 0.5–2 million females live with untreated urogenital fistulae; true prevalence
   is unknown because of limited access to care and documentation.
 • Vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) — abnormal bladder–vagina connection — is the commonest acquired urinary
   tract fistula, >75% of genitourinary (GU) fistulae.
 • History: the earliest evidence is a large VVF in the mummified Queen Henhenit, wife of King Menthotep II
   (about 2050 BCE).

 • Obstructed labour injury complex: multiorgan damage — renal, urinary, reproductive, anorectal,

Hysterectomy and urological injury
 • Scale: about 498,000 hysterectomies a year in the United States; about one-fifth of females report prior
   hysterectomy.
 • Injury rates: historically bladder 1–5% and ureter 0.2–2.5% of hysterectomies. Contemporary meta-
   analyses: urological injury after laparoscopic or robotic hysterectomy is <1% and does not differ from
   vaginal hysterectomy.
 • Risk raised by anatomical distortion: intraoperative bleeding, prior pelvic surgery, endometriosis, fibroids,
   adnexal masses, prior radiation and malignancy; and by concomitant prolapse repair, concomitant
   incontinence procedure, or mesh for prolapse — so VVF risk tracks surgical complexity.
 • VVF after hysterectomy: overall about 0.8 per 1000; lowest after vaginal (about 0.2/1000), highest after
   laparoscopic (about 2/1000). A national database found the highest 30-day fistula rate after
   laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy (LAVH) and the lowest after transvaginal hysterectomy.
 • Radical hysterectomy for cervical cancer: VVF more frequent with the laparoscopic than the open route.
 • Most postoperative fistulae arise from unrecognised injury. Immediate recognition and repair lowers
   VVF rates compared with delayed recognition, and only a small minority of fistulae follow a recognised
   injury.
 • Litigation: urological injury carries a 91-fold higher risk of litigation.
Universal cystoscopy at hysterectomy
 • For: high detection rates in routine-cystoscopy series; an institutional universal-cystoscopy policy reduced
   delayed urological injuries, doubled intraoperative detection and eliminated VVF.
 • Against: a large systematic review found intraoperative detection rose fivefold with no fall in delayed
   injury; a population study found no difference in 30-day delayed lower-tract injury; cystoscopy adds
   operative time (median about 17 minutes) and raises urinary tract infection (UTI) risk.
 • Cystoscopy does not replace careful retroperitoneal dissection and constant awareness of the bladder
   and ureteric course. Methylene blue instillation may also help.

Other causes
 • Pelvic reconstructive surgery, especially with synthetic mesh: the foreign body sustains inflammation
   that prevents healing and maintains a tract; infection, particularly of non–type 1 mesh, may contribute.
 • Radiation (cervical, endometrial, colorectal, bladder cancer): VVF rates up to 13% in high-resource versus
   0.2% in low-resource countries. Mechanism: fibrosis of the lamina propria and obliterative arteritis of
   small and medium arteries → atrophy or necrosis → urothelial ulceration → fistula.
    ◦ 0.6–2% of females irradiated for cervical cancer develop VVF.
     ◦ Typical presentation 1.5–2 years after treatment, but up to 30 years later.
     ◦ Dose dependent; higher in smokers.

 • Bevacizumab (anti–vascular endothelial growth factor [VEGF] monoclonal antibody for advanced cervical,
   ovarian and colorectal cancer) raises fistula risk through its hypoxic effects.
 • Primary and recurrent malignancy itself causes fistula — every VVF in a female with prior malignancy
   must be evaluated for recurrence.
 • Rare causes: chronic infection, congenital, vaginal lasers, endometriosis, foreign bodies (pessaries,
   intrauterine devices).
Concept — obstetric and iatrogenic VVF share one mechanism — ischaemic necrosis followed by a
continuous pressure-driven flow of urine — but differ in scale: prolonged impaction produces a large
devascularised field, whereas a surgical injury produces a small focal defect at the cuff.

Classifications of Vesicovaginal Fistula
 • No universal system: at least 25 exist, almost all for obstetric fistulae. The commonest are Goh and
   Waaldijk (Table 121.3); the World Health Organization (WHO) also classifies fistulae as simple or complex.
 • Both address urethral involvement and size. Waaldijk is built on the closing (continence) mechanism and
   extent of urethral involvement; Goh uses a fixed point — the external urethral meatus — to describe
   location and, combined with size, the extent of urethral loss.

 • Pattern: both systems grade proximity to, or involvement of, the continence mechanism — the closer the
   fistula to the meatus, the worse closure and continence.
 • Predictive value: Goh predicts surgical closure better than Waaldijk in comparative studies, and proximity
   to the meatus predicts closure and continence after repeat repair. In the developed world, Goh type did
   not predict closure but continence worsened with higher type.
 • Trap: Goh type 1 is the most distant (best) and type 4 the closest (worst) — the numbering runs towards
   the meatus.
 • Practical bottom line: classifications serve research and communication; every VVF is unique. Always
   describe size, location (trigonal versus supratrigonal), tissue loss, ureteric and urethral involvement for
   planning.
Concept — fistula classification is really a measure of threat to continence: distance from the meatus and
involvement of the closing mechanism predict the patient who is closed but still wet.

Diagnosis of Vesicovaginal Fistula
 • High index of suspicion above all, then history and examination; office tests, endoscopy and imaging
   confirm an uncertain diagnosis and evaluate adjacent structures.
Clinical presentation.
 • Commonest complaint: urinary leakage. Characterise viscosity, colour, odour, volume, onset relative to
   events, provoking and relieving factors. Coexisting mechanisms — stress urinary incontinence (SUI),
   overactive bladder (OAB), mixed urinary incontinence (MUI), overflow, post-void dribble — may coexist.
 • Classic VVF: constant leakage day and night, regardless of position or activity, often without urge. A
   large tract may abolish the desire to void because the bladder never fills. A small VVF may cause only
   positional or minimal leakage.
 • Also: dysuria, urgency, frequency, recurrent or persistent UTI, gross haematuria or blood-tinged
   discharge; patients may mistake leakage for vaginal discharge. Pain is uncommon except with skin
   irritation or radiation change.
 • Red flags — fever, chills, malaise, nausea, vomiting, flank pain, ileus: suggest urinoma and/or ureteric
   obstruction → evaluate for concurrent ureteric injury and manage immediately.
 • Timing points to mechanism: unrecognised or failed cystotomy repair presents within the first few days;
   thermal or crush injury presents at 7–10 days; radiation fistula presents years later; VVF may be the only
   sign of recurrence — biopsy the tract in anyone with prior malignancy.
 • Misdirected treatment is common: patients may already have been treated for SUI, OAB or MUI without
   benefit.
 • Obstetric history: in industrialised countries, obstetric VVF follows caesarean and forceps delivery
   (unrecognised bladder injury), leaking within days. Exclude overflow from neurological or stretch injury
   after epidural bladder mismanagement.
 • Obtain and review prior operative notes. Gynaecological history: endometriosis, abnormal bleeding,
   intrauterine device, pelvic inflammatory disease, pessary, indwelling catheter.
 • Optimise fitness: smoking cessation, glycaemic control, nutrition, and a support system matching physical
   and psychosocial needs.
Examination findings.
 • Abdomen: distension, tenderness, costovertebral angle tenderness; fresh incisions for infection, leakage
   or dehiscence; confirms prior surgery in poor historians.
 • Perineum: skin irritation from persistent moisture.
 • Speculum: cervix and cuff integrity; pooled fluid for creatinine — fluid creatinine approximating urine
   creatinine confirms urine (soaked pads or tampons can also be tested). Note number, size and location of
   orifices. The vaginal apex is the commonest site of post-hysterectomy VVF.
 • If no defect is seen: backfill the bladder with methylene blue and watch; if still no leak, pack the vagina
   with gauze or tampon and walk for 15–60 minutes — blue at the apex indicates a tract. Granulation or
   friable tissue may signal the tract.
 • Prior malignancy or radiation: biopsy the defect or any friable mass. Severe post-radiation stenosis may
   limit examination → examination under anaesthesia with endoscopy and imaging of ureteric integrity.
 • Vaginal atrophy: offer vaginal oestrogen before repair. Examination also judges tissue quality, feasibility of
   repair and the likely route (vaginal versus abdominal).

Investigations
Dye tests.
 • Simple office confirmation of a connection; small series show high sensitivity.
 • Single dye test: backfill with methylene blue or indigo carmine (about 300 mL) → vaginal examination for
   blue leak → if unseen, tampon and ambulate → inspect.
 • Stain position on the tampon localises: proximal stain → VVF; distal stain → urethrovaginal fistula or
   SUI. Leak through the cervical os indicates vesicouterine fistula.
 • Double dye test distinguishes VVF from ureterovaginal fistula: oral phenazopyridine 100–200 mg → after
   1 hour (urine orange) → backfill blue → tampon → ambulate.

 • Trap: the double dye test does not replace upper tract imaging, which is imperative whenever
   ureterovaginal fistula is suspected.
Endoscopic tests.
 • Cystoscopy is integral (sensitivity for GU fistula about 92–93%) — for diagnosis and for planning.
   Proximity to the ureter, bladder neck involvement or associated mesh mean major reconstruction;
   assess capacity and biopsy suspicious masses. Findings decide route and need for ureteric or bladder neck
   reconstruction, mesh excision, augmentation or diversion.
 • Mature fistulae have smooth epithelialised tracts and are easier to see; small tracts or a trabeculated
   bladder make identification hard → probe erythematous patches, granulation or bullous oedema with a
   guidewire or 5-Fr open-ended ureteric catheter; cannulation shows the course.
 • Flexible cystoscopy (office) allows cystoscopy and vaginoscopy and retroflexion to detect bladder neck
   fistulae. Cystoscopy under anaesthesia allows simultaneous retrograde pyelography and stenting —
   indicated if ureteric obstruction or ureterovaginal fistula is suspected. Inspect the urethra for
   urethrovaginal fistula.
 • Vaginoscopy shows the apex and the vaginal side of the tract, allows biopsy under vision (vital after
   malignancy), overcomes radiation stenosis, can be combined with a dye test (including dye at the cervical
   os for a vesicouterine tract), and evaluates mesh exposure.
Imaging.
 • Cystography diagnoses VVF when uncertain and shows exact location and number of tracts; voiding
   cystourethrogram (VCUG) shows a concomitant urethrovaginal fistula or rare outlet obstruction (for
   example, after pelvic trauma).
 • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), especially T2-weighted, helps in difficult cases. Transvaginal colour
   Doppler ultrasound has been described but is not routine.
 • Lower tract imaging is not mandatory if the VVF is obvious on examination or endoscopy. Upper tract
   imaging is mandatory to exclude ureteric injury — especially when fistula is suspected but no VVF is
   found. Ureteric injury or ureterovaginal fistula coexists in up to 12% of VVF.
 • Renal ultrasound screens for hydronephrosis; in obstetric fistula, absence of grade 3 hydronephrosis has
   a high negative predictive value for ureterovaginal fistula.

 • Excretory urography — computed tomography urogram (CTU) with delayed phase (formerly intravenous
   pyelogram [IVP]) — whenever upper tract involvement is suspected. Partial ureteric obstruction with a
   VVF is a ureterovaginal fistula until proven otherwise.
 • MRI urography is reserved for iodinated contrast allergy or renal disease precluding CT.
 • Abnormal or unclear CTU, or suspected obstruction → retrograde pyelogram in theatre with stenting; if
   stenting fails → percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN). The nephrostomy tract later permits antegrade
   ureteroscopy during repair to locate ureters in adhesions and identify the fistulous ureteric orifice.
Urodynamics.
 • Urodynamics (UDS) rarely help diagnosis but aid planning; about 10–15% of females with fistula also
   have SUI.
 • Video urodynamics (VUDS) separate fistula leakage from SUI, urgency urinary incontinence (UUI), MUI,
   overflow or diverticulum — guiding counselling and concomitant procedures: fascial sling for independent
   SUI; augmentation or diversion for radiation-reduced capacity or compliance; capacity before
   reimplantation in case a psoas hitch is needed.
 • Cystogram distinguishes VVF from other insensible loss — immediate vaginal opacification, best on the
   lateral view; VCUG may show urethrovaginal fistula, stricture or foreign material.
 • Filling a bladder with a large fistula: pack the vagina, study supine, or plug the fistula with a Foley
   balloon; for a distal VVF near the bladder neck, a urethral Foley balloon beside the UDS catheter may
   occlude it → filling cystometry for detrusor overactivity, capacity, compliance and vesicoureteric reflux.
   The voiding phase remains difficult.

Concept — the work-up answers three questions in order: is the fluid urine, where does it come from
(bladder, ureter, urethra, uterus), and what else is damaged — and the last question is why upper tract
imaging is compulsory even when the VVF is obvious.

Treatment
General approach.
 • Goal: eliminate wetness, skin irritation, odour, recurrent infection and occasional pain, following the
   principles of Table 121.2.
 • Management depends on size and number, location and proximity to ureters or bladder neck, timing,
   tissue quality, and surgeon comfort.
Definition of success and prognostic factors.
 • No consensus definition of success. Anatomical closure ignores residual incontinence — a substantial
   proportion remain incontinent at 3 months after successful closure.
 • Follow-up is short or absent (especially in rural Africa) → long-term breakdown and incontinence are
   underestimated and success is overestimated. Future studies should state outcomes explicitly.

Fistula Size
 • Fistulae <3 mm have the greatest chance of spontaneous closure; size <10 mm favours closure with
   continuous drainage.

 • Size also predicts surgical failure and residual incontinence: worse prognosis above about 3 cm (non-
   irradiated) and about 2.7 cm (irradiated) in non-obstetric fistula; size ≥1.5 cm predicts residual
   incontinence and ≥3 cm surgical failure in obstetric series.
 • No clear threshold exists, but size is prognostic for both spontaneous and surgical closure.

Location
 • Most post-hysterectomy VVF are supratrigonal and midline — approachable vaginally or abdominally.
 • Within 1 cm of a ureter → ureteric stenting for identification and to protect against periureteric oedema
   in early healing; stents also let the vaginal surgeon palpate the ureteric course. Vaginal repairs were
   associated with a greater VVF–ureter distance than abdominal ones.
 • Ureteric involvement → reimplantation → abdominal approach.
 • Bladder neck involvement → reconstruction for continence, possibly a pubovaginal sling.
 • Location dictates interposition: a Martius flap is unlikely to reach an apical VVF (about 8–10 cm from the
   introitus) → peritoneal or omental flap instead.
 • Location is prognostic: urethrovesical junction involvement predicts primary failure; distance from the
   external meatus predicts postoperative incontinence regardless of anatomical closure.

Timing
 • Contentious, with little high-quality data. The conventional delay lets acute inflammation settle and
   improves tissue; for obstetric fistula a ≥3-month delay was recommended (resolution of inflammation,
   sloughing of necrosis), with an indwelling catheter meanwhile.
 • Counter-argument: delay increases psychosocial stress without improving outcome.
 • "Early" is not uniformly defined — days, <6 weeks or <3 months. Waaldijk defined early as <3 months
   and found immediate repair of obstetric fistula achieved very high first-attempt closure. Others describe a
   difficult window at 4–12 weeks (immediate <4 weeks; delayed >12 weeks).
 • Comparative series: early and delayed repair give comparable closure in most series; one small series
   found more recurrence with repair <6 weeks.
 • Minimally invasive early repair is feasible: laparoscopic repair within 4 weeks and robotic repair of
   recurrences achieved high closure, though series were non-comparative.
 • European Association of Urology (EAU) consensus: no minimum delay before robotic repair, provided
   there is no active infection and no recent irradiation.

History of Radiation Therapy
 • Radiation VVF presents weeks to years after treatment. In the largest series, first-attempt transvaginal
   closure was under half; failures were re-repaired after ≥6 months.
 • Ideal delay: ≥12 months after the end of radiation, so acute inflammation subsides; meanwhile
   catheterisation, protective skin ointments and surveillance of the tract for recurrence.
 • Radiation is one of the strongest predictors of repair failure and brings coexistent dysfunction — ureteric
   stricture, poor compliance, intrinsic sphincter deficiency. Evaluate all lower tract function and consider
   urinary diversion when dysfunction is severe.

Conservative Treatment
Indwelling catheters.
 • Continuous bladder drainage is a treatment in itself; low-level evidence suggests a cure rate of about
   15%. Candidacy depends on size, time since injury and aetiology; drainage duration and type vary.
 • Size: no cut-off; spontaneous closure is reported essentially only below 1 cm, and some units trial
   drainage only below 5 mm (selection bias).
 • Obstetric protocol (Waaldijk): only VVF with necrotic edges received a catheter trial — weekly review for
   4 weeks, then surgery if unhealed; fresh (clean) edges went straight to surgery. About 15% healed with
   catheter alone — probably an underestimate, since clean-edged fistulae were never trialled.
 • Time from injury is decisive because the tract epithelialises (table below); small post-hysterectomy VVF
   catheterised at once on diagnosis at 10–12 days have all closed.
 • Duration is arbitrary: 4 weeks (obstetric series), up to 8 weeks (when started <6 weeks from injury). EAU
   consensus: if attempted, trial for a minimum of 12 weeks.
 • Aetiology: radiation and malignancy fistulae almost never heal with drainage — vascular damage
   precludes healing.
 • Hypothesis: indwelling devices may harm reconstruction by inflammation (as shown in male urethral and
   ureteric reconstruction) — removing the catheter several days before repair may improve closure.

Cystoscopic techniques.
 • Electrocoagulation of the tract with prolonged drainage was first reported by O'Conor and Sokol.
   Fulguration (transvaginal and/or cystoscopic) plus 2 weeks' drainage succeeded in most fistulae <3.5 mm;

   added value of fulguration is uncertain.
 • Tissue adhesives: a systematic review reports success comparable to surgery, but it pooled adhesives used
   as interposition layers as well as plugs.

Surgical Repair of Vesicovaginal Fistula
 • Indication: VVF persisting after an adequate trial of drainage, or when conservative care is inappropriate.
 • Variables: size and number; relation to trigone, ureters, bladder neck and urethra; concurrent procedures;
   tissue quality and need for flaps; access; comorbidity; surgeon experience. The best results come from
   the procedure the surgeon is most comfortable with.
 • Tract excision and interposition are not proven superior, but are standard reconstructive principles and
   are of particular value where recurrence risk is high — irradiated, recurrent, poor tissue.
Preoperative requirements.
 • Urine culture; treat UTI fully. Biopsy the tract if there is any history of malignancy (may change the plan).
   Assess fitness and optimise nutrition.
Preoperative counselling.
 • General risks: bleeding, infection, venous thromboembolism, injury to adjacent structures.
 • Vaginal routes risk vaginal shortening and dyspareunia, particularly the Latzko procedure or colpocleisis
   → document baseline sexual function and pre-existing dyspareunia.
 • Prolonged catheterisation, catheter care and pre-removal studies (for example, cystogram); bladder
   spasms are self-limiting, and antispasmodics are reasonable.
 • Persistent incontinence despite anatomical closure is possible; failure and reoperation are always
   possible, although most repairs succeed.
Choosing a surgical route.

Vaginal VVF repairs — history.
 • Henry van Roonhuyse (17th century, Dutch): speculum exposure in lithotomy, pared edges approximated
   with swan-feather quills and silk.
 • J. Marion Sims first published a systematic, successful vaginal repair using silver wire.
 • The belief that gynaecologists repair vaginally and urologists abdominally is outdated: in a United States
   registry, most vaginal VVF repairs were performed by urologists.
Transvaginal versus open abdominal repair.
 • No randomised controlled trials (RCTs) — infeasible and ethically difficult.
 • Pooled cohorts and meta-analyses: similar first-attempt closure (about 90% by either route); one pooled
   analysis suggests slightly better closure transvaginally.
 • Transvaginal advantages: less blood loss, shorter operation and stay, less pain → some regard it as the
   gold standard unless specifically contraindicated; also more cost-effective in a United Kingdom analysis
   (similar success and reoperation).
 • Transvaginal disadvantages: vaginal shortening (especially Latzko); poor exposure in a deep narrow
   vagina without apical descensus; complex tracts; ureteric injury in trigonal or peritrigonal fistulae;
   unfamiliarity with cuff anatomy.
 • Abdominal (especially laparoscopic or robotic) advantages: exposure of the fistula and surrounding
   structures (for example, the ureter), and shared video view for teaching.
 • The optimal approach is the one in which the surgeon is most confident.
Transvaginal Repair Techniques
 • Two classic techniques since Sims — flap-splitting (layered) and Latzko; both much modified; no data
   show either superior.
 • Flap-splitting was popularised by Raz and colleagues. Key components: (1) continuous drainage via
   suprapubic and urethral catheters, (2) adequate exposure, (3) vaginal wall flaps to avoid overlapping
   suture lines, (4) opportunistic Martius or peritoneal flap.

Laparoscopic and Robotic Approaches
 • Firsts: laparoscopic VVF repair 1994; robotic 2005.
 • Open versus minimally invasive transabdominal: non-randomised comparisons show equal closure with
   less blood loss, shorter stay and less analgesia after laparoscopic or robotic repair; operative time similar.
   Minimally invasive surgery removes morbidity without compromising success.

 • Robotic advantages: relatively rapid learning curve, three-dimensional view, 7 degrees of freedom, dual-
   console teaching, ergonomics.
 • Transvaginal versus minimally invasive abdominal: the only comparison (a vaginal series against pooled
   systematic-review data) favoured transvaginal repair for operative time, blood loss and stay.
Concept — every VVF operation, vaginal or abdominal, is the same reconstruction — separate bladder from
vagina, close each without tension, keep the suture lines apart and drain — and the route is chosen by which
one best exposes that plane in the individual patient.

LAPAROSCOPIC AND ROBOTIC VESICOVAGINAL FISTULA REPAIRS
 • Indications mirror open abdominal repair: ureteric involvement, ancillary abdominal procedures, narrow
   vagina with minimal descensus, surgeon preference.
 • Against: dense abdominopelvic adhesions or prior inflammation. Conventional laparoscopy: limited
   degrees of freedom make intracorporeal suturing hard for all but advanced laparoscopists, limiting
   adoption.

Transvesical and O'Conor Approaches
 • Three techniques adapted from open surgery: transvesical, extravesical and mixed (O'Conor).
 • Distinguish O'Conor from pure transvesical: in the pure transvesical technique the cystotomy is not
   extended down to the fistula, and interposition options are much more limited; the literature often blurs
   the two.
 • Technique: posterior-wall cystotomy extended to the VVF (incorporating the defect) → bladder mobilised
   off vagina ≥2 cm beyond the tract → vaginal stent (Lucite or an end-to-end anastomosis [EEA] sizer) for
   counter-traction → optional edge excision → vagina closed in one or two layers → bladder in one or two
   layers (barbed or unbarbed) → interposition of peritoneum, omentum or sigmoid epiploic appendages.
 • Closure 85–100% in non-irradiated VVF, comparable to open and vaginal repair; modifications limit
   cystotomy size.

Extravesical Approach
 • First described laparoscopically in 1998 (von Theobald). No intentional cystotomy → fewer sutures that
   could create new tracts or cause lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). Steps as for open extravesical
   repair.
 • Finding the plane: backfill 150–300 mL plus vaginal stent or tampon; fluid spills into the field when the
   VVF is reached; a cystoscopically placed catheter through the tract and ureteric stents help.
 • Indocyanine green (ICG) with near-infrared imaging assesses vascularity of the bladder and vaginal edges
   — an advantage of minimally invasive repair.
 • Outcomes: very high laparoscopic closure even largely without interposition; robotic extravesical repair
   (first with sigmoid epiploic appendages as interposition) gives similar closure. Concomitant ureteric
   reimplantation or Burch colposuspension is possible.
 • Transvesical versus extravesical: equal success (cohort and systematic review); transvesical shorter
   operative time. Transvesical suits low-lying fistulae and avoids adhesiolysis; extravesical suits
   supratrigonal VVF and minimises new suture lines.
 • Single-port laparoscopic and robotic repair is feasible; data too scant for conclusions.

URINARY SYMPTOMS AFTER VESICOVAGINAL FISTULA REPAIR
 • Few studies look beyond closure. Preoperatively, concurrent SUI and detrusor overactivity (DO) are both
   common in fistula patients; after repair, persistent symptoms were mainly urgency and UUI, occasionally
   SUI, despite anatomical cure.
 • De novo urinary symptoms (UUI and SUI) occur in a minority. UUI was more frequent after open than
   transvaginal repair, attributed to the bivalving and long suture lines of the O'Conor technique;
   International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire – Short Form (ICIQ-SF) scores did not differ.

 • Other comparisons: no difference by route in urodynamic SUI, OAB or UTI; validated questionnaires show
   symptoms improve after both routes; no difference between open and laparoscopic repair in UTI, SUI or
   spasms.
 • Postoperative retention is uncommon; predicted by vertical (versus horizontal) bladder closure; prior
   repair status was also associated.
 • Residual incontinence after obstetric repair is predicted by Goh types 3 and 4, more than one repair, a
   positive cough stress test and a positive pad test.
 • Residual incontinence drives QoL and mental health: all patients without residual leakage reported
   improved QoL against about half of those with it; every patient with a depressive disorder, and all with
   suicidal ideation, still leaked.
 • International Continence Society (ICS): recommends pelvic floor physical therapy (PFPT) for all obstetric
   fistula patients and for persistent symptoms after repair; access is limited worldwide, but risk is low.
Concept — anatomical closure is not cure: the patient's experience is set by residual incontinence, which is
predicted by how near the fistula lay to the continence mechanism and how much repair the bladder has
needed.

SEXUAL FUNCTION AFTER VESICOVAGINAL FISTULA REPAIR
 • Little is known. On the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) (19-item validated), overall sexual function
   improved after both routes, but pain, arousal and orgasm domains did not — attributed to vaginal pain
   and dyspareunia.
 • After transvaginal repair in Malawi, a minority reported intercourse problems (half from pain, half from
   persistent incontinence). The Female Sexual Distress Scale–Revised (FSDS-R) improved overall, but some
   stayed in the dysfunction range; reduced vaginal calibre and fistula size ≥3 cm predicted dysfunction.
 • ICS Physiotherapy Committee: pelvic floor relaxation for dyspareunia, vaginismus and hypertonicity after
   any fistula repair; cost and access limit it.

URINARY DIVERSIONS FOR THE TREATMENT OF VESICOVAGINAL FISTULA
 • When repair is unfeasible or unwise: multiple failed attempts, coexisting abdominopelvic disease, severe
   radiation damage, massive soft-tissue loss, comorbidity.
 • Fit for surgery but not for repair → conduit or continent reservoir.
 • Unfit for any surgery → percutaneous ureteric occlusion with permanent nephrostomy.
 • Resource-poor settings (no catheters or stoma appliances) → ureterosigmoidostomy as a last resort,
   accepting its metabolic and neoplastic risks.

URETEROVAGINAL FISTULA
 • Ureterovaginal fistula (UVF) — ureter-to-vagina connection, most commonly from unrecognised
   iatrogenic injury to the distal third of the ureter during pelvic surgery.
 • Rarely from obstetric or infertility procedures, malignancy, radiation, endometriosis, inflammation or
   infection, retained foreign bodies.
 • Typically follows a mundane benign hysterectomy with no untoward intraoperative event.

Aetiology
 • About three-quarters follow unrecognised injury at abdominal hysterectomy, owing to the intimate
   relation of the uterine artery and the ureter.

 • Ureteric injury at hysterectomy: 0.2–2.4%. Registries disagree on route: one found GU injury commonest
   after abdominal hysterectomy; another found ureteric injury commonest after laparoscopic and rarest
   after vaginal; a population study found no difference in delayed injury by route. Radical hysterectomy is
   assumed higher risk, though published incidences are similar.
 • Mechanisms: laceration, partial or complete transection, avulsion, crush, thermal injury, suture ligation,
   ischaemia from devascularisation.
 • Diagnosis is delayed in 18–88%. Routine cystoscopy series detect several times more ureteric injuries than
   series without it — cystoscopy uncovers injuries that would otherwise go unrecognised.
 • Delayed detection raises ureteric fistula risk about fourfold; combined bladder and ureteric injury raises
   fistula risk further, dramatically so when detection is delayed.
 • Up to 25% of VVF have a coexisting UVF.

Prevention
 • The optimal goal is prevention, founded on pelvic anatomy.
 • Sites of injury: (1) pelvic brim, medial to the infundibulopelvic ligament; (2) where it passes under the
   uterine artery in the cardinal ligament; (3) lateral to the uterosacral ligaments; (4) over the anterolateral
   vaginal fornix just proximal to the ureterovesical junction.
 • Most injuries are in the distal 6 cm, slightly favouring the left — and these distal injuries cause UVF.
 • Risk factors: Black race (self-reported), prior abdominal surgery, endometriosis, route of surgery,
   concomitant apical suspension, longer operation, lower body mass index (BMI), blood loss ≥800 mL,
   uterine size. After radical hysterectomy: prolonged surgery, diabetes, American Society of
   Anesthesiologists (ASA) 3–4, postoperative infection.
 • Preoperative IVP does not reduce injury and is not cost-effective (hundreds of IVPs per injury prevented).
 • Prophylactic ureteric stents — controversial. For: haptic feedback, rapid recognition. Against: iatrogenic
   injury, UTI, time. An RCT in major gynaecological surgery showed no reduction; a later meta-analysis
   showed a small reduction with a number needed to treat of 224; cost-effective only if injury risk >3.2%. In
   caesarean hysterectomy for placenta accreta spectrum, stents reduced bladder and ureteric injury →
   selective, not routine, use.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis
 • Consequences of unrecognised injury: flank pain, anuria, ileus, peritonitis, sepsis, stricture, fistula, loss of
   renal function.
 • Commonest presentation: continuous leakage, usually within 4 weeks of surgery, ± fever and flank pain;
   in retrospect a difficult recovery (ileus, fever, slow progress).
 • Key contrast with VVF: normal voiding plus constant wetness, because the contralateral ureter fills the
   bladder. Exceptions: bilateral injury or concomitant VVF.
 • Confirm: pooled vaginal urine (creatinine); double dye test (VVF, UVF or both); sometimes a visible defect.
 • Upper tract: CTU, IVP or retrograde pyelogram. Urography commonly shows hydronephrosis, hydroureter
   or a non-functioning kidney — none pathognomonic. Ultrasound alone is not definitive (no excretory
   phase), although absence of grade 3 hydronephrosis has a high negative predictive value in obstetric
   fistula.
 • Retrograde pyelogram: abrupt termination of contrast, or ureteric continuity with a fistula → attempt
   stenting.
 • Exclude coexisting VVF: cystogram with oblique and lateral views; CTU and MRI show both and are
   superior to plain radiography and ultrasound.

 • Goals: dryness and preservation of renal function. Management has moved from nephrectomy

   able to be stented, most resolve with stent alone (stents typically left in for weeks to a few months);
   stricture may follow.
 • If stenting fails or is impossible → surgery (Figure 121.10). Timing has no consensus — early surgery risks
   oedema, inflammation and necrosis; delay prolongs distress. Early reimplantation took less time and
   blood loss with equal success; a systematic review found equal success before and after 6 weeks.

   periureteric adventitia, especially laterally where the blood supply enters. Alternatives for length:
   downward nephropexy, ileal ureter.
 • Success ≥90% (pooled near 100%). Laparoscopic and robotic repair match open; vaginal reimplantation
   (with psoas hitch or Boari flap) has been reported; spontaneous closure also occurs.
 • Complications: hydronephrosis, infection, stricture (ischaemic).
 • Follow-up (no consensus): ultrasound 1–2 weeks after stent removal; some add a renal scan at 6 weeks;
   stricture usually appears within a year but up to 30 months → follow beyond this.

VESICOUTERINE FISTULA

Aetiology
 • Vesicouterine fistula (VUF) — bladder–uterus connection; historically about 5–7% of GU fistulae, but
   rising sharply where caesarean rates rise (it made up a large share of fistulae in a recent Rwandan series).


                                                         page 21 of 37
Chapter 121 — Urinary Tract Fistulae                                                          VESICOUTERINE FISTULA

 • The vast majority follow a low transverse caesarean delivery; operative vaginal delivery also. Other
   causes: hysterectomy, intrauterine devices, dilatation and curettage, induced abortion, manual removal of
   placenta, uterine rupture, traumatic catheterisation, brachytherapy, uterine embolisation.
 • Mechanism: apposition of an unrepaired uterine incision to an occult bladder injury (for example, at
   hysterotomy closure), or incorporation of the bladder in the hysterotomy closure.

Clinical Presentation
 • Usual site: uterine corpus just above the internal os ↔ posterior bladder.
 • Continence is usually preserved because the cervix is competent.
 • Youssef syndrome: cyclical haematuria (menouria), amenorrhoea, first-trimester miscarriage and
   absence of incontinence.
 • Incontinence occurs if the tract lies below the internal os or the cervix is incompetent (common in some
   series).
 • Timing: direct injury presents early; obstructed labour or irradiation presents later (necrosis).
 • Differential of menouria: bladder endometriosis (a fifth to a quarter develop menouria) and congenital
   anomalies.

Diagnosis
 • Dye test: blue saline from the cervical os after bladder backfill.
 • Saline-infusion sonohysterography and hysterosalpingography may show the uterine defect and contrast
   entering the bladder — operator dependent. Retrograde cystogram shows extravasation into the uterine
   cavity. MRI confirms.
 • Excretory urogram is required to exclude concomitant upper tract fistula — MR urography can serve both
   purposes.
 • Cystoscopy (indicated for haematuria) shows the defect, usually supratrigonal posterior, and its relation
   to the ureters; the scope can often enter the uterine cavity — seeing both tubal ostia is highly suggestive.

Management
 • Usually surgical — spontaneous closure is rare (about 5%, possibly hormone related). Fulguration plus
   prolonged catheterisation has closed an isolated case.
 • Timing is controversial: early for psychosocial reasons, or ≥3 months for inflammation; after caesarean,
   wait 3–4 months for full uterine involution; earlier repair works but is harder.
 • Approach: abdominal, increasingly minimally invasive. Transperitoneal access allows complete
   separation of bladder and uterus for a tension-free bladder repair.
 • Fertility wishes decide the operation (table below); uterus-sparing repair follows VVF technique with
   omentum or peritoneum between bladder and uterus; with hysterectomy, tract excision is optional.
 • Transvaginal repair (commoner before 1947): dissection as for vaginal hysterectomy → vesicocervical
   then vesicouterine spaces → tract → bladder mobilised for two-layer tension-free closure → uterine
   defect closed perpendicular to the bladder → colpotomy closed; or vaginal hysterectomy. Martius flap is
   generally not feasible (too far from the introitus).
 • After uterus-sparing repair pregnancy and term birth are achievable; the ideal contraceptive interval is
   unknown, caesarean delivery rests on extrapolated rupture risk, and recurrence reaches about 20% over
   long follow-up.

URETHROVAGINAL FISTULA

Aetiology
 • Developing world: overwhelmingly obstructed labour, often with a VVF; urethral and continence-
   mechanism damage increases complexity and failure (as Goh and Waaldijk encode). Nearly half of GU
   fistulae in one Ethiopian series were urethral or juxtaurethral. Others: radiation, pelvic trauma, traumatic
   catheterisation, malignancy.
 • Developed world: iatrogenic — diverticulectomy, anti-incontinence surgery, anterior repair with or
   without mesh.
    ◦ Urethrovaginal fistula complicates about 1–8% of diverticulectomies; diverticulectomy accounts for
      20–25% of urethrovaginal fistulae.
     ◦ Slings (autologous and synthetic) are the commoner anti-incontinence cause, but periurethral bulking
       has also been implicated.
     ◦ Also pessaries, gender-affirming surgery, feminising genitoplasty and cloacal repair in children (early or
       late), and pressure necrosis from an indwelling catheter (bladder neck and urethral destruction).

Clinical Presentation
 • Symptoms depend on position relative to the sphincter and on size: distal to the sphincter → largely
   asymptomatic; proximal near the bladder neck → near-constant leakage or SUI.
 • Split or splayed stream, vaginal voiding, post-void dribble on standing; local pain, dyspareunia.
 • After mesh: bleeding, partner awareness during intercourse (hispareunia); fistula may be found
   incidentally with mesh exposure.
 • Also perineal skin irritation, recurrent UTI, vaginal candidiasis.

Diagnosis
 • Speculum examination usually suffices (distal site); assess coexisting prolapse, SUI, atrophy for
   simultaneous treatment. Prominent rugae may hide it → methylene blue dye test.
 • VCUG (lateral view) shows vaginal contrast; with VUDS it characterises associated incontinence.
 • Cystourethroscopy is essential — size, location, exposed synthetic material. Flexible scopes are better
   for the urethra because outflow is at the lens; rigid scopes eject fluid 1–2 cm proximal to the lens and
   distend the lumen poorly. Also evaluates bladder, bladder neck, orifices and other fistulae.
 • About 20% coexist with a separate VVF — search for it. Evaluate the upper tract.

Management
 • Timing debated — early if tissue allows, or delay until inflammation settles.
 • Among the most challenging reconstructions: the difficulty is less the location than the scarcity of viable
   tissue for flaps, especially after trauma or radiation. Historically, urethral destruction was the reason the
   few irreparable fistulae in a large series needed ureterocolonic diversion to achieve continence.
 • Standard: transvaginal repair with rotational vaginal flaps (Table 121.11). Primary urethral repair is
   occasionally possible, but most repairs add tissue — Martius flap most often, occasionally omentum or
   gracilis; an autologous rectus fascia patch is an alternative.
 • Concomitant autologous (pubovaginal) sling for preoperative SUI or high SUI risk (bladder neck or
   sphincter involvement) — synthetic material is avoided because it may erode the poorly vascularised
   urethra.
 • Near-total urethral loss: tubularised anterior bladder flap as a neourethra; a posterolateral bladder tube
   has also been used.
 • With prior mesh: remove all mesh visible in the repair field.
 • Position: usually dorsal lithotomy; prone can improve urethral and anterior wall exposure.
 • Outcomes: anatomical closure 90–95% with vital-tissue adjuncts, but continence only 50–80%;
   postoperative SUI can reach half; some need further SUI surgery even after a concomitant sling.
 • Strategy for continence: simultaneous sphincter repair with Martius flap and pubovaginal sling, or staged

UROENTERIC FISTULAE

Rectourethral Fistula
Aetiopathogenesis and Epidemiology
 • Rectourethral fistula (RUF) — lower urinary tract to distal rectum; rare; congenital (a subgroup of
   anorectal malformation) or acquired (trauma, neoplasm, infection, inflammation).
 • Most acquired RUFs are iatrogenic — radical prostatectomy (RP), cryosurgery, external beam
   radiotherapy or brachytherapy. Historically TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate) and open simple
   prostatectomy; today mostly prostate cancer (CaP) treatment.
 • Burden: raised mortality and morbidity, poor QoL, long stay, high cost.
 • Symptoms: faecaluria and pneumaturia (most typical); other gastrointestinal disturbance common;
   clinically recurrent cystitis and dysuria.
 • Radiation-induced RUF: commonest after combined external beam and brachytherapy, then
   brachytherapy, then pelvic irradiation (prostate or anal cancer).
 • Non-radiation RUF: mainly surgery or instrumentation (most after prostatectomy), then trauma
   (penetrating, pelvic fracture, road traffic), then inflammatory bowel disease.
 • Rectal injury during RP by approach: highest with retropubic and laparoscopic, lower with perineal,
   lowest with robot-assisted; a United States population study likewise ranked open > laparoscopic >
   robotic.
 • Other energies: primary whole-gland cryotherapy — low and falling RUF rate with better selection;
   primary high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) — low; salvage HIFU after radiation — up to about 9%,
   much lower when post-radiotherapy selection parameters are used.
 • Females: RUF is virtually impossible because the vagina lies between urinary tract and rectum.
 • The greatest challenge is the recurrent fistula.

Diagnosis
 • Digital rectal examination may feel the fistula on the anterior rectal wall.
 • Cystoscopy and sigmoidoscopy show the tract, allow biopsy to exclude recurrent malignancy and assess
   tissue vitality.
 • VCUG or retrograde urethrography (RUG) is usually conclusive — size and location for planning. Image
   the upper tract to exclude ureteric injury. Pelvic MRI for complex cases.
 • Assess continence and sphincter function (elderly, post-RP): repairing the RUF alone often fails to restore
   continence in severe SUI; an artificial urinary sphincter (AUS) is possible later but with higher failure and
   erosion. UDS or VUDS with rectal packing help when leakage limits filling.

Treatment
Conservative therapy.
 • If no sepsis and no faecaluria: low-residue diet with urinary and/or faecal diversion (urethral or
   suprapubic catheter, nephrostomy, ileostomy, colostomy).
 • A simple fistula closes in about 12 weeks; persistence beyond this → surgery. Complex and irradiated
   RUF rarely close → conservative care inappropriate.
 • Post-RP series: small fistulae without faecaluria closed with prolonged catheterisation (weeks to months);
   early post-laparoscopic RP fistulae healed with catheter and intravenous nutrition, some needing
   temporary colostomy.
 • Bottom line: predictors are unknown; conservative care is a reasonable first step in most, while
   inflammation settles (≥12 weeks, debatable) and preparatory steps (colostomy) are taken.
Surgical treatment.
 • No universally accepted operation — the number of techniques reflects rarity and difficulty. Decision
   factors: surgeon preference, fistula location, prior failed repair, radiation.
 • A diverting colostomy usually precedes repair, whatever the technique.

Vesicoenteric Fistula
Aetiopathogenesis and Epidemiology
 • Vesicoenteric fistula (VEF) — bowel–bladder communication; infrequent but highly morbid.
 • Types: colovesical (commonest), rectovesical, ileovesical and appendicovesical (<5%).
 • Simple: small, solitary, non-irradiated. Complex: larger, multiple tracts, irradiated tissue, often with pelvic
   abscess or colonic obstruction.
 • Sixth and seventh decades; male : female 3 : 1 — the uterus shields the bladder from the sigmoid; prior
   hysterectomy is frequent in affected females.
 • Causes: diverticular disease (commonest), then Crohn disease; malignancy (mainly colorectal, also
   bladder and prostate) a small share; appendicitis, trauma, radiation, iatrogenic.
 • Frequencies: fistulae in 4–23% of those admitted with diverticular disease; colovesical fistula (CVF) in 2–
   23% of diverticular disease; VEF in 2–8% of Crohn disease.
 • Urinary symptoms dominate: pneumaturia (50–70%) and faecaluria (up to 51%); also frequency,
   urgency, suprapubic pain, recurrent UTI (Escherichia coli, coliforms, mixed growth, enterococci),
   haematuria. Over 75% have pathognomonic features.
 • Gouverneur syndrome — the distinctive picture: suprapubic pain, frequency, dysuria and tenesmus.

Diagnosis
 • No accepted gold standard.
 • Cystoscopy: usual but findings (erythema, oedema) nonspecific; detection varies widely (35–90%).
 • Colonoscopy: poor at showing the fistula, but 10–15% of CVFs have an underlying neoplasm → defines
   the bowel disease.
 • Poppy seed test: ingest poppy seeds → inspect urine voided over 48 hours; seeds in urine strongly
   indicate a uroenteric fistula. Highest detection of any test in direct comparisons, cheap and simple — but
   gives no location or type.
 • Ultrasound with compression: echogenic "beak sign" joining peristaltic bowel to bladder; reasonable
   sensitivity in Crohn internal fistula; operator dependent, rarely used.

Vesicocutaneous Fistula
Aetiopathogenesis and Epidemiology
 • Vesicocutaneous fistula (VCF) causes: infection, neoplasm, bladder pathology (stones, diverticula,
   trauma), radiotherapy, and surgery — open prostatectomy, total hip arthroplasty, radical hysterectomy,
   opening of an ileocystoplasty (for example, for stones), one case after transobturator sling.
 • Commonest in routine practice: persistence after long-standing suprapubic cystostomy.
 • Persistence factors: well-epithelialised tract, infection, foreign body, neoplasm.
 • Suprapubic fistula follows about 1% of open prostatectomies for benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). Tracts
   run mostly to the thigh or anterior abdominal wall.
 • Presentation: abscess or cellulitis, dysuria, fever, UTI, suprapubic pain, watery wound discharge.
 • Exclude bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) — raised intravesical pressure drives formation and
   persistence.

Diagnosis
 • Confirm with cystography, retrograde fistulography, CT cystography, MRI (complex tracts).
 • Cystoscopy shows irrigant leaking through the fistula, sometimes an orifice, and — crucially — distal
   obstruction requiring treatment or bypass. Methylene blue backfill or intravenous indigo carmine can
   help.

Treatment
 • Drain abscesses and give antibiotics.

 • Relieve BOO to lower bladder pressure.

Urethroperineal Fistula
Aetiopathogenesis and Epidemiology
 • Mostly after hypospadias repair; less often congenital.
 • Iatrogenic urethrocutaneous fistula (UCF) is relatively common in developing countries because of
   circumcision by unauthorised practitioners.
 • Other causes: pelvic fracture, infection, urethral or penile surgery (urethroplasty, Fournier debridement,
   priapism shunt, penile fracture repair), neoplasm, radiotherapy, gender-affirming surgery, distal
   obstruction (impacted stone).
 • UCF is the commonest complication of hypospadias surgery (up to 20%). Contributors: distal
   obstruction, ischaemia, technical error, poor tissue handling, overlapping suture lines, infection.
 • Recurrence after UCF repair about 25%; some need up to 15 attempts; presentation can be as late as 13
   years.
 • Congenital UCF may accompany anorectal or genital malformations (ventral curvature, hypospadias) →
   multidisciplinary care.
 • Neurogenic self-catheterisation: false passages in bulbar or membranous urethra plus pressure ulcers.

Diagnosis
 • Perineal dribbling with a visible cutaneous orifice; sometimes recurrent UTI and LUTS.
 • RUG or fistulography diagnose; cystoscopy shows the fistula and any stricture; MRI shows a fluid-filled
   tract from urethra to perineal skin.

Treatment
 • Goal: durable, recurrence-free and stricture-free urethra. The first repair matters most — redo surgery
   faces distortion, scar and short urethral stumps.
 • Principles: reconstruct the urethra, add a waterproof layer, ensure skin cover — often from several flaps.
 • Decision factors: early or late, single or multiple, acute or chronic. Early acute fistulae → conservative.
   Mature → surgery.
 • Size rule: single fistula <1 cm → local tissue; ≥1 cm → regional skin and fascial flaps with good blood
   supply, especially after repeated surgery.
 • After hypospadias repair wait ≥6 months for inflammation to resolve.
 • Techniques for single post-hypospadias UCF: tubularised incised plate (TIP) urethroplasty, Mathieu
   urethroplasty, double ligation, simple closure, trap-door — recurrence differed modestly; choose by
   diameter, location and glans dehiscence.
 • Large penoscrotal UCF (1–2 cm): three-layer repair with a scrotal flap — no recurrence in the reported
   series.
 • Covering layer: recurrence after repair was lower with a tunica vaginalis flap than with dartos fascia.
   Scrotal flaps are rich in blood supply and easily advanced.
 • Extensive tissue loss (after Fournier debridement) → gracilis myocutaneous flap.
 • Neurogenic patients with pressure ulcers → ileal conduit diversion (± pressure-ulcer surgery) — excellent
   outcomes; may be the option of choice.

Nephrocutaneous Fistula
Aetiopathogenesis and Epidemiology
 • Renal fistulae to adjacent organs are not uncommon; kidney-to-skin is rare, and rarer still without prior
   surgery.
 • Causes: prior renal surgery, stones (usually large or staghorn), trauma, tumours, chronic UTI including
   XGP. Tuberculosis causes ≥10% — consider it and its risk factors.
 • Drainage routes of least resistance: lumbar triangle of Petit and lumbar quadrilateral of Grynfeltt.
 • The kidney is poorly or non-functioning in the vast majority of nephrocutaneous fistulae (NCF).
 • Slight female predominance; left side commoner.
 • Transplant: calyceal–cutaneous fistula in about 3% of allografts, 12% with multiple renal arteries — from
   segmental infarction.
 • Symptoms: flank discharge (majority), loin pain (about one-third); flank sinus, occasionally a lumbar mass.

Diagnosis
 • Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT is preferred — diagnosis, cause, renal function, planning. Fistulography
   and retrograde pyelography assess the tract directly.

Treatment
 • Most spontaneous NCF need surgery — by diagnosis much parenchyma is lost and inflammation
   advanced, especially with an obstructing stone or staghorn.
 • Open nephrectomy is preferred, because about 90% of kidneys are poorly or non-functioning.
 • Conservative options: heminephrectomy in a duplex system; functioning kidney with minor parenchymal
   involvement → stent or nephrostomy may cure.
Concept — urocutaneous fistulae persist for the same reasons as all fistulae — epithelialisation, infection,
foreign body and distal obstruction — so the vesical and urethral varieties usually close once flow is diverted
and obstruction relieved, whereas the nephrocutaneous fistula signals a kidney already destroyed.

Pubovesical Fistula
Aetiopathogenesis and Epidemiology
 • Pubovesical fistula (PVF) — rare tract running anteriorly between the pubic symphysis and bladder,
   prostate or urethra.

 • Typical setting: radiotherapy for CaP plus endoscopic outlet procedures — TURP, photoselective
   vaporisation of the prostate (PVP), bladder neck incision (BNI), urethral dilatation or incision. Incidence is
   rising with multimodal cancer treatment. Also after posterior urethral reconstruction (sutures near the
   pubis).
 • Proposed sequence: irradiated tissue + repeated endoscopy for stenoses → urine leaks into the
   retropubic space → fistulation into the symphysis.
 • After AUS implantation: raised outlet resistance increases bladder pressure → rupture of poor-quality
   irradiated urethra.
 • Series: the great majority had prior radiation (external beam, brachytherapy or both); a small minority
   developed PVF after prostatectomy alone; median interval from radiation to diagnosis about 10 years;
   most had a bladder neck endoscopic procedure beforehand.
 • Symptoms: pain on ambulation — the commonest, irradiated or not; collections, abscess, urosepsis in
   both; cutaneous fistulation only in irradiated patients; in non-irradiated, UTI, haematuria, worsening
   LUTS.

Diagnosis
 • High index of suspicion: pain on walking or pubic pain after BNI, transurethral prostate surgery, or
   posterior urethral or bladder neck reconstruction → investigate for PVF. Imaging usually combined.
 • Nuclear medicine: sensitive but nonspecific. CT: excellent reconstructions; cortical thickening, sclerosis,
   draining sinuses; earlier than plain films but inferior to MRI.
 • MRI is the most sensitive and specific (and non-invasive): tract with low central signal and enhancing
   walls on T1 fat-suppressed post-gadolinium; parasymphyseal cortical erosion with symphyseal
   separation; collections, abscess, marrow signal change, muscle and tendon inflammation. MRI is the
   primary diagnostic tool in the great majority.
 • Cystoscopy shows the tract and outlet dysfunction; cystography shows the fistula; UDS assesses the
   bladder.
 • CT-guided pubic bone biopsy with cultures guides antibiotics. Bone cultures are usually positive —
   commonly Candida, Enterococcus, Pseudomonas; concordance with urine culture is inconsistent (do not
   rely on urine culture).

Treatment
 • Multidisciplinary: urology, orthopaedics, infectious disease. Antibiotics against urosepsis.
 • Conservative care usually fails (75–94% need surgery). Option for non-septic, previously untreated or frail
   patients: antimicrobials, drainage of collections, suprapubic catheter or nephrostomies.
 • Surgery: remove infected bone, excise the fistula, divert urine.

     ◦ Symphysis resected and adjacent rami removed to bleeding bone; aerobic, anaerobic and fungal
       cultures; defect filled with a pedicled rectus abdominis or omental flap.
     ◦ 4–6 weeks of (usually intravenous) antibiotics for osteomyelitis.
 • QoL after extirpation: physical function improves and opioid use falls; mental health scores unchanged.
 • Robotic cystectomy with holmium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet (Ho:YAG) laser debridement of the

UROVASCULAR FISTULA

Renovascular Fistula
Arterial Fistula
 • Artery-to-collecting-system fistulae (renal pelvis or calyces) are rare but life threatening; typically after
   transplantation or PCNL.
 • Mechanisms: inadvertent procedural injury, and raised collecting-system pressure causing forniceal
   rupture.
 • Arteriocalyceal fistula (ACF) also after retrograde intrarenal surgery, prolonged nephrostomy drainage
   and trauma; often coexists with pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula.
 • Angioembolisation is the treatment of choice; open partial nephrectomy is exceptional.

Ureterovascular Fistula
Aetiopathogenesis and Epidemiology
 • Ureteroarterial fistula (UAF) — once exceptional, now increasingly recognised: ageing population, better
   cancer survival, multimodal pelvic cancer treatment. A medical emergency.
 • Primary (15%): intrinsic vessel weakness — aortoiliac aneurysm, rarely arteriovenous malformation.
 • Secondary (85%): fibrosis, inflammation or displacement from vascular (grafts, endovascular procedures,
   surgery), urological (diversion, indwelling stents, strictures) and other factors (pelvic surgery, cancer,
   radiation).
 • Main risk factors: pelvic surgery, long-term ureteric stents, malignancy, radiotherapy. Cancers: cervical,
   bladder, colorectal.
 • Site: common iliac artery most often, then external iliac; less often internal iliac or aorta. Ureterovenous
   fistulae and involvement of colon, small bowel and skin are reported.
 • Latency: months to more than 10 years after pelvic treatment.
 • Presentation: haematuria in essentially all (often the only sign), possibly intermittent; transient retention,
   fever or flank pain from clot distending the collecting system; about 20% haemodynamically unstable.

Diagnosis
 • Hard to confirm — imaging insensitive; the aim is exact localisation for treatment; intermittent
   haematuria makes it harder.
 • Pelvic angiography is the most effective test: contrast leaks through the defect and fills the ureter.
   Sensitivity 25–50%, rising to 60–100% with provocation — removing catheters or stents during
   angiography, or thrombolytics.

 • Provocation can precipitate severe haemorrhage → do it only with immediate treatment ready. A
   negative provocation does not exclude UAF if haematuria persists with risk factors.
 • Cystoscopy: pulsatile blood from one ureteric orifice lateralises. Retrograde pyelography is unreliable —
   blood prevents the pressure gradient needed.
 • CT angiography: sensitivity 38–50%; useful for abscess, bowel and vessel anatomy for planning; usually
   shows only nonspecific clot and hydronephrosis, not the wall defect.

Treatment
 • Coordinated urinary and vascular treatment by radiology, urology and vascular surgery.
 • Endovascular repair is preferred (less invasive, complex comorbid anatomy). Stent graft is the main
   treatment, embolisation occasionally; internal iliac embolisation is often combined with stent grafting.
   Haematuria stopped in all cases.
 • Complications over follow-up: rebleeding, stent thrombosis, retroperitoneal abscess, urosepsis, iliac
   thrombosis, claudication; a few later nephrectomies. Mortality is high overall, but mostly from the
   underlying disease rather than the fistula.
 • Open repair is reserved for ureteric–intestinal fistula, abscess, or graft infection after endovascular
   treatment: bypass grafting, proximal arterial ligation, open vessel repair, nephrectomy if the collecting
   system is irreversibly damaged.
Concept — UAF is the fistula that bleeds rather than leaks: suspect it in any patient with a long-term stent
after pelvic surgery and radiation who has haematuria, and remember that the test sensitive enough to find it
— provocative angiography — must be done with the endovascular cure ready on the table.

RENAL FISTULA

Posttrauma
Aetiopathogenesis and Epidemiology
 • Renal injuries are 1–5% of trauma, often with polytrauma; typically young males; blunt in about 90%
   (vehicle accidents, falls, sport, assault) versus penetrating (stab, gunshot — unpredictable and severe).
 • Urinary leak follows 1–7% of renal injuries; up to about a quarter of grade IV (and similar in grade V);
   higher (up to 30%) with penetrating trauma.
 • Presentation: besides instability and other injuries — haematuria, flank pain, sepsis, renal dysfunction.

Diagnosis
 • CT is the gold standard in stable patients: multiphase from diaphragm to ischial tuberosities —
   precontrast, arterial, nephrographic and 10-minute delayed phases. Delayed phase may be omitted if
   kidneys are normal and there is no perinephric, retroperitoneal, pelvic or perivesical fluid.
 • Ultrasound: fast, available, cheap, no radiation, good for free fluid, collections, hydronephrosis and follow-
   up; excretory CT preferred where immediately available.
 • MRI: no advantage; for severe contrast allergy. Retrograde pyelography when CT cannot exclude
   collecting-system injury or ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) avulsion — non-opacification of the distal ureter
   suggests UPJ injury.
 • Reimaging: advisable for severe injuries managed conservatively (bleeding, persistent or new
   extravasation, vascular malformation); practice varies — some repeat CT at 36–72 hours for grade III–V,
   others only on deterioration. Always image for fever, flank pain, flank mass or bleeding. Routine and
   indicated reimaging find similar complications, all within the first 3 weeks.

Treatment
 • Non-operative management is standard — better renal salvage, fewer complications, fewer explorations
   and emergency nephrectomies.
 • Most extravasation resolves spontaneously (76–87%). Persistent leak or urinoma → retrograde stent or
   percutaneous nephrostomy; percutaneous urinoma drainage is seldom needed.

Postsurgical
Aetiopathogenesis and Epidemiology
 • Partial nephrectomy (PN) is preferred over radical nephrectomy (RN) for T1 tumours and increasingly for
   T2 — renal function preserved with equivalent oncological control; larger tumours raise leak and bleeding
   risk.
 • Urine leak after PN: 0.5–20%.
 • Risk factors: collecting-system repair at renorrhaphy, volume of parenchyma removed, tumour size and
   central location, operative and warm ischaemia time, early surgeon experience, moderate–severe chronic
   kidney disease.
 • CT predictors: higher RENAL nephrometry score (Radius, Exophytic/endophytic, Nearness to the
   collecting system, Anterior/posterior, Location) — R and N components, not E; longer ischaemia. On
   multivariable analysis only pelvicalyceal contact independently predicted leak.
 • Robot-assisted PN has a low leak rate (under 1% in a multicentre series).

Diagnosis
 • Drain output with drain-versus-serum creatinine; leaks may appear after drain removal. Confirm by
   multiphase CT, less often retrograde pyelography.

Treatment
 • Most are managed conservatively with the drain alone; also ureteric stent, percutaneous drain or
   nephrostomy.
 • "Active" treatment for persistent leaks succeeds in nearly all (mean about 11 days to resolution):
    ◦ Percutaneous: cyanoacrylate (most) or fibrin glue injection; infundibular dilatation plus nephrostomy;
      laser ablation; CT-guided cryoablation.
     ◦ Retrograde: glue injection, or widening drainage — double stents, Malecot catheter, exteriorised stent,
       stent plus endopyelotomy stent, retrograde aspiration system.
     ◦ Other: renal artery embolisation, desmopressin.
 • Routine drains may add nothing after robot-assisted PN (stay, complications, renal function), given the
   low leak rate and spontaneous resolution.

VESICOURETHRAL ANASTOMOTIC FISTULA

Etiopathogenesis and Epidemiology
 • The vesicourethral anastomosis (VUA) in RP largely determines continence, catheter duration, and the
   risks of stricture and leakage; minimally invasive surgery allows a more precise watertight anastomosis,
   shorter catheterisation and faster recovery.
 • Suture techniques: continuous (CS) versus interrupted (IS); in robot-assisted radical prostatectomy
   (RARP) the continuous Van Velthoven anastomosis prevails.
 • Radiological leak: 3.2–33%, depending on definition and catheter duration.

 • Patil pioneered quantifiable leak definitions: with pre-removal cystography, a significant leak meant

Diagnosis
 • Pre-removal cystography is used, but leak extent may not correlate with outcome. Prolonged
   catheterisation is linked to poorer function and more complaints; conversely leakage is linked to
   retention, anastomotic stricture and voiding complaints.
 • Risk-adapted cystography for men with large prostates and preoperative LUTS, with longer
   catheterisation if a leak is shown.
 • Clinically significant leak: drain fluid with raised creatinine, or substantial extravasation on a day-7
   cystogram that extends the planned catheter period.

Treatment
 • Extended catheterisation is the mainstay; gentle catheter traction is common.
 • Correct catheter position: for a non-fenestrated catheter — radiological repositioning, balloon deflation,
   external fixation.
 • Drain manoeuvres: switch active to passive suction, pull back, withdraw.
 • Persistent leak → ureteric stents and percutaneous nephrostomies before surgery.

URINARY FISTULA AFTER GENDER-AFFIRMING SURGERY
 • Rising numbers mean urologists meet unfamiliar anatomy; genital reconstruction is multistage and
   complex, so complications are common.

Female to Male Gender-Affirming Surgery
 • Urethral lengthening is the major challenge — strictures, obstruction and fistulae.
 • After phalloplasty and metoidioplasty, stricture and fistula are both common; fistula relatively
   commoner after metoidioplasty; both typically appear within about 3 months. Most need revision, after
   which most void from the tip of the phallus.
 • Colpectomy (primary) roughly halves urethral fistula rates after single-stage urethral lengthening.
 • Management: small fistulae often close with prolonged catheterisation; persistent → excision and
   primary repair or complex regrafting.

Male to Female Gender-Affirming Surgery
 • Urethrovaginal (urethroneovaginal) fistula is rare — continuous incontinence and recurrent UTI.
   Conservative first (suprapubic diversion); failure → formal urethroplasty with omentum or other
   interposition if complex. Rates are low after both penile inversion and penoscrotal vaginoplasty.
 • Vesiconeovaginal fistula (about 0.9%) — usually from intraoperative bladder injury; conservative first,
   then perineal or transabdominal repair with interposition in complex cases.
