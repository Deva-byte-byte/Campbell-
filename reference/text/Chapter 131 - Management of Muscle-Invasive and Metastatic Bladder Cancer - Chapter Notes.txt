Chapter 131 — Management of Muscle-Invasive and Metastatic Bladder Cancer


 Chapter 131 — Management of Muscle-Invasive and
 Metastatic Bladder Cancer
  • Burden: in the United States in 2022, an estimated 81,000 new cases and 17,000 deaths; bladder
    cancer is the fourth commonest malignancy in males (about 6% of all cancer diagnoses). Less common
    in females, yet female sex is associated with worse survival.
  • Stage at presentation: most patients have non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC); 20–30%
    present with muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) and about 12% with node-positive or metastatic
    disease.
  • Care is multidisciplinary — urologist, medical oncologist and radiation oncologist — from diagnosis
    onwards.

 NATURAL HISTORY
  • Primary versus secondary MIBC: most MIBC is muscle-invasive at diagnosis (primary); about 20%
    progress from NMIBC during initial management (secondary).
  • Five-year survival: localised disease about 70% (a figure that includes NMIBC); regional disease about
    39%; distant disease about 8%.
  • Secondary MIBC does worse. Initial understaging contributes, but secondary MIBC also shows lower
    pathological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) — evidence of a biological and possibly
    genomic difference from primary MIBC.
  • Death after adequate local therapy is systemic: most deaths occur within 2 years of treatment, and
    non-locoregional relapse reflects micrometastatic disease already present at diagnosis. Hence the
    central place of neoadjuvant and adjuvant (multimodal) therapy.

 Risk Factors
  • Tobacco is the number one risk factor, accounting for nearly half of all bladder cancers; the
    carcinogens are aromatic amines and N-nitroso compounds.
      ◦ Smokers carry a relative risk of about 3.5 versus non-smokers; risk rises with amount and duration
        smoked and falls after cessation.
      ◦ Screen every patient for smoking status and advise cessation at diagnosis. The effect of cannabis,
        e-cigarettes and vaping is unknown.
  • Occupational exposure: aluminium production, rubber production, dye work, coal-tar pitch, dry
    cleaning, hairdressing, printing and textile manufacture; carcinogens range from 2-naphthylamine to
    4,4′-methylene-bis compounds and tetrachloroethylene. Occupation accounts for about 7% of cases
    in males; no association has been found in females.
  • Other factors (detailed in Chapter 129): radiation, environmental exposures, chronic cystitis and
    schistosomiasis (both squamous cell carcinoma), cyclophosphamide and pioglitazone, genetic and
    dietary factors.
 Concept — Muscle-invasive bladder cancer is a systemic disease at diagnosis in a large minority: death
 after local therapy comes from micrometastases already present, which is why every curative pathway
 now wraps local treatment in systemic therapy.

 HISTOLOGY
  • Urothelial carcinoma (UC) accounts for 90% of bladder tumours.

    use and timing of NAC and/or radiation.
  • Variant histology at RC is more often upstaged, with more extravesical disease, lymphovascular

 CLINICAL STAGING AND DIAGNOSIS
  • Clinical stage estimates disease burden before RC; pathological stage comes from microscopy of the RC
    and pelvic lymphadenectomy specimens.
  • Clinical staging combines: depth of invasion on transurethral resection (TUR) → bimanual examination
    under anaesthesia (EUA) → abdominopelvic cross-sectional imaging with and without contrast, with
    excretory phase for the upper tracts → chest imaging.

  • Clinical–pathological discordance is common: in clinical T2 disease, pathological upstaging occurs in
    about 40–54% and downstaging in about 17%.
        ◦ Predictors of upstaging: LVI, variant histology, female sex; LVI also predicts nodal disease at RC;
          secondary MIBC is upstaged more often than primary.
        ◦ Understaging increases progression and death from disease.
 Transurethral resection
  • TUR is the standard diagnostic procedure; a genitourinary pathologist should report depth of invasion
    and histological subtype.
  • Document the location, size, number and appearance of tumours; consider a separate deep specimen
    from each tumour, especially if invasive (TUR technique: Chapter 132).
  • Resect all macroscopic tumour when safe and feasible: this lowers local burden, may optimise NAC
    response, and improves the chance of successful bladder preservation with chemoradiotherapy.
  • pT0 at RC confers a survival benefit in randomised NAC trials whether or not NAC was given —
    suggesting a benefit from aggressive TUR itself.
 The prostatic urethra and the female urethra
  • Inspect the prostatic urethra (males) and urethra (females) at TUR.
  • Risk factors for prostatic urethral involvement: tumour at the trigone or bladder neck, carcinoma in
    situ (CIS), high-volume multifocal disease.
  • Why it matters: prostatic stromal involvement influences the need for NAC, the need for
    urethrectomy, and the choice of urinary diversion.
  • Prostatic urethral biopsy at TUR: full loop of tissue from mid-prostate to the mid-to-distal
    verumontanum → at 5 and 7 o'clock adjacent to the verumontanum (highest density of prostatic
    ducts; commonest site of CIS) → plus any abnormal-looking tissue. A full-thickness resection shows the
    pathologist the interface of urethral mucosa, prostatic ducts and stroma, permitting accurate staging.
  • Frozen section at cystoprostatectomy:
        ◦ Negative TUR biopsy: the European Association of Urology (EAU) suggests the intraoperative frozen
          margin may be omitted; however, intraoperative frozen section has a higher negative predictive
          value, so the authors recommend it whenever orthotopic diversion is considered.
        ◦ Positive TUR biopsy → intraoperative frozen urethral margin is required; if negative, orthotopic
          diversion can be considered.
  • Females: urethral involvement is rare; take a proximal urethral margin whenever orthotopic diversion
    is considered. Risk factors: involvement of the bladder neck, bladder base and anterior vaginal wall.
 Bimanual examination under anaesthesia
  • Perform before and after resection, with the bladder drained and no catheter in place; dominant hand
    suprapubic, one or two fingers of the other hand in the rectum (male) or vagina (female); assess
    palpable disease and bladder mobility.

 Imaging and laboratory evaluation
  • National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), AUA and EAU all recommend computed
    tomography (CT) of abdomen and pelvis with and without contrast, with excretory phase (unless
    contrast is contraindicated). Purposes: nodal and metastatic staging, upper tract assessment, and local
    extent and resectability.
  • Image before TUR: post-resection inflammation mimics T3 disease.
  • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or magnetic resonance urography (MRU): for poor renal function
    or iodinated contrast allergy. 3-tesla magnets, dynamic contrast enhancement (DCE) and diffusion-
    weighted imaging (DWI) give better local T-staging than CT; MRI may also predict grade and
    chemosensitivity.
  • Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System (VI-RADS) standardises bladder MRI reporting; a score of 3
    or more is suspicious for MIBC; sensitivity and specificity for muscle invasion are both high (roughly
    80–90%). Requires an experienced radiologist and pre-TUR imaging; expected to do for bladder cancer
    what PI-RADS does for prostate cancer.

 at disease no scan can see.

  • Substaging is prognostic: pT2a outperforms pT2b, and pT3b does worse than pT3a, for recurrence-
    free and cancer-specific survival — the reason both substages are retained.
  • Prostatic invasion (pT4a) requires established stromal invasion — directly through the bladder neck,
    or posteriorly via the seminal vesicles and periprostatic ducts. CIS of the prostatic urethra or ducts
    does not upstage.
  • Organ-confined disease = pT2bN0M0 or less.
  • 8th-edition nodal changes: perivesical nodes are now regional (N1); nodes beyond the common iliacs
    are M1a, because nodal-only distant disease survives better than non-nodal metastasis (M1b).
  • Traps: common iliac involvement is N3, not M1; stage IIIA already includes N1; T4b is stage IVA even
    when N0.

 Staging Pitfalls
  • High-grade T1: repeat resection within 6 weeks of the initial transurethral resection of bladder tumour
    (TURBT), because upstaging is common.
      ◦ No muscularis propria in the specimen → upstaging on re-resection in over 40%; even with muscle
        present, about a fifth to a third are upstaged to MIBC.
      ◦ AUA and EAU: repeat TUR for T1 disease without muscle in the specimen (restaging TUR: Chapter
        130).

 Molecular Classifications
  • Four systems: University of North Carolina (UNC), MD Anderson Cancer Center (MDA), The Cancer
    Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Lund University — derived mainly from MIBC cohorts, each with three to five
    subtypes (e.g. UNC: claudin-low, basal, luminal; MDA: basal, p53-like, luminal).
  • The systems are concordant: MDA basal ≈ Lund squamous cell subtype; MDA p53-like ≈ Lund infiltrated
    (both extracellular matrix–enriched); MDA luminal ≈ Lund urobasal A (both carry activating fibroblast
    growth factor receptor 3 [FGFR3] mutations).

 Circulating Tumor Markers
  • Imaging remains the standard for assessing disease burden; circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) is a
    promising alternative.
  • ctDNA clears during neoadjuvant and adjuvant immunotherapy and tracks overall prognosis.
  • In cisplatin-ineligible patients given neoadjuvant immunotherapy, ctDNA positivity fell stepwise from
    baseline → after immunotherapy → after surgery; preoperative ctDNA predicted higher T and N
    stage; no ctDNA-negative patient relapsed, and most relapses were ctDNA-positive.
  • Over a third remain ctDNA-positive after RC, with poor prognosis; in the IMvigor010 exploratory
    analysis, adjuvant atezolizumab increased ctDNA clearance and improved disease-free and overall
    survival in ctDNA-positive patients.
  • ctDNA may become a prognostic and predictive decision aid.
 Concept — Pathological stage is prognosis: organ confinement (≤pT2b) and nodal status carry most of the
 information, and molecular subtype and ctDNA are emerging as the tools that will say who needs more
 than surgery.

 TREATMENT
  • Discuss all curative options in a multidisciplinary setting before treatment — urologist and medical
    oncologist, plus a radiation oncologist if bladder preservation is considered — weighing disease factors,
    comorbidity and patient goals.
  • Do not delay: a delay of more than 12 weeks (3 months) from diagnosis to RC worsens outcome —
    more extravesical tumours and nodal metastases, poorer disease-specific and overall survival —
    consistently across surgical series, population data (roughly doubled disease-specific and overall
    mortality) and meta-analysis.

 RADICAL CYSTECTOMY AND BILATERAL PELVIC LYMPH NODE DISSECTION
  • RC with bilateral pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND) is the standard of care for localised, resectable
    MIBC.
  • Most comparative studies show poorer survival with bladder preservation, but those patients are
    older and more comorbid, and quality of life was not assessed.
  • About 80% of MIBC patients having definitive treatment undergo RC.
  • Excellent local control: pelvic recurrence as low as about 4% in node-negative disease.
  • Randomised data show superior outcomes with NAC (see Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy).

 Organ-sparing cystectomy in males
  • Options: preservation of the neurovascular bundles, the prostatic capsule or entire prostate, and the
    seminal vesicles.
  • Prostate-sparing requires: no bladder neck disease + negative prostatic urethral biopsy + negative
    prostate biopsy.
  • Why caution: incidental prostate cancer in roughly a quarter to a half of cystoprostatectomy
    specimens (up to a third clinically significant), and UC involving the prostate in a substantial
    proportion; predictors are bladder neck tumour, multifocality and CIS.
  • Evidence is small retrospective series: function tracks the amount of tissue spared; apical-, posterior-
    and total-sparing techniques all report good continence and erectile function.
  • Capsule-sparing versus nerve-sparing: a small randomised trial found no clinically significant
    difference in oncological or functional outcome; a retrospective comparison found capsule- and
    seminal-vesicle-sparing gave comparable oncology and better sexual function — conflicting.
  • Reserve for carefully selected men who wish to preserve sexual function and fertility.
 Organ-sparing cystectomy in females
  • UC involving the gynaecological organs is uncommon (about 5–6%).
  • Complete urethrectomy can be omitted unless the bladder neck is involved, permitting orthotopic
    substitution.
  • No tumour at the bladder base or trigone → uterus and anterior vagina may be spared: better
    neobladder support, less urinary retention, preserved autonomic nerves and less postoperative pelvic
    organ prolapse.
  • Premenopausal ovarian preservation in women without breast or ovarian cancer risk maintains
    hormonal homeostasis. Increased endometrial, breast or ovarian cancer risk → salpingo-
    oophorectomy in women beyond childbearing and older than 40.
  • In selected patients: similar oncological control, better function.
  • Counsel both sexes about the impact of RC on sexual function.
 Concept — An organ may be spared only where it is oncologically silent: in men the gatekeeper is the
 prostatic urethra and bladder neck, in women the bladder neck, base and anterior vaginal wall.

 BILATERAL PELVIC LYMPH NODE DISSECTION
  • A meticulous PLND is essential. About 25% have nodal metastases at RC, and nodal status is the most
    powerful surrogate for long-term recurrence-free and overall survival.
  • Node-positive: about 70–80% recur, versus about 30% after a negative PLND.
  • Extent of PLND independently predicts survival and local recurrence, even adjusting for chemotherapy
    and pathology. What constitutes an adequate dissection, and its therapeutic value, is less clear.

 Number of Lymph Nodes Identified at the Time of Cystectomy
  • Higher yield → higher sensitivity for nodal metastasis; "adequate" yields quoted as 12–25 nodes.
  • Modelling: removing 25 nodes gives about a 75% chance of detecting nodal disease (about 50% with
    15–25 nodes, 90% with 45) → 25 nodes as a reasonable staging minimum.
  • Node count carries prognostic and staging information in both node-positive and node-negative
    patients.
  • A therapeutic threshold is unproven (no prospective data; most studies cite 9–16 nodes): in a
    Southwest Oncology Group (SWOG) 8710 reanalysis, fewer than 10 nodes doubled the risk of death;
    another series linked 16 or more nodes to better survival. Ten nodes is the commonest threshold for
    pelvic recurrence.
  • Node count reflects surgery and pathology alike — template extent and the number of packets
    submitted.

 Lymph Node Density and Extracapsular Nodal Extension
  • Lymph node density = positive nodes ÷ total nodes removed.
  • Early series: density below 20% → better DSS, and better response to adjuvant chemotherapy; other
    studies found no advantage over simple node status; a meta-analysis linked density to poorer DSS, but
    with marked heterogeneity.

 INTRAOPERATIVE DECISION MAKING
 Grossly Positive Nodes and T4b Disease
  • Clinically node-positive before RC → cisplatin-based chemotherapy first.
  • Radiographic complete or partial response → evaluate for consolidative RC: pathological complete
    response in about 14–25%, and complete responders have markedly better cancer-specific survival;
    responders who forgo surgery mostly recur (data preceding maintenance immunotherapy). Strongly
    consider consolidative RC in fit responders.
  • Unexpected lymphadenopathy at RC: frozen section to confirm → proceed to extended PLND and RC
    only if bladder and nodes are completely resectable and the nodes lie within the true pelvis.
  • Do not proceed with RC if: nodes are unresectable; extensive periureteral disease; bladder fixed to
    the pelvic sidewall; invasion of the rectosigmoid colon.
  • Aborted RC → very poor prognosis: most die of progression within about 2 years; no 5-year survivors
    in one series.

 Intraoperative Frozen Sections of the Ureter
  • Distal ureteric involvement at RC: about 6–8%; the final ureteric margin independently predicts upper
    tract recurrence (UTR).
  • UTR is uncommon — about 4–10% — occurs mostly 2–4 years after RC, and is often locally advanced
    and worse than de novo upper tract UC.
  • UTR risk factors: positive ureteric margin, prior bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG), bladder CIS,
    preoperative hydronephrosis.

    negative margin has not consistently improved outcome.
  • Frozen section accuracy: sensitivity roughly 70–77%, specificity 83–96%; a positive frozen section
    makes a positive final margin far more likely.
  • Sequential resection: most initially positive margins can be cleared, but "converted" margins still carry
    higher UTR risk than initially negative ones, and persistently positive margins the highest; failure to
    convert predicts worse cancer-specific mortality.
  • Skip lesions (negative distal frozen section, positive proximal final margin) occur predominantly with
    CIS.
  • Practice: clear the distal ureter when feasible; frozen section whenever gross tumour is encountered;
    for CIS alone, resect maximally without compromising length for the diversion — the benefit of a CIS-
    negative margin is debatable given skip lesions.

 Prostatic Urothelial Carcinoma and Management of the Distal Urethra
  • Male urethral recurrence after RC: 1–8%; most are symptomatic; most occur within 2 years (median
    about 8–28 months); periodic cytology helps in high-risk men.
  • Risk factors: prostatic urethral involvement, prostatic stromal invasion (highest risk — up to about
    30%), bladder neck tumour, CIS, multifocality; stromal invasion outranks prostatic urethral CIS and
    ductal or acinar involvement.
  • Urethrectomy: consider for diffuse CIS of the prostatic urethra or prostatic stromal invasion; the
    authors perform en bloc urethrectomy for invasive prostatic urethral disease; some reserve it for a
    positive apical urethral margin.
  • A positive preoperative prostatic urethral biopsy does not preclude orthotopic diversion: take an
    intraoperative frozen margin, and if negative, orthotopic diversion may proceed.
  • Frozen urethral margin: specificity near 100%, sensitivity variable (about 33–93%). Perform it in any
    patient considered for orthotopic diversion.
  • Small low-grade papillary urethral tumours: resect before RC and retain the urethra if there is no
    other indication for urethrectomy.
  • Lower urethral recurrence after orthotopic diversion is more likely selection bias than a protective
    effect of urine exposure (no physiological evidence).

 Managing the Female Urethra
  • Only T4 tumours involving the urethra and/or vagina mandate complete urethrectomy (no urethral
    preservation).
  • Urethral involvement: about 2–12%. Risk features: primary tumour at the bladder neck, posterior
    bladder base or anterior vaginal wall, or inguinal lymphadenopathy.
  • About 60% of bladder neck tumours have no urethral tumour on final pathology, so urethrectomy is
    not automatic.
  • Proximal urethral frozen section correlates well with the final margin — perform in every woman
    considered for orthotopic substitution.
  • Isolated urethral recurrence after orthotopic substitution: about 1%.

 Neoadjuvant Immunotherapy and Chemoimmunotherapy Combinations
  • Programmed death-1 (PD-1) on activated T cells binds programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1); tumour
    cells express PD-L1 to neutralise T-cell activation. Cytotoxic T lymphocyte–associated antigen 4
    (CTLA-4) on activated T cells engages receptors on antigen-presenting cells to inhibit T-cell
    proliferation. Blocking either increases T-cell activation and proliferation → antitumour response.

 MULTIMODAL BLADDER PRESERVATION THERAPY
  • Best candidates: small solitary tumour, no pelvic lymphadenopathy, no CIS, no hydronephrosis, good
    bladder function.
  • Historically reserved for those unfit for surgery; now a viable option in selected MIBC. RC carries the
    second-highest 30-day readmission rate in Medicare claims, and patients are typically older and
    comorbid.
  • Two populations: (1) high operative risk from comorbidity and frailty; (2) surgically fit patients with
    limited disease, adequate bladder urothelium and function, motivated to keep the bladder. Careful
    selection and counselling are essential.
  • Trimodality therapy (TMT) — the best-studied approach = visibly complete TUR + radiosensitising
    chemotherapy + radiation. Complete TUR with radiation improves local control; radiation also treats
    adjacent nodes; chemotherapy sensitises.
  • Concurrent sensitising chemotherapy is superior to radiation alone — established by two randomised
    trials and many retrospective studies.

 cystectomy in 10–30% is the designed safety net, so selection — T2, no CIS, no hydronephrosis, visibly
 complete TUR — determines success.

 SINGLE-MODALITY TREATMENT
 Chemotherapy Monotherapy
  • Chemotherapy's role is neoadjuvant or within TMT; alone it rarely produces durable remission,
    though some patients are pT0 at cystectomy.
  • Complete responders who declined RC: at 5 years, about two-thirds alive and half with an intact
    bladder, but about a third died of bladder cancer; long survivors had small, solitary, low-stage,
    completely resected tumours. An MVAC + TUR series: complete response about 50%; 60% alive
    without further therapy at about 5 years.
  • Intravesical recurrence is common and early — nearly half at a median of about 5 months in a cT0
    series.
  • RETAIN BLADDER (phase II): surveillance after NAC for patients with DNA damage repair mutations
    (ATM, ERCC2, FANCC, RB1) and no visible disease → half recurred at about 15 months, several with
    MIBC.
  • Not recommended as primary therapy for localised MIBC.

 Radiation Monotherapy
  • (Here including debulking TUR before radiation; excludes TMT and adjuvant radiotherapy.) Inferior to
    radiation with sensitising chemotherapy; reserve for quasi-palliative situations.
  • Evidence: 5- and 10-year OS about 40% and 19%, inferior to chemoradiation; local control as low as
    27% in T3; the BC2001 radiation-alone arm was inferior to chemoradiation; in patients aged 80 or
    more, chemoradiation improved 2-year OS over external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) alone.
  • Predictors of local failure (as for radical TUR): tumour size, multifocality, hydronephrosis, CIS, higher
    clinical T stage, incomplete TURBT.
  • Curative bladder dose: 64–66 Gy in 32 fractions, or 55 Gy in 20 fractions; treat nodes at the physician's
    discretion, especially if node-positive.
  • Not routine; use in patients unfit for surgery or chemoradiation, including for symptom control.

 Radical Transurethral Resection
  • Viable only for tumour confined to superficial muscle. Rationale: pT0 in about 10% of patients treated
    with RC alone.
  • Limitations: understaging (pT3 up to about 40% and pT4 about 9% of cT2 cystectomy specimens) and
    about 25% occult nodal metastasis.
  • Long-term series (>10 years): after restaging TUR showing no tumour or residual NMIBC, a third
    developed MIBC, and nearly half of those died; another cohort: over half recurred.
  • Highly selected contemporary series (tumour <3 cm, no hydronephrosis; intravesical therapy for CIS;
    re-resection at 3 and 6 months; 3-monthly cystoscopy for 2 years): cancer-specific survival about 82%,
    80%, 77% at 5, 10, 15 years, but progression-free survival with intact bladder falling to about 58% at
    15 years.
  • Not a primary treatment; counsel patients who are unfit or decline RC and TMT about the substantial
    recurrence risk.

 Partial Cystectomy
  • Associated with higher mortality than RC (no randomised comparison). Advantages over radical TUR:
    full PLND for staging, and full-thickness wall with perivesical fat removed.
  • Historically poor (5-year OS as low as 24%; frequent local and wound recurrence); acceptable with
    strict selection, when long-term results may approach RC.

 FOLLOW-UP
  • Goals: detect local (pelvic or bladder), distant and urothelial recurrence; schedules differ after RC and
    after bladder preservation.
  • Local recurrence: in the pelvis, usually within 24 months (rarely up to 5 years); 5–15% with aggressive
    disease (high pT, node-positive, positive margins); poor prognosis; treated with chemotherapy and
    radiation.
  • Distant recurrence: historically 32–70%, mostly within 2 years — probably lower with modern NAC and
    adjuvant immunotherapy, though late recurrence occurs; commonest sites distant nodes, lung, liver,
    bone; survival after recurrence is poor.
  • Urothelial recurrence: urethra about 4%; upper tract 4–10%. Urethral risk: tumour in the prostatic
    urethra (males) or bladder neck (females). The upper tract is the commonest site of late recurrence,
    and most such patients die of metastasis; biggest risk factor: positive ureteric margin; detection is best
    with upper tract imaging plus cytology.
  • Whether detecting asymptomatic recurrence improves survival is controversial — retrospective data
    conflict; no prospective data.

 MANAGEMENT OF METASTATIC BLADDER CANCER
  • Immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted agents have transformed the field, but cisplatin-based
    combination chemotherapy remains first-line for metastatic UC; many patients face barriers to its
    delivery.
  • Cisplatin ineligibility (consensus working group — any one): GFR <60 mL/min, grade ≥2 hearing loss,
    grade ≥2 neuropathy, WHO or ECOG performance status >1, NYHA class ≥III → nearly 50% of patients
    (Table 131.5).
  • Prognostic factors (Bajorin model, MVAC-treated series): Karnofsky performance status <80% and
    visceral metastasis (lung, liver, bone) — median survival about 33, 13 and 9 months with 0, 1 and 2
    factors. Node-only disease: far better 5-year survival than visceral disease (about 21% vs 7%).

 First-Line Chemotherapy for Cisplatin-Eligible Patients
  • Regimens: ddMVAC and GC.
  • Most (40–70%) respond initially, but most progress: median survival about 14 months, 5-year survival
    5–20%.
  • Split-dose cisplatin is occasionally used for GFR 40–60 mL/min when renal function is the only barrier
    — no randomised comparison with standard dosing.

 Second-Line Therapy
  • No clear standard second-line chemotherapy; patients failing first-line chemotherapy have a poor
    prognosis, and conventional salvage agents give suboptimal responses — immunotherapy has changed
    this.

 Second-Line Chemotherapy
  • Re-treat with platinum combination (GC or ddMVAC) if the last platinum was ≥12 months earlier;
    response is better in prior responders (MVAC after GC: about 44% vs 14%), and platinum-combination
    re-treatment gives better OS than non-platinum chemotherapy.
  • Single agents (NCCN alternatives): paclitaxel, docetaxel, gemcitabine — modest responses and
    survival (Table 131.10); docetaxel causes myelosuppression requiring dose reduction in most.
  • Gemcitabine-paclitaxel: responses vary widely (0–60%) by schedule; a phase III trial of short vs
    prolonged therapy found no OS or progression-free survival difference.
  • Vinflunine (antitubulin derived from a vinca alkaloid) vs best supportive care → low response (8.6%)
    but OS benefit on updated analysis (HR 0.72) → approved in Europe (2009) as second-line; not used
    in the United States.
  • Larotaxel (semisynthetic taxane, claimed activity in taxane-resistant cells and blood–brain barrier
    penetration) + cisplatin vs GC first-line: closed early (sponsor decision); no OS difference, worse
    progression-free survival → no gain over GC.

 Future Directions
  • Biomarker-driven risk stratification (tumour, urine, serum) and combination therapies across
    neoadjuvant, adjuvant and advanced settings are expected to keep improving survival.
 Concept — Metastatic treatment is sequenced by platinum eligibility: platinum first for anyone who can
 take it, then avelumab to hold the response, then checkpoint inhibition, antibody–drug conjugates or FGFR
 inhibition at progression — immunotherapy alone first-line is the exception reserved for the platinum-
 ineligible.


   KEY POINTS
    • Optimal MIBC care is multidisciplinary (urological, medical and radiation oncology); clinical staging
      lacks the sensitivity and specificity to predict final pathological and nodal stage — a major limit on
      pretreatment decisions.
    • Gold standard for organ-confined MIBC: radical cystectomy, with neoadjuvant cisplatin-based
      chemotherapy for eligible patients.
    • Multimodal bladder preservation (chemoradiation with maximal TUR) gives outcomes comparable
      to cystectomy in carefully selected patients (clinical T2, small solitary tumour, no hydronephrosis, no
      concurrent CIS).
    • Despite aggressive surgery, about 50% of cystectomy patients ultimately die of disease.
    • Neoadjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy gives a modest but significant survival advantage on
      meta-analysis; adjuvant evidence is weaker, so neoadjuvant chemotherapy should be considered in
      all cisplatin-eligible patients.
