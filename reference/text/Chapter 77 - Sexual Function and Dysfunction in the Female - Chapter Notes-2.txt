 ANATOMY AND PHYSIOLOGY OF FEMALE SEXUAL ORGANS

 Introduction
  • Anatomy is presented from a clinical perspective; accurate examination of the external and internal
    genitalia is mandatory for understanding female sexual function and dysfunction.
  • Physical examination yields critical information because biological and psychosexual factors are closely
    interrelated.

 Female Sexual Anatomy
  • Pelvic organs form a continuum, grouped as external genitalia (vulva) and internal genitalia (vagina,
    cervix, uterus, fallopian tubes, ovaries); external structures are emphasised because most
    physiological research concerns them. The description follows O'Connell et al. (2008) and centres on
    the distal vagina.
  • The distal vagina is the site of female sexual response, interrelated with the clitoris and the urethra;
    the three share blood supply and innervation and respond as a unit with the overlying highly vascular
    vulval skin.
  • Components of the distal vagina: introitus, vaginal walls, associated exocrine glands.
 Vulva
  • Definition: the wrapping overlying the clitoro-urethro-vaginal complex.
  • Boundaries: symphysis pubis anteriorly, anal sphincter posteriorly, ischial tuberosities laterally.
  • Constituents: mons pubis, clitoris and glands, labia majora and minora, vestibule, hymen, introitus,
    fourchette, perineal body, urethral orifice, glandular ducts.
 Mons Pubis
  • Rounded eminence of subcutaneous adipose connective tissue anterior to the pubic symphysis;
    triangular, apex at the glans clitoris.
  • Across life: flat before adolescence → coarse hair with a horizontal upper boundary in adolescence →
    thins after menopause.
  • Fascial support to the clitoris and urethra is provided by the suspensory ligaments of the clitoris.
 Clitoris
  • Erectile structure homologous to the penis, postero-inferior to the anterior commissure, partially
    enclosed by the anterior bifurcated ends of the labia minora.
  • The corpus clitoridis has two corpora cavernosa of erectile tissue in dense fibrous tissue, separated by
    an incomplete pectiniform septum; each corpus is anchored to its ischiopubic ramus by a crus (the
    crura are the proximal continuations of the corpora).
  • The deep suspensory ligament (DSL) forms a midline septum separating the left and right dorsal
    nerves, which run along the dorsal clitoral body superficial to, not beneath, the tunica albuginea.
  • Glans: small round tubercle with high cutaneous sensitivity; histologically nonerectile vascular tissue.
  • Suspensory ligament and two ischiocavernosus muscles attach to the crura.
  • Unlike the penis, the clitoris is separate from the urethra.

  • Gynaecological perineum: the interval between posterior labial commissure and anus, 2.5–3 cm.
 Vestibule
  • Cavity between the labia minora containing the vaginal and external urethral orifices, openings of the
    two greater vestibular glands and numerous mucous lesser vestibular glands; vestibular fossa lies
    between vaginal orifice and frenulum.
  • Greater vestibular (Bartholin) glands are homologues of the male bulbourethral glands: small reddish-
    yellow bodies flanking the vaginal orifice, overlapped by the posterior vestibular bulb; each duct (~2
    cm) opens in the groove between hymen and labium minus.
 Introitus
  • Almost closed in childhood and in some adult pathological states; highly elastic to permit the fetal
    head; resting diameter ~3–4 cm.
  • Skin over the introitus and clitoris: hairless, moist, squamous epithelium.
 Hymen Vaginae
  • Thin mucous-membrane fold just within the vaginal orifice; the orifice appears as a cleft between its
    folds.
  • Morphology: usually annular, widest posteriorly; semilunar (concave to mons); cribriform or fringed;
    absent; or imperforate. Carunculae hymenales (myrtiformes) are remnants after rupture.
  • No established function, yet extreme sociocultural significance; hymenal appearance does not reliably
    indicate virginal status given normal variation. The topic interrelates with female genital mutilation.
 Fourchette
  • Midline skin of the posterior margin of the introitus; important in parturition.
 Perineal Body
  • Pyramidal block of fibromuscular connective tissue between vagina and anal canal; an extremely
    elastic zone at the confluence of the perineal support muscles, small at rest but stretching without
    tearing for the fetal head.

 Concept — The vulva straddles a limb-derived anterior–superficial territory (femoral, external pudendal,
 ilioinguinal) and a pelvic posterior–deep territory (internal pudendal, perineal, sacral); arteries, veins,
 lymphatics and cutaneous nerves all divide along the same line.
 The Clitoral Complex
  • Comprises clitoris, distal vagina and urethra, responding as a unit with the overlying vascular skin.
  • Female erectile organs are the clitoris and the "vestibular" bulbs; the bulbs are inappropriately
    named, relating directly to the clitoral components and urethra.
  • Architecture: clitoral body (corpora) → bilateral crura; separate bulbs posterior to the crura; all unite at
    the root of the clitoris, covered by the vestibule, related to the distal urethra and meatus, posterior
    limit the introitus.

  • Urethra lies immediately deep to the introital skin, anteriorly in the midline, palpable bulging the
    anterior distal vaginal wall (epithelium moist, hairless, rugated). Embedded in the anterior vaginal wall
    on one face, surrounded by erectile tissue (crura) on all others; well shown by magnetic resonance
    imaging (MRI).

  • Skene (paraurethral) glands (Skene, 1880): source of urethral secretions at high arousal;
    immunopositive for prostate-specific antigen (PSA); homologous to the prostate.
  • Female ejaculation is reported but lacks firm evidence.
  • Gräfenberg spot (G-spot): anterior vaginal wall over the mid-urethra, sensitive in some women;
    Gräfenberg located it as arising from the urethra; anatomical basis controversial.
 Concept — Female erectile anatomy is one integrated complex wrapped around the distal urethra; the
 urethra is vaginal on one face and erectile-encircled on all others, which is why urethral and anterior
 vaginal wall surgery cannot be separated from sexual function.
 Clitoral Complex: Vascularisation and Neuroanatomy
  • Supplied and drained from four directions (anterolateral and posterolateral bilaterally) plus deep
    midline drainage.
  • Arteries: prepuce ← external pudendal arteries; erectile tissue ← dorsal clitoral arteries, perineal
    arteries (bulbar and urethral branches), deep arteries central in the corpora.
  • Venous: erectile tissue → deep dorsal vein → subpubic canal → vesical venous plexus; it does not
    accompany the arteries.
  • Lymphatic: prepuce → superficial inguinal; glans → deep inguinal directly. The glans periphery is a
    watershed between external and internal pudendal vessels and between superficial and deep inguinal
    lymphatics.
 SOMATIC VS VISCERAL INNERVATION OF THE CLITORAL COMPLEX
  Somatic                                                            Visceral
  Pudendal nerve → dorsal nerve of clitoris → skin                   Cavernous nerves → arteries of erectile tissue
  Afferent limb: sensation                                           Efferent limb: engorgement

  • The female sexual reflex is unique: engorgement is its motor component, it can be voluntarily
    augmented or suppressed, and it pairs somatic afferents with visceral efferents.
  • Reflex arc: receptors of clitoral complex and vulva → somatic afferents (pudendal: dorsal clitoral and
    perineal branches) → S2–S4 → visceral efferents (pelvic parasympathetics via nervi erigentes through
    pelvic ganglia) → effectors (erectile tissue) with sub-bulbar and urethral gland secretion. Visceral
    afferents from the complex are possible but undemonstrated.
  • Engorgement: parasympathetic → dilatation of dorsal and deep clitoral arterioles → increased inflow
    with restricted outflow; the vulva swells, distal vagina and urethra secrete, and vulval components
    normally hidden by the labia minora become exposed and reddened.
 Concept — Clitoral erection is a mixed reflex: somatic (pudendal) in, parasympathetic (pelvic) out,
 integrated at S2–S4; pudendal injury removes the trigger, pelvic or cavernous nerve injury the response.

 Female Sexual Physiology
  • Urological conditions and procedures can markedly influence female sexual wellness; sexual function
    both influences and is influenced by urological disease, and the urologist should address it.
 Female Sexual Response
  • Sexual response cycle: the sequence of physical and emotional changes during arousal and sexually
    stimulating activity (intercourse, masturbation). Masters and Johnson (1966): excitement → plateau →
    orgasm → resolution.

 Hormonal Regulators of Female Sexual Response
 Oestrogens
  • Oestradiol is the principal oestrogen; ER-α predominates in genital tissue, ER-β also present.
  • Maintains genital tissue integrity: epithelium, stroma, muscularis, rugal thickness, lubrication.
    Menopause reduces genital sensitivity, vaginal thickness, collagen, moisture and acidity (pH rises),
    possibly via VIP downregulation.
  • Neural: oestradiol expands pudendal touch-receptor fields (animal), lowering sensory thresholds.
    Vascular: vasoprotective and vasodilatory, protecting the hypogastric–pudendal arterial bed from
    atherosclerosis.
  • NO machinery: oestrogen loss (ageing, surgical castration) → reduced vaginal NO and NOS, wall
    fibrosis, epithelial and smooth muscle apoptosis; replacement reverses all three.
  • Deficiency → thin, fragile, dry, less acidic vagina → dryness and dyspareunia. Serum oestradiol ≤50
    pg/mL markedly increases risk of dryness and sexual pain.
  • Vaginal or systemic replacement → more interest and enjoyment, less pain, greater orgasmic potential.
    Menopausal urogenital symptoms (frequency, urgency, dysuria, incontinence) may be exacerbated by
    intercourse.
 Testosterone
  • Low testosterone is associated with reduced arousal, libido, responsiveness, genital sensation and
    orgasm. Premenopausal production is roughly equal from adrenals and ovaries.
  • Sex hormone–binding globulin (SHBG)-bound testosterone is biologically inactive; albumin binding is
    weak. Androgen receptors (ARs) are present in genital tissue; AR gene CAG-repeat length may
    modulate response (scant evidence).
  • Efficacy: parenteral oestrogen–androgen combinations outperform oestrogen alone for desire, libido,
    energy, well-being, arousal, fantasies, enjoyment and orgasm; testosterone with oestrogen treats
    dyspareunia, vulvovaginal atrophy and poor lubrication; topical testosterone has been used in vulval
    lichen disease.

 Concept — Oestrogen maintains the substrate (epithelium, vessels, NO machinery), so its loss produces
 structural complaints; testosterone drives the motivational layer, so it is the agent for HSDD, traded
 against virilisation.
 Mental Aspects of Sexual Response
  • Psychosocial variables interact significantly with sexual function: mood, trauma, relational factors,
    personality; also medical and mental illness, medications, family and cultural beliefs, early experiences,
    partners, stressors. Exploring them reveals modifiable obstacles.
  • Depression: substantial concurrent depression among women with arousal disorders. Anxiety is
    bidirectional — anxiety disorders worsen function, yet a minority report increased interest when
    anxious.
  • Stress: daily stressors → more problems, less satisfaction; chronic stress lowers laboratory genital
    response. Childhood and adolescent sexual abuse is significantly associated with adult dysfunction.
  • Relational factors: conflict, longer duration, dissatisfaction, desire or script discrepancy, lack of intimacy
    or trust, incompatibility, poor stimulation, non-optimal context, partner's dysfunction or poor
    technique; data mainly self-reported and cross-sectional.
  • Conservative gender stereotypes (woman as submissive recipient) impair arousal and orgasm; fading
    but prevalent in some societies.
  • Assess need for psychotherapy: stressors, social situation, relationships, psychiatric history, sexual
    trauma.
 Concept — Female sexual function is biopsychosocial, and the psychosocial arm is frequently the
 modifiable one; because the same variable (anxiety) can inhibit or facilitate, causation must be
 established in the individual, not assumed.

 EVALUATION OF SEXUAL WELLNESS

 History Taking
  • Patients are reluctant, so the clinician should raise sexual health; only a small minority of women with
    sexual problems seek advice.
  • Barriers: inadequate training and confidence, perceived lack of treatments, lack of time, patient
    hesitancy, underestimated prevalence.
  • Use a broad, sex-positive, health-focused framework; sexual health is a right for all (American College
    of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) Committee Opinion No. 706, 2017).
  • LOFTI (listening, observing, feelings, thinking, interpreting) for psychosexual problems.
  • Assessment integrates biological, psychological, sociocultural and interpersonal factors.

  • Partner involvement is of utmost importance; partners of women with FSD often have problems of

 Physical Examination
  • A focused internal and external pelvic examination identifies causes, especially in dyspareunia and
    vaginismus. Common findings: vaginal atrophy, dryness, infection, vulval dermatoses, pelvic floor
    muscle (PFM) dysfunction, adnexal masses, deep pelvic pain. A normal examination is reassuring.
  • External: inspect mons → labia majora → labia minora → clitoris → vestibule gently with a gloved hand
    and cotton swab. Vestibular redness and pain → vestibulitis; flattened pale labia → oestrogen
    deficiency or POP; also hair distribution, labial redundancy, atrophy, lesions.
  • Internal: palpate PFMs, urethra, anus; bimanual for uterine size, flexion, adnexal masses; fornix
    tenderness → endometriosis; POP and urethral hypermobility at rest and straining. Normal PFMs
    contract and relax voluntarily and are not tender.
  • Neurological: lower-limb sensory and motor; lumbosacral screen — PFM strength, anal resting tone,
    voluntary anal contraction, perineal sensation; genital neuropathy by heat/cold, vibration, toothpick
    or pin; abnormal screen → full examination.
  • Continence: lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) associate with sexual problems, so perform cough or
    Valsalva stress test and Q-tip test.

 Laboratory Tests
  • Rarely indicated without a specific suspected condition; baseline serum chemistry, lipids, glycated
    haemoglobin (HbA1c) may reveal DM.
  • Testosterone: no female normal range, no correlation with libido — random measurement is
    worthless; measure only if a hyper- or hypoandrogenic state is suspected.
  • Suspected hormonal abnormality: screen for prolactinoma, thyroid and adrenal disorders;
    dehydroepiandrosterone sulphate (DHEA-S) for adrenal insufficiency, being solely adrenal.
  • Androgen insufficiency syndrome (free testosterone below lowest quartile): low interest and well-
    being, fatigue, vasomotor symptoms persisting despite oestrogen, low motivation.
  • Routine oestrogen measurement is not useful; progesterone has very limited value.

 FEMALE SEXUAL INTEREST DISORDER
  • About 40% of women experience sexual dysfunction in their lifetime; lack of desire is the commonest
    at all ages (HSDD in DSM-IV-TR; within FSIAD in DSM-5). Distressing low desire affects a minority.
  • With ageing, desire falls but so does the distress it causes — low desire nearly universal in older
    women, HSDD uncommon.
  • Distressing low desire is associated with lower health-related quality of life (HRQoL), dissatisfaction
    with sex life or partner, frustration, hopelessness, anger, low self-esteem, loss of femininity; mental-
    health decrements; in postmenopausal women more fatigue, depression, memory problems, back pain
    and lower quality of life.

 FEMALE ORGASMIC DISORDER

 Introduction
  • FOD: marked delay, infrequency or absence of orgasm, or reduced intensity, for ≥6 months in 75–
    100% of sexual encounters. Subtypes: lifelong or acquired; generalised or situational; psychological or
    combined.
  • Second commonest FSD, closely behind HSDD; prevalence higher in Asia than in the US, Europe and
    Central/South America. Often coexists with another dysfunction — arousal most, then lubrication,
    desire, pain; vaginismus rare.
 DSM-IV-TR VS DSM-5 FEMALE ORGASMIC DISORDER
                     DSM-IV-TR                                        DSM-5
  Prerequisite       Despite a normal excitement phase                Removed — FOD can occur with intact arousal
  Dimensions         Frequency and latency                            Frequency, latency and intensity

  • Exclude before diagnosis: medical conditions, drugs (SSRIs, other psychotropics), psychosocial causes
    (severe interpersonal distress, stressors), and insufficient stimulation — a woman whose partner's ED
    or premature ejaculation deprives her of stimulation cannot be diagnosed with FOD.
  • Distress is mandatory: about half of women with inconsistent orgasm are not distressed.
  • Normal variation: clitorally triggered orgasm is normal; most women reach orgasm by masturbation,
    partnered orgasm is less frequent as many need added manual stimulation — coital anorgasmia
    without clitoral stimulation is not FOD.

 Assessment
  • Comprehensive sexual history of nature and onset, with other domains (FOD often accompanies
    interest and arousal problems).

 Medication-Induced Female Orgasmic Disorder
  • SSRIs commonly impair desire, arousal and orgasm. Sequence: establish onset (depression itself
    causes dysfunction; treatment may help over weeks) → if a side effect occurs while depression
    responds, wait for tolerance (weeks to about 4 months) → persistent beyond 3–6 months: reduce the
    dose or switch — bupropion or mirtazapine (or an SNRI).

 Surgical Treatment
  • Reversible structural causes such as clitoral phimosis may be treated surgically.

   KEY POINTS: FEMALE ORGASMIC DISORDER
    • Exclude medical, pharmacological and psychosocial causes before diagnosis.
    • A comprehensive history of nature and onset is essential.
    • FOD often accompanies interest and arousal problems.
    • Education is the hallmark of all FOD treatment.
    • Psychosocial options: directed masturbation, sensate focus, coital alignment, mindfulness.
    • Hormonal treatment for FOD needs further safety and efficacy research.

 Concept — FOD is a diagnosis of exclusion anchored in distress — inadequate stimulation, partner
 dysfunction, drugs and disease must go first, and coital anorgasmia alone is normal variation — so
 treatment splits on whether arousal is intact: if not, treat arousal; if so, increase stimulation.

 CLINICAL PRESENTATION

 Menopause and Dyspareunia
  • Sexual activity continues into old age. Vulvovaginal atrophy needing treatment affects a substantial
    minority of postmenopausal women, and many with atrophy report dyspareunia.
  • Symptoms: dryness, burning, poor lubrication, pain, impaired function; urgency, dysuria, recurrent UTI.
    Current term: genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM).
  • Menopausal dyspareunia is often vestibular — atrophy more marked in the vestibule than the vagina.

 Previous Genitourinary Surgery
  • Synthetic mesh in POP surgery is under scrutiny for life-changing complications: erosion, pain,
    infection, bleeding, dyspareunia, organ perforation, urinary problems — possibly years later; most
    RCTs lack medium- and long-term safety data. Detailed discussion online and in Chapter 119.

 Hysterectomy
  • Commonest pelvic operation in women, for benign or malignant indications; sexual function is often
    not assessed afterwards.
  • Subtotal hysterectomy: most unchanged or improved, a minority deteriorate, attributed to vaginal
    sensory loss and the indication for surgery; clitoral thermal and vibratory sensation unchanged.
  • Radical hysterectomy at 6 months: lubrication distress and dyspareunia commonest; most women
    active before the cancer diagnosis are active again at 12 months, at lower frequency. Ovarian cancer
    surgery gives similar rates.
  • Abdominal versus vaginal approach for simple hysterectomy makes no difference to postoperative
    sexual function.

 Pelvic Organ Prolapse Repair
  • Concomitant hysterectomy ± cervical resection is controversial. Native-tissue vaginal repair: function
    improves in about half, unchanged in over a third, worse in a minority.
  • Transvaginal mesh gives sexual function comparable to native tissue (two systematic reviews); vaginal
    length does not affect sexuality; persistent dyspareunia is linked to anterior vaginal mesh versus
    laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy; dyspareunia higher after vaginal than abdominal repair.

 Cystectomy
  • Sexuality-determining factors: (1) preserve nerves along the lateral vaginal walls; (2) avoid clitoral
    devascularisation by preserving a distal urethral stump; (3) vaginal reconstruction may alter canal
    dimensions.
  • Sexuality-preserving technique: urethral transection at the bladder neck (clitoral vasculature) +
    retrograde dissection of bladder off vagina (neurovascular bundle). Reconstruction orientation affects
    vaginal tightness. Diversion type does not change sexual outcome.
  • Genital-sparing cystectomy, when oncologically possible: earlier return to sexual activity (~6 vs 14
    weeks) and better function.
  • Counselling gap: far more patients want information than ask — the onus is on the clinician; a third of
    cancer patients wished for counselling and sex-therapy or partner referral; after benign simple
    cystectomy, most were not counselled preoperatively and almost none asked about sex at follow-up.

 Genital Cosmetic Surgery and Female Sexual Dysfunction
  • Procedures: labiaplasty, clitoral hood reduction, hymenoplasty, perineoplasty, labia majora
    augmentation, vaginoplasty, G-spot amplification. Rising interest, limited evidence — no standard
    nomenclature, few functional, psychosexual or complication data.

  • Pathway: screen for body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) → refer if psychological concerns → surgeon

    wanting alteration does not mean she is abnormal.
  • Surgeons need sexual-medicine training to withhold surgery in sexual dysfunction, mental
    impairment or BDD, and surgical expertise to deliver satisfaction.

 Introduction
  • LUTS: the subjective indicator of disease or change as perceived by patient, carer or partner.
    International Consultation on Incontinence–Research Society (ICI-RS) 2017 think tank addressed LUTS
    and sexual function. 5th International Consultation on Incontinence (2012): LUTS in nearly half of
    adults ≥20 years, OAB about one in ten, urinary incontinence (UI) fewer, severe stress urinary
    incontinence (SUI) a small minority.

 Sexual Lifestyle and Communication
  • Sexual health: physical, emotional, mental and social well-being in relation to sexuality, not merely
    absence of disease. Three National Surveys of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles (Natsal) show major
    change; in women age reduces frequency and range.

 Epidemiology
  • About 40% of women have a lifetime sexual problem, but only ~8–12% a distressing, diagnosable
    disorder (IUGA and International Continence Society (ICS) joint report; Shifren et al., 2008).
  • Pelvic floor disorders (PFDs) and sexual function: debated (heterogeneity), but generally harmful —
    dyspareunia, low arousal, infrequent orgasm; up to nearly half of women with UI/LUTS have sexual
    dysfunction, pain and HSDD most frequent; function tracks body image and POP bother, especially
    stage ≥2, in desire and satisfaction; chronic pelvic pain (CPP) causes dyspareunia and avoidance.
  • Pelvic floor strength correlates with better function; high tone with dysfunction. Treating PFDs often
    improves sexual function.

 Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms and Female Sexual Dysfunction: Links and Treatments
 Urinary Incontinence and Overactive Bladder
  • A quarter to a half of women with UI have FSD; all UI forms affect all phases and domains. Burden:
    concealment strategies, apathy, guilt, markedly more major depression, threatened femininity, shame,
    isolation. Main drivers: low libido and self-esteem from fear of leakage; severe UI roughly doubles
    odds of low libido, dryness and dyspareunia.
  • OAB is contradictory: EpiLUTS shows association; in a large treatment study only a minority reported
    sexual impact, and OAB did not predict loss of interest in women (unlike men); tolterodine
    (immediate and extended release) improved desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm and satisfaction for
    up to 6 months.
  • Pairings: HSDD with SUI; orgasm problems with OAB and urgency urinary incontinence (UUI); SUI or
    OAB → poorer quality of life (King's Health Questionnaire), satisfaction and marital relations.
  • Severity of FSD by LUTS type: bladder pain syndrome (BPS) > urodynamic detrusor overactivity (DO) >
    UUI and mixed urinary incontinence (MUI) > SUI > dry OAB > voiding LUTS.
  • Mechanisms: fear of leakage, coital UI at orgasm, interrupting intercourse to void, postcoital urgency,
    dyspareunia, PFD.

   KEY POINTS: LOWER URINARY TRACT DYSFUNCTION AND FEMALE SEXUAL DYSFUNCTION
    • Almost half of women with UI and/or LUTS have sexual dysfunction.
    • Sexual symptoms coexist with UI, POP and pelvic pain.
    • UI harms sexual function through libido and self-esteem.
    • Midurethral slings can cause FSD through neurovascular injury or de novo dyspareunia.
    • Treating UI medically or surgically can improve many sexual domains.
    • Sexually active women with POP may have lower satisfaction, less frequent intercourse and
      difficulty reaching orgasm by masturbation.

 Concept — LUTD affects female sexual function mostly indirectly — fear, leakage, pain, self-image —
 which is why curing coital incontinence predicts sexual recovery; where the damage is direct (TVT
 denervation, pelvic floor hypertonicity, pudendal dysfunction reversed by SNM) it points to a shared
 neurovascular substrate, as with male LUTS and ED.

 CONCLUSIONS
  • Female sexual function and dysfunction are integral to urological practice; urologists must recognise
