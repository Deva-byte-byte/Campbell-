COMBINATION THERAPY

Antimuscarinics and β3-Agonists
 • Different mechanisms of action → rationale for combining an antimuscarinic with a β3-adrenoceptor
   agonist in overactive bladder (OAB) and detrusor overactivity (DO), particularly when the chosen
   antimuscarinic dose gives intolerable adverse events (AEs) or insufficient symptom improvement.
 • Solifenacin 10 mg plus mirabegron 50 mg reduced urgency episodes more than solifenacin 5 mg; the
   combination roughly doubled constipation against solifenacin 10 mg alone, with no difference in heart
   rate, blood pressure or electrocardiogram.

Antimuscarinics With Vibegron
 • Benefit extrapolated from mirabegron; direct evidence limited, validation awaited.
 • Two-part phase IIb study: part 1 → vibegron 3, 15, 50 or 100 mg, tolterodine ER 4 mg (positive control) or
   placebo for 8 weeks, or vibegron/tolterodine 50/4 mg for 4 weeks → vibegron 50 mg for 4 weeks; part 2
   → vibegron 100 mg, tolterodine 4 mg, the 100/4 mg combination or placebo for 4 weeks.
 • Vibegron/tolterodine 100/4 mg → greater fall in daily micturitions and urgency than either monotherapy;
   numerically greater fall in urgency incontinence (UUI) in wet OAB.

 KEY POINTS: COMBINATION OF ANTIMUSCARINICS AND Β3-AGONISTS
   • Different mechanisms → combination is useful in OAB/DO, especially with intolerable AEs or
     insufficient response to the antimuscarinic.
   • Solifenacin fails → add mirabegron: efficacy and safety well documented; other antimuscarinics with
     mirabegron not investigated.
   • Mirabegron first line fails → add an antimuscarinic: effective and safe (solifenacin 5 mg, propiverine
     20 mg, imidafenacin 0.2 mg, tolterodine ER 4 mg).
   • No trial of antimuscarinic–vibegron coadministration after failure of either monotherapy.


Concept — The evidence is directional and drug-specific: mirabegron on solifenacin and an antimuscarinic
on mirabegron are each proven, and nothing else is. Against a dose-escalated antimuscarinic, the
combination gains tolerability, not efficacy.

α-Blockers and Desmopressin
 • α-blocker plus desmopressin → consider for nocturia resistant to first-line treatment.
 • About 30% of men aged ≥60 years with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) have nocturnal polyuria (NP)
   (phase IV study), a cause of nocturia that α-blockade does not address.
 • α-blocker-resistant BPH: oral desmopressin → most patients achieve a ≥50% fall in nocturnal voids (two
   non-placebo-controlled studies).
 • Low-dose oral desmopressin 0.1 mg in men with BPH aged ≥65 years, over 12 months → fewer nocturnal
   voids and lower nocturnal urine volume.
 • No placebo control → placebo contribution unknown.

Botulinum Toxin A and Antimuscarinics
 • Neurogenic DO (NDO) from spinal cord injury (SCI) or multiple sclerosis (MS). In the pooled DIGNITY
   studies, onabotulinumtoxinA (onabotA) 200 U reduced UI equally in anticholinergic users and non-users,
   with:
     ◦ identical proportions achieving 50% and 100% UI reduction
     ◦ similar rises in maximum cystometric capacity and falls in maximum detrusor pressure
     ◦ similar Incontinence Quality of Life (I-QOL) scores.
 • Unanswered: whether prior partial anticholinergic responders gain from combination.
 • EMBARK (idiopathic OAB, onabotA 100 U) enrolled only anticholinergic-refractory patients → no user vs
   non-user comparison.
 • In practice, anticholinergics or β3-agonists are likely used to prolong the tail of onabotA 100 U —
   unevaluated.

Vitamin D3 Receptor Agonists
 • Inverse association between vitamin D3 and LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms): higher levels ↔
   higher urinary flow; lower levels ↔ UI, independent of ethnicity and age. In men, low vitamin D ↔
   higher OABSS in winter only.
 • Elocalcitol (formerly BXL-628): no effect on Rho kinase expression; delayed bladder-strip contraction;
   increased L-type Ca2+ channel expression (potentially enhancing contraction).
     ◦ Animal models: improved bladder function after acetic acid, bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) and
       cyclophosphamide cystitis.
     ◦ Maxacalcitol reduced retinyl-acetate-induced DO.

Calcium Antagonists
 • Ca2+ channels set smooth muscle tone via free intracellular calcium. Voltage-gated channels (Q- and L-
   type), not store-operated channels, are implicated in the bladder.
 • L-type evidence: inhibitors block human bladder contraction in vitro; knockout mice have impaired
   contractility.
 • Clinical paradox: despite decades of cardiovascular use, no major reports of impaired bladder
   contractility → no clinical evidence for calcium channel blockers in storage.

Potassium Channel Openers
 • K+ channel opening → hyperpolarisation → smooth muscle relaxation; many K+ channel types regulate
   bladder tone.
 • K+ channels also regulate cardiomyocytes → the class has probably been tested at excessively low doses
   in OAB to avoid cardiovascular AEs.
 • Flupirtine data support developing KCNQ agents without liver toxicity.
 • Big potassium (BK, maxi-K) channel, large-conductance and voltage- and calcium-activated, is highly
   expressed on bladder smooth muscle → target of gene therapy (URO-902).

α-AR and β-AR Agonists
 • α-AR agonists aim to increase urethral closing power in SUI; systemic agents lack urethral α-AR
   selectivity → the cardiovascular toxicity in the table. β-AR agonists: positive studies never followed up.

Botulinum Toxin Subtype B
 • Dose escalation 2,500–15,000 U at 10 bladder sites (women with OAB): longest effect only at the highest,
   poorly tolerated doses → short duration at safe doses limits usefulness.
Concept — Failure is mostly a problem of selectivity or therapeutic window, not a lack of bladder effect.
Calcium antagonists, K+ channel openers and α-agonists all act on bladder or urethra, but the same targets in
heart and vessels cap the tolerable dose.
