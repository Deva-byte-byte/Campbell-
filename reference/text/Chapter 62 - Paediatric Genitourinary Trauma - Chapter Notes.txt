Chapter 62 — Paediatric Genitourinary Trauma
RENAL INJURIES
Epidemiology
 • The kidney is the commonest site of paediatric genitourinary (GU) trauma.
 • The child's kidney is thought more vulnerable (table below); a more pliable spine also allows more
   ureteric stretch injury. Whether children are in fact more susceptible remains unproven.
 • A minority of children with blunt trauma — roughly 5–20% — have a renal injury.
 • National trauma registry data (United States and Japan): about one-quarter of all renal injuries occur
   before 19 years of age; these are overwhelmingly blunt and mostly grade 1–3. Mean age is 14 years;
   children under 5 years, and especially infants, are a small minority.
 • Open surgery rises steeply with grade: a minority of grade 4 and about half of grade 5 injuries underwent
   open operation in United States data; in Japanese data nephrectomy occurred only in grade 4–5, never in
   grade 1–3.
 • Minimally invasive treatment (angioembolisation, ureteral stent) is uncommon overall and more frequent
   in grade 4–5; angiography is used more liberally in Japan than in the United States.
 • Nephrectomy is rare — about 3–5% of admissions — but more likely when children are managed in
   adult rather than paediatric trauma units.
 • Causes: motor vehicle and motorcycle collisions, falls, bicycle accidents and sport; all-terrain vehicle
   injuries may be increasing.
 • Congenital anomalies such as hydronephrosis and ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) obstruction are found
   three to five times more often in children screened by computed tomography (CT) for trauma than in
   adults. Whether an anomaly raises the risk of injury is unclear; injured anomalous kidneys usually sustain
   low-grade injury.

Presentation
 • Blunt trauma predominates: motor vehicle collisions, falls, sport, pedestrian versus vehicle, all-terrain
   vehicle and motorcycle collisions.
 • Other organ injuries accompany about half of blunt renal trauma — liver, spleen, head, orthopaedic.
 • Rapid deceleration (road collision, fall from height) is the historical feature associated with renal injury.
 • Examination: flank tenderness or ecchymosis in severe injury; rib fractures; tachycardia and hypotension
   with major blood loss.
 • Hypotension is late and unreliable in children (table below) — a widely cited paradigm resting on limited
   data. Absence of haematuria does not exclude significant injury.
 • The urologist is usually consulted after the diagnosis is made; the decision to image lies with emergency
   and trauma physicians.

INDICATIONS FOR RENAL IMAGING
 • Adult triggers: gross haematuria, or microscopic haematuria with hypotension (systolic blood pressure <
   90 mm Hg). Highly sensitive for blunt and penetrating injury but poorly specific — most patients meeting
   them have no identifiable GU injury.
 • In children both triggers are weaker: up to two-thirds with a grade ≥ 2 injury may have a normal
   urinalysis; hypotension is late.
 • Mechanism is therefore a central predictor in children: significant deceleration or high-velocity injury —
   fall from more than 10 feet (about 3 m), high-speed motor vehicle collision, or a blow to abdomen or
   flank from a foreign object.
 • Associated injuries that predict GU injury: thoracic or intra-abdominal organ injury; fractures of ribs,
   spine, femur or pelvis.
 • Four criteria in children — haematuria, hypotension, associated injuries, mechanism — give more than
   98% sensitivity for significant GU injury.
 • Penetrating trauma: every abdominal gunshot wound requires GU evaluation; stab wounds to the flank,
   upper abdomen or lower chest require renal imaging.
 • American Urological Association (AUA) Urotrauma Guideline and European Association of Urology (EAU)
   Guidelines on Urological Trauma endorse the criteria in Table 62.1, adding associated abdominal injury.

markers fail precisely in children.

IMAGING STUDIES FOR DIAGNOSIS
As Low as Reasonably Achievable
 • CT for blunt abdominal trauma is ubiquitous and probably overused for mild trauma in children.
 • CT radiation carries a very small absolute increase in later cancer (leukaemia, brain tumours), greater
   the younger the child at scanning → ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) must govern both
   diagnostic and follow-up imaging.

Focused Assessment With Sonography for Trauma
 • Focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST): sensitivity for renal injury highly variable and
   often low (reported range 22–96%).
 • Why: performed by emergency clinicians to look for free fluid in dependent regions, not to examine
   organs.
 • A normal FAST plus normal serial physical examination over 24 hours reliably excludes clinically
   significant renal or intra-abdominal injury.
 • None of the studies was paediatric-specific.

Dedicated Renal Ultrasonography
 • Little studied against CT in children or adults.
 • Bottom line in stable children: missed injuries have been grade 1 and insignificant; focused and colour
   Doppler studies reliably detected grade 3–5, and every grade 3–5 injury had gross haematuria or ≥ 50
   red blood cells per high-power field (RBC/hpf).
 • Contrast-enhanced ultrasonography is used by some centres for diagnosis and follow-up; prospective
   comparative studies are lacking.
 • AUA: ultrasound may be used initially, CT preferred; EAU Guidelines on Paediatric Urology: ultrasound
   to screen, CT to diagnose and stage.

COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY
 • In the haemodynamically stable patient, CT is the most sensitive and specific test to diagnose and
   exclude GU trauma.
 • AUA and EAU: intravenous (IV) contrast-enhanced CT of abdomen and pelvis with immediate and
   delayed phases.
 • Children often receive a single contrast phase — to limit radiation, or from unfamiliarity with the
   recommendation.
 • Cost of a single phase: reduced accuracy for urinary extravasation and isolated ureteral injury, especially
   with a urinoma or deep or multiple lacerations.
 • Counterpoint: in the era of non-operative management, whether a routine delayed phase changes
   outcome in children is uncertain.

CLASSIFICATION AND GRADING OF RENAL INJURIES
The American Association for the Surgery of Trauma Grading System
 • The American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) renal Organ Injury Scale (OIS), 1989, is the
   most widely used; a proposed revision in 2011 aimed to clarify high-grade (4–5) injuries.
 • Grade predicts intervention and complications in adults and children.
 • Because intervention, complications and loss of function are negligible below grade 4, grade 1–3 = low
   grade, grade 4–5 = high grade.

 • Within grade 4–5, specific CT findings predict intervention:
     ◦ Medial contrast extravasation → possible UPJ disruption.
     ◦ No contrast in the ureter with a grade 4 injury → later intervention for urine leak (paediatric series).

Blunt Versus Penetrating
 • The distinction determines imaging and exploration.
 • Any significant penetrating injury to abdomen, flank or lower chest → GU evaluation, even if stable and
   without haematuria.
 • Unstable, or laparotomy needed for other injuries → inspect retroperitoneum and ureters, with high
   suspicion for renal or ureteral injury, especially with high-velocity gunshot wounds.

Management
 • Most paediatric renal trauma, including high-grade injury, is managed non-operatively; initial non-
   operative management is a strong recommendation of the EAU paediatric and Pediatric Trauma Society
   guidelines.
 • Grade 1–3: the vast majority need no intervention, let alone open surgery.
 • Grade 4–5: higher risk, but most still need no intervention. Interventions are usually endoscopic or
   percutaneous — percutaneous nephrostomy, ureteral stent, percutaneous drain, angioembolisation.

Nonoperative
 • Numerous adult and several paediatric series confirm safety and efficacy. Nephrectomy rates are higher
   with surgical exploration than with non-operative management, in adults and children.
 • Eligibility: haemodynamically stable, or resuscitated adequately and remaining stable.
 • Ideal candidate: grade 1–3 blunt injury, no associated injury requiring surgery, haemodynamically stable.
 • Grade 4–5 injuries have been managed non-operatively with success in several centres; non-operative
   management of penetrating renal trauma also has favourable outcomes.
Nonoperative Management Protocol
 • Sequence: confirm haemodynamic stability → grade the injury → bed rest → serial haemoglobin/
   haematocrit → serial abdominal examination → bed rest until gross haematuria resolves and blood
   counts are stable.
 • Grading imaging: AUA and EAU state dedicated renal ultrasonography is an option in children, but
   contrast CT abdomen and pelvis with immediate and delayed phases is preferred. In future, ultrasound
   may suffice to exclude significant retroperitoneal haematoma, deep laceration or urinoma and so avoid CT
   in stable grade 1–3 injury.
 • Intensity scales with severity:
     ◦ Grade 1–2, no gross haematuria, stable haemoglobin → 23-hour observation with two blood tests.
     ◦ Grade 4 with large perirenal haematoma, vascular extravasation and ongoing gross haematuria →
       intensive care, bloods every 4–6 hours, possible transfusion and angioembolisation.
Early Repeat Imaging
 • Guidelines diverge (table below): AUA and EAU Urological Trauma favour repeat CT for high grade; EAU
   Paediatric favours ultrasound first.
 • Adults (two large studies and a systematic review): early repeat imaging may be unnecessary even in high
   grade, if asymptomatic and progressing well.
 • Children with grade 3–5 injury: no difference in complications, delayed procedures or readmissions with
   or without early repeat imaging; symptoms, not imaging, predicted complications and delayed
   procedures.
 • Repeat CT offers no clear advantage over ultrasound in a stable child. Authors: ultrasound at 48–72
   hours for high grade; CT only if ultrasound is inadequate or symptoms concern. Even routine 48-hour
   ultrasound in well grade 4–5 is questioned.

Outcomes of Nonoperative Management
 • Lower nephrectomy rate with non-operative than operative management, in adult and paediatric series.
 • The vast majority of children are managed non-operatively. Intervention risk is significantly higher in
   grade 4–5 and penetrating injuries, but most stable patients succeed non-operatively.
Management of Complications in Nonoperative Patients
 • Complication risk rises with grade.
 • Grade 1–2, and grade 3 without devitalised segments: complication risk close to zero.
 • Grade 3 with devitalised segments to grade 5 (stable patients): about 25% bleeding requiring
   angioembolisation, about 15% urinoma requiring intervention, and only about 5% open exploration for a
   complication beyond percutaneous or endoscopic rescue.
Persistent Urine Leak
 • Grade 1–3 by definition do not enter the collecting system; grade 4–5 can leak.
 • Commonest leak site: the parenchymal laceration; also renal pelvis rupture, ureteral injury or UPJ
   disruption.
 • AUA and EAU: renal injury with urine leak is managed non-operatively initially.
 • Always evaluate the renal pelvis and ureter for UPJ disruption or pelvic rupture — delayed-phase CT or
   retrograde pyelography.
 • Up to 85% of urine leaks resolve spontaneously; the rest need percutaneous urinoma drainage or
   diversion by nephrostomy and/or ureteral stent.
 • Foley catheter is often placed for 2–3 days to drain the bladder — benefit for speed or likelihood of
   resolution unproven.
 • IV antibiotics (typically cefazolin) are common with a leak — no evidence that they reduce infection;
   practice varies.
Indications for Intervention for Urine Leak or Urinoma
 • Classic triad of symptomatic persistent urinoma: ipsilateral flank pain, ileus, low-grade fever.
 • Shared decision-making with the family rather than a strict algorithm: very large, symptomatic urinoma
   → early intervention; small, mildly symptomatic urinoma → longer observation, which may avoid
   intervention.

Ureteral Stent
 • Authors' sequence: Foley kept 1–2 days after urinoma symptoms resolve → ultrasound for urinoma size
   → remove Foley → observe at least one further day for recurrence → stent removed at 4–6 weeks,
   considering ultrasound first.
 • IV cefazolin while the Foley is in place, and prophylactic antibiotics while the stent is in place, are common
   but unsupported by good evidence.
Percutaneous Drain
 • Fig. 62.6 Grade 4 laceration, fever and pain at day 2 with persistent extravasation → drain alone, no Foley
   → resolved in 3 days, removed without anaesthesia.
 • Sequence: drain (discuss a Foley as an adjunct) → follow output → remove when output is minimal and
   ultrasound shows resolution. Ultrasound after symptoms settle is of unclear necessity but usual.
Nephrostomy Tube
 • Rarely used alone — stent or drain preferred first because nephrostomy risks renal injury and bleeding;
   with an antegrade stent it resolves most leaks, and it suits the child who cannot be placed in lithotomy.

Angioembolization of Isolated Renal Segment
 • Segment not in continuity with the collecting system, leaking despite stent, nephrostomy and drain →
   embolise it. Very rare.
Renal Exploration With Repair of Urine Leak
 • Rare; only after prolonged observation and failed minimally invasive procedures. Sequence: debride
   devitalised tissue → close collecting system with absorbable suture → repair parenchyma over a bolster →
   drain with ureteral stent and perinephric drain. Counsel about renal loss or nephrectomy.
Concept — Non-operative management is the default at every grade; grade and the high-risk CT criteria set
only the intensity of observation and the likelihood of a minimally invasive rescue. Urine is diverted from the
least invasive route outward — stent, drain, nephrostomy, embolisation of an orphan segment, open repair
last — and absent distal ureteric contrast is the one finding that forces early retrograde study.

PERSISTENT OR DELAYED BLEEDING
Indications for Angioembolization
 • About 25% of grade 3–5 injuries managed non-operatively develop persistent or delayed bleeding
   needing intervention. Predictors are the high-risk criteria: haematoma size, vascular extravasation, medial
   laceration.

 • Superselective angioembolisation succeeds in more than 80% of persistent and delayed bleeds, in adults
   and children; a failed first attempt can be repeated successfully.
 • Performed by interventional radiology; aim: embolise the least viable parenchyma that stops the
   bleeding. Some functional loss is inevitable, depending on the injury and the procedure.
 • Retained differential function: grade 4 about 35–45%; grade 5 about 10–30%.
 • Complications:
     ◦ Postembolisation syndrome — self-limiting flank pain, fever, possible ileus; < 10%; resolves in 3–4
       days.
     ◦ Persistent fever → suspect postembolisation abscess → repeat CT → drain any concerning collection.
     ◦ Persistent or recurrent bleeding in a minority → repeat embolisation if stable → exploration if
       bleeding persists after repeat embolisation.

Renal Exploration
 • Needed in a small percentage of non-operatively managed patients with persistent or delayed bleeding;
   these patients are usually more stable than those explored at presentation.
 • Goals, in order: control renal vasculature → expose the whole injury → debride devitalised tissue → close
   collecting system defects → close parenchyma over a bolster → drain.

 KEY POINTS: NONOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT AND COMPLICATIONS DURING NONOPERATIVE
 MANAGEMENT
   • Non-operative management for most, including grade 4–5 (higher intervention risk).
   • Bleeding in about 25% of grade 3–5 → superselective angioembolisation, repeatable.
   • About 15% of urine leaks need a stent and/or drain.
   • Repeat CT at 48–72 h: not for well grade 1–3; optional, of unclear value, in well grade 4–5.

Concept — Renal bleeding is solved endovascularly: superselective embolisation is first line for persistent
and delayed haemorrhage alike and is repeated before surgery is contemplated — its price is a measured
loss of parenchyma, larger the higher the grade.

OPERATIVE
Indications
 • Primary indication: persistent haemodynamic instability from renal bleeding.
 • Pulsatile or expanding retroperitoneal haematoma at laparotomy (no prior imaging) → explore the
   kidney.
 • Renal bleeding uncontrolled by selective angioembolisation.
 • Stable, non-pulsatile haematoma (inadequately staged, or known devitalised segments): once explored
   after penetrating trauma; now left alone, even after gunshot wounds — nephrectomy rates may be
   lower, and high-grade paediatric injury tolerates non-operative care. EAU agrees; if another organ
   obviously explains the instability, avoid renal exploration.

One-Shot Intraoperative Intravenous Pyelography or Ultrasonography
 • Purpose: confirm a contralateral kidney when preoperative imaging has not done so.
 • Technique: 2 mL/kg IV contrast bolus → single radiograph at 10–15 minutes.
 • It shows only whether a contralateral kidney is present; it does not grade the injury. It informs
   nephrectomy versus reconstruction.
 • Intraoperative Doppler ultrasonography is an alternative (presence and perfusion of the contralateral
   kidney) but less reported and less recommended.

Control of Renal Vasculature
 • Early vascular control: incision in the posterior mesentery medial to the inferior mesenteric vein, over
   the aorta → vessel loops around renal vessels, tightened if needed.
 • Retrospective series suggested early control lowered nephrectomy rates; the one randomised trial found
   no difference in nephrectomy and longer operating time with early control.
 • Sensible in the unstable patient with an expanding haematoma; AUA and EAU guidelines do not address
   it.

Reconstruction Versus Nephrectomy
 • Goals: stop life-threatening haemorrhage and salvage the kidney whenever feasible and safe (EAU).

 • Sequence: confirm contralateral kidney → open Gerota fascia → control main vessels → expose entire
   injury → debride → oversew bleeding vessels and close collecting system (absorbable) → small defects:
   capsular sutures; large: close over haemostatic bolster or omentum (authors: sliding-clip renorrhaphy).
 • Nephrectomy if: the injury is irreparable or would leave a very small remnant; or the patient is unstable
   — hypothermia, coagulopathy, ongoing blood loss — and speed saves life.
 • Penetrating (especially high-velocity gunshot) injury: inspect and repair the ipsilateral ureter.

Renal Arterial Laceration
 • The kidney lacks good collateral arterial flow, so segments devitalised by arterial injury are not
   salvageable.
 • Avulsed or significantly bleeding main renal artery → unstable → explore expecting nephrectomy;
   repair attempts carry poorer outcomes.
 • Exception: solitary kidney or bilateral arterial injury → attempt repair, with vascular surgery if available.

Renal Arterial Occlusion
 • Complete or partial occlusion after blunt trauma from renal artery embolism or an intimal flap.
 • Observation has succeeded; expect post-infarction syndrome (fever, flank pain) and follow for
   hypertension.
 • Nephrectomy for uncontrolled pain or infection during observation.
 • Percutaneous revascularisation (angioplasty and/or endovascular stent) has salvaged some completely
   or partially occluded kidneys.

Ureteropelvic Junction Disruption
 • Rare but important not to miss.
 • Mechanism: high-velocity acceleration–deceleration and torso hyperextension — the mobile kidney is
   displaced away from the fixed proximal ureter.
 • Associated injuries: Chance fracture (flexion–distraction injury: anterior vertebral body compression with
   a transverse fracture through the posterior vertebra) and other severe injuries, such as aorta and
   duodenum.
 • Associated injuries often prompt laparotomy without preoperative imaging; haematuria is usually
   present but absent in a significant minority → diagnosis is often delayed → retroperitoneal abscess,
   fistula, infected urinoma.
 • Delayed clues: persistent postoperative fever, ileus, flank pain, urine in intra-abdominal drains, enlarging
   urinoma → repeat CT.
 • Classic CT findings: medial extravasation near the UPJ, no contrast in the distal ureter, no parenchymal
   laceration. A laceration does not exclude UPJ disruption. Any suspicion → retrograde pyelography.

 • Duplex kidneys, fusion anomalies, hydronephrosis, hydroureter and UPJ obstruction are occasionally found

Follow-Up and Long-Term Issues
 • At recovery, address follow-up imaging, residual function of the injured kidney, long-term risk of
   hypertension and chronic kidney disease (CKD), and activity.
Late Repeat Imaging and Assessment of Renal Function
 • Grade 1–2: scarring negligible — AUA does not recommend repeat imaging.
 • Grade 3: scarring in about 50–60%; grade 4–5: close to 100%, with some decline in differential function.
 • Options: CT at 3 months (some) or ultrasonography (others). Dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA) or
   mercaptoacetyltriglycine (MAG3) renography measures function but rarely changes management —
   usually reserved for suspected major loss of function or hypertension.
 • Authors (ALARA): ultrasonography 2–4 months after grade 3–5 injury; DMSA discussed for suspected
   functional loss (especially with hypertension), a very abnormal ultrasound, or after angioembolisation.
 • EAU: individualised radiological follow-up.
Hypertension
 • Hypertension developing or persisting 30 days after injury → consider trauma-related hypertension.
 • Uncommon after grade 1–2. Adults after grade ≥ 3: about 5–15%. Children: rare (a small percentage on
   systematic review); risk rises with higher grade and renal arterial injury.
 • Mechanisms: parenchymal ischaemia → renin; parenchymal compression → renin (Page kidney);
   arteriovenous fistula or pseudoaneurysm; significant scarring.
 • Pediatric Trauma Society: blood pressure checks at follow-up — frequency and duration undefined.
 • Workup: nephrology referral, ultrasonography, renal scan for function and scarring, magnetic resonance
   imaging (MRI) or CT if arteriovenous fistula is suspected.
 • Treatment: angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), since
   the hypertension is renin-mediated. Poorly functioning, scarred kidney after grade 4–5 injury or
   angioembolisation → consider nephrectomy with nephrology.
 • Page kidney — subcapsular collection compressing parenchyma — is rare; drainage, spontaneous
   resorption and nephrectomy have all resolved hypertension; long-term outcome unclear.

Chronic Flank Pain
 • Uncommon; not addressed in paediatric series.
 • Exclude a renal cause (stone, UPJ obstruction): urinalysis and culture, ultrasonography, CT, MRI,
   renography; consider musculoskeletal and other causes.
 • Pain management referral (medical treatment, nerve blocks) before nephrectomy, even for a poorly
   functioning kidney — pain may persist after nephrectomy.
Chronic Kidney Disease
 • A concern after nephrectomy, bilateral injury, or near-total loss of one kidney.
 • Hypertension and diabetes are the commonest causes of CKD in people with a solitary kidney →
   promote healthy lifestyle; refer if at risk (for example obesity).
 • Yearly blood pressure for a solitary kidney or a history of grade 3–5 injury; offer nephrology referral.
 • Loss of a kidney from trauma contributes to end-stage renal disease in about 0.5%.
 • Donor solitary kidney: possibly a slight rise in hypertension; effect on CKD unknown.
Follow-Up and Activities With a Solitary Kidney
 • Organised sport carries lower risk, and lower-grade injury, than motor vehicles or motorised sport (all-
   terrain vehicles, dirt bikes); but sport-related renal injury is more often isolated.
 • American Academy of Pediatrics: children with a normal solitary kidney may play sport, with protective
   padding for contact sport (hockey, football) — no study proves padding prevents renal injury.
 • Higher-risk activities: dirt-bike riding, cycling, all-terrain vehicles → counsel about risk; protective
   equipment.
 • Authors clear children with a normal solitary kidney for organised sport with padding for contact sport,
   and remind families that contact sport carries a higher risk of head injury.

 KEY POINTS: FOLLOW-UP AND LONG-TERM ISSUES
   • Scarring: none grade 1–2, about half grade 3, all grade 4–5.
   • Grade 3–5: ultrasound first, then CT, MRI or DMSA if needed.
   • Hypertension rare, after grade 3–5; CKD/ESRD very rare, even after nephrectomy.
   • Solitary kidney: sport allowed, padding for contact sport.

Concept — Late sequelae scale with grade: grade 1–2 need nothing, grade 3–5 need an ultrasound and
lifelong blood pressure checks, because scarring and renin-mediated hypertension — not renal failure — are
the realistic long-term risks.

URETERAL TRAUMA
Epidemiology
 • Rare: in adult series < 5% of penetrating trauma, very rare in blunt trauma.
 • Suspect with: penetrating injury near, or with trajectory towards, the ureter; multivisceral or vertebral
   injury; rapid deceleration.
 • Associations: penetrating → kidney, vascular, bowel and solid-organ injury; blunt → pelvic fracture.
   Mortality about 10%, driven by associated injury. Renal injury in about 20%.
 • Delayed or missed diagnosis → more nephrectomy and longer hospital stay.
 • Iatrogenic: open or laparoscopic rectal, colonic, urological or gynaecological surgery — suture ligation,
   crush, partial or complete transection, devascularisation, electrocautery; rarely ureteroscopy. Highest

       incidence in adults after gynaecological and colorectal surgery (about 1%); in children limited to case

Presentation
 • Blunt: usually with other injuries, including high-velocity pelvic fractures. Penetrating: entry near the

 • Haematuria is absent in 25–50%.
 • Penetrating ureteral injuries are mostly diagnosed intraoperatively — yet about 10% are missed at

   normal or merely contused at laparotomy, then necrose and leak days later → place a ureteral stent and
   drains around urinary structures if any leak is suspected.
 • Iatrogenic injury: diagnosis delayed in 50–60% — until urine in a surgical drain, urinary ascites, ileus,
   fever or sepsis.

 • Delayed-phase CT is the standard (AUA).
 • Complete transection: no contrast distal to the injury, with extravasation. Partial injury: some distal
   contrast, with extravasation.
 • Confirmation or diagnosis also by retrograde pyelography or intravenous pyelography.

 • Very proximal injury: as for UPJ disruption — spatulated, tension-free ureteropelvic anastomosis.

 • Ureteroureterostomy, or ureteral reimplantation with or without psoas hitch, over a stent, for most

 • Very rare. Options: long Boari flap, ileal ureter, autotransplantation, reconfigured bowel segment (Yang–

GENITAL INJURY IN THE PAEDIATRIC POPULATION
 • Most genital injuries are accidental, but abuse must always be assessed — penile or scrotal trauma in

 • About one in seven boys and one in three girls are sexually abused in childhood, mostly penetratively.
   Signs are not always obvious; involve social services to establish the safety of the home before discharge.
 • When abuse is suspected, exclude concomitant injury — especially to the anus and rectum, which often
   goes unnoticed.

Epidemiology
 • Testicular trauma is rare: < 5% of operations for acute scrotum.

Management
 • Penetrating, tunica vaginalis intact → washout and closure — often in the emergency department under

 • Series bottom line: bleeding the commonest complaint, straddle the commonest mechanism, labia
   mostly involved, urethra spared; ED sedation often sufficed, theatre rarely — more likely with
   uncontrolled bleeding, younger age, difficult examination or another procedure. Examination under
   anaesthesia or sedation allows cystoscopy, vaginoscopy and anoscopy (general surgeon).
Management
 • Straddle injuries usually cause minor labia minora trauma, heal well without scarring, and rarely need
   theatre.
 • Older girls with larger injuries — especially involving the hymen, or with perineal bleeding, haematoma
   or swelling — more often need operative repair.
 • External appearance may not reflect the severity of injury, or whether any trauma occurred.
 • In prospective follow-up of anogenital trauma (more than half from abuse among the girls), few had
   significant anatomical evidence of injury later — most heal without obvious scar; repair of the posterior
   fourchette acutely is recommended to improve healing of the introitus.
 • Urethral, bladder, anal and rectal injuries can be missed → examination under anaesthesia if abuse is
   strongly suspected.
 • Healing: superficial injuries (abrasions, contusions, superficial lacerations) heal rapidly; deep lacerations
   take several weeks; hymenal injuries heal rapidly, mostly without scar. Therefore a normal examination
   does not exclude sexual abuse.

 KEY POINTS: GENITAL INJURY
   • Suspect abuse; exclude anorectal injury.
   • Reduce adhesions → avoid most circumcision injury.
   • Ultrasound rupture → explore.
   • Straddle = most vaginal injury; deeper injury suggests penetration → abuse evaluation.

Concept — In genital trauma the mechanism is itself a diagnosis to be made: straddle injuries stay external,
so any injury reaching the hymen, vagina, anus or adjacent organs implies penetration and obliges an
abuse assessment — while rapid scarless healing means a normal examination never excludes abuse.
