PHYSIOLOGY OF PENILE ERECTION

Historical Contexts for Our Understanding of Erectile Physiology
 • Chapter epigraph (Leonardo da Vinci): the penis acts independently of its owner's will, rising freely even
   during sleep — the first hint of an involuntary, autonomic event.
 • c. 2000 BCE, Egyptian papyrus — oldest recorded description of erectile dysfunction (ED); two types:
   natural (inability to accomplish the sex act) and supernatural (evil charms and spells).
 • Hippocrates — ED among wealthy Scythians, ascribed to excessive horseback riding.
 • Aristotle — three nerve branches carry spirit and energy to the penis; erection produced by influx of air.
   Accepted until da Vinci.
 • Leonardo da Vinci — erection derived from penile blood flow, from the blood-filled erect penis of hanged
   men; writings unknown until the early 20th century.
 • Ambroise Paré (1585) — accurate anatomy (concentric coats of nerves, veins and arteries; two ligaments
   = corpora cavernosa; a urinary tract; four muscles) and the concept of blood rushing into the member
   with lust.
 • Dionis — stressed retention of blood; attributed it to muscular compression of proximal penile veins.
 • Hunter (1787) — venous spasm prevents outflow during erection.
 • 1970s — xenon washout and cavernosography during audiovisual sexual stimulation; technical limits
   gave conflicting results:

 • Resolution: Wagner was right — Doppler work (Lue) confirmed decreased outflow via venous coaptation.
 • 1980s — Doppler ultrasound (Lue et al., 1984–1986): erection is driven by increased arterial inflow
   (arterial dilation) plus decreased venous outflow (venous coaptation).
 • 1990s — nitric oxide (NO) and its synthesising enzymes, endothelial and neuronal nitric oxide synthase
   (eNOS, nNOS), identified as key regulators (Rajfer et al., 1992).
 • 1996 — antierectile activity of phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) identified (Boolell et al.) → 1998 — oral
   PDE5 inhibitors (PDE5Is) for ED (Goldstein et al.).
 • Later determinants: gap junctions, potassium and calcium channels, RhoA/Rho-kinase; and the role of
   tissue elasticity and fibrosis in erection and detumescence.
Concept — Theory moved from pneumatic to vascular to veno-occlusive to molecular, and each step
relocated the therapeutic target, ending at the cyclic guanosine monophosphate breakdown enzyme that
PDE5Is exploit.

Functional Anatomy of the Penis
 • Three cylinders: paired dorsal corpora cavernosa (pubic rami to tip) and a ventral corpus spongiosum
   encircling the urethra, beneath loose subcutaneous tissue and skin. All contain spongy vascular tissue
   able to hold large blood volumes.
 • Flaccid length and girth are set by corporal blood content, which is set by the degree of contraction of
   trabecular smooth muscle.
 • Length and girth vary widely; their relevance to satisfaction is subjective and variable.
 • Natural curvature varies: downward curvature during erection in up to one in seven men, and about one-
   quarter have an erect angle below horizontal.

Corpora Cavernosa, Corpus Spongiosum, and Glans Penis
 • Corpora cavernosa: paired expansile cylinders within the tunica albuginea. Crura arise separately from
   the undersurface of the puboischial rami, merge under the pubic arch and remain attached up to the
   glans.
 • Humans: intercorporeal connections present (incomplete septum) — versus dogs: septum completely
   separates the corpora.
 • Each corpus = interconnected vascular sinusoids, larger centrally and smaller peripherally, separated by
   smooth muscle trabeculae surrounded by elastic fibres, collagen and loose areolar tissue.
 • Spongiosum and glans: same structure, larger sinusoids.
 • Terminal cavernous nerves and helicine arteries are intimately associated with the corporal smooth
   muscle.
 • Flaccid: slow diffusion central → peripheral sinusoids; blood gases venous. Erect: rapid arterial entry to all
   sinusoids raises oxygen tension and pH.

Tunica Albuginea
 • Bilayered fibrous sheath giving flexibility, rigidity and tissue strength (Fig. 71.1).
 • Principal constituent fibrillar collagen — mostly type I, also type III; elastic fibres form an irregular
   latticed network interwoven with collagen (Fig. 71.2) and permit expansion and recoil.

Hemodynamics and Mechanism of Erection and Detumescence
 • Erection = sinusoidal relaxation + arterial dilation + venous compression Seven phases of arterial flow
   and intracavernous pressure described in animal experiments (Fig. 71.5).

Corpora Cavernosa
 • Cavernous smooth muscle and arteriolar/arterial smooth muscle are the key effectors. Flaccid: tonically
   contracted, admitting only a small arterial inflow.
 • Sequence: sexual stimulation → neurotransmitter release from cavernous nerve terminals → smooth
   muscle relaxation → arteriolar and arterial dilation → sinusoidal pooling compresses subtunical plexus
   (latent) → tunica expands; emissary veins compressed between inner circular and outer longitudinal
   layers (tumescent) → maximal occlusion, intracavernous pressure approximately 100 mm Hg (full
   erection) → reflex ischiocavernosus contraction, pressure several hundred mm Hg (rigid erection) →
   pressure above systolic reverses flow in the cavernous arteries.

 • Cavernous nerve drives phases 1–3; the pudendal (somatic) nerve drives phase 4.
 • Trap: arterial inflow is highest in the latent phase, not at full rigidity.
 • Angle of erection set by size and attachments — crura to puboischial rami; suspensory and fundiform
   ligaments to pubis. Large penis or loose suspensory ligament → points downward even at full rigidity.
 • Detumescence — three overlapping phases:
      ◦ Initial — transient pressure rise as trabecular smooth muscle contracts against still-closed emissary
        veins.
      ◦ Slow — slow pressure fall as emissary veins gradually reopen.
      ◦ Rapid — rapid fall as venous outflow capacity returns to baseline.

   occlusion, so pressure stays low (Table 71.1).

 KEY POINTS
   • Erection depends on increased arterial inflow and coaptation of venous structures.
   • The structure of the corporal bodies is essential to the erectile process.
   • Penile vascular anatomy varies substantially between men, which may matter for preservation of
     erection after pelvic surgery.

Concept — Rigidity is a pressure phenomenon produced by trapping, not by inflow: the corpora are a closed
high-pressure chamber because their tunica occludes outflow, whereas the spongiosum and glans are open
low-pressure shunts.

Smooth Muscle Physiology
Cytosolic Calcium and the Calcium Sensitization Pathway
 • Corporal smooth muscle is contracted at rest. Its myosin isoforms have both phasic and tonic
   characteristics.
 • Contractions are spontaneous or induced. Spontaneous phasic contractions depend on an endogenous
   pacemaker — a cytosolic Ca2+ oscillator periodically releasing Ca2+ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum;
   modulated by neurotransmitters and hormones.
 • Two master controls of contraction: cytosolic free Ca2+ and Rho-kinase signalling.

Cytosolic free calcium
 • Contraction: free Ca2+ binds calmodulin → conformational change, ↑ affinity for myosin light chain
   (MLC) kinase → MLC kinase activated → phosphorylates serine-19 of regulatory MLC20 → with
   adenosine triphosphate (ATP), actin activates myosin ATPase → cross-bridge cycling; ATP hydrolysis
   supplies the energy (Fig. 71.7).

Rho-kinase signalling pathway
 • MLCP inhibition enhances contraction by preventing MLC20 inactivation. Rho/Rho-kinase is a constituent
   of the calcium sensitisation pathway in penile smooth muscle (Fig. 71.9).
 • Sequence: agonist → RhoA dissociates from Rho-guanine dissociation inhibitor → Rho-kinase activated
   → phosphorylates the regulatory subunit of MLCP → phosphatase inhibited → contraction sustained.
 • Latch state: tonic contraction also relies on dephosphorylated myosin remaining bound to actin in the
   high-affinity state; calponin binds actin and myosin, stabilising cross-bridges and slowing detachment.
   Critical for basal tone.
 • Rat cavernous-nerve-injury model: Rho-kinase may drive fibroblast → myofibroblast transformation →
   fibrosis.

Cyclic guanosine monophosphate–signalling pathway
 • Nitric oxide — best-characterised cGMP activator. Small; diffuses into target cells; binds iron in heme or
   iron–sulfur complexes. Physiological receptor: soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC), whose heme moiety is
   essential for NO-induced vasorelaxation.
 • The NO–sGC–cGMP pathway mediates many endothelium-dependent vasodilators: histamine,
   oestrogens, insulin, corticotropin-releasing hormone, nitrovasodilators and ACh → NO is the molecule
   principally responsible for initiating erection.
 • NO synthase (NOS): L-arginine + O2 → L-citrulline + NO.

Caveolae
 • Invaginated plasma-membrane microdomains rich in eNOS and caveolins, cholesterol, sphingolipids,
   glycosylphosphatidylinositol-linked proteins; also seven-transmembrane receptors, G proteins, adenylyl
   cyclase, phospholipase C, protein kinase C, Ca2+ pumps and channels.
 • Rodents: erection appears to need association of sGC with endothelial caveolin-1. Caveolin-1 falls in
   aged rats, after bilateral cavernous nerve injury, and in diabetic mice.
 • Trap: in a fructose–streptozotocin diabetic rat model, caveolin-1 rose with reduced eNOS activity —
   relationship complex, species- and cause-dependent.

Molecular oxygen as a modulator of penile erection
 • Partial pressure of oxygen (Po2) — flaccid: venous level (~35 mm Hg); erect: ~90 mm Hg.

 • O2 is a NOS substrate with L-arginine: low flaccid O2 inhibits NO synthesis; the higher erect level induces
   it. Minimum cavernous Po2 for full NOS activity: 50–60 mm Hg.

   PG production.
 • Endothelin synthesis is promoted by low O2 and inhibited by high O2 (as in erection).

Intercellular communication
  • Gap junctions between adjacent smooth muscle cells pass ions (Ca2+) and second messengers. Major
   component connexin-43, a membrane-spanning protein. Most likely explanation for synchronised
   corporal relaxation and contraction.

Concept — Every relaxant pathway, however it starts, converges on lowering cytosolic Ca2+ or releasing
MLCP; the cyclic nucleotides differ in what makes them and what degrades them, not in what they do — and
oxygen itself acts as a switch that locks the flaccid state in constrictors and the erect state in NO.

Intracavernous Tissue Architecture and Erectile Response
 • Trabeculae provide structural support and regulation for endothelial-lined sinusoids and conduct vessels
   and nerves. Relaxation → sinusoidal filling; recoil → expulsion to emissary veins → flaccidity.
 • Decreased smooth muscle and elastin content → impaired erection.
 • Sonic hedgehog (SHH) — regulates organogenesis, cell division and some cancers; present in penis.

   vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), NOS and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF);

Psychosocial
 • A psychogenic component is common and possibly ubiquitous, as primary cause or secondary
   contributor. Hypothalamus, limbic system and cortex send stimulatory or inhibitory messages to spinal
   centres.
 • Two proposed mechanisms of inhibition: (1) direct CNS/spinal inhibition of erection; (2) excessive
   sympathetic activity or raised peripheral catecholamines ↑ smooth muscle tone and prevent
   relaxation.
 • CNS evidence: depressed animals have ↓ serum dopamine and penile dopamine receptors; nucleus
   accumbens dopamine agonist improves intromission and mount latency in stressed rodents. fMRI in
   psychogenic ED: ↓ activity in right anterior insula and right orbitofrontal cortex (abnormal cognitive and
   motivational processing); aberrant network connectivity linked to state anxiety. Clinical salience still
   ambiguous.
 • Peripheral evidence: sympathetic stimulation or systemic adrenaline detumesces the erect rodent penis.
   Chronic water-immersion stress: ↑ response to noradrenaline, ↑ cavernous Rho-kinase-1, ↓ smooth
   muscle-to-collagen ratio. Stress from adulthood worsened the ratio, stress since pre-puberty did not.
 • Men with psychogenic ED have higher serum noradrenaline than controls or men with vasculogenic ED.
 • Social factors: absent partner, partner conflict, differing expectations/mores by demographics (including
   racial identification), culture, beliefs and sexual orientation.
 • Pornography: self-reported problematic use is associated with ED, but use per se is not clearly linked —
   impact is individual.
 • Post-traumatic stress disorder ↑ ED risk. These social and mental-health factors act through psychogenic
   mechanisms.

Neurogenic
 • Erection is neurovascular: any disease, dysfunction or injury (including surgical) of brain, cord or
   peripheral erectogenic nerves (pudendal, cavernous) can cause ED. Neuropathy does not exclude other
   causes; confirming a neurogenic cause is difficult.
 • Implicated brain regions: caudate, dorsal anterior cingulate, anterior insula, prefrontal areas, median
   preoptic area, PVN, thalamus, hippocampus.
 • Central disorders: Parkinson disease, stroke, encephalitis, temporal lobe epilepsy, tumours, dementias
   (including Alzheimer disease), multiple system atrophy. Mechanism: insufficiency of dopaminergic and/
   or oxytocinergic pathways; oxytocin into the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis induces erection in rats
   (glutamatergic, nitrergic, dopaminergic).
 • Traumatic brain injury linked to ED; in American football players, concussion history showed a dose–
   response relationship with ED medication prescription.
 • Spinal lesions: SCI (commonest), spina bifida, disc herniation, syringomyelia, tumours, multiple sclerosis.
   Nature, level and extent dictate effect.

Endocrinologic
Androgens
 • Low serum testosterone (T) is frequent in ED. AUA 2018: measure T in all men with ED — morning,
   fasting (glucose and protein intake acutely suppress androgens).
 • Androgens act via the androgen receptor (AR); effects on libido and sexual behaviour well established. In
   low T, supplementation ↑ interest, ↑ erection and improves non-sexual quality of life.
 • Thresholds: T for normal nocturnal erections ~200 ng/dL; many men have symptoms (incl. loss of
   nocturnal erections) below ~320 ng/dL; androgen-deficiency symptoms become progressively more
   frequent as T falls.
 • AUA T guideline: biochemical low T = <300 ng/dL on a morning draw.
 • Free T — may predict ED where total T is borderline; controversial and not in AUA guidance.
 • AR CAG repeats (exon 1): longer repeats ↓ AR transcription; weak association with ED only in men with
   at least low-normal T (≥330 ng/dL); not replicated in all studies.
 • Low libido is the commonest sexual symptom of low T. Older men more often symptomatic (cause or
   effect unclear). Many men with low T are asymptomatic.
 • Endocrine Society and AUA: consider T only with at least two clearly low morning levels plus symptoms
   clearly attributable to low T.
 • Correlates of low T in ED: age, DM, high total cholesterol, anaemia, waist circumference, obesity (body
   mass index, BMI, ≥30 kg/m2).
 • Low T associates with ↑ fasting insulin, glucose and glycated haemoglobin in men who develop diabetes
   → hypogonadism may be a sentinel event for diabetes; androgen deficiency links to insulin resistance,
   type 2 DM, metabolic syndrome (MetS) and visceral fat. Visceral fat acts as an endocrine organ
   (inflammatory cytokines, endothelial dysfunction).
 • Mechanisms: T regulates NO/cGMP, RhoA/Rho-kinase, adrenergic tone and smooth muscle. Castration →
   ↓ arterial inflow, venous leak, ↓ nerve-evoked erection; ↓ smooth muscle, nNOS and eNOS; ↑

Vascular
 • Arteriogenic — atherosclerotic or traumatic occlusion of the hypogastric–cavernous–helicine tree → ↓
   perfusion pressure → longer time to maximal erection and ↓ rigidity. Usually part of generalised
   atherosclerosis.
      ◦ Risk factors: hypertension (HTN), hyperlipidaemia, tobacco, DM, blunt perineal/pelvic trauma, MetS,

      ◦ Penile Doppler abnormalities associate with tobacco use, vascular disease and major cardiac events.
      ◦ Focal stenosis of common penile or cavernous artery → young men after blunt pelvic or perineal
        trauma.
      ◦ Modifiable: tobacco cessation, weight loss, regular exertion improve erection (arteriogenic fitness).
 • Cardiovascular diseases — ED highly prevalent with HTN, hypercholesterolaemia, coronary artery disease
   (CAD), peripheral vascular disease. Five mechanistic links: (1) endothelial dysfunction (eNOS, oxidative
   stress); (2) smooth muscle defects (hyperplasia, altered contractility); (3) autonomic dysregulation
   (neuropathy, ↓ nNOS); (4) hormonal disruption (hypogonadism); (5) metabolic defects (hyperlipidaemia,
   advanced glycation end products).
      ◦ Artery-size hypothesis: penile arteries are smaller than coronary arteries, so endothelial dysfunction
        shows in the penis first. In men with CAD, ED preceded CAD in most cases by a mean of ~2 years.

   spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHR); RAS → NADPH oxidase → oxidative stress. Rho-kinase inhibition

Corporal Structural Defects
 • Failure to restrict venous outflow (veno-occlusive dysfunction) is one of the commonest causes of
   vasculogenic ED. Causes: degenerative tunical change, fibroelastic structural alteration, insufficient
   trabecular relaxation, venous shunts.
 • Tunica: degeneration (senescence, DM) or trauma (penile fracture) impairs subtunical and emissary vein
   compression. Peyronie surgery may disrupt the subtunical areolar layer or tunica.
 • Priapism shunt surgery → persistent glans–cavernosum or cavernosum–spongiosum shunting and
   venous leak; ED may also reflect priapism-related fibrosis.
 • Fibroelastic trabecular and smooth muscle alterations → venous leak; seen in vasculogenic and
   neurogenic ED and animal nerve-injury and vascular models.
 • Sonic hedgehog protein — regulates endothelial growth, angiogenesis, migration, neuronal
   differentiation and axon guidance. ↓ SHH after cavernous nerve injury and in DM → nerve degeneration,

   corporal apoptosis, ↓ smooth muscle. SHH replacement in nerve-injured rats restores erection, possibly
   via BDNF.
 • Endothelium — source of NO, EDHF, PGI2, hydrogen peroxide; mediates flow-mediated dilation;
   influences mitogenesis, platelet aggregation and neutrophil adhesion — via chemical mediators or
   myoendothelial gap junctions. Dysfunction is an early indicator of vascular disease. Mechanisms in ED:
   ↓ endothelial cell density; ↓ junction proteins (claudin-5, vascular endothelial–cadherin, platelet

Concept — Veno-occlusion is an emergent property of healthy tissue — an elastic tunica compressing veins
against fully relaxed trabeculae — so anything that stiffens the tunica, fibroses the trabeculae or prevents
relaxation presents as "venous leak".

Medication Induced
 • Medication is commonly associated with self-reported ED. Many conditions in the ED population
   (depression, DM, cardiovascular disease) themselves cause ED, and their treatment may add to it.
 • Some drugs affect desire, ejaculation or orgasm, which patients may report as "ED" or which may
   secondarily impair erection. Interpret self-reported and questionnaire drug-ED data with caution.
Antihypertensive agents
 • Almost all list ED as a side effect, but risk differs by class and some are beneficial. The underlying
   hypertensive disease may be the principal driver of ED in treated men.
 • Thiazide diuretics — principal action: inhibition of the Na–Cl cotransporter in the distal convoluted
   tubule (weak carbonic anhydrase inhibition causes cellular alkalinisation and vasodilation). Higher ED
   likelihood even versus other antihypertensives; mechanism unknown.
 • β-Adrenergic blockers — only ~10% of penile adrenoceptors are β; their stimulation mediates relaxation.
   In vitro this is attenuated by nonselective propranolol (possibly a prejunctional α2 effect) but not by
   cardioselective practolol.
      ◦ Nonselective (propranolol) → more ED than ACE inhibitors.
      ◦ Carvedilol (nonselective β plus α1-blocking vasodilation) → worsened sexual function.

      ◦ Nebivolol (selective β1 with NO-mediated vasodilation) → no reduction, sometimes improvement,
       versus metoprolol and atenolol.
 • α-Adrenoceptor blockers — α-adrenoceptors (especially the α1L subtype) are prevalent in penile vascular
   and cavernous tissue and promote contraction. α1 antagonists increase/prolong cavernous relaxation;
   prejunctional α2 modulates noradrenaline release. α-Blockers for HTN/benign prostatic hyperplasia
   (BPH), e.g. doxazosin, are not associated with ↑ ED.
 • α2 agonists (clonidine) ↓ erectile function by peripheral and central mechanisms.

 • ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) — no evident interfering action; given the RAS role, neutral to beneficial in most
   human studies; central ACEI enhanced centrally induced erections in diabetic rats.
 • AT1 receptor antagonists (angiotensin receptor blockers, ARBs; losartan, valsartan, candesartan) —
   reverse penile vascular structural change in hypertensive and ageing animals; seem to improve erectile
   function in humans. Valsartan versus carvedilol crossover: valsartan improved pre-existing dysfunction
   with no adverse sexual effect over 12 months; losartan for 3 months improved function.
 • Ca2+ channel blockers — no generalisable adverse effect on erection.
 • Aldosterone receptor antagonists — mineralocorticoid receptors in corporal tissue; hyperaldosteronism
   ~threefold ED risk. Spironolactone: nonselective, with progesterone and AR affinity → ↓ libido,

 • Mostly anecdotal case reports or post-marketing alerts, not controlled trials.

 • Pattern: diabetes damages the NO source (nerve and endothelium) while sparing the smooth muscle's

Metabolic syndrome
 • MetS = glucose intolerance, insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, HTN, often with visceral adiposity; strongly
   linked to ED; associated with ageing and ↓ T.
 • ↑ Oestrogen in MetS; an oestrogen receptor antagonist mitigated ↓ smooth muscle and impaired ACh
   relaxation in an animal model.
 • Endothelial dysfunction, inflammation (↑ TNF-α, CRP, IL-6), adipokine resistin; nNOS uncoupling and
   dysfunctional nitrergic relaxation (Fig. 71.13). Exercise reversed cavernous changes in high-fat-fed rabbits.
Renal insufficiency/failure
 • ↓ Desire, ED and ejaculatory impairment common. Up to 80% of men with end-stage renal disease have
   arterial and veno-occlusive ED.
 • Uraemic contributors: hypothalamic–pituitary–testicular disturbance, hyperprolactinaemia, accelerated
   atheroma; ↓ NO production/bioavailability, pudendal artery calcification, ↓ endothelial reactivity;
   autonomic neuropathy (abnormal vascular and bulbocavernosus reflexes) — nerves chronically
   depolarised, likely potassium-related, mitigated by dialysis; depressive symptoms independently predict
   dysfunction on haemodialysis.
 • Transplantation may improve function, but ED persists in about half.
Hyperuricaemia
 • ↑ Uric acid → worse erectile function; animals: ↓ NOS isoforms, oxidative stress.
COVID-19
 • ED risk factors overlap with predictors of severe severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-
   CoV-2) illness; cytokine storm, hyperinflammation, vascular injury may ↑ ED. Spike protein found in
   corporal endothelium (case report). Durability and mechanism unclear.
Other chronic medical conditions
 • HIV appears a direct cause, plus stress (transmission fear, body image, comorbidity, stigma, obligatory
   condom use) → psychogenic ED.

 • Also: cirrhosis, inflammatory bowel disease, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,

Nutrient deficiencies
 • Hyperhomocysteinaemia — vascular disease and ED; from deficiency of vitamins B6, B12, folate and
   genetic defects such as 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) polymorphisms.
 • Folate: inverse association with ED, possibly independent of homocysteine — conflicting data. Low
   vitamin D ↑ ED risk. Mechanisms likely vascular.
Concept — Almost every systemic disease reaches the penis through the same three doors — endothelial NO
failure, Rho-kinase-driven contraction and TGF-β-driven fibrosis — which is why ED behaves as a sentinel for
cardiometabolic disease.

Primary Erectile Dysfunction
 • Lifelong inability to initiate and/or maintain erection from the first sexual encounter or masturbation.
 • Primary psychological ED: performance anxiety and/or abnormal desire from adverse childhood events,
   traumatic early sexual experience, conception concerns, misinformation. All primary ED likely has a
   psychological dimension.
 • Congenital organic causes: hypoplastic cavernous arteries; aberrant cavernous venous drainage (venous
   leak); congenital isolation of the corporal bodies; fusiform megalourethra with distal corporal atresia;
   congenital absence of the corpora.
 • Micropenis: functionally normal but small — stretched length more than 2.5 standard deviations below
   the mean for age — may present as "ED".
 • Functional ED with normal haemodynamics — penile buckling: axial load tolerance depends on
   intracavernous pressure, penile geometry and corporal expandability; consider curvature (chordee,
   Peyronie disease) when rigidity is full but intercourse fails.

 KEY POINTS: OTHER CAUSES OF ERECTILE DYSFUNCTION
   • Ageing contributes through central regulation, hormones, neuronal integrity, vascular flow and penile
     structure.
   • DM, MetS and cavernous nerve injury cause particularly severe ED phenotypes.
   • ED accompanies many chronic conditions and may be a sentinel event for serious disease.
   • Drugs most commonly implicated: antiandrogens, antidepressants, antihypertensives.
   • Primary ED: psychogenic causes, congenital penile anomalies or vascular defects.

Concept — Lifelong ED is psychogenic until proven otherwise but not exclusively so; the organic causes are
developmental failures of the same three components — arteries, veins and corporal bodies — that fail later
in acquired ED.

PERSPECTIVES
 • Progress in erectile physiology has revolutionised care of ED; molecular biology, signal transduction,
   growth factors, microarrays and stem cells will continue to improve it.
 • The psychosocial context of human sexuality remains essential, whatever the basic-science advances.
