DEFINITION AND HISTORICAL PERSPECTIVE
 • American Urological Association (AUA) 2015 Guideline definition: Peyronie disease (PD) is an acquired
   penile abnormality characterised by fibrosis of the tunica albuginea, which may be accompanied by pain,
   deformity, erectile dysfunction (ED) and/or distress.
 • Originally termed induratio penis plastica. The eponym honours François Gigot de la Peyronie (1743),
   the first to describe the condition and offer treatment.
 • A historical review found possible earlier descriptions only by Guilielmus de Saliceto (13th century) and
   Gabriele Falloppio (16th century); no other presumed precursor properly described or treated genuine
   PD.

GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS
 • PD is an acquired wound-healing disorder of the tunica albuginea. Injury activates an abnormal healing
   response, which produces an inelastic scar (the "plaque").
 • The resulting deformity may be curvature, indentation, hinge effect or shortening, and ED is frequently
   associated.
 • Cardinal feature: the scar does not undergo normal remodelling, so the scar and the deformity persist.
 • Because the pathophysiology is incompletely understood, there is no preventive strategy and no reliable
   way to reverse the scar.
 • One theory for the middle-age predominance is that the ageing tunica is more easily injured in
   susceptible individuals.

 BOX 76.1 PEYRONIE DISEASE CAVEATS — MISCONCEPTIONS CORRECTED
   • PD is not rare. Prevalence is 3–20%, and higher in diabetes mellitus and ED.
   • Spontaneous resolution is rare. Up to half of patients worsen if untreated in the first 12–18 months;
     the advice to "wait 6–12 months before treating" is a myth.
   • Not only middle-aged men: PD occurs from the teens to the late 70s.
   • Not only White men.
   • Trauma to the flaccid as well as the erect penis can activate scarring in susceptible individuals.
   • ED is common in PD.
   • Calcification is not a marker of mature, chronic-phase disease.
   • There is an active phase of scar growth, progressive deformity and pain.
   • Once stabilised, PD usually does not progress further, and pain typically subsides.


ANATOMIC CONSIDERATIONS
 • The tunica albuginea is multilayered and composed mainly of type I collagen, arranged as an inner
   circular and an outer longitudinal layer interlaced with elastin, with an incomplete septum anchored into
   the inner circular layer. It measures 1.5–3.0 mm depending on position.

 • The septum is essential to structural integrity. Computer models show that a single contiguous corporal
   body at full erection would generate enough stress to rupture the tunica.
 • Septal anchor sites are prone to microvascular trauma and delamination, a proposed trigger for PD.
   Intracavernous pillars further reinforce the structure, with finer pillars at 5 and 7 o'clock (Fig. 76.2).
 • 60–70% of plaques are dorsal, usually with a septal association. Coital pressure may delaminate the
   layers and trap the healing process within the tunica.
 • The longitudinal layer is thinnest at 3 and 9 o'clock and absent between 5 and 7 o'clock. This favours
   dorsal buckling and explains why dorsal curvature is the most common.
 • In normal tissue the layers slide on one another, giving flexibility, rigidity and strength despite the thin
   tunica.
 • Plaque histology (Fig. 76.3): collagen fibrils are disorganised and non-polarised, and elastin is reduced
   and haphazard. This causes asymmetric corporal expansion, with deviation toward the side of the
   plaque. A circumferential plaque produces an hourglass deformity.
 • Pathogenetic cascade (Fig. 76.4): transforming growth factor (TGF)-β1, platelet-derived growth factor,
   fibroblast growth factor, interleukins, tumour necrosis factor, nuclear factor-κB, inducible nitric oxide
   synthase, reactive oxygen species and plasminogen activator inhibitor.

Etiology/Pathophysiology
 • The exact cause is undefined. The consensus is that an injurious stimulus in a susceptible individual
   triggers the cascade.

Trauma
 • Trauma may be a single event or repetitive microtrauma. It was first proposed as the primary cause in
   1957.
 • Mechanism: erect intracorporeal pressure is high and rises further with external force during intercourse.
   This can exceed tunical elasticity and cause a microfracture. Repetitive microtrauma causes delamination
   of the tunical layers and the vessels between them, then microhaemorrhage, which initiates the wound-
   healing cascade.
 • Trauma to the flaccid penis (motor vehicle accident, sport) can also trigger PD. Once the scar develops,
   an inflammatory response causes pain, both flaccid and on pressure.
 • Dorsal and ventral shear stresses during coitus may explain the predominantly dorsal plaque location.
 • An electron-microscopy study found that plaques from PD and from delayed surgery for penile trauma
   share the same ultrastructure. This supports cavernous body trauma as the initiator.
 • Prior invasive procedures and genital or perineal trauma increase the risk of PD.
 • Only about 16–40% of patients recall antecedent trauma. The most commonly recognised event is injury
   during sexual activity. Anecdotally, the partner-on-top position is most often implicated, through a "faux
   pas de coït" (missed thrust) that produces high intracorporeal pressure.
 • Trauma alone is insufficient. In a large penile fracture series, no patient developed PD. Proposed
   cofactors are genetic predisposition, autoimmunity, aberrant local wound healing and infection.
 • Medicolegal caution: do not attribute PD to ED treatment, flaccid trauma, catheterisation or endoscopy.
   These provide an opportunity for disease in the genetically susceptible individual rather than causing it.
Concept — PD is trauma meeting a susceptible host. Septal delamination triggers a scar that never
remodels, and the scar's position dictates the deformity.

PRESENTATION — SYMPTOMS
 • The commonest presenting features are pain, erect deformity, palpable plaque and ED. Many patients
   present having self-diagnosed ED. Not every patient has pain or a palpable plaque, but shortening, hinge,
   distal softening and curvature are readily recognised.
 • Pain:
      ◦ In the acute phase, pain occurs flaccid on palpation, with erection or during intercourse, and most
        resolves once the disease is stable.
      ◦ Some patients keep "torque" pain, a pulling sensation on the plaque during a strong erection. It must
        not be confused with acute inflammatory pain.
 • Curvature and function:
      ◦ Many patients can have intercourse with up to 60° of curvature, especially dorsal, gradual curves.
        Ventral and lateral curves make intromission harder.
      ◦ Partners commonly report no discomfort, whatever the degree or direction.
      ◦ Patient estimates of curvature are unreliable and tend to be overestimates.
 • Kelami classification (1983): ≤30° mild, 31–60° moderate, >60° severe. Some patients have no curvature
   but an hourglass deformity with an unstable erection.
 • Plaque configurations: cords, nodules, coin-like, dumbbell and I-beam.
      ◦ Virtually all plaques have a septal component, which supports the delamination theory.
      ◦ Pure septal plaques may cause narrowing or shortening, or no recognised deformity.
 • Plaque orientation defines the deformity:
      ◦ A simple dorsal plaque produces dorsal curvature.
      ◦ Transverse or spiralling scars (partial or circumferential) produce indentation or hourglass deformity,
        leading to an unstable penis or hinge effect, because the penis cannot tolerate axial load (Fig. 76.9).
 • Distal softening beyond the plaque: dynamic infusion cavernosometry and cavernosography (DICC)
   shows equal pressures proximal and distal to the plaque. The cause remains speculative: local cavernosal
   fibrosis or site-specific venous leak.

Active vs. Stable Disease
 • Full spontaneous resolution is extremely rare; when it occurs, it is more likely within 12–18 months.
 • Up to 50% worsen untreated.
 • Active phase: scar growth, progressive deformity and pain. Stable phase: usually no further progression.

Differential Diagnosis
 • PD must be distinguished from congenital conditions: chordee without hypospadias, congenital penile
   curvature, and epispadias with curvature.
 • Early PD may be only focal tunical or septal thickening, hard to see even on ultrasound.
 • Deformity without an obvious plaque: consider post-traumatic cavernosal fibrosis, chronic inflammation,
   and benign or malignant tumours. Epithelioid sarcoma of the penis is rare, presents as a focal lesion and
   can mimic PD.

Evaluation and Diagnosis
History
 • Symptoms: pain, deformity and plaque; gradual or sudden onset; timing; and any inciting event —
   trauma to the flaccid or erect penis, or instrumentation.
 • Personal or family history of fibrotic disorders: Dupuytren disease (DD) and Ledderhose disease (Fig.
   76.5).
 • Key question: can the patient achieve intromission, given the deformity and/or rigidity?
 • Predictive question: "If your penis was straight with the same quality of rigidity that you have now, would
   it be adequate for penetrative sex?"
      ◦ This is an effective predictor of postoperative erectile function.
      ◦ If the answer is "no," with or without medication, direct the patient toward a penile prosthesis with
        straightening. Other approaches may straighten the penis but will not give a functional erection.
 • Also ask about: vascular risk factors for ED (diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia and
   smoking), premature or delayed ejaculation, and medications, which may reveal predisposing conditions.
 • Peyronie's Disease Questionnaire (PDQ): 15 items in 3 domains — psychological and physical symptoms
   (6 items), penile pain (3 items) and symptom bother (6 items). Each domain is scored independently, with
   no total score; higher scores mean greater negative impact.
 • Home erect photograph: controversial, because a two-dimensional image cannot capture a three-
   dimensional deformity. Smartphone photographs from above and from the side give a useful general
   impression.

Physical Examination
 • General: femoral pulses, appearance of the flaccid penis, circumcision status.
 • Examine the penis on stretch to identify the plaque (Fig. 76.6).
 • Record plaque location, but size measurement is inaccurate by any modality, because the plaque is
   rarely discrete, has irregular borders and extends into a septal cord. A reduction in plaque size after
   treatment does not correlate with improved deformity.
 • Stretched penile length (SPL) is a critical baseline, since patients overestimate their length loss.
      ◦ Grasp the glans and stretch at 90° from the body.
      ◦ Measure dorsally from pubis to corona — two fixed points that make the measurement repeatable.
 • Consistency: a "rock-hard" plaque suggests calcification; confirm by imaging, preferably ultrasound.
Calcification.
 • On ultrasound, calcification is hyperechoic with acoustic shadowing (Fig. 76.7). Computed tomography
   and magnetic resonance imaging add little.
 • Calcification can occur early and is not a marker of chronic, severe or mature disease. It likely reflects a
   genetic subtype with osteoblastic gene activation.
 • Extensive calcification predicts:
      ◦ Poorer response to non-surgical therapy, because intralesional verapamil and interferon (IFN) cannot
        penetrate mineralised tissue.
      ◦ A greater likelihood of needing a prosthesis.

Intracavernosal Injection
 • Specialist centres routinely perform duplex ultrasound with intracavernosal injection (ICI), especially in
   surgical candidates.
 • AUA 2015 (Expert Opinion): clinicians should perform an in-office ICI test, with or without duplex Doppler
   ultrasound, before any invasive intervention. History and examination may suffice for diagnosis, but ICI is
   required before invasive therapy.

Duplex Doppler Ultrasonography and Other Objective Tests

 BOX 76.3 VALUE OF PENILE DUPLEX ULTRASONOGRAPHY FOR PEYRONIE DISEASE
     • Identifies and measures calcification.
     • Identifies corporeal fibrosis.
     • Shows the erectile response to vasoactive ICI, which can be compared with the home erection.
     • Measures vascular parameters: peak systolic velocity, end-diastolic velocity and resistive index (RI).
     • Gives the optimal objective measure of erect deformity: curvature, girth irregularity and hinge.

 • Penile duplex Doppler ultrasonography (PDDUS) findings are critical for choosing the surgical technique
   (Fig. 76.10). In early disease, PDDUS can detect non-palpable septal plaques that cause distal rigidity loss,
   length loss or pain without deformity.
 • Curvature is measured with a goniometer (Fig. 76.8).
 • Preoperative erectile function correlates strongly with postoperative results.
 • Ventral curvature is most associated with cavernous veno-occlusive dysfunction, which supports
   concern about postoperative ED after grafting ventral curves.
 • DICC can assess venous leak but is rarely performed. It adds invasiveness and cost for little gain over a
   well-done PDDUS.
 • Sensation:
       ◦ There is no standard test; light touch and biothesiometry can be used, and the AUA advises
         biothesiometry when sensory change is present, to establish a baseline.
       ◦ Its validity is controversial. Vibration sense is assumed to be a surrogate for sexual sensation, with the
         index fingers as positive control and the anterior thighs as negative control.
 • Morning serum total testosterone is recommended in PD with ED (per the AUA ED guideline), not in PD
   without ED.

 KEY POINTS — EVALUATION
     • History: symptom onset, vascular risk factors, patient-estimated deformity and rigidity.
     • Questionnaire: the PDQ documents the effect of PD.
     • Examination: plaque palpated on stretch, stretched flaccid length, and pain on palpation.

TREATMENT
 • Algorithms are guides only. Management is individualised to the history, examination, PDDUS and patient
   goals.

Medical Therapies
 • Older studies were weak: small, uncontrolled, non-randomised and with few objective measures.
 • AUA 2015: all oral and topical agents except non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are
   "should not offer."
 • Reassurance alone may suffice if there is no pain or difficulty with penetration. Reassure the patient that
   PD is not cancerous and not life-threatening.
 • Natural history under conservative management:
      ◦ Shortening is common and improvement uncommon. Pain usually resolves, but a notable proportion
        have pain years later, including new-onset pain, possibly from repeated trauma to the curved penis.
      ◦ Another long-term survey found curvature improved, stabilised or worsened in varying proportions.
        Erectile function and satisfaction declined, psychosocial distress was high, and traction users did
        better.

Oral Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs
 • AUA Expert Opinion: may be offered for pain in active PD. Assess pain on a visual analogue scale (VAS)
   and reassess periodically.

Pentoxifylline
 • A methylxanthine used for claudication. It reduces blood viscosity, improves red-cell deformability and
   reduces platelet aggregation.
 • Antifibrotic actions: blocks the TGF-β1 pathway, prevents type I collagen deposition, acts as a non-
   specific phosphodiesterase inhibitor, increases fibrinolysis, and inhibits elastogenesis via an α1-
   antitrypsin-related mechanism. It reduced plaque in an animal model.
 • Human evidence is small, retrospective and confounded by concurrent traction. AUA: efficacy uncertain.

Phosphodiesterase Type 5 Inhibitors
 • Phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (PDE5Is) are safe and effective for ED in PD.
 • Tadalafil plus extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) improved International Index of Erectile
   Function (IIEF) and quality-of-life scores, but gave no deformity benefit. AUA: ESWT should not be used
   to reduce curvature or plaque size.
 • Antifibrotic rationale: raised cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) inhibits collagen synthesis and
   induces fibroblast and myofibroblast apoptosis. Low-dose daily tadalafil remodelled isolated septal
   scars.
 • AUA: PDE5Is are recommended for ED but not for PD itself.

Intralesional Injections
Collagenase Clostridium histolyticum (Xiaflex).
 • The first Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drug for PD (2013). It selectively degrades
   collagen types I and III, even in the presence of tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), which are
   elevated in PD. It spares elastic fibres, vascular smooth muscle and axonal sheaths.

Chapter 76 — Diagnosis and Management of Peyronie Disease                                                 Treatment
 • In early trials, response was inversely related to curvature severity and plaque size, and most patients
   had reduced plaque size.
 • Modelling is essential: collagenase Clostridium histolyticum (CCH) plus modelling outperformed both
   placebo and CCH without modelling.
 • IMPRESS I and II — landmark phase III trials (Investigation for Maximal Peyronie's Reduction Efficacy and
   Safety Studies):
      ◦ Protocol: up to 4 cycles, 6 weeks apart → each cycle is 2 injections of 0.58 mg, 24–72 hours apart →
        modelling 24–72 hours after the second injection. Maximum 8 injections.
      ◦ Randomised 2:1 CCH to placebo, stratified by curvature 30–60° vs 61–90°.
      ◦ Result: 34% improvement (−17°) vs 18.2% (−9.3°) with placebo. The PDQ symptom bother score was
        significantly better.
      ◦ Excluded: extensive calcification, ventral plaques, and disease duration <12 months.
      ◦ Anti-CCH antibodies developed in virtually all patients, without resulting adverse events.
      ◦ Adverse events: ecchymosis and bruising were commonest. Serious events were corporal rupture and
        penile haematoma, managed surgically or by percutaneous drainage.
 • Most serious complication: penile (corporal) fracture (0–4.9%), because the injection weakens the tunica
   around the plaque. Pooled data show that most adverse effects are minor and serious events are rare.
 • Protocol modifications: most of the gain occurs in the first 3 cycles, with no significant gain in the 4th.
   Shortened regimens gave IMPRESS-equivalent results. There is no consensus protocol, and cost is a major
   concern.
 • Acute-phase use: improvement was comparable to stable disease, but FDA approval is for stable disease
   and more data are needed.
 • Subsequent plication or grafting is not more difficult after CCH.
 • Long-term (up to 5 years): no safety signals, and curvature and PDQ improvements were maintained
   without further injections. Results were similar when calcification did not interfere with injection.
 • Real-world multicentre data: about 30% curvature reduction, with more cycles giving greater benefit.
 • Ventral and lateral curves: relative improvement exceeded dorsal curves, and no urethral complications
   were seen in a small series. CCH is not yet routine for ventral curves, because the corpus spongiosum
   and urethral submucosa also contain type I and III collagen.
 • Since approval, CCH is increasingly the first-line choice.
 • Predictors of poor response: calcification (including grade 3), curvature ≥60°, severe indentation or
   narrowing, and hinge. Pretreatment IIEF-5 ≥17 is associated with a better response.
 • AUA: CCH for stable curvature >30° and <90° with intact erectile function, with or without medication;
   counsel on risks.
Interferon Alfa-2b.
  • Mechanism (in vitro): dose-dependent reduction in plaque fibroblast proliferation, reduced extracellular
    collagen and increased collagenase.
 • The first multicentre, randomised, placebo-controlled trial of intralesional therapy for PD (single-blind)
   found interferon alfa-2b (IFN α-2b) significantly better than saline for curvature, plaque size and density,
   and pain. Clinical significance was questioned given cost and side effects. It also showed that saline
   injection has little or no effect.
 • Side effects: flu-like symptoms (fever, chills, arthralgia), sinusitis, minor penile swelling and ecchymosis.
   Pre-injection NSAIDs are effective, and effects last <36 hours.
 • IFN versus verapamil: similar outcomes, but verapamil is far cheaper.

 • AUA: may administer IFN α-2b, with counselling on adverse events. It has had limited US availability
   since 2022, which has increased verapamil use.
Verapamil.
 • Mechanism: calcium channel blockade inhibits proline incorporation into extracellular matrix (ECM),
   reduces fibroblast proliferation and ECM synthesis and secretion, and increases collagenase activity. In a
   rat model, collagen, elastin and alpha-smooth muscle actin (ASMA, a myofibroblast marker) all decreased.
 • Verapamil was the first new intralesional agent since steroid injection.
 • Uncontrolled series showed improvement in pain, narrowing, distal rigidity and curvature.
 • Controlled trials are conflicting: one single-blind trial showed improved plaque size, narrowing and
   erection quality but no curvature benefit, and one placebo-controlled trial showed no difference from
   saline. At a minimum, verapamil can stabilise deformity.
 • Dilution: 10 mg/10 mL is standard, but greater dilution (10 mg in 20 mL) gave a better response.
 • Poor candidates: extensive calcification, curvature >90°, and ventral curvature, where adequate
   infiltration is difficult.
 • Predictors of success: younger age (<40 years) and curvature >30°.
 • Randomised head-to-head trial against CCH (non-blinded, same modelling protocol): CCH gave greater
   curvature improvement, more complete resolutions, and better PDQ and IIEF-5 scores, but more minor
   adverse events (bruising, swelling, pain).
 • AUA: may offer verapamil, with counselling on injection-site effects.

Penile Traction Therapy
 • Indication: a non-invasive, non-surgical first option.
 • Mechanism — mechanotransduction: extrapolated from DD, controlled stretch remodels collagen,
   reduces myofibroblast activity and increases matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Tension reorients
   collagen parallel to the axis of stress. In PD cells in vitro, ASMA decreased and collagen-degrading MMPs
   increased, without upregulation of heat shock protein 47 or the TGF-β1 receptor.
 • None of the classic clinical studies were controlled.

 • Pilot data (2–8 hours a day for 6 months): curvature, SPL, girth and hinge all improved, and results were
   maintained at 6 months after stopping, with no adverse events.
 • In stable disease with curves ≤50°, curvature change was minimal, but satisfaction was reasonable,
   mainly from length gain. Selection of stable, often calcified disease may have underestimated efficacy.
 • Acute phase (progressive curvature >15° and/or pain in the last 12 months):
      ◦ Regimen: 6–9 hours a day, not during sleep, removed for 30 minutes every 2 hours to prevent glans
        ischaemia.
      ◦ Results: curvature, pain and penetration ability improved, and untreated controls worsened. The need
        for surgery fell, and surgery was simplified from grafting to plication in some.
      ◦ Adverse effects: balanopreputial erythema and discomfort; no sensory change.
 • The barrier to adoption is long daily wear time. RestoreX, a counter-bending device worn 30–90 minutes
   a day, improved length, curvature and IIEF-5 in a randomised controlled trial of a difficult cohort (long-
   standing disease; hourglass and complex curves not excluded). Most patients preferred it as first line.
 • AUA 2015 makes no recommendation on penile traction therapy (PTT), although adoption is increasing.

Vacuum Therapy
 • Rationale: indirect, repeated stretch reorganises ECM, analogous to the management of DD.
 • 10 minutes twice daily improved curvature compared with non-users, with the greatest gain in larger
   curves, independent of age and disease duration. Short stretch durations are unlikely to produce true
   mechanotransduction.
 • Complications (with constriction rings or excessive pressure): PD itself, urethral bleeding, skin necrosis
   and ecchymosis.
 • AUA: no recommendation. A vacuum erection device (VED) is an option for patients who choose
   conservative management.

Combination Therapy
 • PTT plus verapamil plus pentoxifylline plus L-arginine: both arms improved, with no difference in
   curvature. Traction preserved or gained SPL, while non-users lost length. Benefit showed a dose-
   response at ≥3 hours a day.
 • Verapamil (injection and iontophoresis) plus a multi-agent supplement pill: greater reduction in plaque
   and curvature.
 • PTT plus IFN: SPL gain without curvature change, only with ≥3 hours a day.
 • AUA: no recommendation.

Conclusion — Non-Surgical Therapy
 • The minimum goal is to prevent progression during the acute phase. The ultimate goal is to reduce
   deformity and restore sexual function.

 KEY POINTS — NON-SURGICAL TREATMENT OF PD
   • Patients with no pain or difficulty with penetration may need reassurance only.
   • Conservative treatments give inconsistent or modest improvement, because the underlying
     mechanism is poorly understood.
   • No oral agent shows clinically meaningful curvature benefit in placebo-controlled trials. NSAIDs for
     pain are the only AUA-endorsed oral therapy.
   • Topical therapy and ESWT do not reduce deformity.
   • Verapamil and IFN α-2b reduce curvature and improve function in mostly uncontrolled studies, and
     appear to stabilise deformity in the acute phase.
   • CCH is the only FDA-approved agent: 34% (17°) vs 18.2% (9.3°) with placebo in phase III.
   • Counter-bending traction devices (RestoreX) with short wear times improve curvature, length and
     erectile function, even as monotherapy.

SURGICAL MANAGEMENT
 • Practice varies widely internationally. Few surgeons are high-volume (defined as >20 plications a year),
   and most perform fewer than 10 graft procedures annually.

Indications
 • Surgery is the gold standard for the most rapid and reliable correction of deformity. A penile prosthesis is
   added when ED coexists.
 • Deformity that precludes intercourse, causes pain to either partner, or causes distress at the appearance
   of the erect penis is an indication.

 BOX 76.4 INDICATIONS FOR SURGERY
   • Stable disease: ≥12 months from onset, with the deformity stable for ≥6 months. This is expert
     consensus and has not been formally studied.
   • Inability to have satisfactory penetrative intercourse because of deformity and/or inadequate rigidity.
   • Failed conservative treatment.
   • Desire for the most rapid and reliable result.

 • No single operation is the standard of care. The choice depends on severity and direction of curvature,
   hinge, erection quality and patient goals (Fig. 76.10).

Preoperative Consent
 • Patients are often emotionally devastated. Dissatisfaction often stems from expecting a return to the
   pre-PD appearance, so shared decision-making and expectation-setting are essential.

 BOX 76.5 PREOPERATIVE CONSENT
   • Persistent or recurrent curvature:
        ◦ Uncommon (up to 16%), and rarely needs reoperation.
        ◦ The goal is "functionally straight": ≤20° (expert opinion). The European Association of Urology
          (EAU) defines success as ≤15°.
   • Length:
        ◦ Some length loss occurs with every procedure, and it is more likely with plication than grafting.
        ◦ 70–80% have already lost length from the disease, so document SPL preoperatively.
   • Diminished rigidity:
        ◦ About 5% in all studies, grafting more than plication; about 30% if preoperative rigidity is
          suboptimal. Up to 50% have some reduction, which may respond to a PDE5I.
        ◦ Preoperative erection quality is the most reliable predictor of postoperative ED.
        ◦ Straightening does not usually improve rigidity, although correcting geometry improves it in some.
        ◦ Refractory ED can be treated with a PDE5I, ICI, a VED or a later prosthesis, placed with little added
          difficulty.
   • Decreased sensation: about 20% report some reduction, which rarely compromises orgasm or
     ejaculation. Acute hyperaesthesia or hypoaesthesia usually settles within months, and stabilises by 6–
     12 months.

Tunical Shortening Procedures
Plication and Corporoplasty
 • Principle: shorten the long (convex) side to match the short side, always on the side opposite the
   curvature.
 • Advantages: short operating time, good cosmesis, minimal effect on rigidity, simplicity, safety and
   effective straightening.
 • Disadvantages:
      ◦ It shortens the penis and does not correct existing shortening.
      ◦ It does not correct, and may worsen, hourglass or hinge, leaving an unstable penis.
      ◦ The plaque is left in situ.
      ◦ Narrowing or indentation, knot pain and suture granulomas can occur.
 • Nesbit failure predictors: impaired preoperative erectile function, shortening >2 cm, and curvature >30°.

Tunical Lengthening Procedures
 • Indications for plaque incision and grafting (PIG) or partial plaque excision and grafting (PEG): complex
   disease with some or all of curvature ≥60–70°, shaft narrowing, hinging and extensive calcification.
 • The patient must have strong preoperative erections. Ask: "If your penis was straight, would your current
   rigidity allow penetrative sex?" If he hesitates, grafting should not be performed unless he accepts the
   risk of worse ED and a possible later prosthesis.
 • Some patients refuse a first-line prosthesis, and some plication candidates refuse because of fear of
   length loss. Grafting can be offered to both groups, since a prosthesis can be placed later with minimal
   added difficulty.
 • Grafting advantage: it corrects curvature, restores shaft calibre, and may recover 0.5–3.0 cm of length.
 • Predictors of postoperative ED (from single-centre data): age >55 years, veno-occlusive dysfunction on
   PDDUS (RI <0.80), large defect or graft, ventral curvature, and curvature >60°.

 • The strongest criterion is preoperative erection quality; preoperative and postoperative ED are linearly
   associated.
 • Ventral deformities do poorly with grafting, because of associated veno-occlusive dysfunction.

Plaque Incision and Grafting
 • Total plaque excision has been abandoned, because large grafts caused unacceptable ED.
 • Incision options:
      ◦ The Gelbard modified-H or double-Y incision at the point of maximum curvature expands the tunica,
        corrects curvature and calibre, and minimises cavernosal exposure. Multiple small incisions with
        grafts give a smoother correction.
      ◦ Multiple transverse incisions with a rhomboid defect were the first description of PIG.
      ◦ The Egydio incision (near-circumferential extension of the H) is associated with more ED.
 • Mechanism of postoperative ED: cavernosal fibrosis and disruption of the veno-occlusive mechanism.
 • Systematic review: no incision type is superior, and straightening is 80–96%. Many patients depend on
   erectile aids afterward, which exposes a weakness of the IIEF-5 (Sexual Health Inventory for Men): it does
   not control for aid use. Sensory change is mostly transient.

Plaque Excision and Grafting
 • Preferred when the area of maximum deformity must be excised, as with severe indentation. Otherwise
   the straightened penis may remain narrowed and unstable.
 • Dart the corners of the defect radially to correct narrowing.
 • Egydio's geometric principles size the graft, but are considered unnecessarily complex and carry higher
   ED rates.
 • Key to reducing ED: minimise trauma to the underlying cavernosal tissue, preserving the veno-occlusive
   relationship between cavernosal tissue and graft.
 • AUA: may offer PIG/PEG if rigidity is adequate for coitus (± pharmacotherapy or VED) (Moderate
   Recommendation; Evidence Grade C).

Graft Materials
 • The ideal graft approximates the strength and elasticity of tunica, has minimal morbidity and tissue
   reaction, is readily available, thin, pliable, easy to size and suture, cheap and infection-resistant, and
   preserves erectile capacity.

Grafting Surgical Technique
 1. General anaesthesia, intravenous antibiotics and deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis → measure dorsal
    SPL.
 2. Artificial erection: vasoactive drug (papaverine, Trimix or prostaglandin E1) through a 21-gauge butterfly
    needle via the glans → saline infusion to full rigidity → document curvature, indentation and hinge.
 3. Circumcising incision 1.5–2 cm proximal to the corona (or through an old circumcision scar) → deglove to
    Buck fascia → bipolar haemostasis, using loupe magnification.
 4. Dorsal or dorsolateral curve: parallel incisions just lateral to the urethral ridge → elevate Buck fascia with
    the neurovascular bundle off the tunica by sharp dissection, using bipolar cautery for adhesions.
 5. Recreate the erection → mark the area of maximum deformity.
      ◦ Even a pure lateral curve requires the incision or excision to cross the dorsal septum, the anchor
        point of the scar, or residual curvature remains.
      ◦ Extensive calcification can be shaved off the inner circular fibres, leaving the outer lamina intact.
 6. Create a rectangular defect → dart the corners radially → keep the lateral sides of equal length, a
    simplified geometric principle that corrects lateral and dorsal curves. The proximal transverse side is
    usually longer because the shaft tapers distally. Dorsal length typically gains 0.5–3.0 cm.
 7. Place 4-0 PDS stay sutures at the four corners and the transverse midpoints → measure the defect on
    stretch.
      ◦ Size the graft: pericardium ≤10% larger than the defect; porcine SIS 25% larger.
      ◦ Secure with running 4-0 PDS. For large defects, add interrupted septal sutures to limit sub-graft blood
        collection. TachoSil needs no suturing.
 8. Recreate the erection → plicate any residual curvature, which is needed in up to 25%.
      ◦ For a long curve, or an indentation with a distal curve, graft the indentation and plicate the residual
        curve.
      ◦ A single graft does not carry higher ED than multiple grafts and is faster.

Postoperative Management
 • Rehabilitation is as important as after orthopaedic joint replacement: it reduces ED and length loss and
   optimises straight healing.
 • At 2 weeks → massage and stretch: grasp the glans, stretch, and massage the shaft for 5 minutes twice
   daily for 2–4 weeks. The partner can take over in the second fortnight, which helps overcome fear of
   reinjury.
 • Nightly PDE5I: vasodilation supports graft take, reduces scar contraction and may preserve cavernosal
   tissue.
 • Postoperative traction reduces length loss and can produce length gain after both plication and PEG. The
   regimen is ≥3 hours a day, from 3–4 weeks after surgery, for 3 months.
 • Grafting outcomes: straightening 74–100%, postoperative ED 5–54% (with no uniform definition). Long
   term: ED up to 24% and recurrence 8–12%.
 • AUA: may offer plaque incision or excision and/or grafting when rigidity is adequate for coitus (Moderate
   Recommendation; Evidence Grade C).

Penile Prosthesis Surgery
Indications
 • In PD with ED refractory to PDE5Is, the prosthesis is the procedure of choice: a single operation treats
   both problems.
 • Adjunctive straightening: manual modelling, tunical incision ± graft, or transcorporal incision or stretch of
   the plaque from within the corpus.
 • An inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) is preferred: cylinder pressure allows better correction with
   modelling and better girth. Malleable devices gave narrow, cold, unnatural erections.

Surgical Technique and Adjunctive Procedures for Penile Straightening
 • Approach: penoscrotal, with a high-pressure inflatable cylinder. All three-piece and two-piece devices
   have been used successfully.
Manual modelling (Wilson and Delk, 1994) — the first-line step:
 1. Place the cylinders → close the corporotomies → fill to full rigidity through a surrogate reservoir.
 2. Clamp the tubing between pump and cylinders with shodded haemostats to protect the pump from
    high pressure.
 3. Bend opposite to the curve → hold 60–90 seconds (in practice often ≤30 seconds) → reinflate and repeat
    until straight.
 4. Bend gradually, not violently, to avoid tunical tears and neurovascular injury.
 5. Place the bending hand on the shaft, not the glans. Pressure on the glans drives the cylinder tips through
    the fossa navicularis and causes urethral injury.

 6. The other hand supports the base over the corporotomies to protect the suture lines.
 • Modelling outcomes: straightening in 86–100%, with no increase in device revision. Sensory deficit is
   rare but must be discussed. Modelling is appropriate as first-line therapy even for severe curves.
 • Alternative: plication contralateral to the curve before prosthesis placement. For a dorsal curve, define
   the deformity with vasoactive drug and saline infusion, then place Lembert sutures ventrally.
 • Residual curve >30° after modelling, or indentation causing cylinder buckling → elevate Buck fascia →
   make a relaxing tunical incision over the maximum curve.
      ◦ Incise with the cylinders deflated, preserving cavernosal tissue over the implant.
      ◦ Use cautery <30 W with Titan cylinders to avoid thermal injury.
      ◦ Reinflate and remodel.
 • Graft if the defect is >2 cm in any dimension, to prevent scar contracture and cylinder herniation. Use
   pericardium or SIS; never locally harvested dermis, which risks transferring bacteria to the prosthesis.
   Collagen fleece has also been used.
 • Home modelling, for residual curvature <45° after IPP placement and modelling: start at 4 weeks → fully
   inflate → bend opposite the curve and hold 30 seconds → deflate. Continue cycles for 20 minutes, 3 times
   daily, for 6 months. Most patients reach ≤10°.
 • A transverse penoscrotal incision reaches almost the whole shaft, so degloving is rarely needed, except
   for a distal dorsal curve.
 • Sliding technique (with a prosthesis): restores length and girth in debilitating length or girth loss, with
   mean gains of about 3 cm. Most patients received malleable devices, all curves were corrected, and
   experience is from a single institution.
 • Long-term IPP outcomes in PD:
      ◦ Most patients needed modelling; a minority needed incision ± pericardial graft.
      ◦ Additional maneuvers did not increase mechanical failure or infection.
      ◦ Satisfaction with curvature was lower than overall satisfaction, which underlines the need for
        expectation-setting.
 • Functional straightness: a residual curve of ≤20° is acceptable and may improve with time through
   cylinder-driven tissue expansion.
 • The two North American three-piece devices do not differ in reliability, infection or satisfaction.
 • Table 76.6 (Outcomes of Penile Prosthesis Implantation for Peyronie Disease): most series used
   inflatable devices. The need for additional straightening maneuvers varied widely, and satisfaction was
   generally good.

Complications
Length loss.
 • Length loss is the commonest complaint after a prosthesis. Objective loss is under 1 cm by 1 year, which
   is especially distressing in PD.
 • Pre-prosthesis traction (3–4 months at ≥3 hours a day) resulted in no further length loss after
   implantation, and most patients gained length.
 • Postoperative prolonged inflation: 10–30 minutes a day for 3 months, starting 6 weeks after surgery. This
   maintains length and reduces residual curvature.
 • With the sliding technique, subcoronal degloving carries a risk of ischaemia or necrosis. A non-
   degloving ventral incision preserves the skin, dartos and neurovascular bundle and has avoided vascular
   complications.

 KEY POINTS — SURGICAL MANAGEMENT
   • Surgery (± prosthesis) is the gold standard when deformity or rigidity compromises or prevents
     penetration.
   • Thorough consent about limitations improves satisfaction.
   • Satisfactory rigidity plus a curve <60–70° without significant indentation → plication. No technique is
     proven superior, as there are no head-to-head trials.
   • Severe or complex deformity plus strong erections plus no venous insufficiency on PDDUS → PIG/
     PEG.
   • Complications of all straightening operations: incomplete straightening, recurrence, shortening,
     reduced sensation and ED.
   • Outcome depends on patient selection and technique, not graft type.
   • Inadequate rigidity → prosthesis with straightening maneuvers as first-line surgery.
   • AUA 2015:
        ◦ Prosthesis for PD with ED and/or deformity preventing coitus despite pharmacotherapy or VED
          (Moderate; Grade C).
        ◦ Adjunctive modelling, plication or incision/grafting for persistent deformity (Moderate; Grade C).
        ◦ Use an inflatable device.


CONCLUSION
 • PD is far more common than previously thought and is a growth area for both clinical practice and
   research.
 • Recognise the acute and stable phases, and operate only once the scar and deformity are stable (≥12
   months from onset, stable ≥6 months).
 • Complete correction of curvature, indentation and shortening is unlikely; the goal is sexual function.
 • Recognise the devastating psychological impact, and offer counselling where indicated.
 • In the acute phase, the only guideline-endorsed oral therapy is NSAIDs for pain.
 • Surgical risks: incomplete straightening, recurrence, further shortening, altered sensation and, most
   importantly, diminished rigidity.
 • Drug-refractory ED plus PD → prosthesis with straightening maneuvers, which addresses both in one
   operation.
