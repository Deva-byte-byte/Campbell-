HISTORY OF ADRENAL SURGERY
 • First printed description of the adrenal glands: Eustachius, mid-1500s. More than three centuries passed
   before the first surgical excision.
 • 1889, Thornton — first reported adrenal excision: performed as an "abdominal nephrectomy" for a very
   large tumour that proved to be adrenal and hormonally active, through a T-shaped subcostal (chevron or
   hemi-chevron) incision.
 • 1905 — first adrenalectomy for a preoperative diagnosis of adrenal pathology ("enlarged" glands). 1934
   — first series of adrenalectomies, all for Cushing syndrome, with mixed pathology (carcinoma,
   hyperplasia, adenoma and normal glands).
 • Preoperative steroid cover for Cushing syndrome was the greatest early advance: operative mortality,
   previously substantial even after subtotal excision, fell to zero once steroids were given preoperatively
   (reported 1949).
 • Imaging refined selection: ultrasonography (US), computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance
   imaging (MRI) and [131I]-meta-iodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scintigraphy separated patients who benefit
   from surgery from those who do not.
 • Access migrated cephalad as the goal shifted from metabolic control to tumour excision: subcostal →
   flank with 11th or 12th rib resection → 1932: transpleural, transdiaphragmatic long posterior intercostal
   incision, forerunner of the thoracoabdominal incision — virtually unparalleled exposure at the price of
   thoracic complications and a painful, protracted recovery. Anterior, posterior, flank, transperitoneal and
   retroperitoneal approaches all retain advocates and critics.
 • Open surgery was the gold standard for about a century, until 1992. Since then, surgical adrenal disease
   has been synonymous with minimally invasive surgery (MIS), with very few exceptions.

 • Arterial supply — three arteries to each gland: superior adrenal (from inferior phrenic artery), middle
   adrenal (from aorta), inferior adrenal (from renal artery). Accessory vessels from intercostal arteries and
   the left gonadal artery.
 • Lymphatics: lateral aortic chain from diaphragm to ipsilateral renal artery; may end in thoracic duct or,
   after piercing the crura, posterior mediastinal nodes.
Concept — three arteries but one vein: adrenalectomy is organised around the vein, and the right vein's
shortness and direct caval entry make the right the more hazardous side.

INDICATIONS FOR ADRENAL SURGERY
 • Guidelines: American Association of Endocrine Surgeons (AAES) adrenalectomy guideline, 2022; American
   Association of Clinical Endocrinologists (AACE)/AAES recommendations, 2009 — still widely practised.

Adrenal Masses
 • Incidentalomas occur in about 4% (range 1–8.7%) of the population.
 • Hormonally active tumours (phaeochromocytoma, aldosteronoma) → excise regardless of size. All
   suspected phaeochromocytomas and adrenocortical carcinomas (ACCs) → excise. <1 cm and non-
   functioning → observe.
 • Solid enhancing lesion may be a metastasis (primaries: lung, breast, melanoma, kidney, thyroid, colon —
   ranked order under Surgery for Metastatic Lesions). With a known primary, strongly consider removal
   when it is the solitary site of spread or will improve quality or quantity of life; low MIS morbidity has
   made surgeons more aggressive, with encouraging results.

Hormonally Active Lesions
 • Adrenalectomy or partial adrenalectomy when systemic manifestations cannot be controlled medically.

Uncontrolled Hypertension
 • Blood pressure (BP) control is a priority before, during and after surgery — prevents myocardial events,
   cerebrovascular accidents and renal barotrauma.
 • Unopposed β-blockade is highly contraindicated until α-blockade is titrated and BP stable: loss of β2-
   mediated vasodilatation leaves catecholamine-driven α-vasoconstriction unopposed → hypertensive
   crisis, sometimes fatal.
 • Other hypertensive adrenal conditions often need combinations — angiotensin-converting enzyme
   inhibitors (ACE-Is), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), calcium-channel blockers (CCBs), diuretics, β-
   blockers — to reduce lability and spikes.

Uncontrolled Coagulopathy
 • Contraindication to most surgery; given adrenal vascularity, normalise platelets, bleeding time,
   prothrombin time (PT), partial thromboplastin time (PTT) and international normalised ratio (INR) first.
 • Low-dose aspirin (81 mg) does not increase intra- or postoperative bleeding; do not discourage it where
   benefit outweighs risk.

Untreated Bacterial Peritonitis
 • Avoid transperitoneal surgery; an RP approach may be used, with great care not to breach the
   peritoneum.
 • Insufflation pressure (trans- or retroperitoneal) can exceed central and systemic venous pressure →
   abdomino-venous or abdomino-lymphatic backflow → bacteraemia, sepsis. Eradicate infection first.

Pregnancy (Relative)
 • Defer elective adrenal surgery until after delivery. Phaeochromocytoma, cyst rupture or tumour
   haemorrhage may compel surgery during pregnancy.
 • Historical versus current view: second trimester was held safest (first → spontaneous abortion; third →
   preterm labour). American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and Society of American
   Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons (SAGES) now advise that surgically necessary treatment is not
   delayed, regardless of trimester.
 • Open and MIS both acceptable; obstetric service aware, available and present.

Past Medical and Surgical History Relevant to Adrenal Surgery
 • Clues to prior or ongoing adrenal disease: hypertension (especially uncontrolled), easy bruising,
   palpitations, headaches, hirsutism, stunted growth, anisomelia.
 • Prior nephrectomy may have removed the ipsilateral adrenal — contralateral adrenalectomy is then
   dangerous if unrecognised. Flank scars are a clue; review cross-sectional imaging and consider serum
   testing for intact adrenal tissue.
 • Pneumoperitoneum: pulmonary disease, cardiac insufficiency, ocular disease, morbid obesity or prior
   steroids raise inspiratory pressures and lung-complication risk; gasless laparoscopy is an option, not
   widely adopted.
 • Prior abdominal surgery → adhesions, prolonged or impossible transperitoneal MIS, bowel injury → RP
   access may be superior.
 • Open surgery: large, painful incisions; steroids impair healing; acute pain hard to control.
Concept — each contraindication is a correctable state — blood pressure, clotting, infection, gestation — so it
contraindicates operating now, not adrenalectomy itself.

THE INCIDENTALOMA
 • Definition: adrenal mass ≥1 cm found on imaging for an unrelated reason; usually <4 cm. Management is
   multidisciplinary (endocrinology, radiology, surgery).
 • Frequency: about 9% at autopsy; about 5% of abdominal imaging studies.
 • Two questions — functional? malignant? ACC in about 2%; autonomous hormone secretion in up to 20%.

 • Choice depends on disease, tumour size, patient factors, surgeon experience, equipment and preference;
   laterality poses distinct challenges.

Transperitoneal
 • Oldest documented approach. "You can never be too high" — the adrenal's superior position limits
   exposure.
 • Midline: early, excellent vascular control and view of great vessels; no muscle cut → less pain, better
   healing, fewer hernias and dehiscences. Subcostal/chevron: crosses several muscles → more pain, but
   "higher" exposure; excellent for smaller tumours and hormonally active adenomas or hyperplasia.
 • Layers — midline: skin → subcutaneous tissue → linea alba → transversalis fascia → preperitoneal fat →
   peritoneum. Subcostal/chevron: skin → subcutaneous tissue → anterior rectus sheath and rectus
   medially; external oblique, internal oblique, transversus abdominis laterally → transversalis fascia →
   preperitoneal fat → peritoneum.
 • Fig. 147.1 — arcuate line: above it the rectus lies within anterior and posterior sheaths (internal oblique
   aponeurosis splits); below it all aponeuroses pass anterior, leaving rectus on transversalis fascia.
 • Closure: laterally two fascial layers (transversus abdominis; external oblique), converging to one layer
   medially. Peritoneal closure is debated — conflicting evidence on hernia prevention.
 • Ports: laparoscopy — all-midline, subcostal, "L" or reverse-"L"; no configuration proven superior. Robotic
   — subcostal most common: excellent exposure, no instrument collision (Figs 147.2, 147.3). Single-site —
   one medium incision, gel platform or SP robot.
Right Adrenal

Open Approach
Positioning. Lateral decubitus, affected side up; table flexed at costal margin; optional kidney rest; axillary
roll; arms extended away, upper arm on padded elevated rest; lower leg bent, upper straight (Fig. 147.4).
Incision. Flank; divide latissimus dorsi, external oblique, internal oblique, transversus abdominis; reflect
parietal pleura upward; resect 12th (occasionally 11th) rib.
Rib resection: scrape anterior periosteum (periosteal elevator) → strip posterior periosteum from posterior
and superior aspects, protecting the neurovascular bundle in the groove on the posteroinferior border
(nerve injury → flank bulge) → divide distally with rib cutter → file edge; electrocautery and bone wax →
enter lumbodorsal fascia, develop RP space bluntly with finger, sweep peritoneum off transversalis fascia →
divide overlying muscles; fixed retraction.
Dissection — right. Medially over IVC, dividing posterior peritoneum → superiorly to adrenal vein; if needed,
first control short hepatic veins between silk ties for 1–2 cm more superior exposure (posterosuperior
adrenal vein) → ligate adrenal vein between silk ties → superior extent between adrenal and liver →
adrenal–kidney attachment last, as a handle for downward retraction.
Dissection — left. Medially to aorta → superiorly to left renal vein → along it to adrenal vein; ligate between
silk ties → superior extent, protecting pancreatic tail and splenic vessels → adrenal–kidney attachment last.
On both sides the multiple arteries are controlled with electrocautery devices.
Closing. Inspect for haemostasis (warm sterile water irrigation helps) → two-layer fascial closure, running
bidirectional looped polydioxanone 0 or #1: deep — transversus abdominis and internal oblique; superficial
— external oblique → skin staples or absorbable monofilament.
Concept — whichever cavity is entered, the operation is the same: follow the renal vein to the adrenal vein,
control the vein, and keep the adrenal–kidney attachment until last as a retraction handle.

MINIMALLY INVASIVE SURGICAL APPROACHES
 • Laparoscopic and robot-assisted approaches both suit adrenal pathology; lateral or prone positioning;
   instruments by surgeon preference.
 • RP MIS versus transperitoneal MIS: avoids bowel and viscera — preferred after prior abdominal surgery.
   RP MIS versus open RP: less pain, shorter stay, though regional anaesthesia narrows the gap.
 • Robot particularly suits the RP approach — small working space, angled instruments.

SURGERY FOR PHAEOCHROMOCYTOMA
Preoperative Management and Coordination With Anaesthesia
 • Presentation: tachycardia, hypertension, diaphoresis, headache, arrhythmia, left ventricular dysfunction,
   impaired glucose tolerance.
 • Work-up: electrocardiogram, echocardiogram, assessment of hypertensive end-organ damage; plan
   jointly with anaesthesia — hypertensive spikes and tachyarrhythmias are expected.
Preparation: α-blockade 2 weeks before surgery — phenoxybenzamine (irreversible non-selective) or
doxazosin (selective α1) — titrated to normotension → β-blockade only if tachycardia or arrhythmia →
volume expansion (hydration or high-salt diet), because chronically vasoconstricted patients become
hypotensive once vasodilated.

Intraoperative Considerations
 • Invasive monitoring; tell anaesthesia whenever the mass is being manipulated; pause manipulation
   during lability. Ligate the adrenal vein early if size allows.

Postoperative Considerations
 • Invasive haemodynamic monitoring of BP, heart rate and blood glucose.
 • Lifelong recurrence up to 16% → regular long-term BP monitoring and biochemical screening.
Concept — phaeochromocytoma surgery is a two-phase haemodynamic problem — catecholamine surge
while the tumour is handled, catecholamine withdrawal once the vein is tied — and α-blockade with volume
expansion prepares for both.

SURGERY FOR MALIGNANCY
Adrenocortical Carcinoma
 • Only two primary adrenal malignancies in adults: ACC and phaeochromocytoma; neuroblastoma is
   paediatric and not covered.
 • ACC: rare, of cortical origin, 0.5–2 per million per year.
 • Who operates: European Society of Endocrinology (ESE) and European Network for the Study of Adrenal
   Tumours (ENSAT) — surgeon >6 adrenalectomies per year, preferably ≥20, with extensive oncological
   experience, and equally experienced anaesthesia and oncology teams.
 • How: open transperitoneal, removing all visible tumour, with en-bloc adjacent organ resection as needed
   (liver, kidney, spleen, intestine, IVC, aorta, musculoskeletal soft tissue).
 • Intact specimen is essential — rupture gives dismal results. Challenged by many MIS surgeons, but the
   National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) also supports open surgery.
 • Advanced-stage 5-year survival <15%. Global recommendation: open adrenalectomy with intact removal
   for suspected ACC, especially larger tumours; multidisciplinary care (medical and radiation oncology,
   radiology, endocrinology, anaesthesiology, pathology) and tumour boards.

Surgery for Metastatic Lesions to the Adrenal
 • Metastases are the commonest malignant adrenal tumour, and the second commonest adrenal tumour
   after benign adenoma: 2.8–6.7% of adrenal tumours; prevalence 0.5–3.8%; median size about 3 cm;
   mostly unilateral, about a quarter bilateral.

SURGERY FOR ALL OTHER TUMOURS
Surgery for Myelolipoma
 • Benign: mature adipose plus haematopoietic tissue; usually asymptomatic and incidental; macroscopic
   fat with variable calcification on non-contrast imaging.
 • Frequency: up to 0.4% at autopsy; 10–15% of incidentalomas.
 • Presentation when symptomatic: abdominal pain, spontaneous retroperitoneal haemorrhage.
 • Most need no treatment. Operate if >7 cm, symptomatic, or adverse imaging features suggesting
   malignancy.
 • Still screen for phaeochromocytoma and Cushing syndrome — excludes phaeochromocytoma before any
   adrenalectomy.

Surgery for Metabolic Syndrome
 • Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)-independent Cushing syndrome from a functional tumour →
   abdominal obesity, hyperglycaemia, dyslipidaemia, hypertension; insulin resistance rises and insulin
   secretion falls.
 • Dyslipidaemia: high very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides and
   total cholesterol; low high-density lipoprotein (HDL); hepatic steatosis.
 • After adrenalectomy the response is variable: insulin resistance and impaired secretion may persist;
   complete resolution also described.

Surgery for Systemic Diseases
 • Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH): loss of a steroidogenic enzyme, overwhelmingly 21-hydroxylase
   → impaired cortisol and some aldosterone → precursors shunted to androgens.
 • Medical therapy since the 1950s: glucocorticoid ± mineralocorticoid; supraphysiological doses needed to
   suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary axis → iatrogenic Cushing syndrome.
 • Bilateral adrenalectomy: deliberate adrenal insufficiency with fixed replacement — reproducible, avoids
   Cushing syndrome. Concerns: adrenal crisis; gonadal (ectopic) adrenal rest tumours.
 • Meta-analysis: symptomatic improvement in most (hyperandrogenism, growth velocity, cushingoid
   features), with adrenal crisis and adrenal rest tumours in a minority.

PARTIAL ADRENALECTOMY
 • History: 1918 animal model — survival without insufficiency after removal of up to seven-eighths of the
   gland. 1934 — first human: subtotal bilateral adrenalectomy for hyperadrenalism via staged dorsal
   lumbotomies, no mortality. First partial adrenalectomy for tumour: performed 1965, reported 1983 —
   bilateral phaeochromocytoma, right total and left partial; off steroids 15 years later with a mass-free
   remnant.
 • Indications: children; bilateral phaeochromocytoma; Conn syndrome; non-functioning adenomas with
   severe hypertension; adrenal cysts; familial syndromes; prior contralateral adrenalectomy.

Surgical Technique
 • Approach as for the respective side; only the resection differs.
 • Arteries enter inferiorly, medially and superiorly → lateral dissection is safe; elevating the lateral portion

 • Preserve the arterial supply; the main adrenal vein can be divided — rich collateral venous drainage

 • Margins: intraoperative US; indocyanine green (ICG) to assess remnant viability.

 • Subcostal layers: anterior rectus sheath, rectus, external oblique, internal oblique, transversus abdominis,

 • Flank sequence: skin, subcutaneous fat, Scarpa's fascia → partially divide latissimus dorsi laterally and

Concept — incision choice trades exposure against morbidity along one axis — from the posterior approach
(most direct, least exposure) to the thoracoabdominal (most exposure, most morbidity) — with tumour size
and suspicion of malignancy deciding how far along it to go.

ABLATION OF THE ADRENAL
 • Ancient principle: Hippocrates held that what the knife cannot cure, fire cures. Most solid-organ ablation
   data come from liver, extended to prostate, kidney and now adrenal.
 • Routes: transcutaneous, percutaneous, laparoscopic; mostly by interventional radiology, but urologists
   must know the technology. Biopsy before or at ablation.

RFA mechanism: generator alternating current → electrode with uninsulated tip placed under US or CT →
ionic agitation around tip → frictional heat >50–60 °C → protein denaturation, coagulative necrosis. Cooled-
saline tip protects normal tissue and improves tumour conductivity; grounding pad required.
 • Ablation of phaeochromocytoma or ACC is highly discouraged outside a stringent research protocol.

FUTURE OF ADRENAL SURGERY
 • Robotics, big data, AI, genomics, virtual reality and AR with improved imaging — tools to strengthen
   care, never replacements for decision-making and technique.
 • AI in surgery is in its infancy: preoperative planning, intraoperative assistance, robotics. More used in
   orthopaedics and neurosurgery; abdominal barriers are patient diversity, complex and changing anatomy,
   and multimodal input. Reconstruction of the patient's anatomy allows simulated rehearsal.
 • Digital surgery: technology enhancing preoperative planning, surgical performance, therapeutic support
   or training to improve outcomes and minimise harm — already present (tissue discrimination, landmark
   identification, video review).
 • Most new urologists train and operate robotically; open surgery keeps a place by pathology; surgeons
   should shape next-generation platform design.
