Chapter 25 — Molecular Genetics and Cancer Biology
 • Molecular understanding of cancer follows from Watson and Crick's structure of DNA (1953); the first
   human genome sequence (early 2000s) and expanding sequencing technology accelerated discovery of
   genetic and epigenetic determinants of cancer.
 • Carcinogenesis is multistep and often takes many years; a normal cell must clear multiple hurdles.
 • At least eight attributes of the malignant cell: (1) genetic instability and mutation; (2) autonomous
   growth; (3) insensitivity to antiproliferative signals; (4) resistance to apoptosis and other induced cell
   suicide; (5) unlimited division potential; (6) angiogenesis; (7) local invasion — the feature that uniquely
   distinguishes malignant from benign; (8) immune evasion.
 • Cancer cells must also tolerate the stresses of their abnormal physiology and increase energy
   metabolism; tumour-associated inflammation can drive preneoplastic lesions to invasion; many acquire
   the lethal attribute of metastasis.

BASIC MOLECULAR GENETICS

Transcription
 • DNA → messenger RNA (mRNA) → protein. RNA polymerase II synthesises pre-mRNA complementary to
   the template, containing exons (coding) and introns (non-coding).
 • Three nuclear modifications before translation: 5′ capping, 3′ polyadenylation, splicing (introns removed,
   exons joined).
 • Alternative splicing lets one gene encode several proteins. Androgen receptor (AR) splice variants lacking
   the ligand-binding domain are active without ligand → may drive prostate cancer refractory to androgen
   ablation.

Protein Synthesis
 • Translation occurs in the cytoplasm on ribosomes.
 • Frameshift vs substitution: a single-base insertion or deletion shifts the frame and usually gives a non-
   functional protein; a single-base substitution may or may not change the amino acid, and may or may not
   alter function.
 • Transfer RNA (tRNA): 75–85-base adapter carrying one amino acid and a three-nucleotide anticodon.
   Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): ~80% of cellular RNA, part of the ribosome.
 • Stages: initiation (often rate-limiting; 40S subunit binds mRNA; start codon AUG → every protein begins
   with methionine) → elongation (fastest; several ribosomes may read one mRNA at once) → termination
   at one of three stop codons.

Chromosome and Gene Structure
 • Linear chromosomes are packaged by histones into chromatin; local chromatin structure governs
   expression and differs by cell type.
 • Euchromatin (actively expressed DNA) vs heterochromatin (inactive, possibly structural).

Tumour Suppressor Genes
 • Negatively regulate growth; roles in cell cycle, DNA repair, signalling. Loss → dysregulated growth and
   division despite stress and damage → further mutation.
 • Both alleles usually must be lost, by: (1) homozygous deletion; (2) loss of one allele + mutation of the
   other; (3) mutation of both; (4) loss of one allele + epigenetic silencing (promoter methylation) of the
   other. Classic examples: RB1, TP53.
 • Knudson's two-hit hypothesis (retinoblastoma): familial cases — often bilateral — inherit one non-
   functional allele, so a single somatic hit suffices.

 • Exceptions to two hits: dominant negative — the mutant protein disables the wild-type partner where
   proteins act as multimers (e.g. TP53); haploinsufficiency — one lost allele lowers protein enough to raise
   carcinogen susceptibility (e.g. CDKN1B / p27Kip1).

Oncogenes
 • Mutated, activated forms of normal proto-oncogenes that drive proliferation. Activation by: (1) activating
   mutation; (2) amplification (↑ copy number [CN] → ↑ transcript and protein; arises from replication
   errors, aneuploidy); (3) chromosomal rearrangement.
 • Examples: ERBB mutation → constitutively active epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). MYC (early-
   response transcription factor): amplified in prostate cancer, linked to immortalisation of prostate
   epithelium; amplified/overexpressed in bladder cancer (more than half of papillary and invasive tumours).
   MET (hepatocyte growth factor receptor): overexpressed in RCC, more in high grade; germline missense
   activation in hereditary papillary RCC. TMPRSS2–ERG fusion in a large proportion of prostate cancers.

 KEY POINTS: TUMOUR SUPPRESSOR GENES AND ONCOGENES
   • Somatic and germline testing identify alterations governing cancer risk and progression.
   • Tumour suppressors restrain growth; lost by four routes; some escape two-hit rule (dominant negative,
     haploinsufficiency).
   • Proto-oncogenes activated by mutation, amplification or rearrangement.


CELL CYCLE DEREGULATION
 • Division is tightly balanced against cell loss; human cells integrate external growth-stimulatory and
   growth-inhibitory signals to choose division or quiescence. In cancer, activated oncogenes and lost
   suppressors tip the balance permanently towards proliferation.
 • G0 = reversible quiescence, the default. The cycle is ordered and unidirectional: G1 → S (DNA synthesis)
   → G2 → M (mitosis), about 24 hours; each step requires completion of the previous.
 • Checkpoints monitor DNA integrity → pause for repair → apoptosis if irreparable. Oncogenes and
   suppressors act largely by disabling checkpoints and apoptosis; loss of damage sensing → genetic
   instability, driving progression, resistance and metastasis.

Cyclin-Dependent Kinases and Cyclins
 • Cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs): conserved serine/threonine kinases that phosphorylate phase-specific
   substrates.

Mitosis
 • Sequence: chromosomes condense (condensins); sister chromatids held by cohesins → centrosomes
   move to opposite poles and assemble spindle microtubules, which attach at centromeres via
   kinetochores → nuclear envelope and lamins dissolve (cyclin B–CDK1) → chromosomes align at midline
   (metaphase) → motors pull complements to poles (anaphase) → nuclei re-form, chromosomes
   decondense → cytokinesis.
 • Mitotic abnormalities in cancer were noted by von Hansemann (late 1800s); Boveri (1914) proposed they
   cause tumours.

Mitotic Arrest: The Spindle Assembly Checkpoint

Retinoblastoma (RB1) Tumour Suppressor
 • RB1 controls the late-G1 restriction point, beyond which the cell is irreversibly committed to divide;
   continuous proliferation in cancer is largely from loss of RB1 signalling.
 • Sequence: hypophosphorylated RB1 binds E2F and recruits histone deacetylases (HDACs) → S-phase
   genes silent → during G1, cyclin D–CDK4/6 and cyclin E–CDK2 phosphorylate RB1 at eight sites →
   hyperphosphorylated RB1 releases E2F → E2F (at least seven members) dimerises with DP1–3 →
   transcribes S-phase genes (DNA polymerase, S-phase cyclins) → after mitosis RB1 is dephosphorylated
   and represses E2F again.
 • Inputs converging on RB1 (Fig. 25.3): TGF-β via p15/p27; contact inhibition; DNA damage via ATM/ATR →
   TP53 → p21; oncogene stress via p19ARF; replicative senescence.
 • Ways cancers disable RB1: mutation or deletion; promoter hypermethylation; overexpression/
   amplification of cyclin D or E2F; loss (mutation, deletion, methylation) of CDKIs. The RB1 pathway (cyclins
   D/E, CDK4, E2F, p16, p27) is probably altered in almost all human cancers.

TP53 Tumour Suppressor
 • TP53 links checkpoints to genome integrity: DNA damage (via ATM/ATR → CHK1/CHK2) or oncogene
   activation (via ARF, which blocks MDM2) → phosphorylated, active TP53 → cell-cycle arrest, DNA repair,
   senescence or apoptosis; MDM2 is its negative regulator.
 • TP53 is the most commonly mutated gene in cancer and in a pan-cancer screen of metastatic disease;
   prominent in GU malignancies.
Concept — The cycle is a one-way ratchet driven by cyclin–CDK pulses and braked by CDKIs; RB1 guards
entry (G1/S) and TP53 guards integrity (G1/S and G2/M). Cancer needs to break both, which is why these
two pathways are lost in almost every tumour.

 KEY POINTS: CELL CYCLE DEREGULATION
   • Phase-specific CDK phosphorylation orchestrates G1 → S → G2 → M.
   • G1 → S depends on RB1 phosphorylation; RB1 mutations common in urological cancer.

DNA DAMAGE AND REPAIR
 • DNA polymerase plus error correction: about 3 incorrect bases per division in a genome of more than 3
   billion.
 • Cancer-causing change still arises via mutation, CN alteration and epigenetic change (methylation,
   chromatin condensation, promoter access), from endogenous (mitochondrial respiratory by-products)
   and exogenous (chemicals, radiation) agents.
 • Prevention: free-radical scavengers (α-tocopherol, vitamin C, carotenoids, bilirubin, urate) and
   detoxifying enzymes (superoxide dismutases, glutathione peroxidases, glutathione transferases).

DNA Repair Mechanisms
 • About 10 000 oxidative lesions per cell per day, plus spontaneous depurination, depyrimidination and
   deamination.
 • DNA damage response (DDR) = replication proofreading + specific repair systems, tightly integrated with
   the cycle: first step is arrest; ATM and ATR activate TP53, CHK1 and others.
 • Loeb's mutator hypothesis: the spontaneous mutation rate is too low to explain the number of mutations
   in most cancers → early loss of replication-fidelity genes produces a hypermutable phenotype, which is
   harder to treat because therapy rapidly selects resistant clones.
 • Recessive cancer-predisposition syndromes caused by repair defects — xeroderma pigmentosum, Bloom,
   Werner, ataxia telangiectasia, hereditary breast and colon cancer — implicate hypermutability in
   causation.
 • Three systems repair damage to bases themselves: NER, BER, MMR.

Double-Strand Break Repair
 • DSBs are potentially the most disruptive lesion; unrepaired → apoptosis — the basis of radiotherapy and
   many chemotherapeutics, so cells that abrogate this response resist therapy. Misrepair → mutations,
   deletions and the structural rearrangements nearly ubiquitous in solid tumours.
 • DSB response sequence: break recognised → ATM autophosphorylates/activates → phosphorylates
   TP53, CHEK1, CHEK2 (arrest) → phosphorylates histone variant H2AX → γH2AX, which spreads up to 30
   Mbp either side and recruits DDR factors into repair foci.

Concept — Each repair pathway is matched to a lesion (bulky → NER, subtle base → BER, mismatch →
MMR, DSB → HR or NHEJ), and each inherited defect produces a cancer syndrome. The same logic makes
repair defects therapeutic targets: HR loss → PARP inhibitor, MMR loss → immunotherapy.

 KEY POINTS: DNA DAMAGE AND REPAIR
   • DDR = damage recognition and repair; damage rarely causes cancer because repair is redundant.
   • NER — ultraviolet/chemicals; BER — deamination, oxidation, alkylation; MMR — polymerase mispairs;
     DSB repair — ionising radiation, free radicals.
   • Inherited repair defects → marked cancer susceptibility.

Regulators of Apoptosis
 • Balance of positive and negative signals; feed-forward loops make it irreversible once started. Signals
   converge on caspases (cysteine proteases with aspartate specificity; at least 13).
 • Initiator caspases 8, 9, 10 → executioner caspases 3, 6, 7; made as inactive procaspases, activated by
   cleavage in an amplifying cascade.
 • Executioner targets: anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 and Bcl-XL (cleavage releases pro-apoptotic fragments) →
   repair/replication proteins (DNA-PKcs, replication factor C) → nuclear structure (lamins, NuMA, SAF-A) →
   cytoskeleton (keratin, actin) → adhesion (β-catenin, focal adhesion kinase).

Senescence, Telomeres, and Autophagy: Protection Against Cancer
 • Senescence and autophagy (linked to apoptosis) handle oxidative damage and stress of ageing and limit
   proliferation and immortalisation; once cancer exists they also mediate resistance to radiotherapy and
   cytotoxics.
Replicative Senescence
 • First seen in cultured cells after replicative exhaustion (Hayflick); linked to loss of DNA methylation and
   telomere shortening. DNA damage, oncogenic signalling and oxidative stress cause induced/accelerated
   senescence.
 • Senescent cells are viable, metabolically active but growth-arrested (G1 or G2/M) via p16Ink4a,

   p21Waf1/Cip1, p27Kip1; they persist, unlike cells dying by apoptosis, autophagy or mitotic catastrophe.
 • Senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP): cytokines, chemokines, matrix-remodelling enzymes
   — immunostimulatory but potentially pro-tumour.
 • Linked to ageing, prostate cancer and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). Morphology: flattened,
   enlarged, prominent nucleus, granular cytoplasm; stains for senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-
   β-gal).
 • Often bypassed in cancer. p53-dependent route (ATM, Chk2, ATR → p21, key effector; forced p21 induces
   senescence even without p53; p63, p73 also involved; ARF stabilises p53 by blocking MDM2) vs p53-
   independent routes; transformed cells lacking p53 and Rb retain some capacity to senesce.
 • Therapy-induced senescence (TIS): ADT induces senescence in a subset of prostate cancers, and SASP
   may explain ADT-associated inflammation; enzalutamide can induce senescence via ↓ replication-
   licensing factor CDC6, partly through GATA2. Senescent stromal fibroblasts may promote cancer;
   removing senescent cells may improve chemotherapy → senescence is a double-edged sword.
Telomeres and Telomerase
 • Telomeres: repetitive chromosome-end sequences protecting against degradation and recombination;
   lose ~100 bp per division (lagging-strand RNA priming; polymerases cannot copy the ends) → the
   "mitotic clock".
 • Telomere sequence to cancer (Fig. 25.7): proliferation, oxidative stress, DNA damage → moderate
   shortening → DDR → senescence or apoptosis (barrier 1) → if checkpoint inactive, further erosion →
   severe shortening → chromosomal instability (CIN) generating oncogenic mutations but also lethal
   damage (barrier 2) → telomerase activation → stabilised telomeres and immortalisation → cancer;
   without it, death from CIN.
 • Most cancers have abnormally short telomeres; telomere-specific fluorescence in situ hybridisation
   (FISH) shows prostate cancer cells shorter than adjacent normal epithelium. Premalignant bladder and
   prostate lesions already short → early, causal role.
 • Short telomeres in primary prostate cancer predict recurrence independent of age, Gleason sum and
   nodal status; short, variable telomeres in stroma → higher prostate cancer death risk.
 • Telomerase: specialised reverse transcriptase adding telomeric repeats; restricted in somatic tissue to
   rapidly proliferating tissues (intestinal epithelium, endometrium, testis, activated lymphocytes). Forced
   expression → immortalisation. Supplies two functions: quelling CIN and unlimited replication. 85–90% of
   human tumours express high telomerase.
 • Alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT): telomerase-independent recombination, associated with
   ATRX or DAXX inactivation.
 • Therapy: imetelstat (telomerase inhibitor) in myelodysplastic syndrome; small molecules, peptides and
   hTERT-based immunotherapies (UV1, GX301, INO-9012) in early prostate, renal and bladder trials.

Autophagy
 • Conserved intracellular self-defence: organelles and cytoplasm sequestered in autophagic vesicles →
   fused with lysosomes → degraded. Discovered in yeast (Nobel Prize, Ohsumi).
 • Low basal level; upregulated by starvation, growth-factor withdrawal, aggregated proteins, damaged
   organelles, intracellular pathogens. Above the "autophagy threshold" → death by necrosis, mitotic
   catastrophe or apoptosis. Autophagy-dependent cell death is a defined form of regulated death
   (Nomenclature Committee on Cell Death).
 • Signalling: ROS, growth factors, metabolic signals → mTOR and AMPK → ULK1/ULK2 → phosphorylates
   Beclin-1 → autophagy-related (ATG) genes (ATG5, ATG12; about 30 known).
 • Context-dependent role: BECN1 loss raises cancer in mice and is frequent in human breast and prostate
   cancer (tumour suppressor); in early (e.g. KRAS-driven) tumours autophagy limits instability,
   inflammation and damage; but in hypoxic, nutrient-poor tumour cores it supports survival; in BRAF
   models it suppresses benign tumours yet its inhibition accelerates advanced cancers.
 • Therapy-induced autophagy is usually cytoprotective. KRAS-inhibited tumours become autophagy-
   dependent → MEK/ERK + autophagy inhibitors. Chloroquine/hydroxychloroquine (lysosomotropic, raise
   pH) may synergise with chemotherapy; after enzalutamide, clomipramine or chloroquine increased
   apoptosis in CRPC cells. No impact yet on urological cancer management.

Concept — Senescence, telomere attrition and autophagy are barriers that stop damaged cells proliferating,
but each can be co-opted by an established tumour to survive therapy — hence the recurring "induce or
inhibit?" dilemma.

 KEY POINTS: SENESCENCE, TELOMERES AND AUTOPHAGY
   • Replicative senescence = growth arrest from methylation loss and/or telomere shortening.
   • Telomeres = mitotic clock; shortening is a barrier to unrestrained growth.
   • Autophagy clears aggregates, organelles, pathogens; extreme stress beyond the threshold kills.


Cancer Epigenetics
 • Disrupted epigenetic programming is a hallmark of cancer: DNA methylation, histone modification and
   non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) alter chromatin accessibility → silence repair and cell-cycle (suppressor)
   genes and permit growth-promoting genes.
 • Reversible → drug target: monotherapy success in haematological tumours; combinations with
   hormonal, chemo- or immunotherapy promising in solid tumours.
DNA Methylation

   it reflects a balance of pro- and anti-angiogenic regulators from stroma, leucocytes and tumour cells.

 • Tumour oxygen tension is low; via HIF-1, cells shift to ↑ glucose transport, glycolysis to pyruvate and
   lactate, ↓ mitochondrial metabolism and mass.

 • CA9, MCT4, NHE1 keep intracellular pH alkaline (proliferation) and extracellular pH acidic (invasion);

 • Warburg effect (Nobel Prize 1931): aerobic glycolysis — best understood in clear cell RCC, where VHL loss

 • Hypoxia induces N- and E-cadherin, vimentin, MMP2, MMP9 → invasion and metastasis.
 • Immune effect: HIF-1α, VEGF, IL-6, IL-10, PD-L1 and TGF-β enhance MDSC suppression, recruit tumour-
   associated macrophages and block DC maturation → modulating this environment is central to modern
   renal cancer therapy.
Concept — VHL loss is permanent pseudohypoxia: the cell behaves as if starved of oxygen, and
angiogenesis, Warburg metabolism, invasion and immunosuppression all follow from that single signal —
which is why clear cell RCC responds to VEGF, HIF-2α and immune blockade.

Cell Plasticity and Pluripotency
 • Stem cells: self-renewal, generation of progeny, specialised niches, ability to form all cell types of an
   organ; share with cancer cells expanded replicative potential, telomere elongation, damage resistance,
   undifferentiated state — debate whether cancers co-opt stem properties or arise from stem cells.
 • Lineage plasticity: epigenetic and phenotypic change, usually after extreme pharmacological targeting of
   a driver; cells trans-differentiate to another lineage with new drivers and biomarkers.

RNA-Sequencing Panels
 • Whole-transcriptome sequencing (comprehensive, costly — research discovery) → targeted panels
   (cheaper, deeper, more accurate).
 • Expression carries information DNA cannot; cell-cycle/proliferation signatures underpin most clinical
   prognostic biomarkers in localised prostate cancer. Older assays use arrays or quantitative polymerase
   chain reaction (qPCR) rather than RNA-seq.

Cell-Free and Circulating Tumour DNA
 • Metastatic biopsies are infrequent → cfDNA "liquid biopsy" (blood; increasingly urine for GU cancer),
   mostly targeted panels.
 • Only a small (sometimes tiny) fraction of cfDNA is circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) → very deep
   sequencing; more false positives and negatives than tissue, worse without germline.
 • Clonal haematopoiesis (CHIP): common age-related myeloid mutations; since most non-tumour cfDNA
   comes from leucocytes, they mimic somatic tumour variants at single-digit VAF.
 • ctDNA fraction is prognostic across cancers → minimal residual disease (MRD) assays: WES of primary →
   bespoke hypertargeted panel of clonal alterations → ultra-deep sequencing after definitive treatment
   → guide adjuvant therapy.
 • Emerging: cfDNA methylation (confounded by ctDNA fraction) and fragmentomics (fragment size and
   position; detects cancer even at low ctDNA fraction).

Circulating Tumour Cells
 • Haematogenous spread requires tumour cells in blood. Detection by enrichment (e.g. anti-EpCAM
   antibody, size) or capture-free imaging; enumeration is prognostic.
 • Molecular profiling: AR-V7 (splice variant lacking ligand-binding domain; constitutively active),
   neuroendocrine markers, PD-L1 on RCC circulating tumour cells (CTCs).

   50% = 1.5% — undetectable in most trials.

   including both biomarker groups — large samples, so many phase III trials enrol biomarker-positive
   patients only.
