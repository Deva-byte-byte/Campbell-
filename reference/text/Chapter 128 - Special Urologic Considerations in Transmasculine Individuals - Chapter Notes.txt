Chapter 128 — Special Urologic Considerations in
Transmasculine Individuals
  • Gender identity is a person's own sense of gender, which may not conform to the sex assigned at birth,
    its gender role, or primary and secondary sex characteristics.
  • Sex versus gender: sex denotes biological differences (chromosomes, hormonal profile, internal and
    external sex organs); gender denotes the characteristics a society or culture designates as masculine or
    feminine.
  • Gender dysphoria (GD) is the mismatch between gender identity and sex; significant distress meets
    criteria for a formal diagnosis:
FORMAL DIAGNOSTIC TERMS FOR GENDER DYSPHORIA

 Classification                                                                       Diagnostic term

 Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th edition (DSM-IV)          Gender identity disorder (GID)
 DSM, 5th edition (DSM-5; American Psychiatric Association, 2013)                     Gender dysphoria

  • Prevalence (largest epidemiological review): male-to-female (transwomen) 1:11,900 to 1:45,000; female-
    to-male (transmen) 1:30,400 to 1:200,000 — transwomen are more prevalent than transmen.
  • Prevalence is rising with public awareness and research, as is the number of transgender and gender-
    nonconforming (TGN) youth over the last decade.
  • World Professional Association for Transgender Health (WPATH) treatment guidelines have been
    published since 1979; Version 8 Standards of Care (SOC8) appeared in 2022.
  • Gender-confirming surgery (GCS) in transmasculine individuals may include mastectomy, hystero-
    oophorectomy, metoidioplasty, phalloplasty and testicular or erectile prostheses; because genital and
    urinary tracts are intimately related, genital surgery affects the urinary tract.

UROLOGIC ISSUES IN GENDER-NONCONFORMING TRANSMASCULINE YOUTHS
  • Presentation has increased over two decades, with a bimodal peak: children with severe GD, and
    adolescents at or just after puberty.
  • Atypical presentation (mood and behavioural problems) is common, as is bullying and harassment at
    home, school and in the community.
  • A matched retrospective cohort study showed a substantially increased risk of depression, anxiety
    disorder, suicidal ideation, suicide attempt and self-harm.
  • They rarely present to (paediatric) urology, but guidelines exist for social transition, puberty blockers,
    gender-affirming hormones and surgery.
  • Age for surgery: in most countries, the age of consent; in some, chest surgery and gonadectomy from 16
    years. More adolescents are expected to present for gender-affirming surgery (GAS).
  • Urological problems are rare. Some develop lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) from altered toilet
    behaviour: avoiding public toilets can lead to an underactive detrusor (unpublished observation).

  • Early hormonal care alters later surgery (unpublished observations): the oestrogen-naive vagina makes
    vaginectomy much more complicated, and underdeveloped labia majora leave less tissue for scrotal

Concept — Treating youth early prevents female pubertal development, and the tissue that development
would have produced is the tissue the reconstructive surgeon later needs; early medical benefit is paid for in
surgical difficulty.

UROLOGIC ISSUES IN TRANSMASCULINE INDIVIDUALS
  • A long pathway of staged invasive procedures: secondary sex characteristics, removal of female and
    creation of male primary sex organs, secondary prosthetic surgery, and non-genital, non-breast
    procedures.
  • Care is patient-based, not provider-based: the dichotomous view of gender is no longer central, and
    patients choose their own goals from a "menu" rather than receiving the provider's assumed ideal — so
    many requests lie between the binary poles.
  • Most nonetheless seek four goals: a normal-looking male genital appearance; voiding standing;
    preserved erotic sensation; penetrative sexual activity.
  • A dedicated urologist in the gender team is advisable.

Diagnosis and Preoperative Care
  • Preoperative urological screening is mandatory, because GCS affects the lower urinary tract through
    vaginectomy, pelvic floor reconstruction and urethral lengthening.

Diagnosis
  • GD is diagnosed according to WPATH guidelines, in a GD centre with a full multidisciplinary team (MDT):
    psychologists, psychiatrists, endocrinologists, gynaecologists, social workers, plastic surgeons and
    urologists.
  • GCS, if wished, is often, but not necessarily, the last and most considered step.

Gender-Confirming Surgery
  • After confirmed diagnosis and extensive counselling, the patient may choose GCS.
  • Most transgender individuals never undergo external GCS (a minority had surgically transitioned in a
    2011 United States national survey).

Preoperative Care
  • Urethral lengthening, vaginectomy and pelvic floor reconstruction each impair postoperative voiding;
    any pre-existing lower urinary tract dysfunction (LUTD) must be diagnosed and treated before surgery.
  • Work-up: history + uroflowmetry with post-void residual (PVR) + renal ultrasound, excluding stones or
    hydronephrosis, which are harder to treat after GCS, especially after urethral lengthening.
  • WPATH: at least 12 months of continuous hormone therapy before masculinising GCS. The main reason
    is that testosterone induces clitoral hypertrophy, with growth saturating at about 2 years.
  • Hormone-blocking agents — gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists such as triptorelin
    (Decapeptyl) — are increasingly used in under-age individuals, giving time to explore gender
    nonconformity (effects on surgery below).

 GENDER-CONFIRMING SURGERY IN TRANSMASCULINE INDIVIDUALS CAN CONSIST OF
   1. Removal of feminine anatomic structures: mastectomy, oophorectomy, hysterectomy and
      vaginectomy.
   2. Creation of masculine primary sex organs (genitoperineal transformation): phalloplasty with or
      without urethroplasty, metoidioplasty with or without urethroplasty, scrotoplasty, monsplasty and
      perineal reconstruction.
   3. Implantation of testicular and erectile prostheses.

Concept — Every step of genitoperineal reconstruction can worsen voiding, so the bladder is assessed and
treated first, and testosterone is given long enough to grow the clitoris even though it simultaneously makes
the vagina harder to remove.

Removal of Female Anatomic Structures
Mastectomy, Hysterectomy, and Ovariectomy
  • Mastectomy is usually first: subcutaneous — glandular tissue removed, overlying skin and part of the
    nipple preserved; type by anatomy and surgeon.
  • Ovariectomy and hysterectomy preferably laparoscopic (less morbidity and scarring); otherwise, or on
    conversion, Pfannenstiel incision.
  • Ureteric injury: the ureters cross the iliac vessels and run to the trigone beneath the uterine artery.
    Injury → the urologist is called for immediate repair, the type depending on extent and location.
  • Bladder injury (small risk) → immediate repair.
  • Remove the entire cervix and, if possible, the proximal third of the vagina at hysterectomy, to facilitate
    later vaginectomy from the perineum.
  • The three may be combined in one procedure to minimise operations.

Vaginectomy
  • Usually combined with genitoperineal transformation, not performed separately.

  • A hazardous procedure. Major complications, in descending frequency: haemorrhage requiring
    reintervention > bladder injury > rectal injury.
  • Given this severity, and because vaginectomy is not necessary, it is discussed; some keep the vagina for
    intimacy.
  • No vaginectomy → no urethroplasty: lengthening the urethra without vaginectomy significantly
    increases the risk of urethrovaginal fistula.
  • Two options: total vaginectomy; or leave the upper vagina and fulgurate its mucosa to prevent a
    mucocele.
Technique of total vaginectomy.
 1. Hydrodissection: the plane between the anterior vaginal wall and the urethra–bladder is infiltrated with
    dilute xylocaine (lidocaine) (Fig. 128.1).
 2. Introitus incised circumferentially → vagina grasped with two mosquito clamps anteriorly and two
    posteriorly.
 3. Rectovaginal plane: usually easy, by blunt digital dissection → a gauze is placed to protect the rectum
    (Fig. 128.2).
 4. Urethrovaginal plane: sharp dissection with tenotomy scissors; no diathermy, to avoid thermal injury to
    the sphincter (Fig. 128.3A) → then the bladder–vaginal plane by blunt and sharp dissection. A
    submucosal plane in the vaginal wall protects the bladder; a bladder injury is usually midline and closed
    primarily; if the integrity of the ureteric orifices is in doubt → cystoscopy.
 5. Lateral walls: mosquitoes replaced by two Jean-Louis Faure clamps (Fig. 128.3B) → levator dissected off
    the lateral wall → high-energy cautery for haemostasis of the paravaginal plexus; persistent bleeding →
    Vicryl (polyglactin 910) 3-0 sutures.
 6. Rectal gauze removed → haemostasis; haemostatic agents in the space between rectum and bladder.
 7. Levators approximated in the midline with two to three Vicryl 1-0 sutures (Fig. 128.4) → prevents later
    prolapse of bladder or rectum.
 8. Bulbospongiosus closed over the fixed part of the urethra → perineal wound closed in layers.

Scrotoplasty
  • Hair-bearing labia majora form the neoscrotum.
  • Simple midline closure over the former introitus leaves an unaesthetic scrotum between the legs; the
    cisgender scrotum lies in front of the legs — hence flap rotation (Ghent-type scrotoplasty, Fig. 128.9).
 1. Labia majora incised dorsally and semicircumferentially and mobilised → bilateral labial flaps supplied by
    branches of the external pudendal artery.
 2. Remnants of non-hair-bearing labia minora and clitoral foreskin resected from the flaps.
 3. Flaps turned 180 degrees medially and sutured at the top of the perineal skin.
 4. Perineum closed with subcutaneous polyglactin 1-0 — large calibre because tension is significant →
    flaps sutured to each other and perineal skin closed with absorbable Vicryl 3-0.

Phalloplasty
  • Many free and pedicled flaps have been described — meaning no single flap achieves all the ideal
    goals.
  • Usually by a plastic surgeon; flap choice by expertise, patient preference and local anatomy (previous
    pelvic or genitoperineal surgery, body mass index (BMI)).

Metoidioplasty
  • An alternative to phalloplasty for transmasculine individuals for whom penetration is a secondary goal
    or not a goal.
  • First described by Lebovic and Laub (1999) and popularised by Perovic at about the same time.
 1. Hormonally enlarged clitoris → chordee divided.
 2. Clitoris released from its labia minora attachments.
 3. Wide ventral urethral plate released — the plate is what bends the clitoris downward → maximal length
    gained, but the urethra must now be lengthened.
 4. Fixed part reconstructed as described above.
 5. Urethra extended to the tip of the glans with a transverse preputial island flap or a labia minora flap,
    which may be combined with buccal mucosa (authors: transverse preputial island flap).
  • Variants differ in the extent of clitoral release and in scrotal reconstruction.

Postoperative Care, Follow-up, and Urethral Complications
Early Postoperative Care
  • Focus is recovery of micturition. After urethral lengthening:
 1. Suprapubic catheter (SPC) + trans-neourethral catheter left in situ.
 2. Neourethral catheter removed at 14–21 days → patient voids with the SPC in place.
 3. SPC closed with a tap, opened after every void to measure PVR, recorded in a bladder diary.
 4. Voiding may be delayed by swelling or neurogenic bladder impairment after vaginectomy → SPC kept
    until normal function returns. Other care is standard urogenital care.

Transurethral Catheterization in Transmasculine Individuals After Gender-Confirming Surgery
  • Why difficult: an abrupt curve into the native urethra, and two small sinuses toward the Bartholin's
    gland orifices on each side of the native meatus, in which the catheter tip catches.
  • Elective catheterisation by the urologist:
 1. One blind attempt with a Coudé catheter — abort at any difficulty.
 2. Second attempt with a mandrin (stylet), which negotiates the sharp curve at the native meatus.
 3. Any difficulty → stop (to avoid urethral trauma and later stricture) → flexible cystoscopy with guidewire
    into the bladder → open-tip catheter over the wire.
  • Emergency: a suprapubic catheter is always a good solution.

Urethral Complications in Transmasculine Individuals After Gender-Confirming Surgery
  • Urethral complications are frequent after phalloplasty, affecting a large proportion of patients.
Urethral fistula.
  • A common early complication, related to delayed wound healing of the phallus.
  • Site: base of the phallus (commonest) > shaft; perineal fistulae are rare because local tissue covers the
    urethra well there. Without vaginectomy, urethrovaginal fistulae can arise (shown on voiding
    cystography, Fig. 128.10).
 1. Early: the catheter is visible through a wound dehiscence.
 2. Maintain urinary diversion (urethral or suprapubic) to avoid further wound maceration until the wound
    heals.
 3. Diversion removed → voiding through the fistula; small fistulae often close spontaneously.
 4. Persisting beyond 3 months = permanent → surgical closure.
  • Repair principles: excise the tract, close urethra and skin, and interpose a healthy layer — no dartos
    exists, so only local fat is available. Catheter for 10–12 days after repair.
  • Healing is slower than in males: flap skin, not mucosa within spongiosum.
Urethral stricture — incidence.
  • Common, again from phallic healing problems: most lie in the phallic urethra, at the anastomosis or at
    the meatus — the latter two at the flap extremities, where blood supply is poorest.
  • Commonest site: the anastomosis between the fixed and phallic parts — a mucocutaneous junction
    prone to stricture from delayed healing.
  • Most anastomotic strictures are small and appear early after phalloplasty; symptoms and signs are as in
    males.
Diagnosis.
  • Plateau uroflow curve → retrograde urethrography (RUG) is necessary: confirms, locates and measures;
    three urethral parts distinguishable (Fig. 128.11) → urethroscopy if doubt.
Management — principles.
  • Defer any treatment ≥6 months after phalloplasty, until wound healing stabilises; reconstruction during
    acute inflammation is useless.
  • Severe obstruction or retention → SPC first line.
  • Perineal urethrostomy if a long wait is expected, SPC causes severe complaints (e.g. bladder spasm) or is
    refused; closed at or after successful reconstruction.
  • Choice depends on previous interventions, stricture location, length and concomitant phallic problems
    (e.g. flap contracture). The same techniques apply after metoidioplasty.

Urethral hair growth.
  • A skin-flap urethra (the usual case) can grow hair; usually harmless, but complications are important and
    difficult — so prevent.
  • Prevention: non- or minimally hair-bearing flap (e.g. ulnar forearm for a radial forearm flap); otherwise
    preoperative laser depilation (strongly recommended).
  • Substantial growth → hair bezoar with stone formation → difficult voiding, local irritation, urethral
    discharge. Bezoar and calculus formation, not hair growth itself, causes the voiding complaints.
 1. Phallic urethra opened ventrally, calculi removed.
 2. Urethral edges sutured to the opened phallic skin.
 3. Urethral hair treated by laser epilation.
 4. Later: plate retubularised, as in the second stage of a two-stage urethroplasty.
Postvoid dribbling.
  • Nearly universal. The neourethra is a rigid tube without spongiosum elasticity, and reconstructed
    bulbospongiosus expels the last drops poorly.
  • Advice: perineal pressure plus squeezing of the phallus at the end of micturition.

  • Severe dribbling → urethrography or urethroscopy to exclude urethral sacculation or a

Concept — Every urethral complication traces back to the flap: skin in fat, with poorly perfused ends and no

CONCLUSION
  • GCS is done in few specialised centres, but many have undergone it, so any urologist may meet them.
  • Every urologist should be able to manage the acute urological problem; thereafter, refer complications
    to an experienced centre.
