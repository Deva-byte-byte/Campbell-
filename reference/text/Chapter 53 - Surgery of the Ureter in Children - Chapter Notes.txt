URETEROPELVIC JUNCTION OBSTRUCTION

Definition
 • Paediatric hydronephrosis is defined by an anteroposterior (AP) renal pelvic diameter of 12 mm; it
   differs from adult hydronephrosis and is not synonymous with obstruction.
 • Koff's definition is the only gold-standard prognosticator of ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) obstruction
   (UPJO): obstruction is “any restriction to urinary outflow that, if left untreated, will lead to progressive
   kidney damage.”
 • The definition is retrospective: the indication for surgery is often established only after renal function has
   been irrevocably lost.
 • Guidelines try to get around this, but with imprecise diagnostic tools, surgical indications remain
   debatable.
Concept — Obstruction in the child is defined by its consequence, not its appearance. Dilatation is only a
surrogate, so every indication for surgery in this chapter is an attempt to predict harm before it happens.

Clinical Presentation
 • Antenatal ultrasound at about 20 weeks' gestation: urogenital anomalies are the dominant
   malformations, and among them hydronephrosis or hydroureteronephrosis is the commonest.
 • Postnatal diagnosis is by ultrasound, either incidentally or during workup of urinary tract infection (UTI).
   Febrile UTI usually responds to antibiotics; only a minority need nephrostomy when there is coexisting
   dilatation.

Secondary Ureteropelvic Junction Obstruction
 • High-grade vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) with a tortuous dilated ureter can kink or narrow the UPJ.
   Whether it resolves on its own or needs correction of both lesions is unclear.

 • Endoscopic injection is unreliable for high-grade VUR, which pushes towards open reimplantation.
   Simultaneous reimplantation plus dismembered pyeloplasty is discouraged because it risks
   devascularising the ureter from both ends.
 • A subadventitial dismemberment of the UPJ has been described to protect the blood supply. Despite
   weak evidence, the advisable course is conservative management with a two-stage operation.

Lower Pole Ureteropelvic Junction Obstruction
 • Lower-moiety UPJO in a duplex kidney is uncommon.
 • Incomplete duplication → ureteropyelostomy of the upper-pole ureter to the lower-pole pelvis, side-to-
   side or end-to-side.
 • Complete duplication → standard dismembered pyeloplasty, guided by the function of each moiety and
   the length of the stenosis.
 • A non- or minimally functioning moiety (≤5–10% of total function on nuclear scan) → partial
   nephrectomy, not repair.
Figure legend, Fig. 53.3 — In a duplex kidney with lower-UPJ stenosis, a V-plasty can resolve the obstruction.

Associated Anomalies
 • Up to 50% of affected infants have another urological abnormality.
 • The commonest is contralateral UPJO (10–40%), followed by renal dysplasia → multicystic dysplastic
   kidney → renal agenesis.
 • Horseshoe kidney is rare but associated, through either a true stricture or accessory vessels. VATER
   syndrome also has some association.
 • VUR occurs in up to 40% of children with UPJO, with a high rate of spontaneous resolution.

Surgical Indication
 • About one-third need surgery; most cases resolve with time.
 • Accepted indications: increasing AP diameter on ultrasound · low or falling differential renal function
   (DRF) · breakthrough infection on prophylaxis · symptoms (pain in older children).
 • In infants, UPJO can cause failure to thrive, feeding difficulty and constipation.
 • Haematuria comes from rupture of mucosal vessels in the dilated pelvis. UPJO is a rare cause of
   hypertension, through functional deterioration and renin–angiotensin activation.

Surgical Repair
 • In infants, open surgery is preferred, because the incision is hardly larger than laparoscopic or robotic
   access requires. The minimally invasive route is feasible and gives less pain and better cosmesis.
 • Most paediatric urologists prefer the extraperitoneal approach when operating open. For minimally
   invasive surgery, the transperitoneal versus retroperitoneal debate continues, with few proper
   comparative or randomised studies.

Dismembered Pyeloplasty
 • Anderson–Hynes dismembered pyeloplasty is the gold standard whatever the access route. It was
   originally devised for a retrocaval ureter, and its success rate is about 95%.
 • Why dismember: a crossing vessel is preserved (the ureter is transposed anterior to it), the adynamic
   segment is excised and redundant pelvis is reduced.
 • Long dysplastic segment → full renal mobilisation bridges several centimetres. A horizontal incision in
   the lower pelvis adds length for a tension-free anastomosis (“bypass” configuration, Fig. 53.5).
Sequence: open Gerota fascia → dissect the anterior or posterior UPJ, according to access → free the lower
pole so an accessory vessel is not missed, especially retroperitoneoscopically → use bipolar diathermy
sparingly to protect the pelvic and ureteric blood supply → stay sutures in the pelvis above the planned
division and in the ureter at the stenosis → decompress a tense pelvis with a 21-gauge needle to avoid over-
reduction → dismember and reduce the pelvis if indicated → spatulate laterally well beyond the stenosis
into healthy ureter → leave the stenotic segment attached as a handle (“no-touch”, less mucosal oedema)
→ anastomose with 6-0 or 5-0 absorbable suture, interrupted or continuous, tension-free at the crotch of
the “V” → avoid excess stay-suture tension, which kinks the ureter once released → excise the stenotic
segment just before completion → irrigate the pelvis to clear clot → return the kidney and cover the
anastomosis with perinephric fat. A Penrose drain is usually not needed.
 • Stenting (externalised nephro-ureteric versus internal double-J) is a matter of surgeon preference. In
   infants, an externalised stent reduces complications and length of stay compared with no stent.
 • Removing a double-J usually needs another anaesthetic. An extraction string avoids this but raises the
   risk of febrile UTI, especially in girls with previous UTI. Complication rates for pigtail and externalised
   stents do not differ.
 • Stent duration of 1–4 weeks does not affect outcome.

Minimally Invasive Techniques
Endoscopic approach.
 • Antegrade and retrograde endopyelotomy became popular in the late 1980s–1990s with variable results.
   Balloon dilatation under 18 years performs poorly long-term. Endopyelotomy does better for secondary
   (scar) UPJO than for primary UPJO.
 • Antegrade endopyelotomy is limited to older children and adolescents with moderate hydronephrosis.
   It needs preoperative vessel screening by computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
   or endoluminal ultrasound, plus fluoroscopy and its radiation exposure, so it is unattractive in children.
 • Its niche is failed primary pyeloplasty, where the anatomy and the absence of a vessel are already known
   → double-J for 6 weeks. Even so, redo pyeloplasty remains the best choice in most cases.

Laparoscopic pyeloplasty.
 • First reported in 1993; the transabdominal paediatric series and the retroperitoneal approach in infants
   and children followed. It is now the method of choice at many centres.
 • Meta-analysis: laparoscopic outcome is comparable to open, with robotics an attractive alternative.
 • Transperitoneal or retroperitoneal: the retroperitoneal route is theoretically safer and is the open route
   of choice, but comparisons show no significant difference in outcome or complications (see the access
   table above).

Robot-assisted pyeloplasty.
 • Paediatric reports began in the early 2000s. As with laparoscopy, the transperitoneal route is more
   widely used, because the retroperitoneal space in children is confined, orientation is difficult and the
   instruments are relatively large.
 • Shorter, more efficient learning curve than laparoscopy, owing to three-dimensional (3D) vision and
   instrument dexterity.
 • Compared with open surgery: comparable outcome, with faster resolution of hydronephrosis in the
   robotic group.

Vascular hitch.
 • Hellström and Vahlquist's vascular hitch (1951) is regaining favour laparoscopically because it is easier
   than pyeloplasty. The vessel is freed from the UPJ, moved cranially on the pelvis and embedded in pelvic
   tissue with non-absorbable sutures.
 • Only for an anterior lower-pole crossing vessel with NO intrinsic UPJ obstruction. Selection criteria:
   moderate hydronephrosis, no calyceal dilatation, well-preserved cortex, poor drainage with preserved
   split function, lower-pole crossing vessel. At operation, a normal ureter and UPJ with visible peristalsis.
 • Long-term data are limited but comparable to dismembered pyeloplasty. The feared hypertension from
   embedding the vessel has not been observed.

Stenting
 • Most surgeons stent the UPJ, open or minimally invasive. It lowers the risk of leakage and urinoma and,
   transperitoneally, of ileus from urinary ascites.
 • Stentless repair demands meticulous tissue handling to prevent oedema, obstruction and leak. Anderson
   and Hynes themselves held splinting to be “against all the principles of plastic procedure.”
 • Antegrade double-J can be hard to pass through the narrow infant ureterovesical junction (UVJ). Bladder
   position is confirmed with methylene blue instilled into the bladder.
 • Retrograde stent placed just before surgery makes the anastomosis harder. If placed days to weeks
   before, as an emergency, UPJ inflammation makes repair more difficult. Emergency diversion close to
   definitive surgery should therefore be a nephrostomy, which inflames the UPJ less.
 • Extraction-string or magnetic retrograde double-J avoids the second anaesthetic.

Outcome and Complications
 • Pyeloplasty is safe, with 90–100% success. However, “success” is defined inconsistently (symptom relief,
   stable or improved function, falling AP diameter, or, most rigidly, no reintervention), which makes studies
   hard to compare.
 • Bleeding is controlled with bipolar diathermy and is the commonest reason for conversion, followed by
   lack of progress and difficult UPJ dissection.
 • Early failure signs: infection, pain and increasing dilatation. These are often due to clot or oedema, which
   settle in days, sometimes with temporary nephrostomy.

 • Severe ureteric scarring, too short for tension-free repair, plus a dilated lower-pole calyx with thin
   parenchyma → uretero-calicostomy (open, laparoscopic or robotic).

 KEY POINTS: PYELOPLASTY
   • UPJO is intrinsic narrowing or extrinsic obstruction by a lower-pole vessel passing anterior to the UPJ
     or proximal ureter.
   • Commonest associated anomaly: contralateral UPJO, then renal dysplasia and multicystic dysplastic
     kidney.
   • Dismembered pyeloplasty is the favoured repair.
   • Laparoscopy gives excellent views and cosmesis and matches open results in the short term; robotic
     assistance eases the anastomosis.
   • Complications: prolonged drainage, failure of function or hydronephrosis to improve → endoscopic
     treatment or, more often, redo dismembered pyeloplasty.

Concept — Repair of UPJO has one fixed principle and many variable routes. Dismember, excise, spatulate
and anastomose without tension. Access, stent and robot are choices of convenience around that constant,
and failure is managed by repeating the same principle.

Ureteral Strictures
 • Congenital ureteral strictures are rare. They are detected antenatally as dilatation and usually
   misdiagnosed as hydronephrosis or megaureter.
 • Postnatal ultrasound, MRI and retrograde pyelography confirm a mid-ureteric stricture.
 • Treatment: excision and ureteroureterostomy. Excised segments are 1–3 cm long and show increased
   collagen and muscular hypertrophy.

Ureteral Polyps
 • Rare. Usually upper third of the ureter → UPJO. Male and left-sided predominance; single or multiple; up
   to several centimetres.
 • Slow-growing and benign, so they usually present later in childhood with pain and haematuria rather
   than antenatally.
 • Imaging: ultrasound, contrast CT, MR urography. Confirmed by retrograde pyelography or ureteroscopy.
 • Treatment: endoscopic polypectomy; dismembered pyeloplasty with resection when near the UPJ; or
   segmental resection with ureteroureterostomy.
 • Histology: benign fibroepithelial polyp.

Retroperitoneal Fibrosis in Children
 • Extremely rare in children. Mostly idiopathic; also autoimmune, infective or neoplastic.
 • Signs: falling renal function, bilateral hydronephrosis, hypertension. Imaging: ultrasound and MRI.

VESICOURETERIC REFLUX

Definition and Clinical Presentation
 • VUR is the abnormal retrograde flow of urine from the bladder to the upper tract. When symptomatic, it
   causes UTI, pyelonephritic scarring and, at worst, end-stage renal disease.
 • High-grade VUR is associated with voiding dysfunction; which causes which is unresolved.
 • The aim of surgery is one-way, unobstructed, antegrade flow, achieved by reimplanting the ureter in an
   antirefluxing fashion. There are open, laparoscopic and endoscopic options.

Surgical Management

 KEY POINTS: SURGICAL PRINCIPLES OF VUR CORRECTION
   • Exclude or treat secondary causes of reflux first.
   • Handle the ureter gently to preserve its blood supply.
   • Handle the bladder gently to limit postoperative haematuria and spasm.
   • Mobilise enough ureter for a tension-free anastomosis.
   • Tunnel length-to-diameter ratio 5:1 (Paquin).
   • Keep the course straight, allowing for bladder distension, which can angulate or kink the ureter at
     either extreme of filling.

 • Classification of procedures: by access (open or laparoscopic), approach (intravesical or extravesical) and
   tunnel position relative to the original hiatus (suprahiatal or infrahiatal).
 • Open set-up: supine, hips slightly abducted. Prepare abdomen, perineum and genitalia so the urethra can
   be catheterised in the sterile field. Prophylactic intravenous (IV) antibiotics in most cases.

Incision
 • Pfannenstiel incision in a skin crease 2 cm above the symphysis, to the lateral edges of the recti →
   anterior rectus sheath opened transversely and raised from symphysis to just below the umbilicus → recti
   separated in the midline along the linea alba → perivesical space.
 • Vertical lower midline incision for redo surgery, because it allows more cranial ureteric mobilisation.
 • A moderately filled bladder is easier to identify and mobilise.

Intravesical Procedures
Common mobilisation sequence: sweep peritoneum off the dome → open the bladder vertically between
stay sutures to within 2 cm of the bladder neck → pack the dome with saline gauze → Denis Browne ring
retractor (blades on the rectus bellies plus a cranial blade countering the packed dome); lateral walls held by
stay sutures, not blades, to spare friable mucosa → cannulate the orifice with a 5-Fr feeding tube, fixed with
5-0 monofilament as a traction handle → circumscribe the orifice 1–2 mm away with fine low-wattage
needle diathermy → divide intra- and extravesical attachments with tenotomy scissors and bipolar
diathermy, staying in the correct plane to preserve distal blood supply → Langenbeck retractor at 12 o'clock
and peritoneum swept off with a pledget → ureter reaches the contralateral wall without tension → choose
the tunnel.

Politano–Leadbetter Technique
 • Politano and Leadbetter, 1958. Suprahiatal: a new hiatus superomedially with a vertical tunnel running
   straight down to the native hiatus. Suited to high-grade VUR needing a long tunnel.
Sequence: right-angled clamp through the original hiatus towards the planned neo-hiatus, sweeping
peritoneum off the posterior bladder → push through, incise and widen. Danger: bowel, vas deferens,
vagina, so retract the superior lip of the old hiatus and sweep under direct vision → 3-0 guide suture
extravesically from the new to the old hiatus → ureter drawn out and back in through the neo-hiatus → close
the old detrusor defect (4-0) → submucosal tunnel capacious enough not to constrict → shorten ± spatulate
→ hitch to detrusor at 6 o'clock (5-0) → mucosa to mucosa with 6-0 interrupted → close the neo-hiatus
mucosal defect (5-0) → bladder closure (4-0) and Foley for 1–3 days; two-layer closure shortens catheter
time. Stents only in complex cases.

Glenn–Anderson Technique
 • Glenn and Anderson, 1967. Infrahiatal: tunnel advanced distally towards the bladder neck.
 • Advantage: least liable to angulation and kinking, so obstruction is avoided. Limitation: tunnel length is
   capped by the distance to the bladder neck. Success up to 98%.

Cohen Cross-Trigonal Technique
 • Cohen, 1977. Transhiatal, cross-trigonal tunnel towards the opposite orifice, giving a longer tunnel. The
   commonest intravesical procedure: versatile, simple and reliable, with success over 98%.
 • Suited to thick-walled trabeculated bladders (trigone undistorted) and to concomitant bladder-neck
   reconstruction (transverse tunnels leave length for the neck).
 • Main drawback: difficult later retrograde catheterisation for imaging, stents or stones. Solutions are a
   flexible ureteroscope, or cystoscopy plus a suprapubic trocar or IV cannula aimed at the orifice to pass a
   guidewire.
 • Unilateral: new orifice just above the contralateral orifice. Bilateral: the more laterally displaced
   (superior) ureter goes above the contralateral orifice, and the second ureter goes to the inferior edge of
   the first ureter's native hiatus. A common tunnel also gives excellent results.

Extravesical Procedures
 • Lich–Gregoir: Lich (1961) and Gregoir (1964) independently. The bladder is not opened, so no
   postoperative haematuria or bladder spasm. The detrusor is incised down to intact urothelium and the
   ureter laid in the detrusorotomy to create the tunnel.
 • Main concern: transient postoperative voiding dysfunction and retention in up to 10%, needing
   catheterisation, especially after bilateral procedures. The cause is injury to pelvic-plexus innervation of
   the detrusor and trigone.
 • Neuroanatomy: cadaveric work shows the main pelvic plexus lies about 1.5–2 cm dorsal and medial to
   the UVJ, with small branches running along the medial side of the ureter outside the mesoureter. Fetal
   immunohistochemistry with 3D imaging places the nerves on the medial border just outside Waldeyer's
   sheath, extending circumferentially and distal and lateral to the UVJ along the detrusor.
 • Practical rule: dissect hugging the ureter, avoid the dorsomedial UVJ region, and stop at the UVJ. This
   return to the original description has reduced voiding dysfunction.

Minimally Invasive Procedures
 • Laparoscopic extravesical reimplantation was shown feasible in the mid-1990s. It brought less pain,
   haematuria and spasm and a shorter stay, and led on to robot-assisted (da Vinci) surgery.
 • AUA Paediatric Vesicoureteral Reflux Guidelines Panel meta-analysis (Elder, 1997): open reimplantation
   success 95.9% (86 studies; 6,472 patients; 8,563 ureters). This is the open benchmark.
 • Early minimally invasive series fell short of it, with lower reflux resolution and complications unusual
   after open surgery: ureteric injury, obstruction and leak, especially after intravesical vesicoscopic repair.
 • Caveat: minimally invasive series had stricter radiographic follow-up than historical open series. With
   experience, results are converging on open outcomes.

Postoperative Evaluation
 • In expert hands, ureteroneocystostomy for low-grade primary VUR approaches 100% success.
 • Ultrasound at 6–12 weeks postoperatively for all patients (AUA VUR Guideline, 2010, amended 2017).
 • One-year resolution is significantly higher for low-grade than high-grade reflux.
 • Voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) can be omitted after low-grade primary VUR when pre- and
   postoperative ultrasound are normal and there is no voiding dysfunction or recurrent UTI.
 • Postoperative VCUG is still reasonable with a dysfunctional bladder, new hydronephrosis or UTI, or after
   high-grade reflux, where success is lower.

Complications of Ureteral Reimplantation
Early Complications
 • Persistent reflux is usually clinically unimportant and commonly resolves by 1 year. Persistence at 1 year
   is concentrated in children who had high-grade reflux preoperatively. Early low-grade reflux is attributed
   to postoperative bladder inflammation and dysfunction, which settle.
 • Contralateral reflux after unilateral reimplantation occurs in a small minority (roughly one in ten), and
   most resolve spontaneously. Routine bilateral reimplantation is therefore not justified.
 • Risk factors for contralateral VUR: age <6 years, bladder capacity <50% of expected, reimplanted
   refluxing duplex system, and a trend with higher corrected grade. The surgical technique used makes no
   difference.
 • Proposed mechanisms (speculative): distortion of the contralateral hemitrigone; pop-off effect; bilateral
   reflux missed on the initial cystogram; intermittent natural history.
 • Management: observation in most; prophylactic antibiotics in asymptomatic children under 4–5 years;
   intervention for clinical pyelonephritis. Repeat VCUG is unnecessary if infection-free.
 • Obstruction: mild–moderate early hydronephrosis is common and resolves, after both open and robotic
   extravesical repair. Rule of thumb: postoperative dilatation should not exceed the maximal dilatation
   the reflux produced.
 • Acute obstruction results from a twisted or kinked ureter, intramural clot, submucosal haematoma or
   oedema. It presents within the first 2 weeks with pain, nausea and vomiting, and infection here is
   serious. Ultrasound, then delayed excretion on nuclear scan, confirm it. Drain with a retrograde double-J
   or percutaneous nephrostomy, and internalise the nephrostomy early to avoid a “dry reimplant”. Many
   resolve without further surgery.

Long-Term Complications
 • Obstruction has several causes. Distal ureteric ischaemia follows aggressive dissection or high pressure in
   a non-compliant bladder (neurogenic, posterior urethral valves). Angulation follows a neo-hiatus placed
   too lateral or anterior, with kinks that worsen as the bladder fills. Other causes are a constricting,
   underdeveloped tunnel and neo-orifice stenosis.
 • Management of obstruction escalates: balloon dilatation and stenting, or unroofing the distal tunnelled
   ureter while trying to keep the antireflux mechanism → re-reimplantation if these fail.

 • Reimplanting into an abnormal bladder only worsens dilatation and scars the pelvis, which makes later

Reoperative Reimplantation
 • Combined extravesical and intravesical mobilisation; excise ischaemic segments until there is free
   bleeding and peristalsis; create a new hiatus and tunnel.
 • Short ureter → psoas hitch, with non-absorbable sutures, placed before the tunnel is made. The bladder
   is fixed to the psoas sheath either side of the iliac vessels, and in children can reach almost to the
   common iliac bifurcation.
 • Psoas hitch on one side only: bilateral hitches give inadequate length on both. Both ureters short → hitch
   one side plus transureteroureterostomy for the other.
 • Boari flap: a flap from the dome to the anterior wall, based on the posterior wall and rotated up, wide
   enough to tunnel and tubularise.
 • Nipple valve plus a short tunnel: made by spatulating the ureter and folding it back on itself; especially
   useful for dilated ureters.
 • Severely foreshortened ureter → reconfigured bowel ureter (colon or ileum, Monti-type). A long tube
   comes from a short segment without tapering, so there are no metabolic consequences, and the central
   mesentery eases tunnelling. This is superior to the classic tapered ileal ureter.

ENDOSCOPIC TREATMENT OF VESICOURETERAL REFLUX
 • Matouschek introduced endoscopic injection in 1981 using Teflon. O'Donnell and Puri popularised it as
   STING (subureteric Teflon injection), 1986.
 • Teflon was never approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), because of migration of
   polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) particles.
 • Dextranomer/hyaluronic acid copolymer (Dx/HA; Deflux) is now the most used agent. Initial resolution
   of low-grade primary VUR after one injection is about 90%, but later results are less consistent because
   technique and definitions of success vary.

Follow-Up
 • Antibiotics for 3 months. Ultrasound at 2–6 weeks to exclude acute obstruction by the bulking agent.
   Ultrasound ± VCUG at 3 months, depending on the clinical picture; if the child is clinically well, ultrasound
   alone suffices.
 • Persistent VUR → 1–2 repeat injections → then open, laparoscopic or robotic reimplantation.
 • Late-onset ureteric obstruction after injection is increasingly reported, which argues for long-term
   ultrasound follow-up.
 • Open surgery after Deflux is usually not more difficult. The implant is absent or well encapsulated in the
   wrong plane and comes out en bloc. Other agents have caused more difficulty.

Recurrence of Vesicoureteral Reflux After Endoscopic Correction
 • The true incidence is unknown, because VCUG is rarely repeated after an initial negative study outside

   maturation may maintain resolution, so the injection may simply have “bought time.”

MEGAURETER

Definition
 • Megaureter is descriptive: a dilated ureter, whatever the cause. Coined as “megaloureter” by Caulk
   (1923); synonyms are wide ureter and hydroureter.
 • Megaureter = diameter ≥7 mm. The upper limit of normal ureteric diameter up to 16 years is 5.0–6.5
   mm (Cussen; Hellström).
 • Primary (intrinsic to the ureter) versus secondary (bladder pathology: neurogenic bladder, outlet
   obstruction, infection).
 • Four categories by cause: obstructed · refluxing · non-obstructed non-refluxing · refluxing with
   obstruction.
 • Refluxing megaureter is easily diagnosed. The difficulty is telling obstructed from non-obstructed non-
   refluxing megaureter, because dilatation, however alarming, does not prove obstruction.
 • The challenge is balance: catch obstructed ureters before function is lost, without operating on stable,
   balanced dilatation.

Etiology, Occurrence, and Presentation
 • Primary obstructive megaureter (POM): an adynamic terminal segment at or near the UVJ → absent or
   inefficient peristalsis → functional obstruction and dilatation upstream.
 • Developmental basis: ureteric muscle matures craniocaudally, so the juxtavesical segment matures last,
   continuing after birth. Fetal circular fibres become the double layer of the term infant only well after
   delivery, which explains transient UVJ dysfunction and spontaneous resolution with age.
 • Other findings: excess abnormal collagen at the narrow segment; a thick enveloping muscle sleeve; and
   abnormal interstitial cells of Cajal (pacemaker cells for peristalsis).
 • Incidence: 10–23% of antenatally detected upper-tract dilatations. Young males, left side; bilateral in
   25%; contralateral dysplasia or obstruction in 10–15%.
 • Most are detected antenatally and are asymptomatic. Before routine ultrasound, POM was a rare
   diagnosis among symptomatic dilatations; antenatal screening now detects the majority.
 • Symptoms: UTI, abdominal pain, microscopic haematuria (even without infection, from distension or
   calculi). Rarely, an abdominal mass or renal impairment, as in the pre-ultrasound era.

Surgical Indications
 • Most non-refluxing megaureters are benign and resolve in the first few years. Complete resolution and
   its speed are inversely related to ureteric diameter.

 • Reserved for the experienced paediatric urologist.

   transport. It is increasingly done laparoscopically or robotically.

   although pneumovesical excisional tapering with transtrigonal reimplant is feasible.

Outcomes
 • Remodelling and reimplantation for POM is durable, with over 90% success.
 • POM does better than refluxing megaureter, attributed to a higher collagen-to-smooth-muscle ratio at
   the refluxing UVJ, which gives a stiffer, less compressible ureter.
 • Worse outcomes with dysfunctional voiding, neurogenic bladder, posterior urethral valves or outflow
   obstruction.
 • Major complications: obstruction, VUR, persistent dilatation.
 • Obstruction → stent first (may be oedema). Ischaemic stricture, especially after excisional tapering →
   redo ureteroneocystostomy.
 • Reflux → conservative management, especially low grade, because it often resolves. Subureteric
   injection works with few complications. If injection fails or febrile UTIs recur → revision surgery.
 • Transureteroureterostomy is an option for unilateral cases with severe scarring or doubtful blood
   supply.

Dilation and Stenting
Sequence: endoscopic access to the POM orifice → dilate with a ureteric catheter or high-pressure balloon
under fluoroscopy until the waist disappears → double-J for 2–6 months → persistent dilatation or falling
function → re-stent, or reimplant.
 • Short- to medium-term success about 70–80%, but the evidence is weak: mostly retrospective, with
   heterogeneous indications and definitions, and follow-up rarely beyond 5 years.
 • Complications in up to a third: stent migration, infection, haematuria, VUR, stones. There may also be re-
   stenting, open stent placement or ureteric injury needing emergency reimplantation.

 KEY POINTS: MEGAURETER
   • Megaureter is descriptive: a dilated ureter of any cause.
   • Four categories: obstructed, refluxing, non-obstructed non-refluxing, refluxing with obstruction.
   • The challenge is to prevent loss of renal function, whether by conservative or surgical treatment.
   • Surgery for: recurrent UTI, progressive dilatation, DRF <40%, DRF fall ≥5% on comparable sequential
     scans.
   • Excise the stenotic segment → straighten → taper → non-refluxing reimplant at 5:1.
   • Most surgeons use temporary postoperative stenting.

Concept — Primary obstructive megaureter is the UPJO problem moved to the bottom of the ureter: an
immature adynamic segment that usually matures on its own. Surgery is kept for the ureter that is too wide
or losing function, and its size dictates the method: divert or leave refluxing in infancy, fold the moderate
ureter, excise the massive one.
