Chapter 5 — Principles of CT and Radiographic Imaging
 • 1895 — Wilhelm Röntgen discovered x-rays incidentally while experimenting with cathode rays; the first
   radiograph, of his wife's hand with a ring (Fig. 5.1), was published in 1896, and diagnostic radiology began
   at once.
 • Radiographs moved from hard-copy film stored in file rooms to digital acquisition: images are now
   processed to improve quality and reduce noise, stored electronically and viewed by several physicians at
   once.
 • Fluoroscopy moved from continuous exposure with poor images to low-dose pulsed technology with
   last image hold, giving better images at lower dose.
 • Computer image processing produced computed tomography (CT); artificial intelligence (AI) is the next
   expected advance.

RADIATION BIOLOGY AND SAFETY
 • The hazard was not recognised at first. Clarence Dally, an employee of Thomas Edison, repeatedly placed
   his hand in the beam while developing the fluoroscope → erythema, ulceration, hair loss → carcinoma →
   amputation of the left arm and four fingers of the right hand → death from metastatic cancer in 1904,
   aged 39: the first known death from radiation exposure. Edison afterwards declared himself afraid of x-
   rays.
 • Governing principle: ALARA — as low as reasonably achievable.
Mechanism of injury.
 • Absorbed ionising radiation damages cells by direct DNA ionisation or indirect damage from free
   radicals; depending on degree and site, the cell undergoes apoptosis or attempts DNA repair.

Evidence of harm.
 • Hiroshima survivors (high dose): radiation burns and increased leukaemia and solid cancers — bladder,
   breast, lung, brain, ovary and thyroid, among others.
 • Diagnostic doses are far lower, yet medical diagnostic radiation is estimated to cause 1.5–2% of cancers
   in the United States.
 • Health care workers: radiologists have more leukaemia than other specialists; physicians using
   fluoroscopy have more brain tumours. Other effects: cardiovascular disease, thyroid disease,
   inflammatory conditions and cataract — interventional cardiologists have a markedly raised risk of
   posterior lens opacity.

 • Imaging use keeps rising despite low-dose developments, so a risk–benefit analysis precedes every
   ionising study. ALARA in practice: low-dose protocols, alternative studies, avoiding ionising radiation
   where possible — reducing the patient's cumulative dose and often the surgeon's and staff's.

 KEY POINTS: RADIATION BIOLOGY AND SAFETY
   • Radiography, fluoroscopy and CT use high-energy photons, which cause deterministic and stochastic
     effects.
   • Under the linear no-threshold model, stochastic risk is proportional to dose and no amount is risk-free.
   • Follow ALARA: use only the radiation necessary for diagnosis and management.

Concept — Deterministic effects have a threshold and scale in severity; stochastic effects have no threshold
and scale in probability. Because cancer risk follows the linear no-threshold model, there is no safe dose, and
ALARA follows directly from that.

RADIOGRAPHIC STUDIES

Radiographic Radiation Physics
 • Diagnostic x-rays lie at 1016–1020 Hz in the electromagnetic spectrum — enough energy to ionise, hence
   "ionising radiation".
 • High frequency lets x-rays pass through tissue; differential absorption between tissues creates contrast,
   which is the basis of the image.
 • Generation: vacuum tube → potential difference across cathode and anode → electrons accelerate from
   cathode to anode → strike the anode plate, usually tungsten → x-ray emission.
 • Film detection: x-rays strike an intensifying screen exposing the film → developed → black where
   photons arrive, white where radio-opaque material attenuates.
 • Digital detection — indirect: x-rays stimulate a photoluminescent material whose fluorescence is read by
   a digital receiver; direct: a material excited directly by x-ray photons, no fluorescent step. Both yield an
   electrical charge read like a digital camera sensor.

Chest Radiography
 • Chest x-ray (CXR) is the commonest radiograph in hospital: respiratory and cardiac disease; preoperative
   clearance (not always needed); high sensitivity for haemothorax, pneumothorax, atelectasis, effusion,
   infiltrates, infection and trauma; oncological staging and surveillance; position of lines and tubes (chest
   drain, endotracheal and gastric tubes, central venous lines).
 • Dose: 0.07 mSv.

 • Technique: posteroanterior, upright, full inspiration; hands on hips to move the scapulae laterally;
   remove lines and tubes from the lung field where possible; lateral, oblique and expiratory views as
   adjuncts; portable film if immobile.
 • Limitations: poor positioning, motion, rotation or altered exposure impair reading; subtle day-to-day
   change is easily missed; false positives prompt unnecessary intervention and false negatives miss disease
   — CXR augments, never replaces, history and examination.

Plain Abdominal Radiography
 • The kidney–ureter–bladder (KUB) film (Fig. 5.4: kidneys, psoas shadows, liver tip, spleen, gastric gas):
   diagnosis and follow-up of radio-opaque stones; before shock-wave lithotripsy (SWL); pre- and post-
   contrast film; postoperative ileus, obstruction and free air; confirms line, tube, stent and drain position;
   foreign bodies; vertebral and pelvic fractures in trauma.
 • Dose: 0.7 mSv per film; 1.4 mSv for a standard two-view abdomen.
 • Technique: supine anteroposterior (AP) film from the upper renal margin to the inferior pubic rami;
   obliques separate overlapping structures; upright film is most sensitive for free intraperitoneal air.
 • Limitations: cheap and low dose but largely supplanted by CT, which gives the same information more
   accurately. KUB is two-dimensional, so overlying structures hide pathology: its sensitivity for stones, the
   acute abdomen and bowel obstruction is poor and variable, whereas CT is three-dimensional (3D) and
   detects >95% of stones.

Static Cystography
 • Contrast instilled into the bladder shows extravasation, filling defects (stone, foreign body, haematoma,
   tumour), reflux, neurogenic bladder, diverticula, fistulas and leaks.
 • Trauma: for suspected bladder injury the American Urological Association (AUA) recommends plain or
   CT cystography; CT cystography is used more often, but static cystography has similar sensitivity.
 • Postoperative check of genitourinary (GU) anastomotic integrity.
 • After radical prostatectomy: bladder neck–to–pubic symphysis (BNPS) ratio = distance from the superior
   border of the symphysis to the bladder neck ÷ total symphyseal height. A lower ratio predicts better
   continence (Fig. 5.5: continent patient 0.36; incontinent patient with a low-lying bladder neck 1.1).
 • Technique: supine scout film → Foley catheter if not indwelling → at least 300–350 mL dilute contrast by
   gravity → clamp → AP film of full bladder → obliques (posterior diverticula, fistulas, leaks) → drain →
   postdrainage film (extravasation hidden behind dense contrast).
 • Limitations: two-dimensional; sensitivity depends on volume instilled and operator skill; active infection
   risks pyelonephritis or sepsis.

 KEY POINTS: RADIOGRAPHIC STUDIES
   • X-rays are generated by accelerating electrons into an anode metal.
   • KUB detects radio-opaque stones, bowel complications and stent and drain position.
   • Plain films cost less and deliver less radiation than conventional CT.
   • Static cystography needs baseline, filling and postdrainage films and assesses bladder, urethral and
     anastomotic integrity.

Concept — Every plain study trades information for dose and cost against CT: projection loses depth and
hides overlying pathology, so plain films survive where a narrow question (stone follow-up, device position, a
leak) does not justify CT.

FLUOROSCOPY
 • Live x-ray imaging for procedures needing real-time feedback: renal access for stones, nephrostomy and
   ureteric stent placement, InterStim (sacral neuromodulation) implantation, retrograde pyelography,
   cystography and voiding cystourethrography (VCUG).
 • Intraoperatively the surgeon takes part in choosing machine settings to keep dose to ALARA.

Fluoroscopy Physics
 • X-ray production is as in radiography; detection differs. Early systems used a fluorescent screen; modern
   systems use an image intensifier or a flat panel detector.
 • Flat panel detector — works like a conventional digital radiography detector.
 • Image intensifier — x-rays strike the input phosphor (converts to a wavelength suited to the
   photocathode) → photocathode releases electrons → electrons accelerated across a vacuum by an
   applied potential → strike the output phosphor at the anode → converted back to light and recorded.
 • Brightness gain has two sources: electron acceleration (energy gain) and focusing electrons onto a
   smaller area.

Retrograde Pyelography
 • Fluoroscopic study of the upper tract. A CT urogram gives much of the same information, but the
   retrograde pyelogram is convenient intraoperatively: maps the collecting system for retrograde intrarenal
   surgery and aids percutaneous access.
 • Chosen when intravenous (IV) contrast is hazardous: contrast allergy or renal dysfunction.
 • Evaluates haematuria, obstruction, trauma and tumour; detects extravasation (Fig. 5.6: calyceal leak at a
   partial nephrectomy site); shows peristalsis, which a CT urogram cannot; performed and read in real time
   by the surgeon.
 • Technique: dorsal lithotomy → cystoscopy identifies the ureteric orifice → catheterise → gentle injection
   of dilute contrast (often 50%) — over-opacification hides small filling defects → fluoroscopic monitoring
   and mapping.
 • Air instead of contrast reduces radiation but impairs later ultrasound and, very rarely, causes air
   embolism if injected under high pressure.
 • Limitations: usually needs anaesthesia and an operating theatre (cost, complexity); more invasive than CT
   urography; risk of iatrogenic ureteric injury; over-pressurisation causes backflow — pyelovenous,
   pyelotubular, pyelosinus, pyelolymphatic (Fig. 5.7) → bacteraemia and sepsis.

Voiding Cystourethrography
 • Static cystography plus voiding-phase fluoroscopy: sphincter and urethral function and competence of
   the ureterovesical junction.
 • Gold standard for vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) and recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI). Also: posterior
   urethra (posterior urethral valves, diverticula), bladder (diverticula, tumours, leaks), ureters (ureterocele,
   diverticula), kidneys (when reflux present); neurogenic bladder, incontinence, bladder outlet obstruction,
   voiding difficulty.

Retrograde Urethrography
 • Evaluates the anterior urethra: strictures, suspected trauma, postoperative setting; also diverticula,
   tumours, fistulas, foreign bodies. Alone or combined with cystography or VCUG; performed before
   urethral stricture repair; shows distal anatomy other modalities miss.
 • Technique: 45° oblique, right shoulder down → scout → flushed catheter inserted about 2 cm into the
   fossa navicularis → balloon inflated with about 2 mL sterile water (occludes; overinflation injures the
   urethra) → penis on traction to straighten the urethra → gentle contrast → anterior urethra filled to the
   external sphincter → further injection passes the sphincter into the prostatic urethra and bladder. Adult
   volume about 20–30 mL. With a Foley already in place, a 5 Fr feeding tube alongside it in the fossa gives
   a pericatheter urethrogram.
 • The prostatic urethra does not normally distend on retrograde injection — not to be read as stricture.

Loopography
 • Retrograde contrast via the stoma into the bowel segment of a urinary diversion → assesses diversion
   and upper tracts.
 • Refluxing uretero-intestinal anastomosis → contrast ascends the ureters → filling defects, obstruction.
   Non-refluxing → upper tracts not shown; an antegrade study is needed.
 • Indications: haematuria, recurrent infection, pain, declining renal function; surveillance of upper tract
   neoplasia; anastomotic stricture; diversion patency.
 • Safe in mild–moderate contrast allergy or renal impairment — minimal systemic absorption from bowel.
 • Technique: supine scout → catheter through stoma into the loop → instil contrast → films during and
   after filling → obliques to show the whole upper tract → postdrainage film for obstruction.
 • Limitations: balloon overinflation in a small-bowel segment can disrupt mucosa; overlapping loops;
   excessive pressure → pyelonephritis; non-refluxing anastomoses → no upper tract; continent pouches —
   small tumours missed (irregular walls, large contrast volume).

Fluoroscopic Renal Access
 • Access to the collecting system for drainage in infection or obstruction (mostly by interventional
   radiology) and before stone surgery.
 • For percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), ultrasound-guided access is increasing but is harder for upper
   pole access (ribs), obesity and staghorn calculi; fluoroscopy remains the commonest technique.

Specialty Fluoroscopy
 • Cavernosography (corporal leak in erectile dysfunction or trauma), vasography (vas cannulated, contrast
   maps the system and checks patency), and sacral nerve stimulator placement.

Special Fluoroscopic Considerations
Three-Dimensional Fluoroscopy (Cone Beam Computerised Tomography)
 • Surgeons normally rebuild 3D anatomy mentally from two-dimensional images (cognitive fusion). Cone
   beam CT: C-arm rotates (continuous or pulsed) → many images → filtered back projection reconstruction
   as in CT; needs a specialised table for circumferential imaging.
 • Uses: planning and guiding needle access; intraoperative detection of residual stones → immediate
   improvement of stone-free rate.
 • CT scanners with a fluoroscopy mode: poorer surgeon access, harder sterile field, higher dose.

 KEY POINTS: FLUOROSCOPY STUDIES
   • Fluoroscopy gives real-time anatomy during diagnostic and therapeutic procedures.
   • Retrograde pyelography maps the upper tract intraoperatively and is usually safe with contrast allergy
     and renal failure.
   • VCUG captures voiding-phase images and is the standard test for VUR.
   • Retrograde urethrography evaluates the anterior urethra for stricture, leak, diverticulum and fistula.
   • Loopography evaluates the urinary tract after diversion.
   • Fluoroscopy establishes renal access for drainage or before stone treatment.

Concept — Every retrograde contrast study carries the same two liabilities — catheterisation and
pressurised contrast into a possibly infected system — so sterile urine, gentle low-pressure filling and
choosing the direction (retrograde for the anterior urethra, voiding for the posterior) recur across all of them.

Fluoroscopic Radiation Reduction Techniques
 • Fluoroscopy use varies widely between institutions, machines and surgeons; dose reduction benefits
   patients and staff. The United States Food and Drug Administration issued a white paper urging reduction.
   Summary of measures (Fig. 5.8): lead gloves, curtain, vest, glasses, thyroid shield and cap; instruments
   out of the field; collimation; source below the patient; low-dose setting and lower pulse rate; distance
   from the source; last image hold; recording fluoroscopy time.

Fluoroscope Adjustments
 • Time. Dose is proportional to fluoroscopy time. Avoid unnecessary images and studies; use last image
   hold with intermittent spot images; prefer direct endoscopic vision. Ureteroscopy and PCNL have been
   performed with no radiation, using visual and tactile cues, direct vision and ultrasound. Every activation
   must be justified.
 • Collimation. Narrowing the aperture to the region of interest; halving the aperture diameter reduces the
   exposed area fourfold. Excluding radiodense areas lowers patient dose and improves contrast and image
   quality.
 • Low-dose mode. Roughly halves the current (mA), and dose falls by a similar amount; contrast falls but
   images remain adequate for most urology.
 • Pulsed fluoroscopy. Continuous fluoroscopy = 30 pulses per second (pps). Theoretical reduction at 8, 4
   and 1 pps = 73%, 87% and 97%; the real saving is smaller because of the ramp and tail effect of each
   pulse (Fig. 5.9: modern pulsed units have shorter ramp and tail than conventional pulsed units).

 • Dose tracking and reporting. Recording fluoroscopy time builds awareness, sets goals and measures
   reduction strategies; surgeon feedback alone reduces fluoroscopy time. Surgeons should wear
   dosimeters.

Shielding
 • Lead aprons, usually 0.25–0.50 mm lead equivalent, absorb most incident radiation; add a thyroid shield.
 • Standard shielding leaves head, face, neck, arms and legs exposed. With the source below the patient,
   the surgeon's legs receive the most scatter. Lymphocytes circulating through unshielded areas can show
   DNA damage after a single endovascular case.

Environmental Factors
 • Distance. Inverse square law: doubling the distance cuts dose to one quarter — one step back from the
   table matters. All staff should maximise distance.
 • C-arm placement. Adjust table height so the detector is close to the patient and the source far → lower
   patient dose → less scatter. Source below the patient protects the surgeon's chest and abdomen.
 • Instruments. In automatic exposure control mode the machine raises mA and kVp to hold image quality
   when radiodense objects enter the beam — instruments, cords, and especially the table's lateral support
   beam. Keep them out of the field.

 KEY POINTS: FLUOROSCOPY SAFETY
   • Patients, surgeons and staff can receive substantial doses from fluoroscopic procedures.
   • Use collimation and shielding whenever possible.
   • Low-dose and pulsed modes give large reductions with adequate images.
   • Track fluoroscopy time and keep dose ALARA.
   • Core measures: less time, shielding, more distance.

Concept — The surgeon's dose is mostly scatter from the patient, so every measure that lowers patient dose
— collimation, low-dose and pulsed modes, detector close and source below — also protects the team; time,
distance and shielding act on top of that.

COMPUTED TOMOGRAPHY

Introduction
 • Introduced in the early 1970s; Sir Godfrey Hounsfield and Allan M. Cormack — 1979 Nobel Prize in
   Physiology or Medicine (Fig. 5.10). First clinical scan 1971, a brain tumour (Fig. 5.11).
 • First scan: over 5 minutes to acquire, about 2.5 hours to reconstruct; now abdomen and pelvis in 5–7
   seconds and the heart in a single beat. Principles are unchanged.
 • 3D information gives high sensitivity and specificity in benign disease, urological cancer and urogenital
   trauma.

Computed Tomography Physics
 • X-rays are produced circumferentially; detectors opposite the source record attenuation at many angles;
   each angle has a unique profile, and the profiles are computationally reconstructed into a 3D volumetric
   dataset that can be reformatted in any plane.

 • Voxel = small 3D cube of tissue carrying its attenuation value as a CT number. CT numbers are not
   absolute — they vary between machines and within one machine — but remain useful; shown as
   greyscale and usually viewed as sequential two-dimensional slices.
 • Attenuation coefficients are converted to Hounsfield units (HU):
    HU = [(μtissue − μwater) ÷ μwater] × 1000, where μ = linear attenuation coefficient.

 • Water = 0 HU; less dense tissue negative, denser positive. Air: μ ≈ 0 → −1000 HU. Range −1000 to +3000
   HU; low HU displays black, high HU white.
 • The eye cannot separate small HU differences across the full range, so a window selects an HU range and
   rescales it, each grey step covering a smaller span.

Image Quality
 • Image noise: unwanted variation in pixel values → grainy image, poorer contrast resolution. Measured by
   the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR); higher SNR = less noise.
 • Noise determinants: tube current–time product (mAs) — more dose, less noise; slice thickness — thicker
   slices collect more photons, less noise; patient size — larger patients absorb and scatter more, fewer
   photons reach the detector, more noise; reconstruction algorithm.
 • Beam hardening: low-energy photons are absorbed preferentially, leaving high-energy photons →
   artificially low HU centrally or streaks by dense objects. Metal artefact from prostheses: dark bands
   between two metal objects, light or dark streaks from one (Fig. 5.13: bilateral hip implants). Photon-
   counting detectors sort photons into energy bins; high-energy data reduce beam hardening and metal
   artefact.
 • Partial volume averaging: a voxel too large contains two tissues and shows their average. Key
   consequence: pseudoenhancement of small renal cysts from adjacent parenchyma, especially on
   contrast images → cyst misread as enhancing tumour → overtreatment. Fig. 5.14: cyst 16 HU
   unenhanced, 39 HU nephrographic — a 23 HU rise, above the 20 HU threshold, yet a benign cyst.
   Minimise with narrow collimation, thin slices and a central region of interest; ultra-high-resolution CT
   may reduce it.
 • Slice thickness governs cross-plane resolution and precision of organ measurement; thinner
   reconstructed slices increase noise, which the operator offsets by raising mAs (dose).

   diagnosed cancers in the United States — hence the drive to cut dose while keeping image quality.

 • Dose is cut by lowering tube current (mAs) — the commonest method — or tube voltage. Low-dose and
   ultra-low–dose protocols are routine for stone diagnosis and follow-up and spreading to other
   indications.
 • Tube current should be adjusted to patient size; tube potential and rotation speed are usually fixed per
   protocol. Faster rotation → less blurring; lower tube potential → more contrast but more noise. Modern
   scanners modulate dose in real time along the absorption profile.

Image Cross-Sections
 • Axial = horizontal slices; coronal = vertical slices separating front from back; sagittal = vertical slices
   separating right from left (Fig. 5.15).
 • Maximum intensity projection combines slices, placing voxels above an intensity threshold into one
   image → 3D models; used routinely in CT angiography (Fig. 5.16: donor work-up showing three right renal
   arteries and one left, so the left kidney was taken). Post-processing can strip lower-density parenchyma to
   display a stone in 3D.

Image Reconstruction
 • Ideal reconstruction is fast, low-dose and high-quality; most techniques compromise on at least one.
 • SNR compares signal power with the noise corrupting it; contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) compares mean
   signal in a tissue of interest with background. Lower SNR and CNR mean poorer images.
 • Filtered back projection (FBP) — since the 1980s; reconstructs in seconds; low SNR and CNR, higher
   dose.
 • Model-based iterative reconstruction (MBIR) — computationally heavy: solve for the image → forward-
   project the attenuation it would produce → compare with measured data → update → repeat until the
   image fits. High SNR; about half the dose of FBP with better images (Fig. 5.17: superior depiction of
   retroperitoneal fascia and corticomedullary tissue, most obvious at ultra-low dose). Slow — limits acute
   use — and gives a waxy, plastic appearance.
 • Hybrid iterative reconstruction (HIR) — commonly used; vendor-proprietary; statistical modelling with
   forward projection, faster than MBIR, but SNR low like FBP, waxy image, moderate dose.
 • Deep learning reconstruction (DLR) — AI trained on thousands of matched low-dose/high-dose image
   pairs through millions of iterations learns to turn noisy images into high-quality ones; meets all three
   goals. Ultra-low–dose DLR images look natural at a dose close to a plain radiograph; fast enough for
   acute settings; supports metal artefact reduction software. Needs close work with the manufacturer's
   application specialists and the local medical physicist. Fig. 5.18: the same patient at 6.3 mSv with HIR and
   about 1 mSv with DLR plus metal artefact reduction, artefact markedly reduced — below a two-view KUB.

Dual-Energy CT Scan
 • Dual-energy CT (DECT) acquires two energy levels simultaneously (e.g. 80 and 140 kVp) and compares
   attenuation pixel by pixel → material behaviour at each energy, iodine extraction (better iodine detection
   on low-energy images) and virtual unenhanced images, which also sidestep motion and breathing
   artefact between phases.
 • Uses: hyperattenuating cyst versus enhancing renal mass; adrenal nodules; fewer CT urogram phases
   (virtual unenhanced replaces the true noncontrast phase); stone composition — uric acid versus non–
   uric acid with near-perfect accuracy at low dose.
 • Fig. 5.19: after right radical nephrectomy for T1a clear cell RCC, a small hyperdense left lesion was
   indeterminate on arterial and nephrographic phases; virtual unenhanced images confirmed hyperdensity,
   and 40 keV monoenergetic images and an iodine map showed no enhancement → hyperdense cyst.
 • Limitations: motion and beam hardening artefact persist; limited field of view (obesity); more radiation
   than low-dose and ultra-low–dose CT; longer reconstruction; twice as many images → radiologist
   workload.

Noncontrast CT
 • Noncontrast CT (NCCT) uses: diagnosis and follow-up of stones; first phase of renal-protocol CT and CT
   urography (baseline HU); trauma, to delineate extravasation or active haemorrhage on later phases; renal
   impairment — hydronephrosis, lymphadenopathy, larger soft-tissue masses.

Noncontrast CT for Kidney Stones
 • Stones attenuate more than parenchyma and urine, so no IV contrast is needed. NCCT is the gold-
   standard imaging for urolithiasis (values in Table 5.2).
 • All stones are visible except protease inhibitor stones (e.g. indinavir).
 • Gives size, location and indirect signs of obstruction (stranding, hydronephrosis), and composition from
   density: uric acid 200–400 HU; calcium oxalate 600–1200 HU.

 • Emergency acute abdomen, ± chest CT; always includes the hepatic portal phase (soft tissue, bowel,

   ± oral or rectal contrast.

 • Testicular germ cell tumour after orchidectomy: abdomen–pelvis CT for retroperitoneal nodes;

CT Angiogram
 • Uses the early arterial phase: renal artery aneurysm, stenosis, occlusion; living kidney donor work-up for
   accessory or aberrant vessels, often as a four-phase CT (noncontrast, arterial, nephrographic,

 • Suspected renal trauma protocol: noncontrast + arterial + nephrographic (parenchyma) + delayed

 • Three phases: noncontrast, portal venous, and delayed at 15 minutes for washout.

Positron Emission Tomography CT
 • PET-CT images biochemical processes (e.g. glucose metabolism). Radioisotopes emitting positrons are
   coupled to ligands (e.g. glucose) taken up avidly by tumour → positron meets electron → annihilation →
   two 511 keV photons in opposite directions → detected → 3D map of isotope distribution overlaid on CT
   (Fig. 5.31).
 • PSMA PET-CT in prostate cancer: tracers gallium-68 (68Ga)–PSMA and fluorine-18 (18F)–PSMA; uptake
   quantified as the standardised uptake value (SUV), which accounts for injected activity, body weight and
   injection-to-imaging time.
 • Better detection of locoregional and metastatic disease (Fig. 5.32: thoracic and abdominal nodes).
   Biochemical recurrence (BCR) detected earlier: positivity rises with prostate-specific antigen (PSA) level,
   detection is useful even at PSA <0.5 ng/mL, with very high specificity; 18F-PSMA outperforms 68Ga-
    PSMA for BCR.
 • Metastatic detection: PSMA PET-CT is more sensitive than MRI, choline PET and bone scan, and has the
   highest sensitivity and specificity for skeletal metastases.
 • RCC: carbonic anhydrase IX is expressed on almost all ccRCC; zirconium-89 (89Zr)–girentuximab PET-CT
   (antibody to carbonic anhydrase IX) diagnoses localised ccRCC and may separate ccRCC from non-ccRCC in
   metastatic disease.

 • Severe allergic reactions to intravascular iodinated contrast are rare.

 KEY POINTS: CONTRAST MEDIA
   • Contrast aids tissue differentiation but carries rare adverse reactions.
   • Give adrenaline for severe immediate reactions; add oxygen and β2-agonists for bronchospasm.

   • Observe mild reactions or give H1-antagonists, with close monitoring.

   • Premedicate patients at high risk of allergic reaction.

Concept — Contrast carries two unrelated risks: hypersensitivity, which is idiosyncratic and handled by
premedication and adrenaline, and nephrotoxicity, which is dose-related and handled by hydration and less
contrast. Prophylaxis for one does nothing for the other.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE
 • Models used: machine learning, artificial neural networks and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) —
   the commonest in diagnostic imaging. CNNs detect stones on CT with very high sensitivity; artificial
   neural networks detect renal tumours with good accuracy.
 • Main advantage: processing big data rapidly. Growing electronic records supply training data; pattern
   recognition improves the systems.
 • AI may speed interpretation and improve accuracy, but replacing radiologists is distant; its role in imaging
   will grow.
