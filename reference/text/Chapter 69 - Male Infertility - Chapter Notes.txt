EPIDEMIOLOGY
 • Infertility affects approximately 15% of couples, leaving nearly 1 in 6 childless (World Health Organization
   [WHO]).
 • Despite historical bias in the sex split, males and females contribute an equal number of faulty gametes
   — so evaluating the male matters.
 • Male care is largely delivered outside reimbursement systems, so metrics are unreliable. The Society for
   Assisted Reproductive Technology (SART), the professional group of the American Society for
   Reproductive Medicine (ASRM), compels in vitro fertilisation (IVF) clinics to report outcomes — but this
   sees the male contribution through the lens of female assisted reproduction, skewing estimates.
 • National Survey of Family Growth (NSFG): a substantial minority of males in infertile couples are never
   evaluated, supporting underreporting of male factor; of sexually active males who ever seek care for
   difficulty conceiving, only a minority report a clinician-diagnosed male reproductive problem.
 • Urologic Diseases in America (UDA) Project: ambulatory surgery for conditions associated with male
   infertility is used most at 25–34 years, falling with each older band; younger males account for more than
   half of cases, yet nearly 1 in 11 cases occurs in the fifth decade or later. Use is lower in the West than in
   the Northeast and Midwest of the United States.
 • Economics: direct expenditure on primary male infertility is a gross underestimate because much care
   escapes traditional databases; counting the female partner's assisted reproduction, the true cost is orders
   of magnitude larger; males pursuing fertility care spend a substantial fraction of annual income on it.
 • The primary assay, the semen analysis, is a poor predictor — every parameter has a low area under the
   receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve — so men with some sperm are wrongly judged fertile and
   omitted from tallies.
Concept — the male contribution is systematically undercounted, because every data source is filtered
through systems built for the female partner and through an assay that cannot separate the fertile from the
infertile.

HISTORY
 • Male gamete production needs orchestration of endocrine, immune and neural systems, intact anatomy,
   sequenced gene expression and chromosomal events, and normal embryological and postnatal
   development — so myriad disparate conditions cause dysfunction.
 • Approach the history methodically; a pre-visit form completed at home or in the waiting area improves
   efficiency.

 • What the table is for: the breadth of final diagnoses in male infertility.
 • Pattern: idiopathic is the largest single category; varicocele is the commonest identifiable cause, then
   obstruction.
 • Trap: the percentages vary widely with how each condition is assessed — the authors call the table an
   indictment of how poorly male reproductive data are collected; do not treat the figures as population
   truths.
 • Two patients are required for a positive outcome, so the diagnostic approach must be probabilistic.
 • With optimally timed intercourse, most conceptions occur within the first three to six cycles and nearly
   all couples who will conceive have done so by twelve; a minority not conceiving by six cycles still do so.
 • Couples seeking help one or two months after stopping contraception should be counselled to keep
   trying; it is reasonable to begin evaluation after 6 months, accepting that some will conceive shortly
   afterwards. Describe each month as rolling a die or flipping a coin.
 • Ask about intercourse frequency. Semen parameters peak after 1–2 days of abstinence and then decline;
   prolonged abstinence to "accumulate" sperm worsens quality.
 • For conception: daily intercourse around ovulation (for semen analysis, see Evaluation of Semen).
 • Female partner's age is critical to reproductive potential and planning, especially with assisted
   reproduction; fecundity declines precipitously after 35, and faster in some regions — assess her age in
   the context of her locale.
 • Male age affects bulk parameters and sperm DNA packaging only to a limited degree, and males father
   children well into later life. However, the ASRM and American Urological Association (AUA) joint guideline
   (2021) directs clinicians to advise couples with advanced paternal age (≥40 years) of increased risk of
   adverse offspring outcomes — bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, autism, attention-deficit disorder and
   poorer cognitive performance.
 • Ask whether the man and partner have conceived before, together or with others, and the offspring's
   ages: proven post-pubertal fertility eliminates many congenital concerns.
 • Diabetes mellitus (DM), multiple sclerosis (MS) and spinal cord injury (SCI) disrupt coordinated
   ejaculation.
 • Cancer impairs spermatogenesis even before chemotherapy, especially testis cancer; azoospermia may
   reveal testis cancer — consider it in any man with no sperm.
 • Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) and minimally invasive prostatic therapies cause
   ejaculatory dysfunction; retroperitoneal lymph node dissection causes retrograde ejaculation of varying
   degree depending on template and context.
 • Herniorrhaphy can obstruct the vas; mesh incites a dense foreign-body inflammatory reaction that may
   entrap the vas even when not adjacent to it. Infertility from vasal occlusion alone needs both vasa
   occluded (rare) — more often one occluded vas plus contralateral spermatogenic dysfunction.
 • Without a form, use the mnemonic TICS: Toxins, Infectious/inflammatory disease, Childhood history,
   Sexual history.

Spermatotoxicity
Many medications, prescribed and over the counter, impair spermatogenesis, mature sperm function or
delivery.

   suppression is reversible, sperm returning about 4 months or more after stopping; recovery after longer
   use is unknown.

 • Mumps orchitis is so painful and bizarre that even a young boy is unlikely to forget it — the history is
   reliable.
 • Consequences pair: anatomical (e.g. urethritis → stricture) versus functional (direct sperm damage, or
   immune induction in any reproductive organ).
 • Direct effect: C. trachomatis serovar E elementary bodies degrade sperm DNA in a time-dependent
   manner. E. coli harms sperm in vitro, but most bacteria, including E. coli, have little in vivo effect on
   motility; sexually transmitted organisms may be more virulent — differential effects remain unclear.
 • Viruses may integrate into the genome and pass vertically through the germline. Hepatitis C and human
   immunodeficiency virus nucleic material appears in seminal plasma but is not correlated with impaired
   sperm function; human papillomavirus is associated with impaired bulk parameters, and in vitro
   heparinase III lowered viral load without harming sperm function.
 • Indirect mediators: leucocytosis, reactive oxygen species (ROS), interleukin-1 (IL-1), IL-6, IL-8, interferon-γ,
   macrophage migration inhibitory factor, tumour necrosis factor-α, epididymal macrophages, dendritic
   cells — the immune system may lose self-recognition of sperm or overwhelm sperm defences.
 • Non-infectious or post-infectious prostatitis may impair sperm via pyospermia and ROS, antisperm
   antibodies, or altered prostatic ions (zinc, magnesium, calcium, selenium); prostatitis can generate ROS
   without leucocytosis. How much inflammation adds beyond infection is unknown.
Concept — infection damages fertility along two routes, anatomical scarring and functional sperm injury,
and in the functional route the host's inflammatory and oxidative response matters as much as the organism
itself.

Childhood Diseases
 • Early maladies either misroute or obstruct the gamete's path from testis to female tract, or disturb
   production or damage mature sperm.
Pediatric Surgery
 • Childhood hydrocele and hernia repair: low but discrete incidence of vasal obstruction; testis atrophy
   after paediatric inguinal herniorrhaphy is rare.
 • Adolescent hernia repair often uses mesh — consider mesh-related vasal occlusion in an infertile man
   with this history.
 • Posterior urethral valve ablation: scarring and dysfunction in earlier series; rarely seen now.
 • Older bladder neck reconstructions caused retrograde ejaculation; rarely performed today.
Testis Torsion
 • In males ≤25 years, torsion is more than three times commoner than testis cancer.
 • Contralateral biopsy is abnormal in most — implying either unnoticed prior torsion or an underlying
   pathology expressed both as abnormal scrotal anatomy and as spermatogenic dysfunction.
 • About half develop adverse spermatogenic effects; a minority end with low sperm concentration.

 • Torsion disrupts Sertoli cell tight junctions (the blood–testis barrier), so some develop antisperm

Cryptorchidism
Descent sequence: gonadal precursor cells arise on the posterior abdominal wall in gestational week 5 →
differentiation of testicular cell types and compartments, development of the testicular envelope and its
connection to distal ducts → migration towards the nascent inguinal canal → scrotum; completed only in the
third trimester.
 • Regulators (animal models): insulin-like 3 (INSL3), its receptor relaxin/insulin-like family peptide
   receptor 2 (RXFP2), antimüllerian hormone (AMH), HOXA10 and other HOX genes.
 • Some defects only arrest mechanical descent; others also impair spermatogenesis — so infertility
   exceeds the thermal toxicity suffered by an undescended testis.
 • Androgens induce regression of the cranial suspensory ligament in the fourth gestational month,
   permitting descent.
 • Undescended testes affect up to 4% of term newborns, falling to below 1.5% by 1 year. Twin and sibling
   concordance suggests maternal inheritance plus intrauterine environment. Unilateral is about twice as
   common as bilateral.

   lesion lies in the seminiferous epithelium more than in Leydig steroidogenesis.
 • Timing: post-pubertal orchidopexy barely changes bulk semen parameters. AUA guideline (2014): repair

 • About a quarter of cryptorchid boys are born with reduced germ cells; after 15–18 months some biopsies

 • Serum T falls with later age at orchidopexy → earlier surgery spares Leydig function.
 • The old view that germ cells stay quiescent until puberty (so any prepubertal age is equivalent) is wrong:
   the endocrine axis matures and the fetal-to-adult spermatogonial transition occurs very early.
 • Surgical sperm retrieval in azoospermic men with prior orchidopexy: studies conflict, but one series found
   far better retrieval with orchidopexy before about 10 years, and ROC analysis ranks age at orchidopexy
   second only to T in predicting retrieval.

   have sperm retrieved for ICSI.

Genetics
 • Ask whether blood relatives had difficulty conceiving, and about family syndromes linked to reproductive
   dysfunction such as cystic fibrosis (CF).

Sexual History
 • Lifestyle may reduce intercourse frequency below that needed; optimum is daily around ovulation.
 • Mittelschmerz allows some women to predict ovulation, but many misread bodily sensations —
   symptoms are unreliable.
Timing method: basal body temperature or home hormonal kits detect ovulation only after it occurs →
record the day of ovulation for 2–3 cycles → begin daily intercourse several days before the earliest
recorded day. Impractical with advanced maternal age (delays therapy) → more aggressive strategies with
the female fertility specialist.

 • Ask about erectile function: without intercourse, semen is not deposited near the cervical os (Chapters
   71–72).
 • Infertility carries a heavy psychological burden. Intercourse frequency is altered in up to half of males in
   treatment and tracks libido and satisfaction — a revealing question is whether coital frequency has
   changed during evaluation.
 • Coping differs by sex: males distance themselves and problem-solve; females seek social support —
   each may misread the other's normal strategy as a problem.
 • That males conflate fertility with masculinity is a common misconception; it happens only infrequently.
 • Stress may impair semen quality — a vicious circle; once in therapy (including IVF), a male-factor
   diagnosis does not disturb well-being or well-adjusted relationships.
 • Consider referral to a psychologist; framed as problem-solving, many men engage readily.

 KEY POINTS: MALE REPRODUCTIVE HISTORY
   • The most important determinant of a couple's reproductive potential is maternal age.
   • Many conditions affect male reproductive function; organise the history into toxicants, infectious
     processes, childhood conditions and sexual history.

Concept — because reproduction needs two patients and is probabilistic, the history modifies a probability of
conception rather than establishing a diagnosis — timing, frequency and female age set the baseline, and the
TICS exposures adjust it.

PHYSICAL EXAMINATION
General Physical Examination
 • Male and female faces differ; female facial features suggest sex chromosomal and androgenisation
   disorders.
 • Reduced facial, truncal, axillary and pubic hair suggests inadequate androgenisation; pubertal androgen
   failure → high-pitched voice; excess endogenous or therapeutic E2 → gynaecomastia.
 • Eunuchoid habitus (epiphyses fail to close from low pubertal T): arm span >5 cm longer than height;
   lower segment (pubis–heel) >5 cm longer than upper segment (crown–pubis).
 • Klinefelter syndrome is classically described with gynaecomastia, eunuchoid appearance and tall stature
   — but many 47,XXY males lack this habitus.
Obesity
 • ↑ E2 from peripheral aromatisation of T in abundant adipose tissue. A TTTA aromatase polymorphism
   links rising E2 to rising body mass, and carriers show the greatest fall in E2 with weight loss.
 • ↓ T, with four proposed causes: E2 negative feedback → ↓ LH; ↑ leptin; insulin resistance; sleep
   apnoea. LH data are inconsistent → the T deficit may be gonadotropin-independent.
 • ↓ SHBG (attributed to hyperinsulinaemia) → bioavailable T exceeds what total T predicts; an obese man
   may be more androgenised than his total T suggests.
 • Serum inhibin B falls with body mass index (BMI) in adult but not prepubertal males → obesity reduces
   Sertoli cell number during puberty.
 • Endocrine-independent effects: ↓ paternity with ↑ BMI independent of hormones; only extreme obesity
   may act endocrinologically; some studies link BMI to motility but not concentration (implicating
   epididymal function); obesity may degrade sperm DNA integrity and mitochondrial activity.
Concept — obesity impairs fertility on two tracks at once, an aromatase-driven endocrine track and a
hormone-independent track acting on the epididymis and sperm DNA, so correcting hormones alone need
not restore function.

Male Reproductive Physical Examination
 • Most of the system is external and palpable. Examine standing, before a low table or chair — some men
   faint during scrotal palpation; asking about his work distracts him.
 • The partner may add history, but the examination is a tactful moment to ask her to leave so the man can
   speak privately.
Examining the Scrotum
 • Hypoplastic hemiscrotum → contents absent since birth.
 • Asymmetric enlargement → reactive hydrocele or tumour.
 • Visible varicocele.
 • Scrotum lying against the thighs in a large or obese man → insufficient scrotal–body temperature
   gradient.
Examining the Testis and Epididymis
 • The epididymis is normally hard to feel; an easily palpable epididymis is engorged → obstruction.
 • Upper pole discernible but lower pole not → incomplete wolffian duct development.
 • Testis size correlates with sperm production.

Examining the Spermatic Cord
 • Two findings of reproductive significance: is the vas present, and is there a varicocele.
 • The vas is firm and cord-like; vessels are compressible. Veins can mimic the vas, so absence is a difficult
   sign.
 • Meacham's maxim (Randall Meacham): search for the vas as in the first step of vasectomy, delivering it to
   the skin surface; if the presumed vas slips from the fingers three times, it is absent.
 • Unilateral absence → possible complete ipsilateral wolffian failure, including renal agenesis → consider
   renal ultrasound.
 • Bilateral absence → high likelihood of a CF gene mutation → sequence the cystic fibrosis
   transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR); renal agenesis occurs in about one in ten with congenital
   bilateral absence of the vas deferens (CBAVD) → renal ultrasound too.
 • Inspect the upper scrotum for a visible venous plexus, then gently palpate for a varicocele.
 • History: sporadic pre-1955 reports; W. Selby Tulloch (1955) first systematically reported high ligation in
   infertile men with improved counts; Dubin and Amelar studied larger series and educated urologists on
   its pathology and treatment.
 • Varicocele is the commonest nonductal, surgically correctable lesion affecting male fertility: about 1 in 5–
   6 of the general population versus 1 in 3 to 1 in 2 of infertile men. That not all men with a varicocele are
   infertile is one of the field's most perplexing problems.
 • Many 3–4-grade systems exist; a sensible one separates subclinical (treatment does not help) and seen
   from only felt.

Examining the Phallus
 • Semen must be deposited near the cervical os; note phimosis, meatal displacement (hypospadias,

 • Digital rectal examination (DRE) adds little; it may be omitted if the patient is apprehensive.

 • Seminal vesicles are normally impalpable; palpable vesicles are abnormal → engorgement and possible
   ejaculatory duct obstruction (EDO).

 KEY POINTS: MALE REPRODUCTIVE PHYSICAL EXAMINATION
   • Obesity impairs male reproductive potential by endocrine-dependent and endocrine-independent
     mechanisms.
   • Testis size directly reflects spermatogenic mass.
   • Unilateral vasal absence suggests a wolffian duct anomaly; bilateral absence is associated with CF gene
     mutations. In both, renal agenesis may result.

Concept — the examination reads three things directly: androgenisation (habitus, hair, breasts),
spermatogenic mass (testis size) and ductal integrity (epididymis, vas, seminal vesicles) — each points to a
different compartment of the reproductive tract.

LABORATORY EVALUATION OF MALE INFERTILITY
 • Three assessments: endocrine evaluation, semen analysis (the only direct appraisal of the gametes) and
   genomic assessment (a growing field).

Endocrine Evaluation
 • Spermatogenesis depends on intratesticular T, so hypoandrogenism is associated with infertility.
   Adequate androgenisation threshold: 280 or 300 ng/dL total T.
 • Low T is common in men with spermatogenic azoospermia, in oligospermia, and even in a substantial
   share of clinic men with density above 20 million/mL (the 4th-edition WHO threshold).
 • Most men with density ≤22 million/mL do not conceive within a year, so many below it have pathology —
   in about one-third, endocrinopathy. Therefore assess androgenisation in every man presenting with
   infertility, including those above 20 million/mL.
 • No upper limit exists above which endocrine dysfunction is excluded; the WHO 50th percentile (66
   million/mL, 2021 analysis) is a reasonable guide above which a full endocrine evaluation is unnecessary.

 • Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH): testicular adrenal rest tumours and later infertility; monitor
   therapy with 17-hydroxyprogesterone, Δ4-androstenedione, renin and T.
Initial endocrine screen (morning): total T + SHBG + albumin (calculate bioavailable T) → LH and FSH
(pituitary) → E2 (aromatisation).
Concept — one morning sample reads the whole axis: bioavailable T says whether there is a deficit, LH says
where it arises, FSH (or inhibin B) says how much germ-cell mass remains, and E2 says whether
aromatisation is distorting the loop.

Evaluation of Semen
 • Reproduction is probabilistic: the more viable sperm, the greater the chance of fertilisation. The only
   definitive result is azoospermia — only then is a man absolutely sterile.
 • MacLeod (1951): first rigorous statistical comparison of semen from fertile and infertile men (cumulative
   probability histograms, quartiles for concentration, motility, shape). The distributions largely overlap. Low
   values suggest infertility, but values above a threshold do not confirm fertility.
 • The field wrongly assumes a man above threshold (e.g. >15 million/mL) is fertile; the converse does not
   hold.
 • Remedy for overlapping distributions: two thresholds — below the lower, subfertility is probable; above
   the upper, fertility is probable; between, no prediction. Guzick (2001) used classification and regression
   tree (CART) analysis on fertile men versus men whose wives underwent intrauterine insemination (IUI)
   with female factor largely excluded.
Bulk Semen Parameters and the World Health Organization Criteria
 • WHO laboratory manual editions 1–4: thresholds by expert panel and survey (e.g. 20 million/mL) —
   fertile men fall below and infertile men above.
 • 5th and 6th editions: percentile tables of men whose partners conceived within 1 year of stopping
   contraception (Table 69.2). Limitations: derived from a fertile, not infertile, population, and descriptive
   statistics cannot predict outcome.
 • The 6th edition also lists 5th percentiles with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), which has spurred their
   misuse as new thresholds. Best use: show the patient his values against a fertile reference. Pragmatically,
   communicate the 5th percentile as likely infertility and the 50th as typical of conception within a year.

Sperm Density
 • Reported in million/mL. Oligospermia (oligozoospermia) = low density; cryptozoospermia = too few to
   measure reliably.
 • The legacy 20 million/mL threshold was often taken to define oligospermia.
 • Oligospermia is now defined in clinical context: a man with one sample of 10 million/mL who
   impregnates his wife easily may not be oligospermic, whereas one with small testes, raised FSH and
   repeated 20–25 million/mL reasonably is.
 • Total sperm count = volume × density (millions).
Sperm Motility
 • Assess within 30 minutes of liquefaction; low motility = asthenospermia (asthenozoospermia).
 • WHO 6th categories: progressive ("moving actively, either linearly or in a large circle, regardless of
   speed"), non-progressive (all other movement without progression), immotile.
Sperm Morphology
 • Human sperm are highly pleomorphic — bizarre forms outnumber well-formed ones in any man. Excess
   abnormal forms = teratospermia (teratozoospermia).
 • Earlier WHO criteria were generous yet still classed most sperm abnormal. Kruger (strict, Tygerberg)
   morphology: several features assessed, and any one out of range makes the sperm abnormal →
   thresholds around 5% characterise a normal ejaculate. The WHO 6th edition adopted strict morphology.
 • Predictive value is questionable: possibly linked to embryo formation, but most studies show strict
   morphology is unrelated to sperm nuclear integrity and does not predict natural conception, IUI or IVF
   outcome.
 • Technicians scrutinising more closely report ever-lower normal percentages → many men now labelled
   with isolated teratozoospermia likely have adequate fertility.
 • Specific monomorphic defects clearly matter: globozoospermia (acrosome fails to form → small round
   heads); pinhead sperm (basal plate fails to attach to the nucleus opposite the acrosome during
   spermiation → heads absorbed, tails only).
Sperm Vitality
 • Vitality = proportion of metabolically active living sperm; many dead sperm = necrospermia
   (necrozoospermia).
 • Essential with near-total or total asthenospermia, to distinguish cell death from a defect of motility
   machinery.

Genomic Assessment
 • Irony: genes passed from father to son may cause a condition that, untreated, would stop them being
   passed on; genetics plays a significant role in male reproductive dysfunction.
Karyotype
 • Methods: classical dye banding; fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) with sequence-specific probes;
   spectral karyotyping (SKY) colouring every chromosome.
 • ASRM/AUA guideline (2021): genetic testing including karyotype in all men with azoospermia from
   spermatogenic dysfunction and in severe oligospermia (<5 million/mL).
 • Anomaly rates vary by region and cost may be significant → the physician may judge its indication in his or
   her population.
Y Chromosome Microdeletion Testing
 • The Y is among the smallest chromosomes (about 60 megabase pairs), determines male sex, and is the
   only chromosome passed directly father to son; it has a male-specific region and two pseudoautosomal
   regions (PAR1, PAR2).
 • Tiepolo and Zuffardi (1976) located a region on the long arm (Yq) critical for spermatogenesis — the
   azoospermia factor (AZF).
 • About 95% of the Y does not recombine; about a third of that is amplicons (palindromic repeats) — a
   substitute repair mechanism that also creates fragility → microdeletions.

 KEY POINTS: LABORATORY EVALUATION OF MALE INFERTILITY
   • Endocrine assessment includes total T, the portion not bound to SHBG, E2, and LH and FSH.
   • The semen analysis is a probabilistic assessment; aside from azoospermia, no threshold absolutely
     separates fertility from infertility.
   • Semen volume <1.0 mL: differential includes EDO, retrograde ejaculation, and vasal and accessory
     gland maldevelopment (CBAVD).
   • Vitality staining distinguishes complete asthenospermia from necrospermia; common staining
     methods distinguish pyospermia from immature germ cells.
   • Globozoospermia (round heads) indicates deficient acrosome formation; treatment is IVF with ICSI.
   • Blood–testis barrier disruption causes antisperm antibodies, whose significance depends on the
     degree of binding to sperm heads.
   • CFTR screening of men with CBAVD and their partners identifies severe mutations such as ΔF508 that
     may cause overt CF in offspring.

Concept — genetic tests are chosen by phenotype — karyotype and Y microdeletions for failed or severely
impaired production, CFTR for absent or obstructed ducts — and their yield is counselling on retrieval odds
and offspring risk rather than treatment.

IMAGING IN THE EVALUATION OF MALE INFERTILITY

ASSISTED REPRODUCTION
Methods for Sperm Selection
 • ICSI needs only as many sperm as oocytes, and bypasses the natural barriers that normally select the
   fittest sperm.
 • Established: density gradient centrifugation and swim-up — yield fast-swimming sperm with better
   morphology.
 • Newer in vitro selection improves sperm quality but has no conclusive evidence of better reproductive
   outcomes; chemoattractants, microfluidics and optical systems are under investigation.

DIAGNOSES AND THERAPIES
 • Pathophysiology remains incompletely understood and assessment is probabilistic, yet enough is known
   to make reasoned judgements about whether a male cause exists, its basis and plausible therapy.

Genetic Syndromes
 • The human genome was completed in 2004; broad carrier panels exist and whole-genome sequencing is
   used in research, but their use as general screening tools is limited by poor understanding of how
   spermatogenic genes act in concert.
Chromosomal Numerical Disorders
 • Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) is the commonest identified genetic cause of male infertility; 1 in 500–
   1000 live births.
 • Adults: azoospermia in the great majority, small testes, raised gonadotropins; learning disability in most
   children; low T in most men.
 • Tall stature in only about 30% → Klinefelter cannot be excluded by examination.
 • ↑ mediastinal germ cell tumours → broader testicular effects.
 • Karyotype: non-mosaic 47,XXY in 80–90%; remainder mosaic 46,XY/47,XXY or extra/structurally
   abnormal X.
 • About 8% have ejaculated sperm; about 50% have testicular sperm retrievable for IVF/ICSI; earlier
   diagnosis carries a better prognosis.
 • Management trap: boys found after puberty with low T are often given exogenous T alone, suppressing
   residual spermatogenesis. Because spermatogenesis declines progressively, some advocate surgical
   sperm extraction in early to mid puberty, before T and aromatase inhibitor therapy.

Structural Chromosomal Anomalies
 • Y microdeletions: see the AZF comparison — AZFa and AZFb leave no retrievable sperm; AZFc is of
   unclear significance.
 • Deletion carriers with oligozoospermia risk a progressive fall in concentration → consider sperm
   cryopreservation.
 • Ring Y chromosome, r(Y): terminal breaks in both arms with fusion; phenotype varies with material lost.
   Autosomal karyotypic anomalies may also cause infertility.

Testicular Causes
 • Two compartments: seminiferous tubules (gametes) and interstitium (Leydig cells); both are needed, and
   sperm must then exit. Hence three testicular causes: spermatogenic dysfunction, steroidogenic
   dysfunction, microductal obstruction.

Antisperm antibodies
 • Sertoli tight junctions form the blood–testis barrier, protecting haploid germ cells; disruption exposes
   spermatids and spermatozoa to antibody formation → agglutination, impaired motility, reduced
   fertilising potential.
 • Causes: obstruction (e.g. after vasectomy); inflammation (orchitis, prostatitis, sexually transmitted
   infection); heat (varicocele, cryptorchidism, hot tubs); trauma and torsion; genetic (thymic
   maldevelopment, HLA-B28 haplotype).
 • Treatment: condoms and sperm washing lack good evidence — washing removes unbound antibody, but
   the relevant antibody is bound. Corticosteroids: two controlled trials conflict. IVF and ICSI have become
   the common treatment.
Steroidogenic Dysfunction
 • Hypergonadotropic (primary) hypogonadism = impaired T synthesis from Leydig cell dysfunction: ↑ LH,
   ↓ T.
 • Leydig failure may coexist with pituitary insufficiency → low T with variable, non-elevated LH; ageing
   combines falling androgen with a blunted pituitary response.
 • Intratesticular steroidogenesis is an absolute requirement, especially for postmeiotic maturation.
 • Klinefelter men often have low T, but Leydig failure may not be the only mechanism. Genetic causes: LH
   receptor mutations (humans), FSH receptor deficiency (mice); others — orchitis, cytotoxic
   chemotherapy, environmental toxicants.
 • No accepted therapy. Exogenous T is not indicated — it cannot achieve intratesticular levels and
   suppresses LH.
 • Azoospermia + low T + markedly raised LH, desiring paternity → surgical sperm extraction.

Pituitary Dysfunction
 • LH drives Leydig steroidogenesis; FSH drives spermatogenesis via Sertoli cells. Suppression of either,

Prader-Willi syndrome
 • Infantile failure to thrive with poor suck, then loss of satiety in early childhood → marked obesity if
   uncontrolled; about 1 in 15,000–30,000.
 • Features: hypogonadism, small testes, dysmorphic facies, growth hormone deficiency (short stature,

   deficiency presumed hypothalamic.
 • Cause: loss of paternally expressed genes at 15q11–q13 through failed imprinting (normally maternal
   copies silenced, paternal active; in Prader-Willi the paternal copies are also inactive).
Pituitary Tumors and Diseases
 • Sellar lesions — secretory and non-secretory tumours, craniopharyngioma — compress the anterior
   pituitary → variable LH/FSH suppression.
 • Prolactinoma is the commonest relevant pituitary tumour, often with erectile dysfunction, but rare in
   infertile men → routine prolactin in asymptomatic men is questionable.
 • Prolactin 20–50 µg/L: no further evaluation; significant elevation → cranial MRI.
 • Non-surgical prolactinoma: dopamine agonists bromocriptine or cabergoline — cabergoline has fewer
   side effects.
Other Pituitary Lesions
 • Infiltration: infective granulomas, sarcoidosis, histiocytosis; iron deposition (haemochromatosis,
   repeated transfusion).
 • Systemic: morbid obesity, chronic malnutrition, type 2 DM. Treat the underlying condition.

Extrapituitary Endocrine Modulators
 • Exogenous androgens (especially T) suppress gonadotropins; cannabis, antipsychotics, opioids and
   environmental toxicants inhibit the pituitary via oestrogenic and dopaminergic pathways → remove the
   agent.
 • CAH, especially milder forms presenting in adolescence or adulthood → hypogonadotropic hypogonadism
   plus frequent testicular adrenal rest tumours. Corticosteroids may restore fertility, but the long course
   needed for spermatogenesis brings weight gain and skin changes.
Concept — gonadotropin deficiency is the most reliably treatable cause of male infertility because the testis
itself is normal: replace the missing upstream signal — hCG for LH, FSH for FSH, pulsatile GnRH for
hypothalamic failure, or unblock feedback with a SERM or aromatase inhibitor — and spermatogenesis
follows.

Extratesticular Endocrine Dysfunction
 • E2 inhibits gonadotropin release; drugs raising E2 cause hypogonadotropic hypogonadism → substitute
   another agent where possible.
 • Obesity: aromatase-rich adipose raises E2; other suspected links — the "neohormones" leptin and
   kisspeptin, diabetes, sleep apnoea — with or without hypogonadotropism.
 • A large family cohort showed a dose-response relationship between BMI and male infertility, confirmed
   elsewhere.
 • Obesity also lowers LH pulse amplitude and blunts testicular response to LH.
 • Weight loss: rodent diet-and-exercise data positive; human data sparse and inconclusive — still prudent
   to recommend given certain general health benefit. Male obesity worsens clinical pregnancy, miscarriage
   and live birth rates in assisted reproduction.
 • Aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole, testolactone) raise T and lower E2; limited data suggest
   semen improves. Proposed indication: T (ng/dL) : E2 (pg/mL) <10:1 = aromatase overactivity.
 • Caution with long-term aromatase inhibitors: male bone density may be E2-dependent, and long-term
   data are lacking.

Congenital Bilateral Absence of the Vas Deferens
 • Oates and colleagues (early 1990s): men with CBAVD have a high frequency of CF-associated mutations,
   and nearly all men with CF lack both vasa → CBAVD is often a genital form of CF.
 • Two genetic causes: CFTR mutations and alterations in other mesonephric duct genes.
 • Severe mutations on both alleles → CF; a mild mutation on one or both → CBAVD.
 • CF carrier frequency about 1 in 20 in northern Europeans → test the female partner as well.
 • Mild chronic sinus or respiratory symptoms may be overlooked — the urologist may be the first to
   uncover indolent CF.
 • ICSI with surgically extracted sperm is typically effective; one study reported more miscarriage and
   stillbirth with sperm from CFTR-mutation CBAVD men.
 • Genetic counselling (urologist or clinical geneticist) using both partners' CFTR results explains offspring CF
   risk and carrier implications.
Varicocele
 • Imaging is not recommended for screening; diagnosis is clinical.
 • Most men with varicocele have sperm, and female-partner confounders make pregnancy effects
   intractable to study — yet substantial evidence links varicocele to spermatogenic dysfunction.
 • Anatomy: the left internal spermatic vein enters the left renal vein 8–10 cm higher than the right enters
   the vena cava → taller hydrostatic column → left valve incompetence → varicoceles commoner on the
   left.
 • With sensitive techniques (contact thermography, Doppler, venography) most are bilateral; clinical
   significance of these is uncertain.
 • Isolated right varicocele is rare → consider renal pathology such as tumour, especially if of abrupt onset.
 • Origin: venous valvular incompetence; familial clustering in first-degree relatives suggests genetic
   predisposition.
 • Evidence of harm: poorer bulk parameters; smaller testes with progressive atrophy.

Ejaculatory Dysfunction
 • Anatomical, functional or neuropathic → absent emission, resistance or misdirection. Three clinical
   categories: EDO, retrograde ejaculation, anejaculation (treatment of the latter two: Chapters 70 and 74).
Ejaculatory Ductal Obstruction
 • Anatomy: ejaculatory ducts are intraprostatic extensions of the seminal vesicles without their
   musculature — simple conduits. The vasal ampulla joins medially at an acute angle; the duct ends angled
   at the verumontanum, whose two longitudinal muscle layers extend into the urethra.
 • EDO causes <5% of azoospermia. Idiopathic EDO carries CFTR mutations in about half → offer genetic
   evaluation.
 • Causes: infection, inflammation, prior surgery, compression by congenital cysts, anywhere along the
   intraprostatic course.
 • Evaluate when semen volume <1.0 mL (investigations: Imaging). Partial EDO: severe oligospermia or
   azoospermia, ↓ motility, ↓ volume.
Treatment: clinically significant EDO amenable to surgery → transurethral resection of the ejaculatory ducts
(TURED) → depth guided by synchronous TRUS to avoid rectal injury → outcomes: relief of painful
ejaculation and haematospermia, ↑ volume, improved semen quality (Chapter 70).

 • Most sperm in fertile men are eccentrically shaped, so ordinary variation is hard to quantify clinically;
   certain rare discrete defects have overt consequences.

Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia
 • Disrupted microtubular architecture of cilia; the sperm tail shares it → immotile sperm, congenital heart
   disease, chronic respiratory and ear–nose–throat infection, laterality defects.
 • About 1 in 15,000–30,000 live births.

 KEY POINTS: DIAGNOSES AND THERAPIES
   • Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) is the commonest identified genetic cause of male infertility; body
     morphology cannot reliably exclude it.
   • A man with CBAVD needs CFTR assessment of himself and his female partner to define offspring CF
     risk.
   • Severe hypogonadotropic hypogonadism may be associated with anosmia and is treated with
     gonadotropin replacement; milder forms are commoner and may respond to antioestrogens or
     aromatase inhibitors.

Concept — in monomorphic sperm defects the ultrastructure dictates the therapy: ICSI bypasses an absent
acrosome or a paralysed axoneme, but cannot correct the aneuploidy that accompanies macrocephalic
multiflagellate sperm.
