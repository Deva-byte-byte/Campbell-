Incontinence in Females
DEFINITIONS, PREVALENCE, AND COST
 • Urinary incontinence (UI) — International Continence Society (ICS): the involuntary loss of urine.
 • Stress urinary incontinence (SUI) — involuntary loss on effort (sneezing, coughing, exertion); may also be
   a symptom of pelvic organ prolapse (POP).
 • Very common (25–75% of females): over half of US females >20 years have UI — SUI alone largest
   (~26%), then mixed (MUI, ~16%), then urgency (UUI, ~10%).
 • Under-presentation: only 25–45% seek care; ~10% of diagnosed SUI reach surgery.
 • Rising burden: prevalence projected to rise ~55% (to ~28 million US females) and procedures ~47%,
   2010–2050.
 • Risk factors: advancing age, parity, vaginal delivery, obesity, diabetes, smoking, family history, physical
   activity, hormone replacement therapy.
 • SUI at any age: about half of gym-attending females report UI; in nulliparous soldiers, obesity, weight gain
   and low fitness predicted it; in nulliparous females only body mass index (BMI) >25 and age >45 years
   raised odds of pure SUI.
 • Economic cost: substantial ambulatory and out-of-pocket costs; severe UI → high willingness to pay for
   continence.
Concept — Prevalence far outstrips treatment: most affected females never present and few reach surgery,
so demand for durable and acceptable (including mesh-free) operations is set to grow.

GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS

Continence Mechanism
 • Continence needs four elements: functioning striated muscle, well-vascularised submucosa, intact vaginal
   support, intact central and peripheral nervous system; loss of any → reduced outlet resistance → SUI.
 • Two mechanisms of SUI, alone or combined: intrinsic sphincter deficiency (ISD) versus urethral
   hypermobility from failed support. No test can apportion the contribution of each.

 • No consensus on mechanism → no single operation suits all. Suspensions depend on urethral mobility;

Concept — Suspension corrects hypermobility, not the sphincter: it restores urethral position and support,
so it presupposes a mobile urethra; where the defect is intrinsic, a device that compresses rather than
supports (the AUS) is needed.

Retropubic Suspension Procedures
 • Aim: suspend and stabilise the anterior vaginal wall so that the bladder neck and proximal urethra are
   held retropubically; hence most beneficial in SUI with urethral hypermobility.

Artificial Urinary Sphincter
 • History: Foley (1947) — air-inflatable periurethral cuff with removable pump; Scott (1972) — first
   commercial AUS (American Medical Systems). Now the gold standard for post-prostatectomy SUI in
   males.
 • Female role — ISD: reproduces physiological sphincter function.

PATIENT SELECTION AND CONSIDERATIONS IN TREATMENT CHOICES
 • Burch declined as MUS rose (late 1990s–2000s) but remains viable in the settings listed under Indications.

Symptoms
 • Primary indication: SUI. The AUA / Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital
   Reconstruction (SUFU) guideline lists Burch alongside MUS, autologous fascial pubovaginal sling and
   urethral bulking agents.
 • MUI: may be offered to treat the stress component; history establishes the most bothersome component.
 • Not indicated in UUI without SUI.

History
 • Elements: age, bother, amount, triggers and frequency of leakage, goals, prior treatment, medical,
   obstetric and surgical history (neurological disease, multiparity). Quantifying bother sets goals and
   expectations.
 • Triggers separate UUI, urgency- or stress-predominant MUI and pure SUI: cough, sneeze, lift, walk, run →
   ~three-in-four probability of SUI, but a negative history does not exclude it.

Physical Examination
 • History alone is insufficient. Height, weight and pelvic examination: demonstrable SUI, urethral mobility,
   tissue integrity, concomitant POP, oestrogen status.
 • Demonstrating SUI: cough stress test and/or Valsalva supine → if negative → repeat standing with a
   comfortably full bladder.
 • Urethral mobility: by observation or the less-used cotton-swab (Q-tip) test — angle change from rest to
   strain >30° = hypermobility. AUA/SUFU no longer recommends it for diagnosing SUI (a meta-analysis
   found it unlikely to aid diagnosis).
 • POP staged by the Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP-Q) system; note atrophy.
 • Focused abdominal (prior incisions alter the plan) and neurological examination, and overall
   performance status before surgery.

Adjunctive Testing
 • Minimum in the index patient (AUA/SUFU): post-void residual (PVR) and urinalysis. Further options:
   questionnaires, voiding diary, pad tests, urodynamics (UDS).
Questionnaires. Validated instruments assess both UUI and SUI and help separate them; particularly useful
in comparative studies.
Voiding diary. One to three days of intake and voiding pattern; helps when symptoms are unclear, guides
diagnosis and management, and improves the patient's own quantification of bother.
Pad weight tests. Pads per day is not a pad weight test: the weight test confirms urine loss and quantifies
volume (pad weight before versus after).

Urodynamics.
 • Indication: history suggests SUI but no objective leakage on examination or pad test.
 • Record urethral function as Valsalva leak point pressure (VLPP) or maximal urethral closure pressure
   (MUCP): VLPP <60 cm H₂O or MUCP <20 cm H₂O suggests ISD — significance contested.
 • For the Burch, urethral mobility matters more than urethral function. Pressure-flow study and video add
   information on function and patency.

Patient Goals
 • Arguably the most important selection factor. Conservative (behavioural modification, pelvic floor
   physiotherapy ± biofeedback, pessary) through to surgery; choice follows bother and quality-of-life (QoL)
   impact, with accurate expectations.
 • Objective and subjective outcomes correlate poorly. Patients with a "good" baseline QoL on the
   Incontinence Impact Questionnaire (IIQ) should expect smaller QoL gains than those starting "moderate"
   or "poor"; goal-attainment work supports defining pretreatment goals.
 • Discuss mesh preoperatively: most patients know of it from the media; re-education raises acceptance.
   Complicated patients who did not recall counselling were less satisfied → document mesh-complication
   counselling. Burch and autologous slings suit those declining mesh.
Concept — Selection turns on two questions — is the urethra mobile, and what does the patient want?
Mobility decides whether suspension can work; goals (mesh aversion, concomitant surgery, tolerance of risk)
decide whether it should be chosen.

SPECIFIC DISCUSSION REGARDING RETROPUBIC SUSPENSIONS

Pertinent Anatomy
 • Retropubic space (space of Retzius): avascular, preperitoneal potential space. Anterior — pubic
   symphysis; lateral — pubic rami and obturator internus; posterior — bladder; floor — endopelvic fascia
   (fibrofatty outer vaginal wall), paravesical fascia and levator ani fibres. Function: support of urethra and
   bladder.
 • Pubourethral ligaments: at the proximal urethra, fanning from the lower pubis.
 • Arcus tendineus fascia pelvis (ATFP): posterior symphysis → along the obturator internus margin →
   ischial spine. Vaginal attachments here give support; weakness → paravaginal defect.
 • Iliococcygeus (levator ani) arises from the ATFP and passes to the pelvic floor midline.

Indications
 • Assess: age, health, prior surgery, hypermobility, sphincter status, parity, fertility plans, concomitant
   surgery (POP repair, hysterectomy).

 • AUS: endorsed as second-line; best evidence of benefit in ISD, which arises in two settings — recurrent
   SUI after prior anti-incontinence treatment and neurogenic SUI. Extreme form: the "lead-pipe urethra"

Concept — Indications are reasons to avoid the vagina or mesh; contraindications are an absent substrate
for suspension — without a mobile urethra and vagina there is nothing to lift, so the defect must be treated
by a sling or sphincter instead.

Retropubic Surgical Techniques
 • Four mainstays (Burch, MMK, paravaginal, VOS) differ in anchoring point and role of the sutures; all
   elevate the bladder neck, so all need urethral mobility — a well-supported urethra with presumed ISD
   should respond less (speculative).
 • No concomitant abdominal surgery → do not choose a retropubic operation, given successful vaginal
   options that avoid an abdominal incision.

Perioperative Considerations
Positioning and preparation. Low lithotomy or supine with gentle leg abduction (vaginal and retropubic
access together) → prepare from at or above the umbilicus to the perineum, including the vagina → sterile
Foley catheter (drains bladder; identifies urethra and bladder neck) → optional bladder filling to outline
bladder, bladder neck and proximal urethra.

Surgical Technique
Burch Colposuspension
Open Burch. Suspends the anterior vaginal wall and para–bladder-neck tissue to Cooper's (iliopectineal)
ligament. Exposure:
 1. Pfannenstiel or lower midline incision → recti separated in the midline → anterior pelvic reflection
    separated from the bladder.
 2. Optional Cherney incision (rectus tendon divided transversely above the pubis with diathermy, reflected
    cephalad) for access.
 3. Fat and veins swept cranially and laterally to free bladder and urethra.
 4. Bladder neck, proximal urethra and paraurethral vagina identified by palpating the Foley balloon (±
    retrograde filling, intravaginal finger or end-to-end anastomosis (EEA) bowel sizer); prior-surgery scar
    divided sharply off the pubis.
 5. Bladder dissected off the vagina starting laterally; mobility checked with two vaginal fingers.

 6. Fat cleared to expose both Cooper's ligaments — white tendinous condensation over the periosteum of
    the superior pubic ramus.
 • Stay lateral — midline dissection over the urethra risks venous-plexus bleeding, urethrovesical injury,
   entry into tract or vagina, and obstruction if sutures lie too close to the bladder neck.

   • Do not dissect over the urethra or in the midline over the bladder neck.

   • Vagina and urethrovesical tissue are very vascular — identify and avoid Santorini's plexus.

Suture placement. Sutures elevate the bladder neck and induce scarring and fibrosis that maintains the
elevation.
 1. No. 0 or 1-0 delayed-absorbable or non-absorbable suture on an SH tapered needle.
 2. Thimbled finger or small EEA sizer in the vagina identifies and elevates the vagina, confirms wall mobility
    and protects the urethra.
 3. First suture at the level of the mid-urethra, as lateral as possible, through vaginal wall without entering
    the vaginal lumen; contralateral suture likewise.
 4. More cephalad sutures progressively more lateral, at the bladder neck / urethrovesical junction; all
    passed medial to lateral.
 5. Paravaginal sutures passed through Cooper's ligament (or ATFP); heavy bleeding → pressure, clips or
    sutures.
 6. Distal sutures tied first, knots above (anterior to) the ligament, with vaginal elevation.
 7. Confirm space between bladder neck and pubis — about two fingerbreadths.
 • Modification: paravaginal tissue elevated to the internal obturator fascia instead.
Laparoscopic or robotic Burch. Same suspension to Cooper's ligament (first described 1991); intraperitoneal
or extraperitoneal.

Marshall-Marchetti-Krantz Procedure
 • Bladder neck suspended to the periosteum of the pubis either side of the symphysis, replacing the
   urethrovesical junction in an intra-abdominal (retropubic) position; positioning and entry as for Burch.
 • Two pairs of sutures through the full thickness of the vaginal wall either side of proximal urethra /
   bladder neck → periosteum; buttresses the paraurethral region. Modification: include the anterolateral
   urethral wall.

Paravaginal Repair
 • Repairs the fascial weakness where pelvic fascia attaches to obturator fascia / arcus tendineus:
   paravaginal fascia and lateral vaginal sulcus attached to the ATFP, incorporating obturator fascia and
   muscle from pubic symphysis to ischial spine → restores the urethrovesical angle.

Vagino-Obturator Shelf
 • Hybrid of Burch and paravaginal repair: laterally placed vaginal sutures elevate the vagina to the internal
   obturator fascia.
 1. Enter the retropubic space → expose anterior bladder wall at the bladder neck (Foley balloon).
 2. Intravaginal finger elevates the vaginal wall at bladder-neck level.

 3. Sweep endopelvic fascia off the inferolateral bladder neck and proximal urethra to expose obturator

 4. Approximate endopelvic fascia and vagina to obturator muscle bilaterally → ± reinforce with a suture

 • Incomplete emptying — two strategies: suprapubic tube placed intraoperatively (historical, for comfort)
   versus teaching clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC) — less invasive, avoids suprapubic trauma, lets

 • Either way, void and check PVR until consistently <150 mL.
Concept — Anchor height sets the trade-off: high, suture-dependent anchors (Burch, MMK) lift more and
angle the urethra into a V, at a cost of obstruction; low, scar-dependent anchors (paravaginal, VOS) lie flatter
and obstruct less.

OUTCOMES

Important Considerations
 • Few reproducible objective measures; success, perspective, outcomes and tools must be defined.
 • UDS: most objective, poorly predictive. In Burch versus autologous sling arms, urodynamic SUI predicted
   success but leak point pressure did not; normal UDS did not predict postoperative voiding dysfunction.
 • International Consultation on Incontinence (ICI): trials should enrol by symptom-driven criteria; no
   urethral function measure should be the sole measure of SUI severity or predictor of surgical outcome.

Definitions of Success
 • Definitions of cure have varied widely, hindering comparison; standardisation efforts continue.
 • Objective success: cough stress test plus 1-hour pad test.
 • Subjective success: one or more of Urogenital Distress Inventory (UDI/UDI-6), IIQ, King's Health
   Questionnaire (KHQ), ICI Questionnaire–Short Form (ICIQ-SF), Patient Global Impression of Improvement
   (PGI-I).

Patient Perspective
 • Expectations must match what treatment can deliver; counselling needs the qualitative patient
   perspective defined and measured (e.g. Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute, PCORI).

Method of Follow-Up
 • Early postoperative contact (AUA/SUFU); telemedicine is safe after MUS (inferred for suspensions), and
   app-based follow-up gave higher satisfaction than office visits.
 • In-person visit by 6 months: history, examination and PVR; ask about continence, voiding, overactive
   bladder (OAB) symptoms, sexual function and new symptoms; questionnaires optional.

Comparative Studies
 • Burch versus conservative treatment: scant, poor-quality evidence; a subset analysis favoured Burch over
   pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT).
 • Open suspension versus periurethral injection: one randomised trial (surgery pooled: Burch, needle
   suspension, slings) — surgery more successful at 12 months; collagen had fewer and less severe
   complications; no difference in satisfaction or QoL.
 • Versus other non-sling operations (needle suspension, anterior colporrhaphy, MMK, paravaginal repair):
   comparators largely obsolete — see the Cochrane review.

Artificial Urinary Sphincter
 • Evidence base thin — randomised controlled trials (RCTs) called for. Meta-analysis: most patients
   continent, a minority improved; explantation, revision, vaginal erosion and mechanical failure
   substantial; complications — infection, mechanical malfunction, urethral erosion causing recurrent SUI,
   explantation. Surgeon experience is decisive given technical complexity.
 • Minimally invasive versus open (meta-analysis): overall fewer complications with laparoscopic or robotic
   placement — driven by much less mechanical failure and less cuff erosion — although infection and
   atrophy were somewhat higher. Erosion rises with follow-up beyond 5 years.
 • Systematic reviews: most had prior SUI surgery; bladder neck and vaginal injuries, widely varying
   continence, de novo retention; some cannot manage the pump. In non-neurogenic ISD with recurrent
   severe SUI: 0-pad continence in roughly half to most, substantial revision and explantation —
   comparable to transobturator slings in a fixed urethra.

Complications
 • Suture through urethra or bladder → urinary tract infection, irritative voiding, stones, fistula.

 • Intraoperative cystoscopy is mandatory: cystoscopy → confirm ureteric efflux → if absent, release the
   sutures → repeat until ureteric patency confirmed; also check no suture has passed through the Foley
   catheter. Bleeding concern → retropubic drain.
 • Postoperative voiding dysfunction (OAB and/or obstruction); de novo POP after Burch.
 • Osteitis pubis after MMK (0.7–2.5%): presents 2–12 weeks postoperatively with suprapubic pain
   radiating to the lower limbs and a tender symphysis → may resolve spontaneously → or progress to
   osteomyelitis (antibiotics) → worst case, surgical debridement.

Other Considerations
 • Understudied: cost comparison, postoperative sexual function, trainee exposure — procedure volume is
   not keeping pace with trainee numbers.
Concept — Burch is the durable, mesh-free benchmark that slings match or narrowly beat: its niche is set
by patient and context (concomitant abdominal surgery, mesh avoidance, no vaginal access), not by superior
efficacy.

CONCLUSIONS
 • Viable in the properly selected patient: those undergoing an abdominal procedure and those wishing to
   avoid a graft, synthetic or biological.
 • Successful and durable; the Burch remains a guideline recommendation for surgical treatment of female
   SUI.
