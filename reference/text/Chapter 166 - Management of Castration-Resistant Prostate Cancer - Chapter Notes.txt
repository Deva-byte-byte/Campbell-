 Chapter 166 — Management of Castration-Resistant
 Prostate Cancer
   • Suppression of gonadal testosterone is the central principle of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) —
     one of the most effective systemic treatments known for any solid tumour.
   • Progression despite castrate testosterone is universal: almost every patient on ADT eventually shows
     biochemical and clinical progression, reflecting adaptive survival mechanisms in the tumour.
   • Historical outcome with ADT alone (orchiectomy or luteinising hormone–releasing hormone [LHRH]-
     targeted therapy) in metastatic disease: progression-free survival (PFS) 12–20 months, overall survival
     (OS) 24–36 months; conventional endocrine therapy alone improved only modestly over decades.
   • Life-prolonging classes now available in castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC): next-generation
     androgen receptor (AR)-pathway inhibitors (ARPIs); taxane chemotherapy; radiopharmaceuticals
     (radium-223 and prostate-specific membrane antigen [PSMA]-targeted lutetium-177); poly-adenosine
     diphosphate (ADP)-ribose polymerase inhibitors (PARPIs); and immunotherapy.
   • Castration-resistant clones exist early: cells with the castration-resistant phenotype are identifiable in
     the early stages of prostate cancer.
   • The AR stays central: somatic AR alterations are frequent after ADT; a molecularly altered AR can be
     activated by other ligands (oestrogens, progestational agents) and ligand-independently by growth
     factors and cytokines — the rationale for ARPIs such as abiraterone and enzalutamide.
   • Proliferation–apoptosis balance: with androgens present, proliferation exceeds cell death; ablation
     triggers a swift apoptotic cascade; as the tumour progresses the apoptotic threshold rises until
     proliferation again exceeds death, and endocrine-independent cells come to dominate.
   • Lineage plasticity (neuroendocrine/anaplastic differentiation) is common under treatment, ranging
     from bone-predominant disease to aggressive variants with visceral spread and low PSA; these may
     need platinum regimens, as in small cell lung carcinoma.
   • Other targets: peptide growth factors and cell-surface receptors such as PSMA (targets for
     radiopharmaceuticals, antibody–drug conjugates and monoclonal antibodies); BRCA1/BRCA2
     mutations create synthetic lethality with PARPIs (olaparib, talazoparib, rucaparib).
 Concept — castration resistance is not androgen independence: the AR remains the principal driver, so
 the chapter's therapeutic logic runs from deeper AR suppression first to non-AR mechanisms (DNA repair,
 bone, PSMA, immunity) once AR dependence is lost.

 CLINICAL CONSIDERATIONS

 Disease States
   • TNM (tumour, node, metastasis) staging fails in advanced disease: purely anatomic, it does not guide
     management of recurrent or advanced prostate cancer.
   • Clinical states model (Prostate Cancer Clinical Trials Working Group, 2008; updated as PCWG3, 2016)
     classifies patients by response to prior therapy; the chapter's prognostic and therapeutic reasoning is
     built on it.
   • Baseline evaluation: extent of disease; mode and site of progression (PSA rise alone, new bone
     metastasis, visceral or nodal metastasis); symptoms, especially bone pain; response to previous
     endocrine therapy.

 Monitoring progression
   • PSA, computed tomography/nuclear medicine (CT/NM) imaging and symptoms remain the main tools
     for assessing progression.
   • PSA doubling time (PSADT) ≤10 months defined high-risk nmCRPC in SPARTAN, ARAMIS and PROSPER,
     the pivotal trials that set the nmCRPC standard of care.
   • Rising PSA is usually the first sign of progression; historically, with ADT alone, it preceded bone-scan
     progression while patients stayed largely asymptomatic.
   • Radiographic progression without PSA progression is now common because ARPIs are used earlier: in
     ARCHES (enzalutamide + ADT in hormone-sensitive disease) up to two-thirds of radiographic
     progressors had no concurrent PSA progression — PSA alone is insufficient for these patients.
   • Check serum testosterone when non-compliance is suspected or prior regimens do not sustain
     castration (non-steroidal antiandrogen monotherapy, low-dose oestrogens, 5α-reductase inhibitors).

 Continuing ADT
   • Maintain LHRH agonist/antagonist indefinitely, including during chemotherapy and ARPIs; almost all
     CRPC trials mandate continued testosterone suppression (medical or surgical).
   • Rationale: stopping suppression in non-orchiectomised men may worsen progression and survival;
     exogenous testosterone can cause a severe flare (pain, neurological, urological and coagulation
     complications) in a minority. Some studies show no detriment to stopping, but consensus is to
     continue.

 Antiandrogen withdrawal
   • Withdrawal of a first-generation antiandrogen (e.g. bicalutamide; steroidal or non-steroidal) can give
     short-lived PSA falls, symptomatic benefit and occasionally objective responses in a minority.
   • First step when progressing on combined androgen blockade: observed discontinuation of the
     antiandrogen → serial PSA for 4–8 weeks → next manoeuvre.

 Choosing the next therapy
   • Options: second-line hormonal manipulation (first-generation antiandrogen or ARPI), cytotoxic
     chemotherapy, radiopharmaceuticals or molecularly targeted therapy.
   • Second-line hormonal therapy is most common first; bicalutamide lowers PSA with little toxicity, but
     ARPIs have largely replaced first-generation agents over the last decade.
   • Sequential hormonal approach suits limited, asymptomatic metastatic disease (e.g. rising PSA without
     pain).
   • ARPI + PARPI should be considered in patients progressing on ADT who carry homologous
     recombination repair (HRR) mutations, especially BRCA.
   • Sequencing is unknown: the mCRPC trials enrolled men progressing on ADT alone, whereas hormone-
     sensitive disease is now treated upfront with chemotherapy and ARPIs (abiraterone, enzalutamide,
     apalutamide; darolutamide with docetaxel).

 Predicting behaviour
   • PSADT predicts the speed of bone-scan progression and survival; PSADT <3 months → particularly
     rapid course → consider more aggressive management.

   • Symptomatic bone pain or visceral-predominant disease → upfront cytotoxic chemotherapy may
     serve better.
   • Poorly differentiated and neuroendocrine tumours seldom respond durably to further AR-directed
     therapy.
   • Biopsy of metastatic sites in clinically aggressive disease with relatively low PSA may reveal a
     neuroendocrine phenotype on immunostaining; routine biopsy of all such patients is not yet defined.

 Genomic testing
   • mCRPC genomes differ from the primary; the current standard for somatic next-generation sequencing
     is tumour tissue, usually the untreated primary, because metastatic (mostly bone) biopsy is difficult.
   • Liquid biopsy (circulating tumour DNA) is an alternative with limits: clonal haematopoiesis of
     indeterminate potential (CHIP) can produce spurious DNA-repair-gene calls — such patients are
     unlikely to respond to PARPIs despite a "positive" report.
   • Germline testing is recommended for: metastatic prostate cancer; family history of BRCA1/2-, Lynch
     syndrome- or HOXB13-associated cancers; Ashkenazi ancestry.
 Concept — classify by clinical state, not anatomy: prior therapy, pace (PSADT) and phenotype decide
 what comes next, and because ARPIs now come earlier, the old PSA-first pattern of progression can no
 longer be relied upon.

 NONMETASTATIC CASTRATION-RESISTANT PROSTATE CANCER
   • Stage migration created the M0 CRPC group: ADT started at first PSA rise after local therapy, before
     metastases are visible on conventional imaging. The group grew but has likely peaked as PSMA PET
     reclassifies many as metastatic.
   • PSA kinetics are the strongest predictor of metastatic progression (PSA velocity, PSADT); pre-ADT
     factors (pretreatment PSA, PSADT, stage, Gleason score) and response to ADT also contribute.
   • Natural history can be long in unselected men with rising PSA on ADT: about one-third have bone
     metastases at 2 years; median time to bone metastasis about 30 months. Baseline PSA >10 ng/mL and
     PSA velocity independently predict time to bone metastasis and survival.
   • Old approach: sequential first-generation agents (bicalutamide, ketoconazole). Current standard: an
     ARPI when PSADT ≤10 months.
   • Three ARPIs are approved for nmCRPC — apalutamide (first; SPARTAN), enzalutamide (PROSPER) and
     darolutamide (ARAMIS), each improving metastasis-free survival (MFS) and OS (trial details under
     Androgen Signaling Inhibitors).
   • Unresolved: how PSMA PET will redefine this population and whether treatment should follow
     hormone-sensitive or castration-resistant logic.
 Concept — nmCRPC is an imaging artefact defined by kinetics: it exists because conventional scans miss
 disease, so PSADT selects who needs treatment and more sensitive imaging is shrinking the category.

 METASTATIC CASTRATION-RESISTANT PROSTATE CANCER
   • Heterogeneous population; trial eligibility and risk stratification still rest on conventional imaging
     (bone scintigraphy and CT).
   • Most mCRPC remains addicted to AR signalling through AR overexpression, enhancer and gene
     amplification, and transcriptionally active AR splice variants lacking the ligand-binding domain.

 Bone tropism
   • Overwhelming predilection for bone: circulating cells arrest in cortical and medullary bone → adhere
     via receptors for integrins, collagens, laminin and other bone proteins → grow under hormones,
     growth factors and stromal–epithelial interactions in the marrow.
   • Consequences: pain, nerve-root or cord compression, pathological fracture; marrow replacement →
     myelophthisic anaemia and thrombocytopenia.
   • Visceral metastasis in <20%, soft-tissue nodal disease in 30–40%; visceral disease without bone
     involvement is rarer still.
   • Response rate is a poor end point: soft-tissue shrinkage reflects a small fraction of total (mostly bone)
     burden → PCWG3 prefers PFS incorporating bone and soft-tissue radiographic progression.

 Haematological complications
   • Anaemia is the commonest: chronic disease, marrow invasion, blood loss, rarely microangiopathic
     haemolysis with disseminated intravascular coagulation; compounded by pelvic marrow irradiation,
     chemotherapy, radiopharmaceuticals and long-term ADT.
   • Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents: not recommended, or only with caution — they may increase
     mortality in cancer patients.
   • Thrombocytopenia (rarely leukopenia) usually follows extensive radiotherapy or chemotherapy;
     pancytopenia is an end-stage marrow process.
   • Thrombocytosis is non-specific; clotting complications are rare and treatment is usually unnecessary.

 Urological and neurological emergencies
   • Obstructive uropathy (primary tumour or pelvic/retroperitoneal nodes) → infection, pain and renal
     impairment that can preclude renally cleared chemotherapy → relieve obstruction first (internal
     ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy) in chemotherapy candidates.
   • Spinal cord compression is among the greatest oncological emergencies; vertebral involvement makes
     it common and it needs expeditious management (see Palliative Management).
 Concept — bone dominance shapes everything: it produces the complications (pain, fracture, cord
 compression, marrow failure) and explains why response rate misleads and PFS is preferred.

 EVALUATION OF TREATMENT EFFICACY
   • Consistent prognostic factors across settings: performance status, pain, haemoglobin, PSA, alkaline
     phosphatase, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), extent of bone involvement (number or pattern of lesions)
     and visceral disease.

 NEUROENDOCRINE/AGGRESSIVE VARIANT METASTATIC PROSTATE CANCER
   • Neuroendocrine/anaplastic transformation occurs in a small proportion of advanced cases; it is
     inherently endocrine-resistant and needs different treatment.
   • Biology shared with neuroendocrine tumours elsewhere (most often lung): receptors for
     neuroendocrine peptides (somatostatin, chromogranin A, serotonin), parathyroid hormone–related
     protein (PTHrP), TP53 mutation, low PSMA expression.
   • Molecular signature (small cell neuroendocrine prostate cancer): at least two of three — PTEN loss,
     TP53 mutation, RB1 loss.
   • Clinical pattern: subacute, dramatic change — rapidly growing soft-tissue masses (primary site,
     retroperitoneum), liver infiltration, osteolytic bone metastases, parenchymal brain metastases.
   • PSA stops rising or becomes undetectable despite major progression. PSMA PET is thought to be of
     low utility, though some tumours remain PSMA-avid and might benefit from PSMA-based treatment.
   • Biopsy is strongly encouraged → small cell or poorly differentiated neoplasm with neuroendocrine
     immunostaining.
   • Treatment as for small cell lung cancer: cisplatin + etoposide or taxane + carboplatin; doxorubicin-
     containing regimens modestly effective; clinical trials encouraged.
   • Radiotherapy for bulky disease, brain metastases, or local control in critical sites (pain, impending
     fracture, bladder outlet obstruction); combined chemoradiotherapy is often needed.
   • Prognosis poor despite high initial response: survival generally <12 months.

     • Suspect the neuroendocrine subtype in rapidly growing disease with pelvic masses, visceral

       metastases.
     • PSA usually undetectable (or low/declining) despite rapid progression; serum chromogranin A and
       urinary serotonin metabolites may be detectable but are not needed for diagnosis.
     • Invariably hormone-unresponsive, but transiently sensitive to radiotherapy and chemotherapy
       (platinum–etoposide or platinum–taxane).


 ANDROGEN SIGNALING INHIBITORS

 CYP17 Inhibition: Abiraterone and Other Agents
   • AR signalling stays active despite castrate testosterone (<50 ng/dL).
   • Residual androgen sources: LHRH agents do not touch adrenal androgens (up to 10% of total); CRPC
     may synthesise intratumoural androgens; CYP17 (cytochrome P450 17) is overexpressed in CRPC.
   • Pathway: CYP17 converts precursors → dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and androstenedione →
     testosterone → dihydrotestosterone (the most potent androgen). Hence CRPC is not androgen
     independent.
   • Abiraterone acetate: oral selective CYP17 inhibitor; active after ketoconazole (a weak CYP17 inhibitor).

 Abiraterone: practical prescribing
   • Dose: 1000 mg orally daily with prednisone (10 mg daily in mCRPC trials) and continued LHRH agonist/
     antagonist.
   • Why prednisone: CYP17 blockade reduces cortisol → ACTH drive → mineralocorticoid excess →
     hypokalaemia, hypertension, pedal oedema; prednisone blunts the ACTH drive.
   • Other toxicity: raised liver enzymes (case reports of liver injury and jaundice in practice) →
     interruption, dose change or discontinuation; increased mortality in pre-existing cardiovascular
     disease.

   • FDA cautions: cardiovascular disease, adrenocortical insufficiency, liver disease; control hypertension;
     correct hypokalaemia before starting.
   • Take on an empty stomach — food increases bioavailability up to tenfold; alternative: 250 mg daily
     with a low-fat meal (≤5 g fat, ~300 kcal). A reformulation (Yonsa) allows more flexible dosing with
     equivalent efficacy.

 Other CYP17-pathway agents
   • Orteronel (TAK-700): non-steroidal CYP17 inhibitor, more 17,20-lyase-selective; two phase III mCRPC
     trials (chemotherapy-naive and post-docetaxel) and an mCSPC trial did not improve survival → no
     longer pursued.
   • ODM-208: targets CYP11A1; given with steroids; phase I activity.
   • Abiraterone (and Yonsa) are the only FDA-approved CYP17 inhibitors.
   • Moving earlier: LATITUDE and STAMPEDE showed survival gains with ADT + abiraterone/prednisone in
     high-risk metastatic hormone-sensitive disease; in nmCRPC (IMAAGEN, abiraterone + prednisone 5 mg)
     PSA response ≥50% was met, but abiraterone is not approved for nmCRPC. Earlier use limits later
     utility in CRPC.
 Concept — abiraterone treats the androgen the testis does not make; every toxicity (mineralocorticoid
 excess) and every co-prescription (prednisone, continued ADT) follows from blocking CYP17 upstream of
 both cortisol and androgens.

 Androgen Receptor Modulation: Enzalutamide, Apalutamide, Darolutamide and Other
 Agents
   • Resistance mechanisms: constitutively active AR mutations, AR overexpression, bypass through
     accessory pathways (AKT/mTOR/PI3K, HER2/neu).
   • Next-generation antiandrogens gave survival gains that first-generation agents (bicalutamide,
     nilutamide, flutamide) never did.
   • Enzalutamide: potent non-steroidal AR antagonist with no measurable agonist activity, even with
     overexpressed or activated AR; also blocks nuclear translocation and DNA binding at androgen-
     response elements.
   • No steroid needed with enzalutamide — it may be less active with prednisone (prednisone may
     activate a promiscuous AR or act via the glucocorticoid receptor).
   • Apalutamide (ARN-509): pure AR antagonist that also inhibits nuclear translocation and DNA binding;
     does not cross the blood–brain barrier, no seizure signal; notable for rash and hypothyroidism.
   • Darolutamide: blocks androgen binding; structurally distinct; does not cross the blood–brain barrier →
     less fatigue; twice-daily dosing.
   • Class toxicity: fatigue is the most significant; falls and fractures → caution in the elderly (>75 years).

 How New Approvals in the Metastatic Castration-Sensitive Space Affect Treatment for
 mCRPC
   • mCSPC standards now: ADT + docetaxel; + abiraterone/prednisone; + enzalutamide; + apalutamide; +
     docetaxel + darolutamide; + docetaxel + abiraterone/prednisone. Fewer men reach mCRPC on ADT
     alone.
   • Cross-resistance: progression on one ARPI predicts resistance to another; sequential ARPI benefits only
     15–30% — try only with close monitoring.

   • Practical selection factors: comorbidity (infection, diabetes, accelerated cardiovascular disease, liver
     disease, seizure disorder); co-medications; cost (oral co-payments may make chemotherapy the

   • Drug–drug interactions (anticoagulants, antiretrovirals, antidepressants especially):

         substrates.
 Concept — prior exposure now dictates the CRPC menu: ARPI-on-ARPI rarely works, so the logic is to
 switch mechanism — AR ↔ taxane ↔ radioligand — rather than intensify the same one.

   KEY POINTS: ANDROGEN SIGNALING INHIBITORS
     • Next-generation ARPIs improve OS in castration-sensitive disease (abiraterone, enzalutamide,
       apalutamide; darolutamide with docetaxel) and in CRPC (abiraterone, enzalutamide).
     • Cross-resistance limits ARPIs used in sequence.
     • Three drugs approved for nmCRPC: apalutamide, enzalutamide, darolutamide.


 NOVEL ANDROGEN RECEPTOR–TARGETING APPROACHES

 Androgen Receptor Degraders
   • Rationale: resistant mCRPC shows AR overexpression, increased nuclear translocation, and ligand-
     binding-domain mutations or splice variants that stabilise AR by reducing proteolysis → degrade the
     receptor instead of blocking it.
   • PROTAC (proteolysis-targeting chimera): tags a target protein for destruction by the ubiquitin–
     proteasome system; highly selective, fewer off-target effects, can reach "undruggable" targets.
   • ARV-110: phase I in heavily pretreated mCRPC (≥2 prior therapies incl. enzalutamide or abiraterone);
     manageable toxicity; PSA falls ≥50% (PSA50) in 46% of those with AR T878A/S and/or H875Y
     mutations; later trials stratify by AR mutation.

   • ARV-766: degrades wild-type AR and ligand-binding-domain mutants including the commonest, L702H;

 Concept — degradation defeats the mutations that defeat antagonists: when the receptor is mutated or

 CYTOTOXIC CHEMOTHERAPY

 Chemotherapeutic Agents
   • Historically tried: cyclophosphamide, 5-fluorouracil, estramustine, vinorelbine, etoposide, cisplatin,
     carboplatin, doxorubicin, mitoxantrone, paclitaxel, docetaxel.
   • Still in use: docetaxel, cabazitaxel, occasionally platinum, perhaps mitoxantrone — the rest gave
     neither symptom nor survival benefit.
   • Survival on chemotherapy in advanced CRPC: about 16–20 months.

 Docetaxel
   • Mechanism: β-tubulin binding → microtubule stabilisation (GTP-independent polymerisation) → G2/M
     arrest; TP53-independent apoptosis; BCL2 phosphorylation (caspase-3 activation; loss of anti-apoptotic
     function; BAX no longer inhibited); CDKN1B (p27) induction; BCL-XL repression; impairs microtubule
     transport of ligand-bound AR into the nucleus.
   • 2004: drug of choice for mCRPC (TAX 327), superseding mitoxantrone + prednisone.

 TAX 327 (landmark)
   • Design: 1006 chemotherapy-naive men with stable pain; all on prednisone 5 mg twice daily and
     continued ADT; three arms — mitoxantrone 12 mg/m² 3-weekly; docetaxel 75 mg/m² 3-weekly;
     docetaxel 30 mg/m² weekly; planned 30 weeks (10 cycles).
   • Result: OS 18.9 months (3-weekly docetaxel) vs 17.3 (weekly) vs 16.4 (mitoxantrone); 3-weekly
     docetaxel cut death risk by 24% (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.62–0.94, P = 0.009); pain and PSA responses also
     best with docetaxel.
   • First demonstration that chemotherapy prolongs survival in mCRPC.
   • Schedule contrast: 3-weekly → more neutropenia and febrile neutropenia, slightly more neuropathy;
     weekly → more nausea/vomiting, fatigue, nail change, hyperlacrimation, diarrhoea. Quality of life
     (FACT-P) similar between schedules, better than mitoxantrone.

 SWOG 9916
   • Docetaxel 60 mg/m² + estramustine vs mitoxantrone + prednisone: OS 17.5 vs 15.6 months, HR 0.80.
   • Estramustine abandoned: thromboembolism (required warfarin and aspirin prophylaxis),
     gastrointestinal and cardiovascular toxicity, and no added activity over docetaxel alone.

 Failed docetaxel combinations
   • No OS gain from adding anti-angiogenics (bevacizumab, aflibercept, lenalidomide — despite VEGF
     correlating inversely with survival), bone-targeted agents (atrasentan, zibotentan, dasatinib) or high-
     dose calcitriol.
   • Why they failed: marginal partner activity; no randomised phase II before phase III; docetaxel dose
     reductions from overlapping toxicity.

 Docetaxel moved upfront
   • Docetaxel + ADT improves survival in hormone-sensitive disease; triplets (ADT + docetaxel + ARPI) beat
     ADT + docetaxel — PEACE-1 (abiraterone) and ARASENS (darolutamide).

   • At CRPC: re-treat with docetaxel, or move to cabazitaxel if progression occurred on docetaxel.

 Cabazitaxel
   • Second-generation taxane with poor affinity for P-glycoprotein (ATP-dependent efflux pump) → active
     in docetaxel-sensitive and docetaxel-resistant tumours; neutropenia was dose-limiting in phase I.
   • TROPIC (landmark; FDA approval June 2010): 755 men progressing after docetaxel; cabazitaxel 25 mg/
     m² 3-weekly + prednisone vs mitoxantrone 12 mg/m² + prednisone → OS 15.1 vs 12.7 months (HR
     0.70); better PFS, PSA and radiological responses; no difference in pain response.
   • Subgroups: benefit regardless of measurable disease, pain, or progression on vs after docetaxel;
     greatest with ECOG (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group) 0–1 and in those progressing <3 months
     from docetaxel start → little cross-resistance with docetaxel.
   • Toxicity: grade 3–4 neutropenia 82% (febrile 8%), worse >65 years; diarrhoea (worse >65 and after
     prior radiotherapy); fatigue; neuropathy rarely severe (grade 3 ~1%). Growth factor support
     recommended.
   • PROSELICA: 20 mg/m² non-inferior to 25 mg/m² for OS with fewer grade 3–4 events (40% vs 55%) →
     label changed to 20 mg/m² for most; 25 mg/m² reasonable if low neutropenic risk.
   • FIRSTANA: cabazitaxel (20 or 25 mg/m²) not superior to docetaxel first line → cabazitaxel is not
     approved as first-line chemotherapy.
   • CARD: after docetaxel and one ARPI (abiraterone or enzalutamide), cabazitaxel beat a second ARPI —
     OS 13.6 vs 11.0 months (HR 0.64); PFS 4.4 vs 2.7 months (HR 0.52).

 Mitoxantrone
   • First chemotherapy approved for CRPC (FDA 1997, with prednisone), for symptomatic disease:
     semisynthetic anthracycline; best palliation with low-dose corticosteroid.
   • Two randomised trials (vs prednisone alone; vs hydrocortisone alone) improved quality of life and pain
     but not survival.
   • Current role: docetaxel- and cabazitaxel-refractory disease, or marginal performance status.

 Platinum Agents
   • Not approved or widely used in unselected mCRPC.
   • Platinum-sensitive subsets: small cell/aggressive variant (cisplatin–etoposide or docetaxel–carboplatin;
     responses short); a randomised phase II showed cabazitaxel + carboplatin prolonged PFS in aggressive-
     variant but not usual mCRPC; DNA-repair-deficient tumours, e.g. germline BRCA2 responding better to
     docetaxel–carboplatin than docetaxel alone.

 Novel Chemotherapeutic Approaches
   • Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs): antibody to a cell-surface antigen delivers a cytotoxic payload —
     targets include PSMA, TROP-2, STEAP-1, tissue factor, delta-like protein 3 (DLL3).
   • Shared payload (typically a microtubule inhibitor such as monomethyl auristatin E) → sensitivity
     depends on target prevalence; progression on one PSMA-targeted therapy (e.g. 177Lu-PSMA) does not
     preclude response to a PSMA ADC.
   • Toxicity stems from the payload and from target expression on normal tissue.
 Concept — taxanes are the only cytotoxics that extend life in mCRPC: docetaxel first, cabazitaxel after it,
 with cabazitaxel's P-glycoprotein escape explaining why docetaxel failure does not predict cabazitaxel
 failure.

   KEY POINTS: CYTOTOXIC CHEMOTHERAPY
     • Docetaxel is standard first-line chemotherapy: prolongs PFS and OS, relieves pain, improves quality
       of life.
     • Docetaxel toxicity: myelosuppression, fatigue, peripheral oedema, neurotoxicity, hyperlacrimation,
       nail dystrophy.
     • Cabazitaxel is second-line chemotherapy after progression on or after docetaxel; 20 or 25 mg/m² 3-
       weekly both acceptable, the lower dose less toxic.
     • Cabazitaxel toxicity: neutropenia (including febrile), diarrhoea; generally not neuropathy.

 RADIOPHARMACEUTICALS

 Radium-223
   • Evolution: beta emitters strontium-89 and samarium-153 (bone-pain palliation) → largely replaced by
     the alpha emitter radium-223.
   • Alpha physics: path length <100 µm → spares adjacent marrow → limited haematological toxicity.
   • ALSYMPCA (landmark): symptomatic bone metastases, no known visceral disease or bulky nodes; 50
     kBq/kg IV every 4 weeks × 6 → OS HR 0.70, median gain 3.6 months (14.9 vs 11.3); time to first
     symptomatic skeletal event delayed ~6 months (HR 0.66); better quality of life (FACT-P).
   • Tolerability: adverse and serious adverse events fewer than placebo; diarrhoea commonest; anaemia
     in about one-third (grade ≥3 in 13%).
   • Radium-223 + abiraterone/prednisone: trial stopped early for increased deaths and fractures → label
     warning against the combination.
   • With any ARPI (e.g. enzalutamide): start or continue a bone-protective agent — zoledronic acid or
     denosumab (receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand [RANKL] inhibitor).

 POLY–ADP-RIBOSE POLYMERASE INHIBITORS AND DNA REPAIR TARGETING
   • PARP repairs mainly single-strand breaks; cells lacking homologous recombination (the main double-
     strand repair route) depend on it.
   • Two mechanisms: catalytic inhibition (competes with NAD+) and PARP trapping (cytotoxic PARP–DNA
     complexes; trapping potency differs between agents) → replication-fork stalling → double-strand
     breaks → death without HRR = synthetic lethality.
   • Not effective in unselected patients; targeted to somatic/germline HRR mutations — BRCA1/2, ATM,
     PALB2, FANCA and other Fanconi genes. Somatic DNA-repair mutations in mCRPC: up to 20–25%.

   • AR–PARP synergy: PARP1 also regulates transcription and promotes AR signalling; AR maintains HRR
     gene expression; ADT upregulates PARP-mediated repair → rationale for ARPI + PARPI first line (mirrors
     AR inhibition radiosensitising via impaired DNA repair).

 Poly–ADP-Ribose Polymerase Inhibitors as Single Agents
   • TOPARP-A (olaparib, phase II; 50 heavily pretreated mCRPC): response ~33% overall but ~88% with
     DNA-repair mutations (all BRCA2-loss patients had PSA falls >50%; 4 of 5 ATM); better rPFS and OS if
     biomarker-positive → breakthrough designation for BRCA1/2- or ATM-mutated mCRPC after taxane
     and ARPI.
   • TOPARP-B: 98 patients with alterations in 18 DNA-repair genes; composite response 43/98; BRCA1/2
     best, but activity in other HRR genes supported broader HRR use.
   • PROfound (landmark, phase III): progression on a second-generation hormonal agent; olaparib vs
     enzalutamide/abiraterone; cohort A (BRCA1/2 or ATM): imaging PFS 7.4 vs 3.6 months (HR 0.34), OS
     18.5 vs 15.1 months; benefit held across the 15-gene HRR panel → FDA approval May 2020 for a broad
     HRR panel.
   • TRITON2 (rucaparib; 115 BRCA-altered, after ARPI and one taxane): objective response 43.5%
     (independent review) / 50.8% (investigator) in measurable disease; PSA response 54.8%; similar in
     germline and somatic and in BRCA1 and BRCA2, but higher PSA response with BRCA2.
   • TRITON3 (rucaparib vs physician's choice ARPI or docetaxel; 405 randomised after ARPI progression):
     imaging PFS 11.2 vs 6.4 months (HR 0.50) in BRCA; 10.2 vs 6.4 (HR 0.61) intention-to-treat; ATM no
     benefit (8.1 vs 6.8, HR 0.95) → rucaparib approved for BRCA1/2, not ATM.
   • GALAHAD (niraparib; single-arm phase II; BRCA1/2, ATM, FANCA, PALB2, CHEK2, BRIP1, HDAC2; after
     ARPI and taxane): median rPFS 8.2 months in the BRCA cohort → breakthrough designation (2019) for
     BRCA1/2-mutated mCRPC.
   • TALAPRO-1 (talazoparib; single-arm; DDR-HRR alterations; after 1–2 taxanes and ARPI): objective
     response ~30%; grade 3–4 anaemia commonest (31%).

 Biomarkers and End Points in Poly–ADP-Ribose Polymerase Inhibitor Trials
   • Response confined to HRR-deficient tumours; panels vary (up to 15 genes vs limited sets). Evidence is
     clearest for BRCA2; weaker or inconsistent for others (e.g. ATM).
   • Early PARPI use appears advantageous; trials extend into first-line mCRPC and mCSPC.
   • End points: OS is the best yardstick but is diluted by effective subsequent therapy; rPFS =
     randomisation to first radiographic progression by blinded independent central review; objective
     response rate (ORR) = complete + partial response by RECIST, the commonest basis for FDA solid-
     tumour approvals; PSA and CTCs are less direct.

 The Evolving Treatment Landscape of Poly–ADP-Ribose Polymerase Inhibitors and Side
 Effects
   • Hormone-sensitive trials: AMPLITUDE (niraparib + abiraterone in HRR-mutated mCSPC); TALAPRO-3
     (TALAPRO-2 design in DNA-repair-altered mCSPC).
   • Class toxicity: nausea, fatigue, anaemia (watch for sudden haemoglobin falls); rarely myelodysplastic
     syndrome and pulmonary embolism.
   • Switching PARPI for intolerance is reasonable if eligible for more than one (e.g. olaparib or rucaparib in
     BRCA); toxicity matters more as use moves earlier.
 Concept — PARPIs are only as good as the repair defect they exploit: BRCA2 loss gives true synthetic
 lethality, ATM loss does not — and ADT-induced repair dependence is why pairing with an ARPI works.

 NOVEL MOLECULAR APPROACHES

 Targeting ATR
   • ATM-deficient tumours respond poorly to PARPIs, but ATR is synthetically lethal with ATM → ATR
     inhibitors may suit this subset.
   • Berzosertib: randomised phase II, carboplatin–docetaxel vs carboplatin–berzosertib → stopped at
     interim for futility (fewer responses, more severe toxicity).
   • Ceralasertib (AZD6738): tolerable with abiraterone (phase I); TRAP phase II with olaparib, with and
     without HRR deficiency (ATM loss or biallelic BRCA1/2 loss); primary end point response rate (RECIST or
     PSA fall ≥50%); eligibility after ≥1 mCRPC line.

 Targeting DNA Protein Kinase
   • DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK): part of non-homologous end joining (NHEJ); drives
     progression via Wnt transcription; promotes glycolysis (ALDOA, PKM2 activity; pyruvate and lactate
     synthesis); explored as radio- and chemosensitiser; agents in development.
 Concept — each DNA-repair defect needs its own synthetic-lethal partner: BRCA with PARP, ATM with
 ATR — a single "HRR-positive" label hides these differences.

 IMMUNOTHERAPY
   • Why prostate cancer suits immunotherapy: slow growth gives time to mount a response; tissue-
     specific antigens (PSA, prostatic acid phosphatase [PAP]).
   • Approaches tried: dendritic-cell therapy, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)
     adjuvant, viral vectors, whole-cell and single-antigen vaccines, DNA vaccines, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte
     antigen-4 (CTLA-4) and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade.
   • Only sipuleucel-T succeeded; GVAX (allogeneic whole-cell), PROSTVAC-VF (poxviral PSA) and
     ipilimumab failed in late phase.

 Sipuleucel-T
   • Autologous CD54+ dendritic cells (collected by apheresis) loaded with a PAP–GM-CSF fusion protein.
   • Two phase II/III trials missed their time-to-progression end points, but pooled analysis showed OS 23.2
     vs 18.9 months (HR 0.67); OS was not primary, so no approval then; post hoc benefit seemed confined
     to Gleason ≤7.
   • IMPACT (landmark; FDA approval April 2010): 512 patients, 2:1, asymptomatic or minimally
     symptomatic mCRPC; no visceral metastases or opioid use; 85% chemotherapy-naive → OS 25.8 vs 21.7
     months (HR 0.78) despite 64% placebo crossover.
   • No change in PFS or PSA/radiographic response — an immunotherapy class effect (delayed action,
     flare); immune-related response criteria proposed.

 Immune Checkpoint Blockade
   • CTLA-4 blockade (ipilimumab): phase I/II — PSA fall ≥50% in 10–20%, radiological response ~5%;
     immune-related events: colitis 15–20%, hepatitis, endocrinopathies incl. adrenal insufficiency,
     dermatitis/vitiligo, hypophysitis.
   • Post-docetaxel phase III (799; 8 Gy to a bone metastasis then ipilimumab vs placebo): better PSA
     response and PFS, OS not significant (HR 0.85, P = 0.053); best without visceral disease and with
     normal haemoglobin and alkaline phosphatase.
   • Pre-docetaxel phase III (600): no OS benefit (HR 1.11) despite longer PFS.
   • Pembrolizumab: KEYNOTE-028 response 17%; KEYNOTE-199 (post-docetaxel and ARPI) response ~5%.
   • Negative phase III combinations: atezolizumab + enzalutamide; pembrolizumab + enzalutamide
     (KEYNOTE-641), + docetaxel (KEYNOTE-921), + olaparib (KEYLYNK-010) → no role for checkpoint
     inhibitors in unselected mCRPC.

 Pembrolizumab for Microsatellite Instability–High Cancers
   • Tumour-agnostic FDA approval (May 2017): microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair-
     deficient (dMMR) solid tumours progressing on prior therapy; 200 mg IV 3-weekly; response 40%
     across colorectal and non-colorectal cancers.
   • Prostate experience: case-level complete, partial and near-complete PSA responses, concentrated in
     dMMR/MSI-H tumours.
   • Authors' recommendation: pembrolizumab for MSI-H/dMMR mCRPC after ≥1 mCRPC line (e.g.
     abiraterone or docetaxel); also indicated for tumour mutational burden ≥10 mutations/megabase.
   • dMMR prevalence in mCRPC 2–5%; test by next-generation sequencing or immunohistochemistry for
     MSH2, MSH6, MLH1, PMS2 → if positive, genetic counselling for Lynch syndrome.
   • Toxicity: fatigue, pruritus, rash, gastrointestinal upset; immune-mediated colitis, hepatitis,
     endocrinopathy, pneumonitis, nephritis.

 Novel Immunotherapy Approaches
   • B7-H3: T-cell co-regulatory molecule overexpressed in prostate cancer, linked with poor outcome;
     ADCs, monoclonal and bispecific antibodies and CAR-T in development. Enoblituzumab (neoadjuvant,
     localised disease): feasible, no surgical delay, grade 3 events in 12% (including one perimyocarditis).
   • Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells — mostly PSMA-directed: early study tolerable with stable
     disease; TGF-β-resistant PSMA CAR-T: 100% manufacturing success, cytokine release syndrome and
     one death, PSA falls ≥30% in 4 of 13; with low-dose IL-2: universal grade 3–4 toxicity, two partial
     responses. Prostate stem cell antigen CAR-T: dose-limiting grade 3 cystitis, manageable after modified
     lymphodepletion.
   • Efficacy not yet established; toxicity potentially significant.

 Other Vaccine Strategies
   • Vaccine monotherapy is ineffective; checkpoint upregulation causes resistance → combinations with
     checkpoint inhibitors.

     • Sipuleucel-T, the first therapeutic cancer vaccine approved by the FDA, is indicated for

     • Future immunotherapy: novel targets, earlier use, combination with standard therapy.


 EMERGING THERAPEUTIC APPROACHES

 Bipolar Androgen Therapy
   • Bipolar androgen therapy (BAT): depot testosterone while continuing LHRH agonist/antagonist →
     testosterone swings from supraphysiological to near-castrate over each 4-week cycle.
   • RESTORE (phase II; testosterone cypionate 400 mg IM 4-weekly): PSA50 14–30% by cohort; after BAT,
     enzalutamide rechallenge gave PSA50 of 68% in previously enzalutamide-resistant men.
   • TRANSFORMER (randomised phase II; post-abiraterone; BAT vs enzalutamide): primary PFS equal (5.7
     months each; negative); post hoc PFS2 28.2 vs 19.6 months favouring BAT → enzalutamide (PSA50 to
     enzalutamide after BAT 77.8%).
   • Safety: suitable only without opioid-requiring pain or bulky visceral disease; generalised pain, fatigue,
     peripheral oedema; improves energy, social function and sexual/erectile function.

 Prostate-Specific Membrane Antigen Targeting: Bispecific T-Cell Engager Approaches
   • Bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTEs) bind CD3 on T cells and a tumour antigen (PSMA), following
     blinatumomab (CD19/CD3) in B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia.
   • AMG 212, AMG 160, JNJ-63898081: PSA responses, often transient, but cytokine release syndrome
     dose-limiting → inpatient monitoring and tocilizumab (anti-IL-6 receptor); development uncertain.

 PI3K/Akt/mTOR Inhibition
   • Pathway: growth factor → receptor tyrosine kinase (or Ras/G-protein-coupled receptor) → PI3K
     converts PIP2 to PIP3 → Akt → mTOR (protein synthesis, cell growth); Akt blocks apoptosis via BAD,
     BAX, NF-κB, MDM2.
   • PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homologue) dephosphorylates PIP3 → PIP2, terminating signalling;
     PTEN loss in ~40%; pathway altered in nearly half of primaries and virtually all metastases; PIK3CA
     activating mutations rare, PIK3R1/PIK3R3 loss-of-function common.
   • mTOR inhibitors failed (rapamycin, everolimus, temsirolimus ± bicalutamide) — feedback upregulation
     upstream.
   • Reciprocal crosstalk: PI3K inhibition activates AR in PTEN-deficient cells; enzalutamide activates Akt (via
     reduced PHLPP) → rationale for dual blockade; BEZ235 + abiraterone too toxic.
   • Ipatasertib (pan-Akt inhibitor): phase II + abiraterone — rPFS benefit confined to PTEN loss;
     IPATential150 (phase III, 1101 patients): rPFS in PTEN-deficient 18.5 vs 16.5 months (significant);
     intention-to-treat 19.2 vs 16.6 months (HR 0.84, not significant); grade ≥3 events 70% vs 39%;
     diarrhoea prominent. Capivasertib + docetaxel under study.
 Concept — blocking one growth pathway reactivates the other: AR and PI3K signalling compensate for
 each other, so single-pathway inhibition gives modest gains and dual blockade is limited by toxicity.

 PALLIATIVE MANAGEMENT

 Pain and Spinal Cord Compression
   • Palliation and quality of life are the most important objectives; cancer pain is the most debilitating
     symptom.
   • Focal bone pain → external-beam radiotherapy; image painful scan-positive sites with plain
     radiographs or CT to exclude lytic lesions or pathological fracture, especially in limbs and weight-
     bearing bones.

 Epidural cord compression
   • Common in prostate cancer (vertebral and paravertebral spread); early treatment preserves
     ambulation and sphincter function.
   • Mostly from vertebral bodies; less often paravertebral soft tissue — which may show only a
     neurological deficit with normal bone imaging.
   • Spinal MRI is the routine investigation, replacing CT and conventional myelography.
 Sequence: suspected compression → high-dose IV dexamethasone (16–100 mg/day; load 10 mg IV or 20–
 40 mg orally, then 4–10 mg every 6–12 h) → MRI → definitive radiotherapy ± decompressive surgery →
 taper steroids over 2–3 weeks once improved.
   • Decompressive surgery + radiotherapy beats radiotherapy alone (Patchell randomised trial, 2005).
   • Operate if: progression during radiotherapy; unstable pathological fracture; recurrence after
     radiotherapy — weighed against overall prognosis.
   • Chemotherapy rarely used acutely.

 Bone-Targeted Approaches
   • Normal remodelling: osteoclastic resorption → osteoblastic formation repairing the defect left by
     osteoclasts.
   • In prostate cancer: ADT increases osteoclastic resorption; tumour resorbs matrix locally; cytokines,
     growth factors and bone morphogenetic proteins drive both cell types; metastases are predominantly
     blastic.
   • Hypercalcaemia is rare; significant hypercalcaemia usually signals neuroendocrine disease (PTHrP).
   • Two uses: ADT-related osteopenia/osteoporosis (oral bisphosphonates or RANKL inhibitor, per FRAX
     fracture risk) and skeletal-related event (SRE) prevention in metastatic disease (IV bisphosphonate or
     RANKL inhibitor) — fewer fractures, less pain, opioid use and admission, but no antitumour effect.
   • ARPIs and radium-223 also reduce SREs; bone agents strongly recommended with radium-223;
     radium-223 + abiraterone/prednisone not recommended.

 Bisphosphonates
   • Mechanism: inhibit osteoclast activity and proliferation.
   • Zoledronate: reduced SREs vs placebo in progressive CRPC with bone metastases (randomised trial);
     zoledronate and pamidronate increase bone density on long-term ADT.

   • Dose: 4 mg IV every 4 weeks, adjusted for creatinine clearance; supplement calcium 1000 mg/day +

 RANK Ligand Inhibitors
   • Mechanism: tumour cytokines induce RANKL → activates RANK on osteoclasts; blocked by antibody or
     recombinant osteoprotegerin (natural decoy receptor).
   • Denosumab vs zoledronate (landmark phase III, 1904 bisphosphonate-naive mCRPC): time to first SRE
     20.7 vs 17.1 months; first and subsequent SREs HR 0.82; no OS or PFS difference.
   • Toxicity: fatigue, nausea, hypophosphataemia, hypocalcaemia (grade ≥3 5%), ONJ 2–4% → calcium and
     vitamin D strongly encouraged.
   • Dose: 120 mg SC every 4 weeks; no renal adjustment or monitoring.

 Other Palliative Needs
   • Hydronephrosis from pelvic nodal progression: unilateral lymphoedema, rising creatinine, or incidental
     finding. Not all need drainage if the contralateral kidney functions; nephrostomies carry home-care
     burden; internal stents need regular change.
   • Circumoral numbness → skull-base involvement → irradiate the skull base.
   • Paraneoplastic syndromes are rare → consider neuroendocrine/aggressive variant.
   • Weight loss and anorexia: usually treatment toxicity or progression — a normal sign that time may be
     limited; supplements can add distress; address family concern.

 Hospice and End-of-Life Care
   • Shift to symptom-directed care when options are exhausted and performance declines (sedentary,
     housebound); discuss this phase as expected.
   • Hospice adds support for pain and symptom control and improves bereavement; initiate in a timely
     fashion.
 Concept — bone agents protect the skeleton, not the patient's lifespan: they reduce skeletal events
 without antitumour effect, whereas cord compression is the one palliative problem where hours, steroids
 and surgery change outcome.

   KEY POINTS: PALLIATIVE MANAGEMENT
     • Back pain with known bone metastases → evaluate for cord compression: back pain, focal motor/
       sensory deficit, or bladder/bowel change.
     • Immediate spinal MRI and high-dose corticosteroids; definitive treatment is surgical
       decompression (if feasible), radiotherapy, or both.
     • Zoledronic acid and denosumab both prevent SREs in castration-resistant bone metastases;
       denosumab needs no renal dosing; both can (rarely) cause ONJ.
     • Palliative and end-of-life care is multidisciplinary: symptom control, clear communication of
       expected outcomes; hospice adds support.


 CONCLUSION
   • Four open challenges: (1) optimal sequencing of approved and experimental agents; (2) rational
     combinations exploiting feedback loops and alternative-pathway activation to overcome resistance; (3)
     smarter trials that discard failures quickly and fast-track winners; (4) patient selection by clinical and
     molecular features (DNA-repair mutations, PTEN loss).
   • Prospective biomarker-driven trials — with tumour biopsies or correlative samples — are the only
     route to these answers.
 Concept — the problem has moved from finding drugs to ordering them: with many active agents,
 sequencing and biomarker selection now determine outcome.
