## Instalment 1 — Chapter Opening; History: Storage and Voiding Symptoms, Posture and Holding Manoeuvres, Bowel Function, Bristol Stool Scale, Rome IV (Table 40.1), ICCS Constipation Algorithm, Diaries and Estimated Bladder Capacity, Urinary Tract Infection, Neurological Symptoms; Validated Questionnaires (DVSS, DVISS, PinQ, ISI-P); Psychological Screening (CBCL, SSIPPE); Choosing a Patient-Reported Outcome Measure
*(pp. 744–748)*

### Chapter opening

- **Evaluation is tailored to the individual child.** Presentations, underlying causes and phenotypes vary widely, and **age and timing of presentation** change what testing is needed.
- **Most lower urinary tract (LUT) pathology can now be diagnosed from history, examination and non-invasive tests.** This avoids invasive, complex and more expensive studies in many scenarios.
- **Testing has four purposes:** diagnostic work-up, screening, surveillance, and follow-up after a procedure.

### CLINICAL EVALUATION

#### History

- **The history is taken from the child directly where possible**, pitched to age and to the maturation of voluntary bladder and bowel control.
- **The diagnostic logic is a sequence:** exclude an **anatomical or neurological cause** → if both are excluded, define the **type of bladder (and/or bowel) dysfunction**.
- **Components of a complete history:** symptoms; voiding frequency; bowel function; previous urinary tract infections (UTIs); family history; maternal prenatal history, including screening ultrasonography; birth history; developmental milestones, including toilet training; neuropsychiatric conditions; past medical and surgical history; social history; diet; review of systems.

##### Storage Symptoms

- **Ask about urgency, frequency and incontinence.** Characterise incontinence as **continuous vs intermittent**, **day vs night**, and by how often it happens.
- **The history can suggest increased or decreased bladder capacity.** Confirm it with directed work-up.
- **Secondary enuresis** means new incontinence **after at least 6 months of dryness**. Explore new stressors at home or school, and changes in daily activity.
- **Diet: caffeinated drinks are bladder irritants** (soda, tea, coffee, sports and energy drinks) and directly contribute to urgency.

##### Voiding Symptoms

- **Males:** ask about a deviated or narrowed stream, prolonged voiding, incomplete emptying, straining, dysuria and post-void dribbling.
- **Females: toilet posture matters.** Sitting with the legs held together, slouching, perching, hovering, or dangling feet **activates the pelvic floor**. This **raises outlet resistance** → higher voiding pressures → dysuria and incomplete emptying → a greater risk of bladder dysfunction and UTI.
  - **Correct it with a footstool (Fig. 40.1)**, or by having the child sit **"backwards" facing the cistern**, which forces the legs apart.
- **Holding manoeuvres** (the "potty dance", leg-crossing, grabbing the perineum, squatting) are ways of postponing voiding against urge or **detrusor overactivity (DO)**.
  - **Mechanism:** raised perineal pressure **inhibits detrusor contraction through the sacral reflex arc**, probably through the **gate control theory**: increased large-fibre afferent input suppresses small-fibre transmission through the dorsal horn.
  - **The same mechanism is proposed for neuromodulation:** transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), posterior tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS) and sacral neuromodulation.

*Concept —* **Posture and holding manoeuvres work through the same lever, pelvic floor activation, in opposite directions.** Deliberate activation suppresses the detrusor and postpones voiding. Inadvertent activation on the toilet obstructs voiding. The treatment in both cases is to control the pelvic floor.

##### Bowel Function

- **Bowel and bladder dysfunction coexist because they share sacral innervation (S2–S4).** In some children, **bowel dysfunction is the sole cause of the urinary symptoms.**
- **"Is your child constipated?" is not enough.** Parents misunderstand the term. Ask specifically about **stool frequency, faecal incontinence, abdominal pain, stool size and consistency (Bristol Stool Scale)**.
- **Bristol Stool Scale (Fig. 40.2):** seven stool forms, from **1 (separate hard pellets)** to **7 (watery, no solid pieces)**. In the United Kingdom it is also called the "Meyers scale". It was developed at the University of Bristol in 1997.
  - **It is used as a communication aid** to set a target and to **titrate fibre or laxatives**.
- **Target bowel habit: a soft, smooth, daily stool passed without pain, Bristol 3–4.**
- **Rome IV criteria (2016)** give the consensus definition of functional constipation (Table 40.1). The definition of constipation and bowel dysfunction **remains controversial**. The authors find the specific questions above sufficient in practice.
- **An International Children's Continence Society (ICCS) Standardisation Committee algorithm (2013)** covers functional constipation with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) (Fig. 40.3).

**TABLE 40.1 Rome IV Criteria for Functional Constipation in Children** *(modified from Rasquin et al., 2006; Drossman, 2016)*

| | Functional constipation | Functional non-retentive faecal incontinence (FNRFI) *(second block of the printed table; see flag)* |
|---|---|---|
| **Applies to** | Developmental age **≥4 years**, with **insufficient criteria for irritable bowel syndrome** | Developmental age **≥4 years** |
| **Rule** | **Two or more** of the criteria below | **All** of the criteria below |
| **Criteria** | • **≤2 defecations per week**<br>• **≥1 episode of faecal incontinence per week**<br>• **Retentive posturing** or excessive volitional stool retention<br>• **Painful or hard** bowel movements<br>• **Large faecal mass in the rectum**<br>• **Large-diameter stools that obstruct the toilet** | • **Defecation in socially inappropriate places at least once a month**<br>• **No inflammatory, anatomical, metabolic or neoplastic process**<br>• **No evidence of faecal retention** |

- **What the table is for:** a **symptom-based, two-of-six** definition that makes "constipation" objective in the LUTS clinic.
- **Pattern:** functional constipation is **retentive** (infrequent, hard, large stools; posturing; a rectal mass). Non-retentive faecal incontinence is defined by **soiling without retention**.
- **Trap:** the two entities are **mutually exclusive on faecal retention.** A child with a rectal faecal mass cannot have non-retentive faecal incontinence.

**Fig. 40.3 — ICCS algorithm for functional constipation (FC) with LUTS** *(modified from Burgers et al., J Urol 2013)*

- **Sequence:** history and examination → apply **Rome IV** → **7-day bowel diary (with Bristol score) + urinalysis (± culture) + pelvic ultrasound** → treat any positive finding.
  - **Ultrasound triggers:** **raised post-void residual (PVR)** → treat accordingly; **rectal diameter >30 mm** → treat FC; **bladder wall thickness >6 mm empty and >3 mm full** → treat LUTS. *(The text gives >5 mm empty; see Instalment 3 and the flag.)*
- **Then branch on response:**

| Response | Next step |
|---|---|
| **LUTS and FC both improve** | **Maintenance for at least 3–6 months** |
| **FC improves, LUTS persist** | **Uroflow with electromyography (EMG)** → escalate: urotherapy → anticholinergics → β3 agonists → botulinum toxin → biofeedback → transcutaneous electrical neuromodulation |
| **LUTS improve, FC persists** | **Check adherence ± intensify toilet training** → escalate: polyethylene glycol (PEG) ± enemas → biofeedback → transcutaneous neuromodulation → if no improvement, **refer to a paediatric gastroenterologist** (colonic transit study, spinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), defecography, anorectal and colonic manometry) |

- **What the algorithm teaches: treat the bowel first and treat both in parallel.** Whichever component persists defines the next investigation.

##### Clinical Aids

| | **7-day bowel and bladder diary** (Fig. 40.4A) | **48-hour frequency-volume chart** (Fig. 40.4B) |
|---|---|---|
| **Best for** | **Elimination habits**; objective evidence for **functional constipation** | A primary complaint of **voiding dysfunction** |
| **Records** | Timing of voids and stools, **Bristol type**, urinary and faecal incontinence by day and night | **Volumes of intake and output**, incontinence episodes |
| **Practicalities** | Filled in by patient or parent; **removes recall bias** | A measuring "hat" or graduated cylinder; ideally a **normal weekend**; days **need not be consecutive**, but **each covers 24 hours** |
| **Yields** | The complete picture of bladder and bowel together | **Functional or maximum bladder capacity**, **polyuria**, contribution of **drink type and volume** (caffeine, fluids before bed) |

- **A frequency-volume chart is mandatory in spontaneous voiders who fail first-line conservative therapy**, and it is useful in any child with bowel and bladder dysfunction (BBD).
- **Functional bladder capacity is compared with expected (estimated) bladder capacity (EBC):**

| Age | EBC formula |
|---|---|
| **Infants ≤1 year** | **Weight (kg) × 7 mL** |
| **Older children** | **(Age in years + 2) × 30 mL** |

- **Diaries are cheap and simple, and they expose pathological habits** that parents and children do not notice.

##### Urinary Tract Infections

- **Establish whether previous infections were febrile and/or symptomatic.**
- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria is uncommon**, and much rarer in boys than in girls.
- **Establish how the sample was collected. A bagged specimen is unreliable unless it is negative.**
- **A febrile, culture-proven UTI suggests incomplete emptying or stasis.**
- **Distinguish non-specific LUTS of voiding dysfunction from undocumented "UTIs"**, especially when cultures were negative. The family's expectations may need redirecting and educating.

##### Neurological Function

- **Screen for occult neurological disease:** perineal numbness, change in gait, altered sensation in the limbs, new problems with balance or coordination.
- **These are most worrying when new in a previously well child** who presents with **secondary enuresis** or new LUT problems.

#### Validated Questionnaires

- **Validated questionnaires (patient-reported outcomes) objectively measure symptoms, quality of life and emotional impact.** The best ones are tested for reproducibility and for internal and external validity in the target condition and population.

**COMPARISON — Paediatric LUT Questionnaires**

| Instrument | Domain | Items and scoring | Maximum | Abnormal cut-off | Completed by | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Dysfunctional Voiding Symptom Score (DVSS)** (2000) | **Symptoms**: storage, voiding, **posturing, bowel** | **10** items, each 0–3, covering the past month | **30** | Not given in source | Child or parent | **Adapted from the American Urological Association Symptom Index** for benign prostatic hyperplasia. **Best at detecting change with treatment.** |
| **Dysfunctional Voiding and Incontinence Scoring System (DVISS)** | **Symptoms + 1 quality-of-life item** | **14** items (13 symptom, 1 QoL), **mostly yes/no** | 35 + 3 (see flag) | **9, both sexes** | Child or parent | **Best discrimination for diagnosis** |
| **Paediatric Urinary Incontinence Quality of Life Score (PinQ)** | **Quality of life / emotional impact** | **20** items, each 0–4 (no → all the time); **internalising and externalising** subscores | **80** | Not given in source; **higher = worse** | Child; **a validated parent-proxy version** agrees well | **Not influenced by comorbidity**; valid across languages and cultures; **does not track treatment response** |
| **Incontinence Symptom Index–Paediatric (ISI-P)** (2007) | **Symptoms** | **11** items | **44** | **9, both sexes** | **The child, aged 11–17 years** (not the caregiver) | **Self-report by adolescents** |

- **What the table is for:** matching an instrument to its purpose. **Symptom scores (DVSS, DVISS, ISI-P) vs quality of life (PinQ).**
- **Pattern:** DVISS and ISI-P **share the cut-off of 9**. DVSS is the only one built from an adult prostate score. ISI-P is the only one restricted to adolescent self-report.
- **DVSS and PinQ are complementary:** one measures symptoms, the other their effect on quality of life.

##### Psychological Screening

- **Behavioural and emotional comorbidity is common in children with LUT dysfunction.** The rate rises from nocturnal enuresis → daytime incontinence → **faecal incontinence (highest)**.
- **Screen for it as routinely as organic causes are excluded.**

| | **Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL)** | **Short Screening Instrument for Psychological Problems in Enuresis (SSIPPE)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Role** | **Full assessment** | **Short screen** |
| **Content** | **100+ Likert items**: internalising (anxiety, depression) and externalising (acting out, aggression, hyperactivity) behaviour | **13 items**: **7 internalising from the CBCL** + **6 externalising from the Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) scale** of the Disruptive Behavior Disorders Rating instrument |
| **Completed by** | Parents or guardians | Parents or guardians |
| **Ages** | **18 months–18 years**, in two versions (**18 months–5 years**; **6–18 years**) | Children with enuresis |

- **Screening sequence:** SSIPPE → **≥2 positive items in either the internalising or the externalising section** → full CBCL → disturbance confirmed → **child psychology referral**.

##### Selecting an Appropriate Patient-Reported Outcome Measure

- **A head-to-head comparison of DVSS, DVISS, ISI-P and PinQ** was made in children with BBD against healthy controls, before and after about 3 months of standard therapy (urotherapy, anticholinergics or desmopressin):
  - **DVISS discriminated best for diagnosis.**
  - **DVSS, DVISS and ISI-P all correlated with subjective recovery.**
  - **PinQ did not correlate with therapy or recovery.**
- **Why PinQ may not move** (four possible explanations): it measures domains that therapy does not readily affect; emotional states caused by voiding dysfunction are less malleable; emotional change lags behind symptom relief; or symptom relief does not produce emotional change.

*Concept —* **A questionnaire answers only the question it was built for.** Symptom scores diagnose and track treatment. Quality-of-life and behaviour scores measure the burden on the child and do not measure response. Choose the instrument by the question being asked.

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 1

- **Secondary enuresis:** new incontinence after **≥6 months dry**.
- **Sacral roots shared by bladder and rectum: S2–S4.**
- **Bristol scale 1–7:** 1 = hard pellets; 7 = watery. **Target is Bristol 3–4, daily, painless.** Developed in **1997** (Bristol); UK alias **"Meyers scale"**.
- **Rome IV (2016), functional constipation:** developmental age **≥4 years**, **≥2 of 6** criteria; **≤2 stools per week**; **≥1 faecal incontinence episode per week**.
- **EBC:** infant ≤1 year = **weight (kg) × 7 mL**; older child = **(age + 2) × 30 mL**.
- **Diaries:** **7-day bowel and bladder diary**; **48-hour frequency-volume chart** (each day covers 24 hours; days need not be consecutive).
- **ICCS algorithm ultrasound triggers:** **rectum >30 mm → treat FC**; **wall >6 mm empty / >3 mm full → treat LUTS** (text: >5 mm empty).
- **If both FC and LUTS improve → maintenance for ≥3–6 months.**
- **Questionnaires:** **DVSS** 10 items / max 30; **DVISS** 14 items / cut-off 9; **PinQ** 20 items / max 80; **ISI-P** 11 items / max 44 / cut-off 9 / age 11–17.
- **CBCL** 18 months–18 years, 100+ items; **SSIPPE** 7 + 6 items, **≥2 positive → full CBCL.**
- **First-line habit changes:** footstool, legs apart, sitting "backwards"; stop caffeine.
- **Classic traps:**
  - A **bagged urine** specimen is reliable **only if negative**.
  - **"Constipation?"** as a single question misses bowel dysfunction; ask about frequency, consistency, pain, size and soiling.
  - **PinQ does not track treatment response**; use **DVSS** to follow change.
  - **DVSS derives from the AUA Symptom Index** (an adult prostate score), not from a paediatric instrument.
  - **ISI-P is completed by the adolescent**, not the caregiver.
  - **New neurological symptoms with secondary enuresis** = occult neurological disease until proven otherwise.
  - **Functional constipation vs non-retentive faecal incontinence** separate on faecal retention.

## Instalment 2 — Physical Examination: Vital Signs, BMI vs Tri-Ponderal Mass Index, Blood Pressure, Neurological Examination and Table 40.2, Sacral Reflexes, Spinal Stigmata and Imaging (Fig. 40.5), Abdominal and Genital Examination; Key Points (Clinical Evaluation); Laboratory Testing: Urinalysis, Urine Culture, Additional Testing; Key Points (Laboratory)
*(pp. 748–750)*

#### Physical Examination

- **Examine every child presenting with LUT complaints comprehensively**, with emphasis on **vital signs and on the neurological, abdominal and genitourinary systems**.

##### Vital Signs

- **Record height, weight, temperature, heart rate and blood pressure at every visit.**
- **Being underweight or overweight is associated with higher LUTS scores.**

| | **Body mass index (BMI)** | **Tri-ponderal mass index (TMI)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Formula** | **kg / m²** | **kg / m³** |
| **Origin** | 19th century (Quetelet); **validated in adults** | Proposed for children and adolescents |
| **In children** | **Inaccurate**; needs correction to **BMI z-scores (percentiles)**, which is cumbersome | **Classifies healthy vs overweight better than BMI z-scores, with fewer false "overweight" labels** |
| **Thresholds** | Percentile-based | **Overweight: ≥16.0 (M) / 16.8 (F) kg/m³; obese: ≥18.8 (M) / 19.7 (F) kg/m³** |

- **Raised blood pressure** (judged against an **age-, sex- and height-specific nomogram**) may indicate **renal disease**.
  - **Sequence:** repeat to confirm, ideally at separate visits → if still raised, **renal and bladder ultrasound** + **urinalysis, serum creatinine and/or cystatin C**.
  - **Rationale:** renal disease often follows LUT pathology.

##### Neurological Examination

- **Assess reflexes, strength, coordination and sensation in all four limbs.**
  - **Upper limb function does not tell you about the LUT**, but **fine motor control, sensation and handedness decide whether self-catheterisation is feasible.**
- **Document mobility aids** (wheelchair, walker, crutches, **ankle-foot orthosis (AFO)**), knowing that how they are used varies.
- **The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) National Spina Bifida Patient Registry** classifies **functional neurological level** and **mobility status** separately, because **the two do not correlate** (Table 40.2).

**TABLE 40.2 Objective Classification of Lower Extremity Function in Terms of Functional Neurologic Level and Ambulation Phenotype (Age 2 or Older)**

*Functional neurological level — defined by the lowest preserved movement:*

| Level of lesion | Functional ability present |
|---|---|
| **Thoracic** | **None: flaccid lower limbs** |
| **High lumbar** | **Hip flexion** |
| **Mid lumbar** | **Knee extension** |
| **Low lumbar** | **Foot dorsiflexion** |
| **Sacral** | **Foot plantar flexion** |

*Ambulatory phenotype (mobility status) — defined by where the child walks and how much the wheelchair is needed:*

| Phenotype | Walking | Wheelchair |
|---|---|---|
| **Perfect ambulator** | Walks, runs and jumps indoors and outdoors, **unassisted** | None |
| **Community ambulator** | Indoors and outdoors for most activities; may need crutches, braces or a walker | **Long trips outside the community only** |
| **Household ambulator** | **Indoors only**, with aids; transfers with little help | Some indoor activities; **all community activities** |
| **Non-functional ambulator** | **Only in therapy sessions** | **At all other times** |
| **Non-ambulator** | Never | **Always**; can transfer chair ↔ bed |

- **What the table is for:** a standard vocabulary for spina bifida registries and research, from age 2 upwards.
- **Pattern:** neurological level descends hip → knee → ankle dorsiflexion → plantar flexion. Mobility phenotype steps down by **where** the child can walk.
- **Trap:** **level and mobility do not correlate.** A low lesion does not guarantee ambulation, which is why the registry records them separately.

- **Assess deep tendon reflexes, gait, perineal and perianal sensation, and anal tone.**

| | **Anocutaneous reflex ("anal wink")** | **Bulbocavernosus (Osinski) reflex** |
|---|---|---|
| **Stimulus** | **Stroking the perianal skin** | **Squeezing the glans penis or clitoris** |
| **Response** | Contraction of the **external anal sphincter** | Contraction of the **external anal sphincter** |
| **Absent =** | **Interruption of the sacral reflex arc (S2–S4)**, the same arc that controls bladder and bowel | Same |

- **Inspect and palpate the spine** for **occult spinal dysraphism** related to bony vertebral or sacral anomalies.
  - **Most spines are normal**, with a symmetrical gluteal cleft.
  - **Midline skin stigmata in healthy neonates rarely signify an occult spinal lesion.** A **simple sacral dimple has not been associated with dysraphism.**
- **Cutaneous stigmata carry graded risk (Fig. 40.5):**

| Risk | Stigmata | Imaging |
|---|---|---|
| **Low** | **Simple sacral dimple <2.5 cm from the anus** | **Spinal ultrasound up to 6 months of age** |
| **Intermediate (graded between)** | Abnormal-appearing (deviated) gluteal cleft; dermal sinus tract | Graded by the source between low and high; imaging not specified |
| **High** | **Vascular malformation; lipoma or mass; hair tuft**; **multiple stigmata**; features of **VACTERL** (vertebral, imperforate anus, cardiac, tracheo-oesophageal atresia, renal, limb anomalies); **risk of occult dysraphism or tethered cord** | **MRI of the lumbar and sacral spine** |

| | **Spinal ultrasound** | **Spinal MRI** |
|---|---|---|
| **Use** | **Low-risk lesions** | **High-risk lesions** |
| **Age window** | **≤6 months** (the spine **ossifies by 10–12 months**) | Any age |
| **Advantages** | Cheaper; **avoids general anaesthesia** | Definitive |
| **Limitation** | **Reduced sensitivity** | Often needs general anaesthesia in young children |

- **The dimple-to-anus distance is the key discriminator.** A dimple **within 2.5 cm of the anus** is low risk. Stigmata higher up, or of the high-risk types, need MRI.

##### Abdominal Examination

- **Inspect** for asymmetry or distension (bowel gas, faecal retention, bowel dysfunction).
- **Palpate all four quadrants:** enlarged kidneys or bladder, masses, **palpable stool**. Palpable stool is felt **only in severe constipation**, and more easily in small children.
- **Suprapubic tenderness or fullness** suggests **retention or a full bladder**.

##### Genital Examination

- **Look for damp underwear and irritation of the external genitalia** (signs of incontinence).

**COMPARISON — Genital findings by sex**

| | **Male** | **Female** |
|---|---|---|
| **Look for** | Circumcision status; **balanitis** (erythema, swelling, tenderness of the prepuce); **meatal stenosis** | **Pooling of urine at the introitus**; inflammation; **labial fusion**; **ectopic ureteric orifice**; **meatal anomalies (ventral meatal web)** |
| **Key lesion** | **Meatal stenosis, which occurs in circumcised boys.** Irritation of the exposed meatus → epithelialisation → **ventral web** | **Labial fusion**, common **<2 years of age while in nappies**; **rarely obstructs** |
| **When it matters** | **Only with significant deviation or narrowing of the stream**. Concurrent symptoms **may not be due to it, or improve with surgery**. | Pooling → **stasis and colonisation → UTI** |
| **Management** | Inspecting the meatus with a history may suffice. The authors prefer to **watch the stream directly** for calibre and direction. | Labial fusion is **conservative**: it resolves by **puberty** as oestrogens rise. **Topical oestrogen if concern persists; about 10% recur.** Meatal anomalies: surgery has **minimal supporting evidence**. |
| **Continuous incontinence** | — | **Examine the introitus for an ectopic ureteric orifice** |

- **Female meatal anomalies are associated with voiding dysfunction on urodynamics.** However, **not all such anomalies are symptomatic**, so **enthusiasm for surgical correction has waned.**

*Concept —* **The examination hunts for the three causes history cannot exclude:** a neurological lesion (sacral reflexes, spinal stigmata, limb function), an anatomical outlet problem (meatus, labia, ectopic orifice) and retained stool. **The lower back is where occult neurology hides.**

**KEY POINTS — Clinical Evaluation** *(textbook box)*

- **History and examination establish whether the disorder is primarily one of storage or of emptying.** Further work-up and treatment are tailored to this.
- **Components of a complete history:** symptoms, voiding frequency, bowel function, previous UTIs, family history, maternal prenatal history with screening ultrasonography, birth history, developmental milestones including toilet training, neuropsychiatric conditions, past medical and surgical history, social history, diet, review of systems.
- **7-day bowel and bladder diaries and 48-hour frequency-volume charts are invaluable** in simple and complex voiding dysfunction.
- **Questionnaires:** symptoms (**DVSS, DVISS, ISI-P**); quality of life (**PinQ**); behaviour (**CBCL, SSIPPE**).
- **Lower back:** **inspect** (dimples, deviated gluteal cleft, hair tufts, dermal sinuses, vascular malformations) and **palpate** (sacrum present? any mass?).

### LABORATORY TESTING

#### Urinalysis

- **Dipstick urinalysis is available in most clinics.** It detects **glycosuria, proteinuria, haematuria, leucocyturia and bacteriuria**, and records **specific gravity**.

| Finding | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Glycosuria** | Prolonged hyperglycaemia (**diabetes**) → **polyuria** | Investigate for diabetes |
| **Proteinuria** | May be **isolated or transient** | **Repeat on a separate occasion**, or do a **24-hour urine protein** if intrinsic renal disease is suspected. **Persistent proteinuria → serum creatinine ± 24-hour collection** |
| **White cells** (dipstick or microscopy) | Inflammation or infection | Interpret with symptoms |
| **Microscopic haematuria** | Common with infection; **may occur alone in BBD** | **Isolated asymptomatic microscopic haematuria rarely needs work-up** |
| **Leucocyte esterase, nitrite, white cells, bacteria** | Markers of infection | Suspicious → **culture and/or empirical antibiotics** |

- **Urinalysis ± culture is indicated for irritative symptoms** (dysuria, urgency, frequency) to exclude untreated UTI.
- **A negative urinalysis in a child with LUTS is reassuring.** The test is cheap and simple.

#### Urine Culture

- **Do not culture routinely in children with LUT disorders.** It causes **over-testing, false positives and over-treatment**, especially in **colonised** patients.
- **Children on clean intermittent self-catheterisation (CIC) are at high risk of colonisation.** Culture **only when the pre-test probability of symptomatic UTI is high:**
  - fever
  - abdominal or suprapubic pain
  - malaise
  - **new or worsened incontinence**
  - **new or worsened bladder spasm**

#### Additional Testing

- **Blood tests are rarely indicated.** Use them only when history, examination or other tests suggest **kidney disease or risk factors for it** (e.g. persistent proteinuria → serum creatinine ± 24-hour urine protein).

**KEY POINTS — Laboratory Testing** *(textbook box)*

- **Children with LUTS should have urinalysis.** Note specific gravity, protein, glucose, white cells, red cells and bacteria.
- **Isolated malodorous urine** in an otherwise asymptomatic child **lacks sensitivity and specificity for a positive culture.**
- **Blood tests are not recommended** unless upper tract disease is a specific concern.

*Concept —* **Laboratory testing in LUT dysfunction is driven by pre-test probability.** Urinalysis is universal because it is cheap and a negative result reassures. Culture and blood tests are reserved for symptoms or signs that raise the prior, because in colonised bladders a positive result without symptoms leads only to over-treatment.

## Instalment 3 — Urodynamic Evaluation (ICCS Terminology); Non-invasive Testing: Uroflowmetry and Flow Patterns (Figs 40.6A, 40.7), Qmax Standardisation and Flow Index, Caveats; Pelvic Ultrasound: Bladder Wall Thickness, Bladder Volume Wall Index, Post-Void Residual, Rectal Diameter, Urinary Debris (Fig. 40.8); Patch EMG and Lag Time (Fig. 40.9); Functional Bladder Capacity; Key Points (Non-invasive)
*(pp. 750–753)*

### URODYNAMIC EVALUATION

- **Urodynamics spans non-invasive tests** (uroflowmetry, pelvic ultrasound, patch EMG) **and invasive tests** (formal cystometry). All need **one standardised vocabulary.**
- **ICCS standardised terminology of LUT function in children:** 1998 → 2006 → **2014 update**. It **codifies how results are described** so that studies and clinicians compare like with like. **Essential reading for every clinician who manages paediatric LUT dysfunction.**

#### Noninvasive Testing

##### Uroflowmetry

- **Uroflowmetry measures flow over time** while the child voids into a flowmeter toilet (Fig. 40.6A). It is available in most offices.
  - **Outputs:** voided volume, flow pattern, **maximum flow rate (Qmax)**, average flow rate (Qavg).
  - **The three most important variables are voided volume, Qmax and flow pattern.**
- **Can be combined with perineal patch EMG** of the pelvic floor and external urethral sphincter.
- **The ICCS flow patterns guide interpretation but are not absolute:**

**COMPARISON — ICCS Uroflow Curve Patterns**

| Pattern | Shape | Suggests |
|---|---|---|
| **Bell** | Smooth, symmetrical | **Normal**, at any age, sex, race or voided volume |
| **Tower** | **High-amplitude, peaked, short** | **Overactive bladder**: sudden, forceful detrusor contraction. Not every child with overactive bladder shows it. |
| **Staccato** | Irregular and fluctuating, but **never reaches zero** | **Intermittent sphincter overactivity** / incomplete pelvic floor relaxation; **associated with BBD** |
| **Interrupted** | Irregular, **with segments of zero flow** | **Underactive detrusor with Valsalva voiding** (flow stops between strains); or **detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (DSD)**. **EMG distinguishes the two.** |
| **Plateau** | **Flat, low-amplitude, prolonged** | **Outlet obstruction** (bladder outlet obstruction, **posterior urethral valves, urethral stricture**); or an **underactive bladder** emptying by continuous low detrusor or Valsalva pressure |

- **Examinable pairs:**
  - **Staccato vs interrupted: the difference is whether flow reaches zero.** Staccato = sphincter or pelvic floor activity during a continuous detrusor contraction. Interrupted = inadequate detrusor, so flow stops between abdominal strains.
  - **Tower vs plateau:** an over-strong detrusor against a free outlet vs normal effort against a resistant outlet (or a weak detrusor).

*Standardising the flow rate*

- **Qmax is the most relevant flow variable.** **Record it only if the peak lasts ≥2 seconds**, because spikes are often artefact.
- **Earlier normalisation methods were suboptimal or not generalisable.**
- **The flow index** was derived from a **large healthy, asymptomatic population across ages**:
  - It corrects for **total bladder capacity (voided volume + PVR)**, because **flow falls with both under-distension and over-distension**.
  - It expresses actual flow **as a ratio of expected flow**, giving a **single index**.
  - **It cannot classify staccato or interrupted patterns.** Its authors proposed that the ICCS make **smooth, staccato and interrupted sub-types of three primary patterns.**

*Caveats — a flow is interpretable only if:*

- **the child is toilet-trained**;
- it is read as **emptying-phase information only**; it says **nothing about storage**;
- the **voided volume is ≥50% of expected capacity for age**. The **flow index remains valid down to 50 mL**;
- **more than one curve** is obtained, to improve accuracy and reliability.

*Fig. 40.7 (legend)*

- A normal bell curve. **About 50–100 mL is the minimum voided volume** for a qualitative assessment of the pattern.
- The panels show: **(A)** bell (normal); **(B)** obstructive; **(C)** "overactivity" pattern resembling staccato, with flow starting and stopping; **(D)** peaked flow with rapid emptying, which may cause turbulence and symptoms.

*Concept —* **The shape of a flow curve reflects the balance between the detrusor and the outlet.** Too much detrusor gives a tower. Too much outlet gives a plateau. Outlet activity that comes and goes gives a staccato curve. Detrusor effort that comes and goes gives an interrupted curve. Every pattern is one of these imbalances, and **EMG decides which side is at fault.**

##### Pelvic Ultrasound

- **Ultrasound is a mainstay of work-up**, either formal (renal and bladder ultrasound in radiology) or **pre- and post-void pelvic ultrasound in clinic**. It is quick, painless, non-invasive and **non-ionising**, and it shows both **bladder and rectum**.
- **It triages who needs invasive urodynamics.** In voiding dysfunction, nocturnal enuresis and recurrent UTI, bladder volume and wall measurements predict which children will have urodynamic abnormalities.
- **In spina bifida, a thicker bladder wall correlates with higher pressures and lower compliance on urodynamics**, and therefore with a **threat to the upper tracts**.

**COMPARISON — Pelvic ultrasound parameters**

| Parameter | Abnormal | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Bladder wall thickness** | **>5 mm empty or >3 mm full** (text); **>6 mm empty and >3 mm full** (Fig. 40.3 algorithm) | **Increased detrusor workload**; associated with voiding dysfunction, DO and pelvic floor over-activity. **Thickness varies inversely with filling.** |
| **Bladder volume wall index (BVWI)** (bladder volume : wall thickness ratio, normalised to expected, as a %) | **<70% = thick-walled, small-capacity bladder**; **>130% = thin-walled, high-capacity bladder** | **A normal BVWI strongly predicts normal voiding function.** In **nocturnal enuresis, a normal BVWI predicts a better response to desmopressin.** |
| **Post-void residual (PVR)** | Age-specific; see the table below | Incomplete emptying |
| **Rectal diameter** | **>30 mm** in a child **without the urge to defecate** | **Bowel dysfunction / stercoral bolus** (Fig. 40.8B). **Not fully predictive on its own**; correlate clinically. |
| **Urinary debris** | Present (Fig. 40.8C) | Usually **stasis**. It is **associated with a positive urine culture: specific but insensitive**, so its absence does not exclude infection. |

*Post-void residual*

- **Definition:** urine left in the bladder after voiding or self-catheterisation. The normative values apply to **neurologically intact children**.
- **PVR is highly variable within one individual and is distorted by over-distension.** Use **repeat measurements**.
- **Abnormal PVR can be defined as low as >20 mL on repeat voids** without over-distension.

**PVR cut-offs by age** *(from a large healthy cohort with bell-shaped flows and voided volume >50 mL)*

| Age | A single PVR is abnormal if… |
|---|---|
| **4–6 years** | **>30 mL or >21% of bladder capacity** |
| **7–12 years** | **>20 mL or >15% of bladder capacity** |

*Bladder capacity here = voided volume + PVR, expressed as a percentage of EBC.*

- **Conditions for a valid PVR:**
  - bladder **not under-distended (<50% EBC)** and **not over-distended (>115% EBC)**;
  - measured **immediately after voiding (<5 minutes)**.
- **Pattern:** the percentage threshold **falls** with age. **Younger children are allowed a larger residual.**
- **Trap:** a PVR from an over-filled or under-filled bladder, or one taken late, is uninterpretable, not "abnormal".

*Urinary debris — bottom line of the evidence*

- **Two studies that paired same-day imaging with urine culture both found an increased risk of a positive culture when debris was present.**
- **Specificity is high but sensitivity only moderate.**
- **Limitation:** the cultures were screening cultures in largely asymptomatic children.

##### Patch Electromyography

- **Patch EMG is often combined with uroflowmetry but is not routine in every practice.** Perineal or perianal surface electrodes record pelvic floor activity, which **should fall silent during voiding (Fig. 40.9).**
- **Lag time** = the time from the **start of pelvic floor relaxation** (voiding effort) → **the start of urine flow.**

| Lag time | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **<2 s** | **DO**; urgency and frequency |
| **2–6 s** | **Normal** (full bladder) |
| **>6 s** | **Primary bladder neck dysfunction** |

- **EMG adds a non-invasive way to show whether voiding is synergic or dyssynergic** (coordination of pelvic floor, sphincter and emptying).

*Fig. 40.9 (legend; after Blaivas, 1985)*

- **Normal coordinated voiding follows this sequence:** command to void → **external sphincter EMG falls silent and the sphincter opens** → **bladder neck opens** → detrusor pressure rises → flow. **Lag time** is marked between relaxation and flow.
- At "stop": **the external sphincter contracts** → flow ceases.

##### Other Measures

- **Functional bladder capacity** (also called total bladder capacity) is the **working, day-to-day capacity**. It is taken as the **largest voided volume on the diary** or as **uroflow voided volume + PVR**.
- **Normal range: typically 70–115% of EBC (up to 130% in some studies).**

**KEY POINTS — Noninvasive Testing** *(textbook box)*

- **Non-invasive urodynamics** = uroflowmetry, patch EMG and pelvic ultrasound.
- **Uroflowmetry gives the flow characteristics and pattern. Qmax is the most relevant quantitative variable** for outflow.
- **Ultrasound gives PVR, bladder wall thickness, rectal diameter and debris.**
- **EMG shows bladder–sphincter coordination** (synergic vs dyssynergic). It is combined with uroflow for **lag time**, or with urodynamics.

*Concept —* **Non-invasive testing reconstructs the urodynamic picture piece by piece.** Uroflow gives outlet behaviour, ultrasound gives capacity, residual, wall and rectum, and EMG gives sphincter coordination. **Every value is valid only under normative conditions:** an adequate voided volume, no over-distension, repeat measurements. The skill lies as much in rejecting an invalid study as in reading a valid one.

- **ICCS standardised terminology update: 2014** (previous versions 2006, 1998).
- **Most important uroflow variables:** voided volume, Qmax, pattern. **Qmax counts only if the peak lasts ≥2 s.**
- **Uroflow validity:** toilet-trained; **voided volume ≥50% EBC**; flow index valid to **50 mL**; **≥2 curves**; about **50–100 mL** minimum to judge the pattern.
- **Patterns:** bell = normal; **tower = overactive bladder**; **staccato = sphincter or pelvic floor over-activity, BBD (never reaches zero)**; **interrupted = underactive detrusor with Valsalva, or DSD (reaches zero)**; **plateau = obstruction** (valves, stricture) or underactive detrusor.
- **Bladder wall thickness: >3 mm full; >5 mm empty** (text) / **>6 mm empty** (ICCS algorithm).

- **PVR:** abnormal **>20 mL on repeat**. **4–6 years:** single **>30 mL or >21%**. **7–12 years:** single **>20 mL or >15%**. Valid only if the bladder is at **50–115% EBC** and the measurement is taken **<5 min** after voiding.
- **Rectal diameter >30 mm** = bowel dysfunction.
- **Debris:** specific, insensitive marker of a positive culture.
- **EMG lag time:** **normal 2–6 s; <2 s = DO; >6 s = primary bladder neck dysfunction.**
- **Functional bladder capacity: normal 70–115% (to 130%) of EBC.**
- **Classic traps:**
  - **Staccato vs interrupted:** only interrupted reaches zero flow.
  - An **interrupted curve is not automatically DSD.** It may be Valsalva voiding with an underactive detrusor. **EMG decides.**
  - A **plateau curve is not always obstruction.** An underactive detrusor also produces one.
  - **Uroflow says nothing about storage.**
  - **A single flow curve, or a small voided volume (<50% EBC)** → not interpretable.
  - **PVR measured late or after over-distension** is invalid.
  - **A short lag time means DO**; **a long one means bladder neck dysfunction**, not DSD.

## Instalment 4 — Formal Urodynamics: Indications and Neurogenic Conditions; Unique Paediatric Considerations (Setting, Expectations, Pain and Anxiety, Pre-study Infection Screening, Sedation, Catheters and EMG Technique, Natural-Fill Cystometry, Fill Rate and EBC, Infusate Temperature, Stopping Rules, Repeat Filling Cycles); Video Urodynamics (Fluoroscopic vs Contrast-Enhanced Ultrasound); Interpretation of Studies (Reliability, UMPIRE, Reporting Consensus, Pves/Pabd/Pdet, DO vs Underactivity, Compliance, DLPP vs ALPP, Capacity, Pressure-Flow, Upper-Tract Danger Signs); Frequency of Testing; Key Points
*(pp. 753–757)*

#### Formal Urodynamics

- **Paediatric urodynamics resembles adult urodynamics.** It measures filling dynamics, **compliance, capacity, pressures, DSD, DO and PVR**. **With fluoroscopy** it also shows **vesicoureteric reflux (VUR)** and congenital bladder, bladder neck and urethral anomalies.
- **There are no formal recommendations for when to perform urodynamics in children**, and practice varies widely.
- **Established indications:**
  - LUT dysfunction from a **congenital or acquired neurological deficit**;
  - **severe obstructive uropathy**;
  - conditions that could cause **neurogenic bladder, especially with new LUTS**.

| Neurogenic causes | Severe obstructive causes |
|---|---|
| **Spinal dysraphism, sacrococcygeal teratoma, sacral agenesis, transverse myelitis, spinal cord injury** | **Posterior urethral valves, urethral atresia, large ectopic ureteroceles** |

- **Non-neuropathic LUTS: urodynamics rarely yields enough information to justify invasive testing.**
- **Purposes:** baseline or surveillance in known disease; **re-baselining before medical or surgical therapy**; **re-assessment after a new therapy**; ruling specific dysfunctions in or out.
- **Congenital conditions change bladder function over time**, so studies need repeating.
- **Governing rule: perform formal urodynamics only when a new or abnormal result will change management.**

##### Unique Considerations in Pediatric Patients

*Setting and expectations*

- **Experienced, knowledgeable staff and a "kind, understanding and relaxed atmosphere" are essential.** Many children arrive anxious after previous invasive studies.
  - **Parents stay in the room throughout.**
  - **Distract with television, video games or tablets.**
- **Standardise the technique.** A published short protocol with video exists. The machine is shown in Fig. 40.6B.
- **Set expectations beforehand.** Families often wrongly believe urodynamics will **definitively confirm or exclude neurogenic bladder** or reveal the cause.
  - Urodynamics describes **function**, **not the underlying disease**.
  - **An office study may not reflect day-to-day physiology.**
  - **The treating urologist should counsel the family before testing.**

*Pain and anxiety*

- **Pain is usually mild, but a substantial minority report moderate pain.**
- **Predictors of high pain:** **high pre-test anxiety**, **sensory-motor alteration of the lower limbs**, **difficult catheterisation**.
- **Anxiety peaks at catheter placement.**
- **Very difficult catheterisation → place the catheters under general anaesthesia in theatre, then perform the study after the child has woken.**

*Infection screening before urodynamics*

- **Routine pre-study urine culture has low yield.**
  - Bacteriuria is common in at-risk children (e.g. **CIC**), but **post-urodynamic UTI is rare (1.4%)**.
  - **A prospective protocol that dropped routine cultures in favour of targeted ones did not increase the post-urodynamic UTI rate.**
  - **Symptom-based selection for pre-study urinalysis identified very few children who needed testing.**
- **Risk factors for post-procedure infection:**
  - **intermittent or overnight catheterisation**
  - **recent clinical UTI**
  - **immunosuppression**
  - **symptoms on the day of the study**
- **Bottom line:**
  - **Universal screening or prophylaxis → over-treatment.**
  - **Selective screening for new LUTS on or before the day** is the most rational approach.
  - The data on targeted prophylaxis for high-risk children are **inconclusive**.

*Sedation*

- **Sedation may alter bladder function** through neurological pathways or a direct drug effect on the detrusor. In animal models, general anaesthesia reduces detrusor contractions.
  - **General anaesthesia is not recommended; avoid sedation.**
  - **If sedation is needed, confine it to catheter insertion**, done in advance.
  - **During cystometry, ketamine is preferred over midazolam:** faster onset and recovery, better effect, and **possibly safer**.
  - **The only same-patient comparison (with vs without sedation) showed no change** in urodynamic parameters. It was small and has not been repeated.

*Technique — the sequence*

1. **Empty the bladder** before the study, or drain it through the catheter before instillation.
2. **Lidocaine gel** → insert multichannel or microtransducer catheters into the **bladder and rectum**. **Use the smallest catheter, typically a 6 Fr dual-lumen.**
3. **Place EMG patches symmetrically at 3 and 9 o'clock perianally.** Prepare the skin with **alcohol and fine abrasive paper** and **secure the patches** for conductivity.
4. **The system must sample at >1000 Hz** to separate movement artefact from true sphincter activity.

- **Position:** sitting vs supine does **not** affect measurements.
- **Surface patches, not needle electrodes.** Needle electrodes are no longer recommended in children: they are painful and anxiety-provoking, and surface electrodes are equally accurate.

**Catheter routes in altered anatomy**

| Situation | Vesical (Pves) catheter | Abdominal (Pabd) catheter |
|---|---|---|
| **Vesicostomy** | **Foley with the balloon inflated, on gentle traction** to occlude the stoma | Rectal |
| **Catheterisable channel, bladder neck not closed** | **Through the channel or the native urethra** | Rectal |
| **Colostomy** | Urethral | **Through the colostomy** |
| **Females (alternative)** | Urethral | **Vaginal**; gives comparable Pabd |

*Conventional vs ambulatory (natural-fill) cystometry*

| | **Conventional cystometry** | **Ambulatory / natural-fill cystometry** |
|---|---|---|
| **Filling** | **Artificial infusion** | **Physiological diuresis** |
| **Setting** | Laboratory | **Natural environment**; less **Hawthorne effect** and stress |
| **Findings (relative)** | **Larger capacity; larger filling Pdet rise; lower voiding pressure** | **Smaller capacity; smaller filling Pdet rise; higher voiding pressure** |
| **Drawbacks** | Artefact from infusion rate | **Time and effort; no standardised paediatric protocols** |
| **Paediatric method** | Urethral catheters | Suprapubic 6 Fr dual-lumen + rectal 5 Fr balloon catheter, **placed about 24 hours before** |

- **Artificial filling therefore overstates filling pressure rise and understates voiding pressure.**

*Filling*

- **EBC spans newborn (30–50 mL) to young adult (400–500 mL).** **Fill rate must be scaled**, because **too fast a fill produces artefact.**
- **Ideal fill rate: 5–10% of EBC per minute.**
- **Infusate (saline or contrast) should be between room and body temperature, never cold.**
  - **In a randomised trial, room vs body temperature** made **no difference to leak point pressures, voiding pressures or PVR**.
  - Room temperature gave a slightly higher capacity and higher pressure at capacity. This was statistically but **not clinically** significant.
- **Stop filling when any of the following occurs:**
  - **strong urge** to void
  - **discomfort**
  - **micturition**
  - **leakage greater than the fill rate**
  - **Pdet consistently >40 cm H₂O**
  - **instilled volume >150% of EBC**

*Repeat filling cycles*

| | **First fill** | **Subsequent fills** |
|---|---|---|
| **Maximum detrusor pressure, DLPP** | **Highest** | Fall |
| **Number of DO contractions** | Most | Fewer |
| **Capacity, compliance, PVR** | Comparable across fills | Comparable across fills |
| **DO** | **Reproducible across fills in most patients** | Reproducible across fills in most patients |

- **ICCS guidance changed:** earlier editions recommended **≥2 filling cycles**; the **2015 guidance leaves this to the provider's discretion**.
- **Which fill to report is not specified.** Some **interpret the first, usually worst, fill** to be **conservative and protect the upper tracts**.
- **The differences between fills are attributed to the viscoelastic properties of the bladder.**
- **Weigh the marginal gain against a longer test and more anxiety.**

*Video urodynamics*

- **Video urodynamics = urodynamics + imaging**, with **contrast instead of saline**.
- **It adds specificity:** bladder shape and **trabeculation**, **VUR**, **diverticula**, and **bladder neck configuration (open vs closed)** during filling. It also links the **timing of reflux or leakage to bladder volume**.

| | **Fluoroscopic video urodynamics** | **Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) video urodynamics** |
|---|---|---|
| **Contrast** | Radio-opaque | **Ultrasound contrast agent** (compatible with urodynamic equipment) |
| **Radiation** | **Ionising** | **None** |
| **Status** | Established | **Recent innovation** |
| **Parental preference** | — | **Strongly preferred; judged more comfortable** |
| **Accuracy comparison** | **No formal head-to-head comparison of sensitivity or specificity** | Same |

*Concept —* **Every paediatric modification serves one aim: to make an artificial test reproduce the child's natural bladder.** Fill slowly in proportion to EBC. Use warm infusate. Avoid sedation. Keep the child calm. Stop at physiological limits. Any artefact (fast fill, cold fluid, anaesthesia, anxiety) distorts the very pressures the study exists to measure.

##### Interpretation of Studies

- **Interpretation belongs to a trained paediatric urologist, advanced practice provider or urodynamicist.**
- **Reliability:**
  - **Objective parameters** show **good to excellent intra- and inter-rater reliability** (adult multicentre data).
  - **Subjective interpretation of paediatric studies agrees poorly**, even between colleagues who trained together, despite grading schemes.
- **No standardised reporting criteria exist**, which adds to the variability.
- **Urologic Management to Preserve Initial Renal Function (UMPIRE) study** (multicentre, myelomeningocele): **central re-review agreed with local risk categorisation in only about 50%** of cases.
  - **Unresolved questions:** when to call leak vs void; when to stop filling; how far to fill above EBC; how to interpret studies with **high-grade VUR**.
- **A modified Delphi consensus of North American experts (2024)** defined a **standard paediatric urodynamic report in eight sections:**
  1. indications
  2. study conditions
  3. procedural details (which components were done)
  4. filling phase
  5. radiological findings (if video)
  6. incontinence and leak point pressures
  7. voiding phase
  8. summary
  - It uses **discrete, defined options**, some **automatically calculated** and **conditional** fields, and **billing (CPT) codes**. It is planned for **electronic health records with decision support and inline definitions**.

*Storage (filling) phase*

- **Pves** = vesical (bladder catheter) pressure. **Pabd** = abdominal (rectal catheter) pressure.
  - **Both are zeroed to atmospheric pressure at the level of the pubic symphysis** at the start.
- **Pdet = Pves − Pabd**, the inferred detrusor pressure.
- **Normal filling:** Pdet stays **flat**, with **no change on cough, laugh or Valsalva**.

| | **Detrusor overactivity (DO)** | **Detrusor underactivity** |
|---|---|---|
| **Filling** | **Phasic rises and falls in Pdet** | **Large capacity, low filling pressure, no contractions** |
| **Voiding** | — | **No sustained contraction; cannot empty** |
| **Sub-types / pattern** | **Neurogenic** (neuropathology present) vs **idiopathic** | **Valsalva voiding: large Pabd rises** produce emptying |
| **Pitfalls** | **Exclude provoked rises** (cough, Valsalva). Contractions are **often not sensed**. | **Not visible on Pdet itself**; inferred from the combination of findings |
| **EMG** | With an intact sphincter, **DO provokes a guarding rise in pelvic floor and sphincter EMG** | — |

*Compliance*

- **Compliance = ΔV (mL) / ΔPdet (cm H₂O).** It is the bladder's ability to accommodate volume with little pressure rise. **Calculate it in every patient.**
- **It cannot be inferred from Pdet alone.**
- **Small, contracted bladders pose a trap:** absolute Pdet may stay **below the danger zone (<30–40 cm H₂O)** while calculated compliance looks **abnormal (<20 mL/cm H₂O)**. **Here the absolute pressure is the more useful guide to upper-tract safety.**
- **The shape of the Pdet curve matters.** An **early rise = early loss of compliance** → the child spends **most of the day at high pressure** → a worse prognosis.
- **Check that the catheters were zeroed correctly.** A zeroing error can **hide poor compliance** or give **falsely low Pdet**.
- **Marked DO can make compliance unquantifiable.** The **ICCS recommends calculating compliance over the most linear part of the volume–pressure curve**, ideally the whole filling phase.

**COMPLIANCE GRADING**

| Compliance (mL/cm H₂O) | Grade |
|---|---|
| **>20** | **Normal** |
| **10–20** | **Moderately impaired** |
| **<10** | **Severely impaired** |

*Leak point pressures — the governing pair*

| | **Detrusor leak point pressure (DLPP)** | **Abdominal leak point pressure (ALPP)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Pressure at which leakage occurs **without a rise in abdominal pressure and without a detrusor contraction** | Pressure at which leakage occurs **with a rise in abdominal pressure** (cough, laugh, Valsalva), **without a detrusor contraction** |
| **Measured from** | Pdet | **Traditionally Pves** |
| **Reflects** | **The bladder's "pop-off" point**: how high storage pressure climbs before the urethra yields | **Outlet resistance** (bladder neck and sphincter) to abdominal pressure |
| **Clinical emphasis** | **Upper-tract protection; the main paediatric measure** | **Outlet function only** |
| **Thresholds** | **<40 cm H₂O = safe**; **>40 cm H₂O, or no leak despite high pressure = upper-tract risk** | **No paediatric norms.** Adult female values: **<60 = intrinsic sphincter deficiency (ISD)**; **60–90 = equivocal**; **>90 = no deficiency** (cm H₂O) |
| **Abnormal means** | **A high DLPP** = dangerous storage | **A low ALPP** = ISD |

- **McGuire's 1981 myelodysplasia study (landmark):** a **leak pressure (Pves) >40 cm H₂O** was followed by **hydronephrosis or secondary VUR in 85%** of children (source figure).
  - **The exact safe cut-off is undefined. 40 cm H₂O is the accepted upper limit**, and the aim is **the lowest achievable pressure.**
- **Trap: DLPP and ALPP point in opposite directions.** A **high DLPP** is bad (the upper tracts are exposed). A **low ALPP** is bad (the sphincter is incompetent). A child can have both a "good" DLPP and a poor ALPP.

*Bladder capacity*

| Term | Definition | Source of the value |
|---|---|---|
| **Maximum (cystometric) bladder capacity** | **Volume instilled** during the study. **Confounded by heavy leakage.** | Urodynamics |
| **Expected (estimated) bladder capacity (EBC)** | Age- or weight-based norm | Formula (Instalment 1) |
| **Functional bladder capacity** | Volume the bladder **normally holds in daily life** before emptying | **Voiding diary** (best) |

- **Abnormal capacity** (maximum or functional) as given in the source: **>130% of EBC (high)** or **<66% of EBC (low)** (see flag).
- **On its own, abnormal capacity is not alarming.** It usually travels with another finding:
  - **large capacity + infrequent voiding**
  - **small capacity + raised Pdet and/or poor compliance**

*Voiding phase (pressure-flow study)*

- **A pressure-flow study = uroflowmetry + pressures.**
- **Valid only if:** the child can void; **voided volume is at least 50–100 mL**; and it is **≥50% of functional bladder capacity**.
- **Record:** Qmax or Qavg (mL/s), flow pattern, **Pdet at Qmax**, and **PVR**.
- **High Pdet + low Qmax = outlet obstruction**, which is either anatomical or functional:

| | **Anatomical obstruction** | **Functional obstruction: detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (DSD)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Examples** | **Urethral valves, urethral stricture** | Detrusor contraction **without** relaxation of pelvic floor and sphincter |
| **Pelvic floor / sphincter** | **Relaxes appropriately (coordinated)** | **Fails to relax** |
| **Flow** | Plateau | **Intermittent / staccato** |
| **Pdet** | High | **Higher than expected** |
| **EMG** | **Quiet** during voiding | **Increased activity** during voiding |

- **Distinguishing the two needs flow curve + Pdet + EMG read together.**

**Normal Pdet at Qmax**

| Group | Pdet at Qmax |
|---|---|
| **Boys (children, similar to adults)** | **66 cm H₂O on average** |
| **Girls** | **57 cm H₂O on average** |
| **Healthy newborn and infant boys** (catheter in situ) | **Up to 118 cm H₂O** |
| **Newborn and infant, second group** (see flag) | **Up to 75 cm H₂O** |

- **Normal infant voiding pressures are much higher.** An infant Pdet that would be alarming in an older child can be physiological.

**COMPARISON — Urodynamic findings that raise concern for upper-tract injury**

1. **Impaired compliance**: <10 mL/cm H₂O severe; 10–20 moderate; >20 normal
2. **DSD with poor emptying, inability to void and/or high voiding pressures**
3. **Sustained, raised Pdet during filling**
4. **Raised DLPP (>40 cm H₂O)**
5. **High voiding pressures with poor flow**

*Concept —* **Paediatric urodynamic interpretation is organised around one question: how much pressure do the kidneys see, and for how long?** Compliance, DLPP, the timing of the pressure rise, DSD and voiding pressure are all different ways of measuring that exposure. **40 cm H₂O is the line**, because above it pressure is transmitted up the ureters.

##### Frequency of Testing

- **UMPIRE protocol** (CDC National Spina Bifida Patient Registry, 2016): a **research protocol, not a guideline**, running across multiple US centres.
  - **Schedule:** a **baseline study at about 3 months** → repeat at **ages 1, 2 and 3** → then **yearly to age 5** if there is **VUR or a "hostile" bladder**.
- **Clinical practice follows two schools:**

| | **Routine surveillance** | **Event-driven (limited) testing** |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategy** | **Annual urodynamics ± imaging (video)** | Test only on **clinical change**: new UTI, new or worse incontinence, new or worse hydronephrosis |
| **Case for** | **Hydronephrosis is an insensitive marker of bladder dynamics.** In a large spina bifida series, hydronephrosis improved with intensified drainage (more frequent CIC, overnight drainage) while **poor compliance did not improve**, and a minority went on to surgery. | **Cost**; **many studies are needed to detect a change** that requires intervention |
| **Emerging** | — | **Home bladder-pressure monitoring** by children on CIC (investigational) |

- **Consensus minimum: regular follow-up of every neurogenic bladder** to ensure **safe upper tracts, adequate emptying and a low UTI risk**. **Any change → further investigation.**

**KEY POINTS — Formal Urodynamics** *(textbook box)*

- **Urodynamics is the gold standard** for evaluating and diagnosing LUT dysfunction.
- **Universal antibiotic prophylaxis or urine culture before urodynamics does not prevent post-procedure UTI**, which occurs in **1.4%**.
- **Paediatric special considerations:** **sedation** (for catheter placement and/or the procedure) and **fill rate** scaled to the wide range of EBC.
- **Core concepts to master:** capacity, Pves, Pabd, Pdet, compliance, **timing of loss of compliance**, DLPP, ALPP, Pdet at Qmax, DSD, PVR.
- **DLPP and ALPP are different concepts. DLPP dominates in paediatrics** because it relates to the upper tracts.

*Concept —* **Surveillance is a trade-off between the sensitivity of the test and its cost.** Hydronephrosis is cheap but late, and urodynamics is sensitive but costly. That is why protocols front-load urodynamics in infancy, when bladder behaviour is least predictable, and then space studies out unless the bladder is hostile.

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 4

- **Gold standard for LUT dysfunction: urodynamics.**
- **Do urodynamics only if the result will change management.** It is low-yield in non-neurogenic LUTS.
- **Post-urodynamic UTI: 1.4%.** Universal culture or prophylaxis is not effective; screen selectively (new symptoms, CIC, recent UTI, immunosuppression).
- **Catheter: 6 Fr dual-lumen**; **EMG patches at 3 and 9 o'clock**; **sampling >1000 Hz**; surface, not needle, electrodes.
- **Sedation:** avoid; no general anaesthesia; **ketamine > midazolam** if needed; sedate for catheter insertion only.
- **EBC range: newborn 30–50 mL → young adult 400–500 mL.**
- **Fill rate: 5–10% of EBC per minute.** Infusate **room to body temperature, never cold**.
- **Stop filling:** strong urge; discomfort; voiding; leak > fill rate; **Pdet persistently >40**; **volume >150% EBC**.
- **Repeat fills:** ICCS **2015 = at the provider's discretion** (previously ≥2). **The first fill is worst.** Report conservatively.
- **Pdet = Pves − Pabd**; zero to atmosphere at the **pubic symphysis**.
- **Compliance = ΔV/ΔPdet:** **>20 normal; 10–20 moderate; <10 severe** (mL/cm H₂O).
- **DLPP <40 cm H₂O = safe; >40 = upper-tract risk** (McGuire 1981: hydronephrosis or VUR in 85%).
- **ALPP (adult female): <60 = ISD; 60–90 = equivocal; >90 = normal.**
- **Abnormal capacity: >130% or <66% EBC** (source) — see flag (ICCS: <65% / >150%).
- **Pressure-flow valid if** voided volume **50–100 mL** minimum and **≥50% of functional bladder capacity**.
- **Pdet at Qmax: boys 66, girls 57 cm H₂O; infant boys up to 118.**
- **UMPIRE schedule:** baseline at **3 months** → **1, 2, 3 years** → **annually to 5** if VUR or a hostile bladder.
- **UMPIRE:** central vs local risk agreement **about 50%**.
- **Delphi reporting consensus (2024): 8 report sections.**
- **Five upper-tract danger signs:**
  1. poor compliance
  2. DSD with poor emptying or high pressures
  3. sustained high filling Pdet
  4. DLPP >40
  5. high voiding pressure with poor flow
- **Classic traps:**
  - **DLPP vs ALPP:** a high DLPP is dangerous; a low ALPP means ISD.
  - **A small contracted bladder can have "abnormal" compliance with safe absolute pressures.** Rely on the absolute Pdet.
  - **Detrusor underactivity is not seen on the Pdet trace.** Infer it from large capacity, no contraction and Valsalva voiding.
  - **Uninhibited Pdet rises on cough are not DO.** Exclude provocation.
  - **A fast fill or cold infusate** → artefactual pressures.
  - **Natural-fill cystometry** shows **smaller capacity and higher voiding pressures** than conventional cystometry.
  - **Hydronephrosis resolving ≠ compliance improving.**
  - **High Pdet + low flow** is obstruction. **EMG** separates **DSD (active EMG)** from **anatomical obstruction (quiet EMG)**.
  - **High infant voiding pressures are physiological.**
  - Urodynamics **describes function, not the diagnosis**. Counsel families accordingly.

### EVALUATION STRATEGIES FOR SPECIFIC CLINICAL ENTITIES

- **LUT dysfunction covers a wide range of diagnoses.** The common scenarios below set the context for choosing a work-up.

#### Non-neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction

- **This group includes:**
  - **BBD**: overactive bladder, underactive bladder, voiding postponement
  - **non-monosymptomatic enuresis** (daytime symptoms + nocturnal enuresis)
  - **primary monosymptomatic nocturnal enuresis**
- **Presentation:** urgency, frequency, day and/or night incontinence. **UTIs are more common and can confound the diagnosis.** Symptoms may be predominantly **storage or emptying**.
- **Most are diagnosed without invasive testing.** The pathway is a sequence:
  - history and examination → **selective non-invasive tests** → **behavioural and dietary change** → **medication** (anticholinergics, α-blockers, β3-adrenergic agonists).
- **Routine formal urodynamics is low-yield** and rarely changes therapy.
  - **European Bladder Dysfunction Study (2008, prospective):** in children with urgency and voiding dysfunction, **urodynamic findings did not correlate with treatment outcome.**
- **Exception: children who fail conservative, second-line and third-line therapy** are more likely to have **actionable urodynamic findings**. Study them.

#### Spinal Dysraphism

- **Myelomeningocele accounts for 90% of open spinal dysraphism.** These children usually arrive already diagnosed and need **lifelong study and aggressive treatment**, because **LUT dynamics change with time**.
- **The harder group is occult dysraphism** (e.g. tethered cord) and other occult causes of neurogenic bladder. External signs may be subtle, and **the urinary tract may be the only system to change.**
- **Four goals, whatever the cause:**
  1. **normal bladder volume**
  2. **low storage pressure (normal compliance)**
  3. **complete emptying**
  4. **protection of the upper tracts**
- **Urodynamics is central to these goals. Repeat it before any surgery** to confirm the right procedure has been planned.

**UMPIRE revised risk stratification (spinal dysraphism)**

| Risk | DLPP or end-fill pressure | Other features |
|---|---|---|
| **Low** | **<25 cm H₂O** | — |
| **Intermediate** | **25–39 cm H₂O** | **or DO** |
| **High** | **≥40 cm H₂O** | — |

- **DSD was removed from the scheme** because the study team **could not agree which patients truly had DSD.**
- **Trap:** DSD is absent from UMPIRE risk strata. This reflects **poor inter-observer agreement**, not the view that DSD is harmless.

#### Sacral Agenesis

- **Partial or complete absence of the sacrum**, with **abnormal nerve formation at those levels.**
- **Radiographic level and clinical severity do not correspond strictly.**
- **Nearly one-third are diagnosed late in childhood**, presenting with incontinence, failure to toilet-train or UTI.
- **Examination:** **flattened buttocks**, **a short gluteal cleft (upper portion missing)**, **no palpable sacrum or coccyx**.
- **Diagnosis: plain radiograph** (Fig. 40.10). It is sometimes an incidental finding.
- **Urodynamics if there is voiding dysfunction.** Two phenotypes:

| **Lower motor neuron pattern** | **Upper motor neuron pattern** |
|---|---|
| **Areflexic bladder with little external sphincter function** | **DO with DSD** |

*Fig. 40.10 (legend):* (A) a normal paediatric female pelvis on radiograph vs (B) sacral agenesis.

#### Anorectal Malformations

- **Anorectal malformations (including cloacal anomaly) are commonly associated with spinal cord anomalies, especially tethering.**
- **Tethered cord** = the **conus medullaris is fixed within the canal** instead of floating free. **As the child grows, the cord comes under traction** → gait change, altered sensation in the legs and perineum, **LUT dysfunction**.
  - *Correct anatomy:* the conus normally ascends to **L1–L2**. Tethering holds it **abnormally low**, below L2 (see flag).
- **Urinary symptoms may be absent at birth. Most recommend a baseline urodynamic study even without a cord abnormality, especially after repair.** The authors aim for **about 1 year of age after repair**.
- **Monitor and counsel. Any change in urological function → repeat the study.**

#### Posterior Urethral Valves

- **Posterior urethral valves (PUV) are the commonest cause of LUT obstruction in boys.**
- **About 15% progress to "valve bladder syndrome"**, in which bladder dysfunction drives **progressive hydronephrosis and chronic kidney disease**.
- **The natural history is a sequence:** **DO → loss of compliance → myogenic failure.**
- **Polyuria from nephrogenic diabetes insipidus** (acquired concentrating defect) **makes bladder emptying harder to optimise.**
- **Bladder dynamics change with time → long-term urological follow-up. Clinical change → further study and escalation.**

#### Pelvic Tumors Requiring Extirpation

- **Resection of pelvic tumours, typically sacrococcygeal teratoma** (a rare neonatal germ cell tumour), causes **LUT dysfunction in up to 50%** post-operatively.
- **Mechanism: injury to the sacral plexus supplying the bladder.**
- **Symptoms may not show in the newborn period.** Keep a **high index of suspicion and monitor**, to avoid under-diagnosis.
- **Urodynamic findings range widely:** **DO, DSD, areflexia.**

#### Cerebral Palsy

- **Voiding dysfunction affects up to one-third** of children with cerebral palsy.
- **The lesion is typically an upper motor neuron lesion.** Many children have **irritative (storage) symptoms**. **Storage symptoms outnumber voiding symptoms.**
- **Work-up is stepwise:** clinical evaluation → conservative therapy → **urodynamics only for children who respond poorly to escalating treatment** and who want a better outcome.
- **Urodynamic findings:**
  - **increased capacity**
  - incontinence
  - **detrusor underactivity or overactivity**
  - **DSD, but only rarely**
- **Higher risk of upper or lower tract deterioration**, needing closer monitoring:
  - **spastic quadriplegic cerebral palsy**
  - **moderate to severe functional impairment**

#### Spinal Cord Injury

- **Two phases:**
  - **Acute: spinal shock → urinary retention → start a bladder drainage programme.**
  - **Chronic or stabilising:** a **wide variety of voiding phenotypes**.
- **Level determines DSD:** **cervical and thoracic (higher) lesions → more DSD** than lumbar lesions.
- **UK spinal cord injury guidance:**
  - **Baseline video urodynamics at 3–6 months after injury.**
  - **Surveillance urodynamics** for new deterioration, changed management goals, or follow-up of earlier **"unsafe" findings** (e.g. poor compliance).

#### Transverse Myelitis and Other Central Nervous System Disorders

- **Transverse myelitis is an inflammatory myelopathy.** Like spinal cord injury, it has an **acute spinal-shock phase, then a chronic phase.**
- **Motor recovery and bladder recovery are dissociated.**
  - In a long-term series, **about half recovered motor function**, but **most still had voiding dysfunction and most had bowel dysfunction.**
  - **Neither motor recovery nor lower-limb spasticity predicted bladder function.**
- **Commonest initial urodynamic findings, in descending order:**
  1. **DO** (most common)
  2. loss of compliance
  3. **DSD**
  4. **DLPP >40 cm H₂O** (least common)
- **In children with early retention, preserved reflexes predict return of normal bladder function.**
- **Recommendation: early ultrasound and urodynamics + start CIC** to protect the upper tracts.

**COMPARISON — Specific clinical entities**

| Entity | Lesion / mechanism | Typical urodynamic findings | When to perform urodynamics |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Non-neurogenic LUT dysfunction** | Functional (BBD, enuresis) | Rarely changes management | **Only if refractory to second- and third-line therapy** |
| **Spinal dysraphism** (MMC = 90% of open) | Neural tube defect; occult tethering | Variable; risk by DLPP or end-fill pressure (UMPIRE) | **Baseline and serial; before any surgery** |
| **Sacral agenesis** | Absent sacral segments | **Areflexia + weak sphincter**, or **DO + DSD** | **If voiding dysfunction** |
| **Anorectal malformation** | **Associated tethered cord** | Variable | **Baseline, even with a normal cord, about 1 year after repair**; repeat on change |
| **Posterior urethral valves** | Obstruction → bladder remodelling | **DO → poor compliance → myogenic failure**; polyuria | Serial; on clinical change |
| **Sacrococcygeal teratoma resection** | **Sacral plexus injury** | **DO, DSD, areflexia** | Surveillance; **high index of suspicion** |
| **Cerebral palsy** | **Upper motor neuron** | **Large capacity, DO or underactivity; DSD rare** | **Only if refractory** to stepwise therapy |
| **Spinal cord injury** | Acute shock → chronic | **Higher lesion → more DSD** | **Baseline video urodynamics 3–6 months after injury**; surveillance |
| **Transverse myelitis** | Inflammatory; shock → chronic | **DO > poor compliance > DSD > DLPP >40** | **Early**, with CIC |

- **What the comparison teaches:** urodynamics is **mandatory and serial** where the lesion is **structural and progressive**: dysraphism, tethering, valves, cord injury and myelitis. It is **reserved for failed treatment** where the dysfunction is **functional or static**: non-neurogenic LUTD and cerebral palsy.
- **Trap:** **recovery of motor function or a normal-looking newborn does not predict the bladder.** Examples are transverse myelitis, anorectal malformation and sacrococcygeal teratoma.

*Concept —* **The decision to perform urodynamics depends on whether the bladder is exposed to a lesion that changes over time.** Progressive or occult neurology and obstruction mean the bladder can deteriorate silently, so a baseline and serial studies are needed. Functional and static conditions are managed clinically, and urodynamics is reserved for treatment failure.
