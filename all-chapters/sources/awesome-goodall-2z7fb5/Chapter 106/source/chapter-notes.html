Chapter 106 — Evaluation and Management of Males With Urinary Incontinence

Urinary Incontinence

Definition and Determinants

  • Urinary incontinence (UI) — International Continence Society (ICS): the complaint of any involuntary loss of urine. It is a storage lower urinary tract symptom (LUTS); LUTS are storage, voiding or postmicturition (Table 106.1).
  • Male continence depends on the external urethral sphincter (EUS), internal urethral sphincter (IUS), supporting structures and innervation — the EUS is the most important determinant.
  • Risk factors: age (major and independent), LUTS, infection, functional and cognitive impairment, neurological disease, obesity, diabetes, prostatectomy, drugs, smoking, alcohol.
  • DIAPPERS — commonest causes, especially in the elderly: Delirium, Infection, Atrophic urethritis, Pharmacological agents, Psychiatric illness, Endocrine disorders, Reduced mobility, Stool impaction.
  • Male UI is more often multifactorial than female UI → establish aetiology before treating; it carries social, economic and hygienic burden and impairs quality of life (QoL).
  • Describe UI by type, frequency, severity, precipitants, social, hygiene and QoL impact, containment, and whether help is wanted. Distinguish from sweating and urethral discharge.

Epidemiology

  • Less prevalent than in females — male:female ratio about 1:2 — and less studied; male incontinence is described in Ancient Egyptian manuscripts about 4000 years ago.
  • Reported prevalence 1–40%, the spread reflecting definition, type, population, age and survey method.
  • Urgency urinary incontinence (UUI) predominates in men, followed by stress urinary incontinence (SUI), then mixed urinary incontinence (MUI).
  • EpiLUTS reported a far higher male prevalence (46%) because it counted postmicturition incontinence, nocturnal enuresis and leakage without definable cause — definition drives the figure.
  • Rises with age: about 3–5% in young and middle-aged men versus 11–34% in older men — roughly one in ten at 60–64 years, approaching one in three over 85 years.
  • Race and ethnicity: the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) found no effect; other studies found one — highest in non-Hispanic Black men, lowest in Chinese men, with UUI the dominant type.

Key point: Risk factors for urinary incontinence in males

  • Age (major, independent), LUTS, urological infection, functional and cognitive impairment, neurological disease, diabetes, drugs, smoking, alcohol, and surgery (prostatectomy).

Concept — Male incontinence is age-driven, multifactorial and dominated by urgency; stress incontinence in men is largely an iatrogenic sphincter injury, which is why evaluation hunts for the cause rather than assuming a single mechanism.

Types of Urinary Incontinence

Key points: Types of urinary incontinence in males (ICS definitions)

  • SUI — involuntary loss on effort or physical exertion, or on sneezing, laughing or coughing, not caused by a bladder contraction.
  • UUI — involuntary loss associated with urgency.
  • MUI — involuntary loss associated with urgency and with effort.
  • Nocturnal enuresis — intermittent incontinence during sleep.
  • Continuous UI — continuous involuntary loss without normal voiding.
  • UI associated with chronic retention — from failure of the bladder to empty.

Concept — Each type is a pressure contest between bladder and outlet: in SUI abdominal pressure overcomes a weak sphincter; in UUI detrusor pressure overcomes a normal outlet; in chronic retention the bladder never empties and pressure stays high — so new bedwetting in an adult man is a retention symptom until proven otherwise.

Evaluation

History

  • When — on coughing, with urgency, asleep, or otherwise. How often — nightly, daily, other.
  • Precipitants — cold weather, latch-key incontinence (key in the door).
  • Previous surgery — prostate, bladder, or major abdominal surgery that may have damaged the sacral plexus.
  • How much — wets underwear, soaks outer clothing or floor. Containment — pad type, number, size; condom catheter; spare underwear.
  • Medications tried; neurological symptoms or back pain; sexual, erectile and bowel function; most bothersome symptom.
  • General medical, surgical and social history: drug allergies, smoking, quantified alcohol and caffeine intake.

Physical Examination

Sequence (lying): abdomen — scars, masses, distended bladder, hernias → external genitalia — foreskin and meatus (meatal stenosis or severe phimosis can cause UI with chronic retention) → digital rectal examination (DRE) — prostate size, consistency, tenderness (prostatitis); pelvic squeeze to gauge pelvic-floor strength → perineal skin, anal canal, lower rectum — anal tone and sensation, rectal emptying → lower-limb neurology — reflexes, spasticity, power, gait, mobility, sensation → mental orientation → cough test supine, then standing to detect SUI.

First-Line Investigations

Bedside tests, performed after clinical examination.

Measurement of Height and Weight to Calculate Body Mass Index

  • Body mass index (BMI) measures obesity; weight-loss advice may help SUI, but male evidence is weak — extrapolated from females.
  • With an artificial urinary sphincter (AUS): the pressure-regulating balloon is usually 61–70 cm H2O; intra-abdominal pressure exceeding this is transmitted to the bladder and the man leaks through the inflated cuff — less abdominal pressure, less leakage.

Urinalysis

  • Dipstick: leucocytes and nitrites (infection), glucose (diabetes), blood (haematuria), specific gravity (hydration), pH, ketones.
  • Inexpensive, vital preliminary test in all guidelines despite false results. Abnormal → microscopy, culture and sensitivity; haematuria → cytology, cystoscopy, upper-tract imaging per guidelines.

Bladder Diary

  • Objective record of leaks, pads, voiding pattern, day and night frequency (giving mean and maximum voided volume and functional capacity), fluid type and volume, nocturnal polyuria, urgency.
  • Only fully validated diary: International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire Bladder Diary (ICIQ-BD).
  • 3 days gives the same information as 7 without exhausting the patient (confirmed by ICIQ-BD validation); include weekdays and a weekend, representative of normal life.

Quality-of-Life Questionnaires and Patient-Reported Outcome Measures

  • UI causes little morbidity but major QoL impact → assess with a validated questionnaire.
  • International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS; American Urological Association Symptom Index, AUA-SI) is the commonest male questionnaire but does not measure incontinence.
  • ICIQ Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-UI-SF) — simple, short, differentiates SUI from UUI.
  • ICIQ Male LUTS (ICIQ-MLUTS) — long form (Fig. 106.3, online only); covers the storage and voiding items of the IPSS and the bother of each.
  • Diary and questionnaire repeated after treatment measure its effect.

Item 6 is unscored and localises type: before reaching the toilet (urgency), on cough or sneeze and when active (stress), when asleep, after dressing (postmicturition), no obvious reason, all the time (continuous).

Pad Testing

  • Weigh an absorbent pad before and after a specified period under standardised conditions.
  • Unresolved: 1 or 24 hours, how many days, predictive value. Does not distinguish UUI from SUI.
  • Most guidelines do not recommend it; useful to quantify leakage and plan treatment.

Urine Flow Rate and Postvoid Residual

  • Flow rate plus ultrasound postvoid residual (PVR): noninvasive, inexpensive, easy — interpretation is the skill.
  • Fast-rising curve → OAB (urgency) vs slow rise with long falling tail → bladder outflow obstruction (BOO) from an enlarged prostate.
  • High PVR → chronic retention; with high filling pressures → nocturnal enuresis.
  • Flow alone gives no detrusor pressure → cannot separate BOO from detrusor underactivity.
  • At least two flows traditionally; the ICS Good Urodynamic Practice document (2017) accepts one flow if the man confirms it was representative — so ask him.

Measurement of Prostate-Specific Antigen

  • Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is recommended in men with LUTS and life expectancy >10 years in whom a cancer diagnosis would change management; unnecessary if it would not and the DRE is normal.

Blood Tests

  • Tailored: high PVR or diabetes → renal function and glucose. Index UUI or SUI with low PVR → not indicated.
  • Clinicians may still test, since renal insufficiency is otherwise hard to recognise.

Advanced Investigations

  • Imaging (plain radiography, ultrasound) — not routine; only for suspected upper-tract abnormality, high PVR or urolithiasis.
  • Cystourethroscopy — suspected urethral stricture, BOO, blood on urinalysis, or high PVR.
  • Urodynamic studies (UDS) — gold standard for lower urinary tract (LUT) function. Strictly this includes flow rate; in practice it means filling cystometry and pressure–flow studies, with quality control, analysis and documentation.
  • Governing principle: perform UDS only if the result could alter management. International Consultation on Incontinence (ICI), European Association of Urology (EAU) and American Urological Association (AUA) all advise conservative and medical therapy first, UDS before invasive therapy.
  • Other indications: identify contributing factors; predict upper-tract consequences and intervention outcomes; understand effects of intervention; investigate treatment failure.

Filling Cystometry

  • Bladder (index non-neurogenic patient): normal, DO, or poor compliance. Leak with a DO wave = detrusor overactivity incontinence (DOI); mark urgency and provocative manoeuvres on the trace.
  • Urethra (EUS): competent (continent) or incompetent. Valsalva or repeated coughs, upright, at 200 mL; if no leak, repeat at maximum capacity. Leak → record the abdominal (Valsalva or cough) leak-point pressure.

Key points: Initial evaluation of patients with urinary incontinence

  • Essential: history and examination; height and weight for BMI; dipstick urinalysis; bladder diary (e.g., ICIQ-BD); QoL questionnaire (e.g., ICIQ-UI-SF); flow rate; PVR.
  • Optional: PSA; blood tests (e.g., creatinine); upper-tract imaging; endoscopy; invasive urodynamics.

Concept — Evaluation escalates from cheap, noninvasive tests that every man receives to invasive urodynamics justified only when its answer would change treatment — in practice, immediately before surgery.

Management of Urinary Incontinence in Males

Treatment of Urgency Urinary Incontinence

Conservative

  • Reduce fluid intake by 25%, provided intake is >1 L/day; stop smoking; lose weight; avoid caffeinated and carbonated drinks.
  • Add bladder training and PFMT. Continue at least 6 weeks, ideally 3 months.

Antimuscarinics

  • Traditional first-line class absent contraindications. Previous advice: at least two agents, ≥4 weeks each, titrated from low to maximum dose; newer evidence supports one agent, then moving on to other management.
  • Class-wide similar efficacy; extended-release preferred to immediate-release. All have level 1 evidence, grade A recommendation. Choice depends on national licensing, local guidelines and preference.
  • Off-label escalation in selected patients: combining two antimuscarinics or doubling the dose.
  • Anticholinergic burden: use for ≥3 months increases dementia risk by about half (systematic review), plus falls and fractures → higher mortality and resource use.

  • Conservative and medical therapy are usually started together for quicker relief.

After Failed Conservative and Medical Therapy

  • Invasive UDS is valuable but not mandatory before minimally invasive surgery; if DO or DOI is confirmed, offer minimally invasive treatment where available. Whether UDS should precede onabotulinumtoxinA or SNM is unproven and is governed by reimbursement and regulatory bodies.
  • FUTURE trial (United Kingdom) — UDS before minimally invasive treatment in women with refractory OAB; recruitment complete, results awaited, likely to be extrapolated to men.

Refractory UUI

  • Containment: pads, long-term suprapubic or urethral catheter.
  • Major surgery for the significantly bothered: augmentation enterocystoplasty or ileal conduit ± subtotal cystectomy. Poor-quality evidence, but protects the upper tracts and improves QoL; high short- and long-term morbidity.
  • Autoaugmentation is no longer recommended for adult DO — poor long-term success and complications.

Concept — UUI is managed as a ladder of increasing invasiveness — lifestyle, drugs, neuromodulation or toxin, reconstruction — where each rung is climbed only on failure of the last, and cognitive safety now shapes the drug rung as much as efficacy does.

Treatment of Stress Urinary Incontinence

Sequence: supervised PFMT for ≥3 months (pelvic-floor physiotherapist) → ± duloxetine → rule out urethral or anastomotic stricture and bladder dysfunction → surgery.

  • Commonest cause of male SUI: prostatectomy. After radical prostatectomy, no surgery for at least 6–12 months — continence continues to improve spontaneously.
  • While waiting: penile clamp (Dribble-Stop, Pacey cuff); or, more often, condom catheter, pads, suprapubic catheter, or urethral catheter — not long term (traumatic hypospadias). Catheter routes do not differ in urinary tract infection (UTI) or upper-tract change, but suprapubic causes fewer urethral complications.
  • Duloxetine (serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor): off-label in men — licensed in some countries only for women with moderate-to-severe incontinence; male data limited and heterogeneous; short-term success in most post-prostatectomy men despite discontinuation for side effects; long-term efficacy unknown.
  • AUS = gold standard for moderate-to-severe SUI, about 80% continence; first described by Scott in 1973.
  • Mild-to-moderate alternatives (variable efficacy): urethral bulking agents, male slings (fixed and adjustable), adjustable balloon continence therapy (ProACT).
  • Bulking agents enhance sphincter coaptation: inferior, with no proven long-term efficacy whatever the material; short-term improvement with transient haematuria and dysuria. Reserve for minimal leakage or men unfit for more invasive surgery; guidelines advise against them for PPI.

Male Slings

  • Good efficacy, acceptable morbidity in mild-to-moderate SUI; transobturator or retropubic.
  • AdVance (fixed): polypropylene mesh, transobturator, under the membranous urethra; cure rates vary widely because definitions of success differ.
  • Adjustable slings allow postoperative tension adjustment; three main systems.

  • MASTER trial (Men with Urodynamic Stress Incontinence After Prostate Surgery; noninferiority randomised controlled trial, RCT) — AUS vs male sling: both improved symptoms, satisfaction and QoL; equivalent at 12 months; secondary analysis favoured AUS (better QoL, fewer pads and leaks, higher satisfaction); under a strict definition only 15% in either arm never leaked.

Concept — Male SUI is a sphincter injury that often recovers, so the rule is wait and train first, then match the device to severity and to radiotherapy status: slings and balloons for lesser leakage in unirradiated tissue, the AUS for severe leakage.

Treatment of Mixed Urinary Incontinence

  • Treat the most bothersome component first; more challenging than either pure type.
  • The SUI component may be hidden, even on UDS, when UUI predominates → treat DO first (drugs or minimally invasive) → repeat UDS to reveal any SUI.

Treatment of Other Types of Urinary Incontinence

  • Treat the underlying cause.
  • High-pressure, low-flow retention with nocturnal enuresis → catheterise first to decompress → then treat BOO: endoscopic resection, vaporisation or enucleation, or open or robotic prostatectomy.
  • Nocturnal enuresis without residual → usually OAB → antimuscarinic or β3-agonist, possibly desmopressin. After cystoprostatectomy with neobladder, night-only leakage from pelvic-floor relaxation in sleep → conservative.
  • Postmicturition dribble: poorly studied, no approved drug; treat with PFMT (strong squeeze at end of voiding) plus urethral milking.

Urethral milking: wait a few seconds after voiding → fingertips three fingerbreadths behind the scrotum, gentle midline pressure → move forward under the scrotum to the penile base, pushing urine into the penile urethra → milk, squeeze and shake the penis → repeat twice.

Concept — Both algorithms share one spine — simple assessment, empirical conservative and drug therapy by presumed type, then urodynamics and specialist intervention on failure, with red-flag or "complicated" cases diverted straight to specialist care.

Conclusions

  • Assess to identify the cause, then treat conservatively and medically first; reserve surgery for failure of initial treatment when UI significantly affects QoL.

Key points: Treatment of urinary incontinence

  • UUI: fluid manipulation and lifestyle; bladder training and PFMT; antimuscarinics and/or β3-agonists; intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA; SNM; PTNS; augmentation enterocystoplasty; urinary diversion (e.g., ileal conduit).
  • SUI: weight loss and lifestyle; PFMT; duloxetine (off-licence); penile clamp; AUS (gold standard); suburethral sling (fixed or adjustable); ProACT balloon.
