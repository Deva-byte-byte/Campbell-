Chapter 115 — Electrical Stimulation and Neuromodulation in Storage and Emptying Failure

HISTORY OF ELECTRICAL STIMULATION

  • Electrical stimulation of the lower urinary tract (LUT) innervation is successful and multipotent; the idea of evoking a specific organ response through the nervous system is old and not confined to urology.
  • Scribonius Largus (CE 46), Compositiones medicae: live torpedo fish (also ray and eel) for headache and gout. For podagra the patient stood on a wave-washed shore on a black torpedo fish until numb to the knee — relieving the present attack and preventing recurrence.
  • Battery (18th century) → new medical uses of electricity; transistors (1950s) → first implanted pacemakers; cardiac success → rapid spread to urology.

Muscle-based era

  • Aim: contract the bladder (for poor contractility) or the sphincter (for stress incontinence).
  • Sequence: direct detrusor stimulation (Bradley, 1964) → first urethral sphincter stimulator (Caldwell, 1965) → stimulation of sacral cord nuclei in the conus medullaris to induce voiding (Nashold, 1972) → surface, deep and cuff electrodes (Jonas, 1976).
  • Canine work failed because bladder and sphincter were activated simultaneously.
  • Human trials followed in paraplegia and spina bifida (impaired sensation made stimulation tolerable), yet direct stimulation proved painful and only locally effective.
  • Indirect stimulation via afferent nerves needed less current and treated the whole end organ — the start of practical clinical neurostimulation.

Brindley's sacral anterior root stimulator

  • Sacral anterior root stimulator (SARS), Giles Brindley: stimulates the anterior sacral roots → bladder contraction (voiding), defecation and penile erection.
  • Anterior stimulation cannot reduce detrusor overactivity (DO) → combined with sacral dorsal rhizotomy of the same roots; over 25 years this markedly improved quality of life (QoL) in paraplegic patients. Further development by Sauerwein (Bad Wildungen) and Sarrias (Barcelona).

Sacral neuromodulation

  • About 1985: sacral neuromodulation (SNM), or sacral nerve stimulation (SNS) — sacral root stimulation could decrease rather than increase bladder activity. A breakthrough for overactive bladder (OAB), which previously had no effective treatment. Pioneers: Emil Tanagho and Rick Schmidt; priority of discovery unclear.
  • Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) showed the mechanism is indirect: sacral roots are mixed → current excites afferents → central nervous system (CNS) → efferents to pelvic organs. The whole storage–micturition system is influenced, not the organ directly — hence "neuromodulation".

Tibial and newer targets

  • Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS), about 2000: derived from acupuncture point SP6 at the ankle; McGuire (1983) first stimulated the posterior tibial nerve for bladder control; widespread use in the 2000s.
  • PTNS is simpler and cheaper than SNM; with no implant and discontinuous stimulation, a sham-controlled net effect could be convincingly shown.
  • Newer targets: dorsal genital nerve, pudendal nerve, intravaginal, transcutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (TTNS) by patch; small rechargeable devices entering practice.

Concept — Direct organ stimulation failed on pain and locality; every durable technique since (SARS with rhizotomy aside) works indirectly through afferents, which is why the field is neuromodulation rather than muscle stimulation.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF STORAGE AND VOIDING DISORDERS

  • Exclude anatomical causes before any electrical therapy: external urethral sphincter (EUS) weakness, poorly compliant detrusor, bladder stones, bladder cancer, vesicovaginal fistula.
  • How electrical therapy helps functional disorders is unknown; afferent modulation of central pathways probably dominates.

Pathophysiology of Storage Disorders

  • Storage failure arises from the bladder wall (urothelium, detrusor), the outlet, or both. With known neurological disease → neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (LUTD). Electrical stimulation is mainly approved for non-neurogenic disorders.
  • Continence = synergic antagonism of bladder and outlet, EUS the key structure. Filling: bladder relaxed, EUS tonically contracted — involuntary, cannot be voluntarily interrupted.
  • Afferents: bladder filling via Aδ fibres in the pelvic and hypogastric nerves (from urothelium, suburothelium, detrusor); bladder neck and urethra via pudendal and hypogastric nerves → lumbosacral cord (lateral dorsal horn, intermediate zone) → mostly intraspinal proprioceptive connections, some ascending to pons and midbrain; the spinothalamic tract keeps the sensory cortex informed of fullness.
  • OAB: urge cannot be deferred but can be suppressed — pelvic floor physiotherapy, drugs, botulinum toxin, electrical stimulation.
  • Sympathetic outflow fires only from ≈60% of capacity → wall relaxation and smooth-muscle closure of the bladder neck. Sympatholytics or sympathectomy barely affect storage → the sympathetic system is not the main continence mechanism in health.

Pathophysiology of Voiding Disorders

  • Exclude anatomical obstruction first: urethral stricture or stone, benign prostatic enlargement, pelvic organ prolapse.
  • Detrusor cause: underactive detrusor = hypocontractility plus hyposensitivity. Outlet cause: anatomical obstruction, or neurogenic detrusor–external sphincter dyssynergia (DESD).
  • Electrical stimulation cannot restore muscle volume or correct anatomical obstruction.
  • Normal voiding sequence: EUS relaxes → 1–2 s later detrusor contracts. Without EUS relaxation, voiding is impossible.
  • Circuit sequence from the pontine micturition centre (PMC): long axons → parasympathetic preganglionic motoneurons (sacral intermediolateral column) → postganglionic neurons in bladder wall → delayed smooth-muscle detrusor contraction; collaterals → inhibitory γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)/glycine interneurons (sacral intermediomedial column) → EUS motoneurons → immediate striated EUS relaxation. Both arms fire simultaneously at sacral level.
  • Forebrain operates the PMC as a switch: forebrain lesions lose the timing of micturition but the reflex remains intact. Positron emission tomography (PET) and fMRI confirm the PMC in humans. Afferents → thalamus → cingulate and insular cortex → prefrontal cortex decides whether voiding is safe.
  • Kegel exercises add voluntary motor-cortex contraction on top of tonic involuntary contraction; on stopping, tone returns to baseline — so physiotherapy is usually ineffective in retention.

WORKING MECHANISM OF ELECTRICAL STIMULATION OF THE LOWER URINARY TRACT

  • Electrical therapies act not only on the LUT and spinal cord but on supraspinal centres, which in turn modulate the LUT.

Storage Disorders

  • No mechanism is confirmed. Theories concern SNS but probably apply to all electrical therapies.
  • SNS differs in scale: it recruits ≥1000 times more axons, continuously, versus a small or superficial nerve stimulated intermittently; the implant also gives more flexible parameters.
  • A common central mode may extend to faecal incontinence, chronic pain and deep brain stimulation.
  • Storage disorders are attributed to loss of inhibitory control acquired during postnatal maturation of the voiding reflex; stimulation may restore it.

Voiding Disorders

  • Paradox: SNS treats both overactive and underactive bladder.
  • Functional voiding disorders lack effective filling sensation and cannot switch on the PMC. SNS raises awareness of filling and of pelvic floor tone → PMC switched on → EUS relaxes.
  • In underactive bladder (UAB) SNS works mainly by striated sphincter relaxation, much less by detrusor contraction.

Concept — One afferent → forebrain mechanism serves opposite disorders: in OAB heightened control damps PMC firing; in UAB heightened awareness lets the PMC switch on.

INTRAVAGINAL ELECTRIC STIMULATION

  • Alternative conservative therapy for stress urinary incontinence (SUI): non-implantable vaginal electrodes passively activate the pelvic floor directly, perhaps also indirectly via the pudendal nerve; given by physiotherapists or at home.
  • Systematic reviews: minimally invasive and safe, but evidence too low in quality to judge effectiveness; one review found better pad use and subjective outcome. All call for higher-quality trials.

TRANSCUTANEOUS SACRAL DERMATOME STIMULATION

  • Transcutaneous sacral dermatome stimulation (TSDS), established in pain management: current passes skin receptors → posterior root ganglion → spinal centres via Aβ, C and Aδ fibres; applied to suppress DO in OAB.
  • Randomised trial, bilateral S3 dermatome electrodes, refractory idiopathic DO (IDO) and neurogenic DO (NDO): higher cystometric capacity, later first involuntary contraction (significant in IDO only), smaller neurogenic contractions.
  • Multiple sclerosis (MS) with NDO, conditional stimulation (triggered when detrusor pressure exceeded 10 cmH₂O): dorsal genital nerve stimulation suppressed contractions; sacral dermatome stimulation failed in every patient.
  • Children with OAB or dysfunctional voiding: daily S3 stimulation for 3 months (20 min, 10 Hz, 350 μs) → less incontinence, fewer urinary tract infections (UTIs), lower post-void residual (PVR).
  • Synthesis: can raise capacity and perhaps lower maximal detrusor pressure (renal protection), but is not useful for conditional stimulation; studies are few and small.

Concept — Dermatome stimulation modulates chronically but cannot abort a contraction acutely; that gap is what the purely sensory dorsal genital nerve fills.

PUDENDAL NERVE STIMULATION

  • Anatomy: S2–S4 → over the ischial spine → pudendal (Alcock's) canal; gives the inferior rectal nerve proximally, then the perineal nerve and dorsal genital nerve distally.
  • Low-frequency afferent stimulation inhibits OAB; dorsal genital stimulation in complete thoracic or cervical spinal cord injury (SCI) strongly inhibits the micturition reflex.
  • Technique: percutaneous via the pudendal canal with an off-label tined quadripolar lead; posterior approach preferred — perineal approach carries more lead infection.
  • Indications: IDO, NDO, chronic pelvic pain.
  • Versus SNM (blinded crossover, OAB and non-obstructive urinary retention [NOUR]): pudendal gave greater symptom reduction, and most patients chose it for the permanent implant. Also effective after failed SNM → a salvage option.
  • Not approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for chronic stimulation in voiding dysfunction or pelvic pain.

DORSAL GENITAL NERVE STIMULATION

  • Dorsal genital nerve stimulation (DGNS) — dorsal clitoral or dorsal penile nerve, needle electrodes — is experimental for OAB from IDO and NDO; not in routine practice.
  • Theoretical advantages: easily reached by needle (like the tibial nerve); a direct pudendal branch, so a short route to the sacral LUT control; purely sensory, so theoretically best at carrying the stimulus afferently to the brain.
  • Correct stimulation confirmed by the genitoanal reflex.
  • Evidence: acute DO suppression in SCI; short-term capacity increase in NDO, consistent across small studies (meta-analysis); in women with OAB-wet, 7 days of continuous stimulation reduced incontinence and severe urgency; on-demand stimulation (pilot) aims to avoid habituation and save energy.
  • Drawbacks: hard to keep electrodes in contact with the small branches; the stimulation site (dorsal penile base, clitoral area) is problematic for patients. Evidence is mostly acute and small.

Concept — If neuromodulation works through afferents, the purest afferent target should be the most efficient — DGNS is the test of that logic, limited by fixation and site rather than by physiology.

TRANSCUTANEOUS TIBIAL NERVE STIMULATION

  • PTNS: introduced 1999, FDA approval 2000 (office-based OAB treatment), National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence (NICE) approval 2006. Standard: 12 sessions × 30 min, 1–3 times a week, plus maintenance → heavy burden on patients and clinics.
  • TTNS (table below) cuts travel and cost, but the skin acts as a capacitor: higher, often painful amplitude is needed, and lowering it for comfort gives suboptimal stimulation.
  • Systematic review (randomised controlled trials [RCTs] and cohorts; idiopathic and neurogenic OAB — MS, Parkinson disease, stroke, SCI): heterogeneous, underpowered, non-standardised dosing (4–12 weeks; 20–30-min sessions; daily to weekly). Meta-analysis: clinically and statistically significant fall in the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-UI-SF) in older adults; no adverse events. Verdict: potentially effective in idiopathic OAB, safe in neurogenic OAB; no head-to-head PTNS data.
  • Later review of RCTs: TTNS as effective as pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) and PTNS, less effective than antimuscarinics; low cost and self-management make it an attractive alternative. Place in the OAB algorithm unsettled.

PERCUTANEOUS TIBIAL NERVE STIMULATION

  • SNS is the most documented neuromodulation for OAB; PTNS is the extensively documented alternative, cheaper overall, hence wider reach.
  • Anatomy (Fig. 115.9): LUT innervation L2–S4; sciatic nerve L4–S3 → posterior tibial nerve, whose fibres arise mainly from S2–S3.
  • Proposed mechanism: "crosstalk" between sympathetic and parasympathetic postganglionic terminals at neuroeffector junctions alters efferent signalling to the bladder; peripheral nerves (pudendal, dorsal genital, tibial) can thus modulate bladder behaviour.
  • Animal model (cat, intravesical acetic acid = OAB model): naloxone blocked PTNS inhibition of the OAB-model DO but not of normal reflex activity → opioid receptors involved.
  • Somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs): after PTNS versus sham, long-latency SEP (P80, P100) amplitude changed → lasting change in synaptic efficiency (long-term potentiation or depression) → long-term effect of PTNS and SNS may reflect cortical network reorganisation.

HISTORY OF PERIPHERAL NEUROSTIMULATION

  • Derived from acupuncture; Wilhelmus Ten Rhyne (1683), Dutch physician, described it in Europe (Dissertatio de Arthritide … De Acupunctura).
  • SP-6 (SanYin Jiao): medial lower leg ≈6–8 cm above the medial malleolus, near the medial tibial crest; site and target organs closely match PTNS.
  • Electroacupuncture at 2–15 Hz, 10–20 mA resembles PTNS; difference: PTNS uses an anatomical substrate, acupuncture "energetic pathways".
  • McGuire (1983): first tibial stimulation (transcutaneous) in neurogenic OAB, with most improving; Stoller then developed PTNS in pig-tailed monkeys and humans.

Technique

  • Sequence: supine, medial malleolus upward → 34-gauge stainless-steel needle ≈3–4 cm (three fingerbreadths) cephalad to the medial malleolus, between posterior tibial margin and soleus, at 60–90° → tip near, not touching, the nerve → grounding pad on the same leg → 9 V stimulator, 0–10 mA, pulse width 200 μs, 20 Hz → confirm position → stimulate at tolerable intensity → 12 sessions × 30 min, 1–3 per week (Fig. 115.10).
  • Confirmation pair: efferent (motor) = great-toe flexion or fanning; afferent (sensory) = radiating tickling of the sole.

CLINICAL RESULTS

  • Mostly OAB. Subjective success (QoL questionnaires) = improved QoL or willingness to continue; objective success (frequency–volume chart [FVC]) = ≥50% fall in urgency or urgency urinary incontinence (UUI) and ≥25% fall in day or night frequency.
  • Urodynamics conflict: acute DO suppression failed in neurological patients and was rare in others, though one series reported elimination in most; cystometric capacity rose consistently.
  • SUmiT trial (landmark): multicentre double-blind RCT, 12 weeks PTNS vs validated sham, n = 220 — the first validated sham. Primary endpoint: 7-point self-reported global response assessment (GRA), success = moderate or marked improvement: 55% vs 21%. FVC improved in frequency, nocturia, moderate–severe urgency voids and UUI. A second sham-controlled RCT agreed.
  • No standard regimen; most objective data support 12 weeks; shorter regimens may work but be less durable.
  • Relapse is likely → maintenance or tapering keeps the brain "remembering" LUT control. Maintenance sustained results at 2 years.
  • SUmiT extension: 12-week responders on a 14-week taper then personal schedule (median 1.1 treatments per month): at 3 years 77% relapse-free; voids 12.0 → 8.7 per day; UUI 3.3 → 0.3 per day; QoL sustained.
  • Children: when bladder training, pelvic floor therapy and antimuscarinics fail, PTNS is well tolerated and improves most; pain scores low.

PERCUTANEOUS TIBIAL NERVE STIMULATION COMPARED TO OTHER TREATMENTS

  • PTNS vs tolterodine (two RCTs, non-neurogenic OAB): both improved at 3 months, no difference in QoL, urgency or UUI; fewer side effects with PTNS. Other antimuscarinics similar.
  • Cochrane review (peripheral neuromodulation vs antimuscarinics): subjective improvement favoured stimulation, significant only for PTNS, without significant differences in frequency, nocturia, UUI or QoL. Conclusion: antimuscarinics remain established; stimulation may be better in antimuscarinic-refractory patients.

PROGNOSTIC FACTORS FOR PERCUTANEOUS TIBIAL NERVE STIMULATION

  • Few data. Better outcome in patients without DO on urodynamics.
  • Most clinical parameters non-predictive; only low Short Form-36 (SF-36) Mental Component Summary predicted failure — worse disease-specific QoL despite equal severity → mental-health questionnaires may aid selection.

IMPLANTABLE TIBIAL NERVE STIMULATORS

  • Rationale: tapering needs repeated visits (logistics, travel burden), on-demand PTNS is impossible, and surface electrodes are less effective because of skin impedance.
  • Not all PTNS responders respond to the implant — as with sacral stimulation.

CONCLUSIONS

  • PTNS modulates storage and voiding: subjective improvement ≈60%, FVC improvement 47–56%, placebo effect ≈21% (possibly higher in children), 53–77% still improved at 2–3 years with maintenance.
  • Minimally invasive, inexpensive, but time-consuming → implants under investigation.

KEY POINTS: PERCUTANEOUS TIBIAL NERVE STIMULATION

  • PTNS resembles Chinese acupuncture.
  • First urological tibial nerve stimulation report: 1983.
  • SUmiT trial: PTNS superior to sham.
  • First feasibility report of a tibial implant: 2006.
  • Modern tibial implants are nearing clinical introduction.

Concept — PTNS is proven against sham but not durable without maintenance; every later development — tapering, TTNS, implants — is an answer to the maintenance burden, not to efficacy.

OVERACTIVE BLADDER WITH OR WITHOUT URGENCY URINARY INCONTINENCE

  • International Continence Society (ICS): OAB with incontinence = involuntary leakage preceded or accompanied by urgency (sudden compelling desire to void, difficult to defer).
  • Upper limit of normal = 7 voids per 24 h on 1.5–2 L intake; OAB without incontinence = ≥8 voids per 24 h with urgency. The definition does not exclude excess fluid intake → frequency–volume diary before any OAB treatment.
  • Exclude: UTI, underactive bladder with significant PVR, calculi, bladder cancer.
  • Urodynamic DO may or may not be present; DO does not predict SNS success.
  • Common success definition: ≥50% symptom improvement during a 1–2-week test; definitions vary across studies.

NONOBSTRUCTIVE URINARY RETENTION

  • NOUR: non-painful bladder with chronically high PVR needing regular drainage; accepted PVR debated, often ≥100 mL. UAB is usually also hyposensitive with capacity >500 mL.
  • Measured PVR is the only objective marker of success or failure.
  • RCT, SNS vs continued CIC: at 6 months most implanted patients had stopped catheterising, none of the controls. Men respond similarly; younger men respond better.
  • Predictors after two-stage SNS: women with a psychiatric history (including post-traumatic stress disorder) → higher odds; men with previous transurethral resection of the prostate or bladder neck incision → higher odds; increasing age lowers odds in both sexes.

OTHER INDICATIONS

  • Bladder pain syndrome (BPS): not FDA approved. Offered selectively, especially with coexisting OAB, because OAB symptoms give an objective test-phase measure where pain is subjective and analgesic-confounded. Small, mostly retrospective data: voided volume rose, pain fell.
  • Neurogenic bladder: not approved, evidence low. A sham-controlled RCT in first-phase responders favoured SNS-on over SNS-off, but the population was heterogeneous. Systematic review (mostly MS and SCI, mixed OAB and retention): high pooled success, comparable to non-neurogenic.

Sacral Nerve Stimulation Patient Selection and Implantation Procedure

  • Implant = quadripolar lead + neurostimulator; success depends mainly on patient selection.
  • Selected OAB patients could theoretically be implanted directly with ≥90% success, but practice demands a test trial for all — office percutaneous (peripheral) nerve evaluation (PNE) or theatre-based two-stage implant.
  • Pass criteria: OAB — ≥50% improvement in ≥1 parameter (frequency, voided volume, incontinence episodes); retention — catheterisation frequency reduced ≥50%, ideally none, fewer UTIs.
  • Assessment compares a 3–5-day baseline bladder diary with a diary kept during the test.

Percutaneous Nerve Evaluation Test

  • Sequence: local anaesthesia → insulated needle into the S3 foramen by bony landmarks (coccyx, iliac crest, lumbosacral joint) → fluoroscopy optional (anatomy does not always match function) → test stimulation: sensory responses first, motor at higher amplitude → temporary lead through needle, needle withdrawn → lead taped to skin, external receiver on belt, wireless external stimulator → mild non-painful perineal sensation → 5–7 days with bladder diary.
  • Landmarks (Fig. 115.14): S3 lies ≈9 cm from the coccyx tip and 11 cm from the anal verge, then 1–2 cm lateral; fluoroscopic "cross-hair" — midline plus lower sacroiliac joints.
  • Positive if ≥1 diary parameter improves ≥50%, then full SNS (lead + permanent stimulator) in one session. Positive in ≈60% of OAB in the authors' unit; not used for NOUR (higher failure).

Staged Implant Procedure

  • For patients who failed or could not tolerate PNE; introduced because some PNE-positive patients did worse after full implant.
  • Sequence: stage 1 — permanent tined quadripolar lead into S3, placement confirmed fluoroscopically with bellows, ipsilateral toe and genital sensory responses, tines anchoring in the fascia above the sacrum (Figs. 115.13, 115.15) (originally open via 10-cm incision with periosteal fixation; percutaneous since 2003, ≈30 min) → temporary extension tunnelled to the flank → test up to 4 weeks with programme optimisation → stage 2 at 2–4 weeks if ≥50% improvement: neurostimulator in the buttock, extension removed. Failure → lead and extension removed.
  • Prophylaxis: meticillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) cover suggested; authors use intravenous cefazolin + metronidazole.
  • ICS consensus: S3 preferred, S4 sometimes; aim for motor and/or sensory responses on all four contacts at low amplitude, though fewer contacts or higher voltage can succeed.

Additional Perspectives of SNS

Constant Versus Intermittent Stimulation

  • SNS is continuous, whereas PTNS works with 30 min every 1–2 weeks → intermittent SNS was tested: cyclic (16 s on / 8 s off) and 8 h on / 16 h off both equal to continuous.
  • Closed-loop stimulation, triggered just before OAB symptoms, is a future option.

Rechargeable Neurostimulators

  • Rechargeables make battery-sparing protocols less urgent. Two-year OAB results after direct full implantation without a test: high success; all patients able to recharge; claimed life >15 years.

Other Issues

  • Bilateral SNS is not superior to unilateral (RCT) → not standard practice: no proven benefit, higher cost.
  • MRI: all recent Axonics and Medtronic devices have conditional approval for whole-body MRI at 1.5 and 3 T. At replacement, remove all previous components to guarantee MRI safety.

KEY POINTS: SACRAL NERVE STIMULATION

  • SNS is an established third-line option for non-neurogenic bladder dysfunction; FDA-approved for idiopathic OAB and NOUR, not for bladder pain, pelvic pain syndrome or neurogenic bladder.
  • Sacral neurostimulators are 1.5-T and 3-T MRI safe, rechargeable or non-rechargeable, lasting at least 10 years.
  • SNS and non-invasive neuromodulation in storage disorders are thought to activate afferents that modulate forebrain awareness and alertness.

Concept — SNS is governed by selection through a test phase: the objective diary change (≥50%, or catheterisation for retention) decides implantation, and every technical refinement — tined leads, staging, S3 targeting — exists to make that test predict the permanent result.
