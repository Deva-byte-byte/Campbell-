## Instalment 1 — Scope of Adolescent Urology, Definition, Rationale and Candidates for Transition, Consequences of Failed Transition, the Three Ps (Fig. 65.1), Stakeholders, Timing, Transition Tools and Six Core Elements (Fig. 65.2)

### WHAT CONSTITUTES ADOLESCENT UROLOGY?
- **Origin:** the subspecialty arose from recognising that patients born with complex urogenital conditions need **long-term adult care**.
- **Evolution:** it began as a stepwise paediatric → adolescent → adult sequence. The ethos is now **seamless lifelong care**, with adolescent urology acting as the **bridge** between paediatric and adult urology.
- **Why adult reconstructive urologists alone fall short:** they can perform some of the operations but are **underequipped to care for the whole patient**. Four challenges have to be managed together:
  - **Multiple previous operations.**
  - The underlying condition and prior surgery **alter the anatomical, psychological and physiological landscape**.
  - Patients and parents have been in contact with health care since infancy. They are often **expert in their own condition** and unforgiving of a provider who is not.
  - Previous care took place in a **paediatric hospital**, so **managing expectations** of adult care is central to the doctor–patient relationship.
- **Current gap:** many patients are **underserved in adulthood**. Collaboration, both clinical and between centres, is needed to publish **meaningful outcome data**.
- **Role of the adolescent urologist:** **inform and recruit adult urologists** into the field, and publish and discuss outcomes with paediatric colleagues.

*Concept — The gap is in **whole-patient knowledge, not operative skill**. Childhood reconstruction creates an adult whose anatomy, history and expectations differ from those of any other adult patient.*

### DEFINITION OF TRANSITION
- **Got Transition definition:** transition is the **move from a child/family-centred to an adult/patient-centred model of care**, with or without a change of clinician. It has three parts: **planning → transfer → integration**.
- **Two goals:** (1) improve young people's ability to **manage their own health care** and use services effectively; (2) ensure an **organised process in both paediatric and adult practices** covering preparation, transfer and integration.
- **In practice,** transition means three things: **independence** in the health-care environment, **education** about the condition and its treatment, and **navigation** of adult care.
- **Transition vs transfer:** transition is the **whole process** (planning, transfer and integration). Transfer is a **single step within it** and need not involve a new clinician.

### WHY IS TRANSITION NEEDED?
- **Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) report:** on first reading, transition care in its present form **does not appear to make a significant difference**.
- **Rebuttal:** **no robust, reproducible metrics** exist. Markers such as renal function or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) are expected to **worsen with age**, so **stability counts as success**, not improvement. **Better metrics** are needed.
- **Consistent finding:** **fewer patients are lost to follow-up** when they are actively transitioned to a capable adult service.
- **Language:** avoid the word "normal". The **patient is their own baseline**. Comparing an adult born with exstrophy to "normal" is meaningless and may stigmatise. Handle the patient's wish for normality with sensitivity in order to gain their trust.

*Concept — The evidence for transition looks weak because **the wrong endpoint is being measured**. In progressive congenital disease, **stability and retention in follow-up are the outcome**.*

### WHO NEEDS TRANSITION?
- Congenital conditions and conditions acquired in childhood both carry risk from the **condition or from its prior treatment**, so **most patients need adult care**.

**FOUR CATEGORIES REFERRED TO A UROLOGICAL TRANSITION SERVICE**

| Category | Examples | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Congenital, complex needs**; planned monitoring essential | Exstrophy, posterior urethral valves, complex hypospadias, spina bifida (SB) | **Planned lifelong care** |
| 2. Treated congenital condition, now **quiescent or mild**, with risk through puberty | Vesicoureteric reflux (VUR) | **May be discharged** after an early-adult consultation, with open return |
| 3. Significant childhood condition **cured**, but with **treatment sequelae** | Pelvic rhabdomyosarcoma | Surveillance for **late effects of treatment** |
| 4. New condition or concern **beyond paediatric scope** | Fertility, pregnancy | Needs adult expertise |

- The table separates **who needs lifelong follow-up** (category 1) from **who may be discharged** (category 2) and **who is followed for the treatment rather than the disease** (category 3).
- **Trap:** not every transitioned patient needs lifelong follow-up. Category 2 can be discharged.
- **Purpose of successful transition:** reporting **long-term outcomes** drives quality improvement, and **preventive care before catastrophic outcomes** reduces costs.

### WHAT HAPPENS WHEN TRANSITION FAILS?
- **Start discussions in early adolescence**, individualised to the patient's developmental and physical needs.
- **Risks of non-transition:**
  - a young adult who **does not understand their condition** or its treatment
  - **overbearing family control** of an adult's health care
  - **loss to follow-up**
  - **unrecognised complications** and poor outcomes
  - **reliance on emergency care**
  - lack of social support (education, employment, housing, finance)
  - failure to recognise the patient's ability to achieve in society
  - no access to peer and patient support groups
- **Net result:** **non-adherence, more emergency department (ED) attendances and hospitalisations, higher morbidity and mortality**, and family dissatisfaction.

*Concept — Failed transition does its harm through **loss of preventive surveillance**. The patient reappears only through the emergency department, after complications that could have been prevented.*

### TRANSITION AS A PROCESS

#### Constituent Elements of the Process

**FIG. 65.1 THE THREE Ps OF TRANSITION**

| P | Content | Measure of success |
|---|---|---|
| **Preparation** | Supporting independence in the health-care environment. Can begin at any age, but usually starts in **early adolescence (12–14 years)**. Readiness tools such as the **Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire (TRAQ)**; an electronic health record (EHR) tool may ease data collection | Evidence that **all patients and families have been involved** in preparation |
| **Plan** | Expected **clinic schedule, frequency of imaging and blood tests**. Self-care: **catheterisation frequency, bladder irrigation, medications, when and whom to call** | Every patient has a plan, **communicated to them and to their future team** |
| **Provider of the future** | May or may not be a new team. The patient knows **which teams to see and where** | Every patient has a **named future provider** |

- It is **not acceptable** for a paediatric team to "complete" care and leave the patient to find their own way. The adult system must provide space, time and people, but the **paediatric provider is responsible for initiating a formal transition process**.

#### Stakeholders and Their Roles
*Patient.*
- The **primary stakeholder**. The patient must be medically and physically stable for handover, so **avoid transition during a crisis** such as deteriorating renal function or a need for urgent surgery.
- **Exception:** transfer is **expedited** when adult providers become the most appropriate carers even during a crisis. The classic example is **pregnancy**, because obstetric care lies outside a paediatric hospital. Here, **co-management by paediatric and adult teams** keeps the familiar team involved and shows the teams working together.

*Parents.*
- Parents have navigated care and decisions since infancy, sometimes since before birth. **Handing decision-making to their child** can overwhelm them, with a mix of **pride, a need to show trust, and fear** of losing protection or of bad decisions. **Separating from a trusted provider** is itself a significant barrier.
- **Create private time** in each consultation for patient and physician alone. This matters especially for **sex, sexual function and sexual debut**. Afterwards, ask the patient whether they want the conversation shared with their parents.
- These conversations are **often omitted**. In some cohorts most patients report never having discussed sex with a provider.

*Health care providers.*

**PAEDIATRIC VS ADULT SETTING**

| Paediatric | Adult |
|---|---|
| **Multidisciplinary clinics common**; several teams in one visit | **Multidisciplinary care harder to establish** |
| Cross-disciplinary learning | **Communication between specialists often lacking** |
| — | Mitigated by **complex-care schedulers and care coordinators** |

*Health care institutions.*
- Access to adult care is difficult for young adults with complex chronic conditions. The conditions are well defined but **rare and heterogeneous**, so their financial benefit is **hard to quantify**.
- Demonstrate need through **practice growth**. Engage **"allies"**: like-minded specialists, patient advocacy groups and the patients themselves.

#### When to Begin and Complete the Process
- **Begin:** the **American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends introducing transition at about age 12**. Content widens through the teens to cover diagnosis, past decisions, treatments and future considerations.
- **Practical skills:** booking appointments themselves, navigating insurance, knowing whom to call, and what to do in an emergency.
- **The most important element** is teaching the young adult **how and where to find valid information**. Written questions before visits and a **portable health record** help.
- **Complete:** a paediatric environment becomes inappropriate for most patients in their **late teens to early 20s**. **Age 26** is often cited as the **latest age for completing transition**, because it is the upper age limit for health cover on a parent's insurance plan in the United States.
- **Where independent living is not achievable** (cognitive or physical limitation), the care setting is a matter of judgement and needs ongoing reassessment. Ask about future **medical custodianship**, for example by a sibling as parents age or die, and involve future carers in discussions.

#### Available Tools

**INSTITUTIONAL TRANSITION PROGRAMMES**

| Programme | Origin | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Good 2 Go** | Toronto SickKids, Canada | Institutional programme |
| **Ready Steady Go** | United Kingdom | Institutional programme |
| **Got Transition** | United States | Framework of **six core elements** (Fig. 65.2) |

- **The best programme is the one that fits** the provider, the care setting and the practice.

**FIG. 65.2 SIX CORE ELEMENTS — TIMELINE FOR ADOLESCENT AND YOUNG ADULT (AYA) TRANSITION**

| Element | Age (years) | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Policy/guide** | **12–14** | Develop, discuss and share a transition policy |
| **Tracking and monitoring** | 14–18 | Track progress with a flow-sheet registry |
| **Readiness** | 14–18 | Assess self-care skills; educate on identified needs |
| **Planning** | 14–18 | Transition plan with a **medical summary** |
| **Transfer of care** | **18–21** | Transfer to an adult-centred practice |
| **Transition complete** | **18–23** | Confirm completion; **seek patient and parent feedback** |

- **Pattern:** the three 14–18 elements run **in parallel** over the same window; only policy, transfer and completion are sequential.
- **Trap:** transfer (18–21) is not the endpoint. Transition is complete only once **integration is confirmed and feedback obtained** (by 23).

*Concept — Transition is a **process started by the paediatric team and completed in the adult setting**. It begins at about 12, is tracked through adolescence, and ends only when integration into adult care is confirmed, not at the last paediatric appointment.*

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 1
- **Start transition:** AAP **about age 12**. Preparation in early adolescence (**12–14**). Got Transition policy stage **12–14**.
- **Six core elements:** policy 12–14 → tracking, readiness and planning 14–18 → **transfer 18–21** → **complete 18–23**.
- **Latest completion commonly cited: age 26**, the US limit for cover on a parent's insurance.
- **Three Ps:** **Preparation, Plan, Provider of the future**. Every patient has a written plan and a **named future provider**.
- **Four referral categories:** (1) complex congenital → lifelong; (2) quiescent (VUR) → may be discharged; (3) cured with treatment sequelae (pelvic rhabdomyosarcoma); (4) beyond paediatric scope (fertility, pregnancy).
- **Most important element of preparation:** teaching the patient **how and where to find valid information**.
- **Classic exception to "never transition in crisis": pregnancy** → co-management by paediatric and adult teams.
- **Programmes:** Good 2 Go (Toronto), Ready Steady Go (UK), Got Transition (US, six core elements).
- **Classic traps:**
  - The AHRQ report showing "no significant difference" does not mean transition is futile. The metrics are inadequate, and **stability is success**.
  - **Transfer ≠ transition.** Transfer is one step within the process.
  - Not every transitioned patient needs lifelong follow-up. **Category 2 (e.g. VUR) may be discharged.**
  - **Avoid "normal"**; the patient is their own baseline.
  - The **paediatric team initiates** transition. Waiting for the adult team is wrong.

## Instalment 2 — Patient Population and Referral Routes, Models of Lifelong Care, Multidisciplinary Care Needs, Key Points, Developmental Considerations, Timing of Surgical Intervention, Emerging Adults and Capacity, Medical PTSD, Training, the MDT, Institutional Support, Fig. 65.3, Barriers to Transition (Patient/Parent, Providers)

### SYSTEMS AND MODELS OF CARE

#### Patient Population

**ROUTES INTO AN ADOLESCENT UROLOGY SERVICE**

| Route | Status | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Transition and referral** from the paediatric service | **Ideal** | Prepared patient |
| 2. Referral from paediatrics **without transition** | Suboptimal | Integration is easier than route 4 because there is **no concurrent acute need** |
| 3. Referral with a **new problem arising in adolescence** | **Ideal** | New problem, managed from the outset |
| 4. **Emergency referral** after discharge from paediatric care | **Most challenging**; the **most prevalent route in some systems** | Unequipped with skills or knowledge of adult care; **insurance obstacles because of pre-existing conditions** |

- **Trap:** route 3 is "ideal" even though no transition took place, because the problem is new.

#### Potential Models for the Provision of Lifelong Care

**MODELS OF LIFELONG CARE**

| Model | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **1a. Lifelong paediatric urologist** in a combined paediatric/adult department | **Absolute continuity**; no transfer; one urologist follows results throughout life; suits **limited life expectancy** (some paediatric cancers) | **Patient outlives the provider's career**; patient not equipped to navigate other adult specialties; a purely paediatric environment is **unhealthy for the patient and for surrounding paediatric patients** |
| **1b. Paediatric urologist integrated with a separate adult department** | Continuity plus collaboration; mutual learning; co-location allows **joint clinics and joint operating** | **Cost, geography/hospital, clinic privileging**; depends on willingness to use surrounding expertise |
| **2. Transfer to an adolescent/adult specialist at a separate institution** | This specialist also **coordinates the MDT**; joint clinics; meeting the new team in the familiar paediatric setting | **Communication across institutions**; institutional barriers; family fear of leaving; needs careful preparation and **mutual trust** between teams |
| **3. Direct transfer without a transition plan** | None stated; default where systems are slow to recognise the need | Patients **invisible until an emergency**; no deliberate preventive care; **many centres lack adult expertise** |

- **Self-induced model 3:** patients move away for college or work, cannot travel to their original urologist, find no comparable expertise locally, drop out of follow-up, and **present in crisis**.
- **Pattern:** continuity rises from model 3 to model 1a, but so does dependence on one provider. The integrated model (1b) combines continuity with adult expertise.

#### Multidisciplinary Care Models: Which Diagnoses Need Multidisciplinary Care?
- **Needing multidisciplinary care and needing lifelong care are separate questions.** Single-system conditions such as **bladder exstrophy or severe hypospadias** may be managed by a urologist alone, but care depends on understanding the condition's subtleties.
- **Adult urologists are more subspecialised** than paediatric urologists; a stone specialist may not be comfortable with reconstruction or fertility, so subspecialist support is common. **Multidisciplinary surgery** may be needed (gynaecology, colorectal, plastic surgery, urology).

**TRANSIENT VS LONG-TERM MULTIDISCIPLINARY CARE**

| Transient | Long-term |
|---|---|
| Single-system condition; input for a specific episode or operation | **Multisystem** concerns |
| e.g. a joint reconstructive operation | Most complex: **neurological disorders (SB)**, involving orthopaedics, neurosurgery, physiotherapy, urology and colorectal surgery |

- **Psychological care** may be needed for any condition. **Social workers and care coordinators** address housing, employment, transport and benefits.
- **The backbone of the service is nursing:** nurses are the front line of crisis management and care coordination.

*Concept — **Lifelong need and multidisciplinary need are independent axes**. A single-system condition can need lifelong specialist care, and the most complex MDT needs come from multisystem neurological disease such as SB.*

> **KEY POINTS (source box)**
> - Preparation for transition should begin in **early adolescence**.
> - These are complex patients; **many need multispecialty care**.
> - **Collaboration between paediatric and adult providers** is vital.
> - **Failed transition leads to negative health outcomes.**
> - Transition websites and tools help a unit develop a good service.

### DEVELOPMENTAL CONSIDERATIONS
- **Factors in the decision to start treatment:** provider advice, **underlying risk (e.g. general anaesthesia)**, culture and religion, ease of access, and family resources (financial, intellectual, emotional). The **urologist is often decisive**.
- **Developmental paediatricians are usually excluded** from timing decisions, even though the emotional and psychological effects persist into adulthood. Urologists have therefore developed **crude criteria**. For example, **augmentation cystoplasty is withheld until the child can help with catheterisation and says they want to be dry**, a judgement left to surgeon and parents alone.
- **Relevance to the adult urologist:**
  - could surgery have been **deferred until the patient could consent**?
  - the risk of **medical post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)** from childhood treatment.

#### Development
- **Before the age of assent, two considerations apply:**
  - surgery can **affect development**, physiologically and/or psychologically;
  - its **impact differs with life stage**, and **earlier intervention may cause less** emotional or physical morbidity.
- **Weak evidence base:** accepted beliefs about the timing of genital surgery (hypospadias repair, circumcision) rest on **mid-20th-century psychoanalytic research**, mainly observational narrative case histories.

**DEVELOPMENTAL STAGES INVOKED FOR SURGICAL TIMING**

| Age | Stage | Surgical implication |
|---|---|---|
| **First year** | **Bonding** with an attentive caregiver; reciprocal emotion and communication | Avoid hospitalisation and **separation** |
| Second year | Exploration guided by the bonded caregiver | — |
| **18–30 months** | **Individuation**: expressing own desires, interacting beyond the nuclear family | — |
| **3–6 years** | **Phallic–oedipal period**: heightened genital awareness and emotional response to genital injury; Freudian anxiety about genital mutilation rises until puberty | Avoid genital surgery |

**RECOMMENDED WINDOWS FOR GENITAL SURGERY**

| Source | Window 1 | Window 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Developmental theory (maternal attachment and genital awareness) | **< 6 months** (before awareness) | **2–3 years** |
| **AAP with a panel of paediatric urologists, 1975** | **< 6 months** | **4th or 5th year of life** |

- **Both agree on < 6 months**; they **differ on the second window**. There is **no universal agreement**.

**ELECTIVE VS SEMI-ELECTIVE OR NON-ELECTIVE SURGERY**

| Elective (e.g. hypospadias repair) | Semi-elective or non-elective (e.g. bladder exstrophy closure) |
|---|---|
| In practice, timing is driven by **starting school, parental anxiety about "fixing" the child, and surgeon availability** more than by developmental theory | Proceed **as soon as it is safe** from an anaesthetic and physiological standpoint |

#### Timing of Surgical Intervention
- **Near adolescence,** major surgery becomes difficult when it **depends on the adolescent's compliance**.
- **Self-management has four components:** **medical adherence, medical responsibility, self-care skills, condition knowledge**. Mastery commonly varies **into the third decade**, which raises the risk of a failed therapeutic relationship.
- **Emerging adults (18–25 years)** are legal adults and make their own medical decisions **unless specifically found to lack capacity**.

*Informed consent vs capacity.*
- **Informed consent:** the right to decide based on the risks, benefits and alternatives. **Emerging adults may legally give or withhold consent.**
- **Capacity:** it may be lacking because of cognitive or developmental disability or incapacitating illness, in which case a **parent or guardian** is involved. **More often, capacity is present but the processing skills for complex decisions are incomplete.**
- **Spina bifida:** usually **normal intelligence and literacy**, but more likely to have deficits in **working memory and executive function**, the domains critical to medical decision-making. A **trusted urologist may be the only reliable bridge** to safe decisions. Recruit extra resources, such as a nurse practitioner, to engage and consent these patients.

#### Medical PTSD and the Psychoemotional Impact of Chronic Care in Childhood
- **Definition:** medical (iatrogenic) PTSD follows **negative or traumatic experiences of medical care**. Chronic childhood illness also has psychoemotional effects on the child and the family.
- **Effects on young-adult decision-making:**
  - **distrust** of providers and **delay in seeking care**
  - **challenging recommendations** or seeking alternative treatments
  - **avoiding responsibility** for their own health, having always had it managed by others
  - anxiety, panic attacks and reliving past events, **especially around invasive procedures**
- **Evidence:** **no English-language studies** report the incidence or impact of medical PTSD in **adults with congenital urological conditions**. **Congenital heart disease data** suggest two things: risk relates to the **extent of childhood surgical exposure**, and **cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) with psychological support** alongside medical treatment may reduce the long-term impact.

*Concept — Childhood surgical timing has been **governed by weak psychoanalytic theory and practical logistics rather than evidence**. The adult urologist inherits the psychological consequences, so the recurring question is whether the decision could have waited for the patient's own consent.*

### THE PRACTICALITIES: SETTING UP A SERVICE

#### Training
- **There is no defined training pathway.** Paediatric urologists believe they are best suited to this care, and adult reconstructive urologists believe the same of themselves.
- Most providers train in **both paediatric and reconstructive urology**. Transitional sessions at urology conferences are increasing.

#### The Multidisciplinary Team

**MULTIDISCIPLINARY TEAM (MDT) MEMBERSHIP**

| Core | Likely needed | Available as needed |
|---|---|---|
| Paediatric urologist | Colorectal surgeon | Fertility specialist |
| Transitional/adult urologist | Gynaecologist | Orthopaedic surgeon |
| **Specialist nurses** | Rehabilitation specialist | Endocrinologist |
| **Complex-care coordinator** | Social worker | Geneticist |
| **Nephrologist** | Transplant team | |
| **Psychologist** | Neurosurgeon, plastic surgeon | |

- **Building relationships with key specialties is essential, major work** and determines the service's long-term success.

#### Institutional and Political Support
- A dedicated clinic is **impossible without institutional commitment**. These patients need extra clinic time, resources and multispecialty visits, and are **underserved under fee-for-service** insurance.
- **Build the case** with a **business model**: patient numbers and types, and projected resource needs. **Patient and family feedback** to the institution is compelling.

**FIG. 65.3 TIPS FOR HELPING EMERGING ADULTS TRANSITION TO ADULT CARE** *(data from American Academy of Pediatrics, American Academy of Family Physicians, American College of Physicians, et al.: Supporting the health care transition from adolescence to adulthood in the medical home, Pediatrics, 2011)*

| Tip | Content |
|---|---|
| 1. **Decision-making responsibility** | The **patient is responsible** for health-care decisions (starting or continuing therapy, timing and type of invasive procedures), supported by family, friends and the team |
| 2. **Self-management skills** | Praise progress: appointments, prescriptions, web-based electronic medical record (EMR) use. Where the patient struggles, involve a social worker, nurse or family |
| 3. **Consent and confidentiality** | Review the policies at the first visit, **with family present and again after they leave**, so the patient can say privately what may be discussed in front of family |
| 4. **How the practice operates** | Adult practices rely on **web-based EMR communication** and have **fewer ancillary and navigation staff**. Explain how to get questions answered and appointments booked |
| 5. **Routine and after-hours care** | Explain appropriate use of emergency and urgent care; widen access to nurse visits; **standing orders** for routine needs (e.g. urine cultures) to avoid unnecessary ED visits |

- **Purpose:** the figure lists what an **adult practice must explain explicitly** that a paediatric practice did implicitly: who decides, confidentiality, and how to reach help.
- **Trap:** confidentiality is discussed **twice**, first with family present and then alone.

#### Barriers to Transition *(items 1–2; items 3–4 continue on p. 1354)*

**BARRIERS TO TRANSITION AND THEIR SOLUTIONS**

| Level | Barrier | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Patient/parent** | Parents **wedded to the paediatric provider**; patient disengaged; **fear of unknown facilities, systems and providers**; loss of a beloved team | **Build independence early**: address the child directly, teach them to describe their diagnosis, involve them in decisions; **readiness questionnaires** to document progress |

| **2b. Adult provider** | **Biggest barrier: unfamiliarity** with complex congenital conditions (not taught in residency); **financial disincentives**; extra resource needs | **Partnerships and networks**; additional training or reliance on others' expertise |

*Concept — A transitional service rests on **relationships and institutional backing, not on a training pathway**. Each barrier is a failure of **familiarity** (patient with the adult system, adult urologist with the condition) or of **funding**. The concept may be revisited once barriers 3–4 arrive.*

### The numbers to lock in — Instalment 2
- **Emerging adults: 18–25 years.** They are legal adults and consent for themselves unless they lack capacity.
- **Self-management has four components:** adherence, responsibility, self-care skills, condition knowledge. Mastery varies **into the third decade**.
- **Surgical timing windows:**
  - **< 6 months** is common to both sources;
  - developmental theory adds **2–3 years**;
  - **AAP 1975** adds the **4th–5th year**.
- **Developmental milestones:** bonding in the **first year**; **individuation 18–30 months**; **phallic–oedipal period 3–6 years**.
- **Criterion for augmentation cystoplasty:** the child can **help with catheterisation** and **wants to be dry**.
- **Spina bifida:** normal IQ and literacy, but deficits in **working memory and executive function**.
- **Backbone of the MDT: nursing.** Core MDT: paediatric urologist, transitional urologist, specialist nurses, care coordinator, nephrologist, psychologist.
- **Most complex MDT needs:** neurological disease (**SB**).
- **Four referral routes:** 1 and 3 ideal; 2 suboptimal; **4 (emergency after discharge) worst**.
- **Biggest barrier for adult providers:** **unfamiliarity with the congenital condition**.
- **Medical PTSD:** **no English-language data** in adult congenital urology; evidence is extrapolated from congenital heart disease (**CBT and psychological support** mitigate).
- **Classic traps:**
  - Needing an MDT ≠ needing lifelong care. **Single-system conditions (exstrophy, severe hypospadias) can still need lifelong urological care.**
  - Referral route 3 (new adolescent problem) is **ideal** despite no transition.
  - **The lifelong paediatric urologist model (1a)** suits limited life expectancy. It fails when the patient outlives the provider's career.
  - For elective genital surgery, timing in practice follows **school start, parental anxiety and surgeon availability**, not developmental theory.
  - Capacity is usually **present but incomplete**. Consent then needs extra support; it is not taken over by the parent.
  - Confidentiality is reviewed **twice**: with family, then alone.
