Chapter 65 — Adolescent and Transitional Urology

What Constitutes Adolescent Urology?

  • Origin: the recognition that patients born with complex urogenital conditions need long-term adult care.
  • Evolution: from a stepwise paediatric → adolescent → adult sequence to seamless lifelong care. Adolescent urology is the bridge between paediatric and adult urology.
  • Adult reconstructive urologists can perform some of the operations but are underequipped to care for the whole patient, who brings:
    • multiple previous operations;
    • an anatomical, psychological and physiological landscape altered by the condition and prior surgery;
    • lifelong contact with health care, so patient and parents are often expert in the condition and unforgiving of a provider who is not;
    • expectations formed in a paediatric hospital, which must be managed for the adult relationship to succeed.
  • Many are underserved in adulthood. Clinical and inter-centre collaboration is needed to publish meaningful outcome data.
  • Role of the adolescent urologist: inform and recruit adult urologists into the field; publish and discuss outcomes with paediatric colleagues.

Concept — The gap is in whole-patient knowledge, not operative skill. Childhood reconstruction creates an adult whose anatomy, history and expectations differ from those of any other adult patient.

Definition of Transition

  • Got Transition definition: the move from a child/family-centred to an adult/patient-centred model of care, with or without a change of clinician. It has three parts: planning → transfer → integration.
  • Goals: (1) improve young people's ability to manage their own health care and use services effectively; (2) an organised process in both paediatric and adult practices covering preparation, transfer and integration.
  • In practice: independence in the care environment, education about the condition and its treatment, and navigation of adult care.
  • Transition vs transfer: transition is the whole process; transfer is one step within it and need not involve a new clinician.

Why Is Transition Needed?

  • Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) report: on first reading, transition care in its present form does not appear to make a significant difference.
  • But robust, reproducible metrics are lacking. Renal function and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) are expected to worsen with age, so stability is success, not improvement. Better metrics are needed.
  • Consistent finding: fewer patients are lost to follow-up when actively transitioned to a capable adult service.
  • Avoid the word "normal": the patient is their own baseline. Comparing an adult born with exstrophy to "normal" is meaningless and may stigmatise. Acknowledge the patient's wish for normality with sensitivity in order to gain trust.

Concept — The evidence for transition looks weak because the wrong endpoint is being measured. In progressive congenital disease, stability and retention in follow-up are the outcome.

Who Needs Transition?

  • Congenital and childhood-acquired conditions both carry risk from the condition or its prior treatment, so most patients need adult care.

What Happens When Transition Fails?

  • Begin discussions in early adolescence, individualised to the patient's developmental and physical needs.
  • Risks of non-transition: a young adult who does not understand their condition; overbearing family control; loss to follow-up; unrecognised complications; reliance on emergency care; missing social and peer support.
  • Net result: non-adherence, more emergency department (ED) attendances and hospitalisations, higher morbidity and mortality, and family dissatisfaction.

Concept — Failed transition does its harm through loss of preventive surveillance. The patient reappears only through the emergency department, after complications that could have been prevented.

Stakeholders and Their Roles

Patient

  • The primary stakeholder. The patient must be medically and physically stable for handover, so avoid transition during a crisis such as deteriorating renal function or a need for urgent surgery.
  • Exception, pregnancy: obstetric care lies outside a paediatric hospital, so transfer is expedited, with co-management by paediatric and adult teams. This keeps the familiar team involved and shows the teams working together.

Parents

  • Handing decision-making to their child can overwhelm parents who have managed care since infancy: pride, a need to show trust, and fear. Separating from a trusted provider is itself a barrier.
  • Create private time for the patient alone, especially for sex, sexual function and sexual debut. Then ask whether the patient wants the conversation shared with their parents. These conversations are often omitted; in some cohorts most patients had never discussed sex with a provider.

  • Access to adult care is difficult for young adults with complex chronic conditions. The conditions are rare and heterogeneous, so their financial benefit is hard to quantify. When to Begin and Complete the Process

  • Begin: the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends about age 12. Content widens through the teens to cover diagnosis, past decisions, treatments and future considerations.
  • Practical skills: booking appointments themselves, navigating insurance, knowing whom to call, and what to do in an emergency. The most important element is teaching how and where to find valid information. Written questions and a portable health record help.
  • Complete: a paediatric setting becomes inappropriate for most patients by the late teens to early 20s. Age 26 is the commonly cited latest age for completion, being the upper age for cover on a parent's insurance plan in the United States.
  • Where independent living is not achievable (cognitive or physical limitation), the setting is a matter of judgement and needs ongoing reassessment. Plan medical custodianship (for example, a sibling as parents age or die) and involve future carers in discussions.

Available Tools

  • Programmes: Good 2 Go (Toronto SickKids), Ready Steady Go (UK), Got Transition (US; six core elements, Fig. 65.2). The best programme is the one that fits the unit.

Concept — Transition is a process started by the paediatric team and completed in the adult setting. It begins at about 12, is tracked through adolescence, and ends only when integration into adult care is confirmed, not at the last paediatric appointment.

Multidisciplinary Care Models: Which Diagnoses Need Multidisciplinary Care?

  • The need for multidisciplinary care does not define the need for lifelong care. Single-system conditions (bladder exstrophy, severe hypospadias) may be managed by a urologist, but care depends on understanding their subtleties.
  • Adult urologists are more subspecialised, so subspecialist support and multidisciplinary surgery are common.

Key Points
  • Preparation for transition should begin in early adolescence.
  • These are complex patients; many need multispecialty care.
  • Collaboration between paediatric and adult providers is vital.
  • Failed transition leads to negative health outcomes.
  • Transition websites and tools help a unit develop a good service.

Developmental Considerations

  • Factors in the decision to start treatment: provider advice, underlying risk (e.g. general anaesthesia), culture and religion, ease of access, and family resources (financial, intellectual, emotional). The urologist is often decisive.
  • Developmental paediatricians are usually excluded from timing decisions, although the emotional and psychological effects persist into adulthood. Urologists have therefore developed crude criteria. For example, augmentation cystoplasty is withheld until the child can help with catheterisation and says they want to be dry, a judgement left to surgeon and parents alone.
  • Adult relevance: could surgery have waited for consent, and what is the risk of medical post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)?

Development

  • Before the age of assent, two considerations apply: surgery can affect development, physiologically and/or psychologically; and its impact differs with life stage, so earlier intervention may cause less emotional or physical morbidity.
  • Weak evidence base: accepted beliefs about the timing of genital surgery (hypospadias repair, circumcision) rest on mid-20th-century psychoanalytic research, mainly observational narrative case histories.

Timing of Surgical Intervention

  • Near adolescence, major surgery becomes difficult when it depends on the adolescent's compliance.
  • Self-management has four components: medical adherence, medical responsibility, self-care skills, condition knowledge. Mastery commonly varies into the third decade, which raises the risk of a failed therapeutic relationship.
  • Emerging adults (18–25 years) are legal adults and make their own medical decisions unless specifically found to lack capacity.
  • Informed consent: the right to decide based on risks, benefits and alternatives. Emerging adults may legally give or withhold consent.
  • Capacity: may be lacking because of cognitive or developmental disability or incapacitating illness, in which case a parent or guardian is involved. More often, capacity is present but the processing skills for complex decisions are incomplete.
  • Spina bifida: usually normal intelligence and literacy, but more likely to have deficits in working memory and executive function, the domains critical to medical decision-making. A trusted urologist may be the only reliable bridge to safe decisions. Recruit extra resources, such as a nurse practitioner, to engage and consent these patients.

Medical PTSD and the Psychoemotional Impact of Chronic Care in Childhood

  • Definition: medical (iatrogenic) PTSD follows negative or traumatic experiences of medical care. Chronic childhood illness also has psychoemotional effects on the child and family.
  • Effects on young-adult decision-making:
    • distrust of providers and delay in seeking care;
    • challenging recommendations or seeking alternative treatments;
    • avoiding responsibility for their own health, having always had it managed by others;
    • anxiety, panic attacks and reliving past events, especially around invasive procedures.
  • Evidence: no English-language studies report the incidence or impact of medical PTSD in adults with congenital urological conditions. Congenital heart disease data suggest that risk relates to childhood surgical exposure, and that cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) with psychological support alongside medical treatment may reduce the long-term impact.

Concept — Childhood surgical timing has been governed by weak psychoanalytic theory and practical logistics rather than evidence. The adult urologist inherits the psychological consequences, so the recurring question is whether the decision could have waited for the patient's own consent.

The Practicalities: Setting Up a Service

Training

  • There is no defined training pathway. Paediatric urologists believe they are best suited to this care; adult reconstructive urologists believe the same of themselves.
  • Most providers train in both paediatric and reconstructive urology. Transitional sessions at urology conferences are increasing.

Concept — A transitional service rests on relationships and institutional backing, not on a training pathway. Each barrier is a failure of familiarity (patient with the adult system, adult urologist with the condition) or of funding (institution, health system).

Geographic Considerations

  • Volume drives quality, so services are centralised, at the cost of travel. Telehealth helps, but in the US faces payment issues and dual licensing across state lines, and allows no examination.
  • Ideal: hub-and-spoke, a full specialist hub networked with local providers.

Developing a Plan That Fits; Billing

  • No established model. Drivers: the children's hospital's upper age limit, clinician pay, and where adult colleagues work. Needs protected clinic time, a designated institutional lead, a defined access route (call centre or care coordinator) and agreed clinic privileges.
  • Remuneration favours procedures over complexity, coordination and time; complex perioperative risk is not paid in proportion. Many patients are under-insured (underemployment, financial toxicity of chronic illness): involve case managers, social workers and financial counsellors.

Measuring Success in Transition

  • The AHRQ report concedes there is no definition of effective transition.
  • Proposed metric: one attended post-transfer appointment. Measurable, but not necessarily a durable health outcome.
  • Failed transition raises loss to follow-up and ED use; no study has yet shown a benefit of transition to overall health or quality of life.
Key Points
  • Train in both paediatric and adult reconstructive urology; build the team by direct contact.
  • Managing patient and parent barriers is a core skill; specialist nurses are among the most vital members.
  • Work with management to overcome systemic barriers.

Prior Male Urethral Reconstruction

Hypospadias, epispadias, VACTERL association.

Surveillance

  • Adult hypospadias repair complications: 10.1–37.5 % first repair; 27.5–63.6 % after a failed repair.
  • After paediatric repair, young adults report: cosmetic dissatisfaction 17–36 % (about one-third), curvature ~12 %, erectile dysfunction ~19 %. Proximal: about half revised by puberty (median 16.5 years).
  • No agreed follow-up duration (surgeon survey: 2 years, 5 years, puberty or adulthood, each roughly a quarter). After proximal repair, follow into adult life.
  • Bladder exstrophy–epispadias complex (BEEC): incompetent bladder neck, short phallus with dorsal chordee, genital concerns limiting partners. Follow up every 1–2 years.

Persistent or Recurrent Chordee

  • Recurs years or decades later, more after proximal repair. Proximal meta-analysis: ~25 % after dorsal plication alone vs 5 % after ventral lengthening.
  • BEEC: dorsal chordee almost universal, with torsion or partial glans loss (Fig. 65.4: phallic insufficiency with torsion; classic dorsal chordee). Full correction rarely possible (pubic diastasis, corporal disproportion): set realistic goals.
  • Length is the main BEEC concern. Phalloplasty with staged prosthesis: adequate satisfaction, ~50 % complications (mainly implant); most can orgasm.

Fertility

  • Mechanisms: impaired semen flow; retrograde urine and infection into prostatic and ejaculatory ducts; short penis or ectopic meatus; prior orchidopexy or orchidectomy for undescended testis (UDT).
  • Many have never had fertility discussed. Offer one or two semen analyses, plus gonadotrophins if testicular failure is suspected.

Concept — After male genital reconstruction, distress is cumulative: the number of operations predicts it, not the original severity. Fertility is often never raised, so ask explicitly.

Prior Female Genital Reconstruction (BEEC, Cloaca)

Surveillance

  • No society guidelines, but follow through puberty into adulthood: genital malformations affect menarche, sexual function, fertility and pelvic health.
  • After cloacal repair: 46 % void spontaneously, 23 % incontinent, haematometra 38 %, primary amenorrhoea 25 %.
  • Cloaca: CKD in ~half, 17 % reach end-stage renal disease (ESRD); solitary kidney plus reflux is highest risk. Renal function and urine protein at least annually; pre-conception referral to high-risk obstetrics and nephrology.
  • Müllerian and renal anomalies present as dysmenorrhoea or pelvic pain (Fig. 65.5: age 26, solitary kidney with a remnant ureter draining into the vaginal apex).

Sexual Function

  • BEEC: dyspareunia in half; one-third meet criteria for dysfunction, yet 78 % satisfied. Short vagina, introital stenosis; pain introital or cervical, worse with prolapse.
  • Cloaca after vaginal mobilisation: worse; only 42 % sexually active.
  • Screen all, with or without vaginoplasty; early pelvic floor physiotherapy for reactive vaginismus.

Fertility and Pregnancy

  • BEEC hypofertility: 68 % of those trying conceived, often after > 1 year or with ART; only 21 % without ART in year 1. Anorectal malformation (ARM): Müllerian anomalies in over half, commonest uterus didelphys; at least half after complex vaginal reconstruction need ART.
  • BEEC pregnancy: loss ~20 %, fetal loss 5–7 %, more preterm birth. Intermittent self-catheterisation (ISC) fails as the uterus grows (indwelling catheter or nephrostomy); urinary tract infection (UTI) in one-third to two-thirds with supravesical reconstruction. Wide pelvis → breech.
  • Vaginal delivery after perineal reconstruction: high rates of third- and fourth-degree tears.
  • Reflux nephropathy in pregnancy: hypertension odds ratio (OR) 5.55, pre-eclampsia OR 6.04, miscarriage 11–23 %. Permanent renal loss risk: renal scars, hypertension or moderate-to-severe proteinuria; co-manage with nephrology.

Pelvic Organ Prolapse (POP)

  • Mechanism: compromised levator ani, wider and longer hiatus, deficient cardinal ligament.
  • 18–52 %, from late adolescence, with or without pregnancy; acute postpartum prolapse in half.
  • In pregnancy: bed rest and pessary. Surgical series are small, recurrence high, no consensus: experienced prolapse surgeons only.
Key Points
  • Female ARM and BEEC often include genital malformations.
  • Co-manage pregnancy with maternal–fetal medicine, urology ± nephrology.
  • Offer elective caesarean after bladder reconstruction.

Concept — In females with BEEC or cloaca, pelvic anatomy dictates adult care: a deficient pelvic floor gives prolapse, Müllerian and renal anomalies give pain and renal risk, and a reconstructed outlet makes planned caesarean the safe route.

Bladder Reconstruction and Urinary Diversion

Augmentation or Substitution Cystoplasty

  • Late stones, contraction, sacculation. The segment used is often unknown; useful, rarely essential. A cystogram shows emptying, shape, urethra and bladder neck.
  • Ileum: annual vitamin B12 from 5–10 years after augmentation; deficiency is irreversible and masked in SB. Check bicarbonate and chloride for hyperchloraemic acidosis (ileum, colon).
  • Urine pregnancy tests: 57–75 % false positive. Use serum human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG) before teratogenic drugs or procedures.
  • Perforation 0.8–13 % (overdistension, anastomotic ischaemia), linked to substance misuse and missed catheterisation; mortality up to 25 % if diagnosis is delayed. Friable tissue: wide drainage with large-bore catheters. With a ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS), involve neurosurgery and infectious diseases.

Continent Diversion

  • Appendicovesicostomy beats Monti–Yang: ~50 % lower revision. Skin-level stenosis commonest; fascial or valve stenosis presents with perforation.
  • Cannot catheterise: usually overdistension kinking the channel. Drain per urethra or with a floppy-tipped catheter; else percutaneous aspiration (16–18 G), best through the old suprapubic tube site.
  • False passage: endoscopic or fluoroscopic dilatation over a wire, catheter for a few days; then revision. Chronic difficulty (dexterity, obesity, cognition, support): consider incontinent diversion or suprapubic tube.

  • Creates a fixed outlet obstruction. BEEC after BNR: 59 % void per urethra; 64 % of these continent.

Concept — Bowel in the urinary tract brings a fixed list of lifelong risks: B12, acid–base, false-positive urine hCG, perforation, stones, channel stenosis. Surveillance is the annual checklist of that list.

Surveillance Recommendations

Nephrologic Surveillance

  • Risk depends on whether bladder and kidneys are both affected, childhood insults, and comorbidity (diabetes, hypertension).
  • Congenital obstructive nephropathy: highest risk. Puberty is high-risk in congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT). Posterior urethral valves (PUV) progress in late adolescence, just when patients disengage.
  • SB: CKD in one-quarter to one-half; only 62 % of registry patients had creatinine and RBUS within 2 years.

Imaging

  • Annual ultrasound with any upper-tract dilatation or lower-tract dysfunction or reconstruction. Increasing dilatation: video urodynamics and/or MAG3 (mercaptoacetyltriglycine) renogram.
  • "Tempered" MAG3: catheter in place if the bladder or VUR may cause the dilatation; not routine in adults, so request it.

Urinary Tract Infections

  • Bacteriuria in 50–100 % on intermittent catheterisation; treat only symptomatic bacteriuria. Learn each patient's own signs.
  • Prevention: irrigation ≥ 240 mL, good fluid intake, catheter technique and adherence.
  • Same organism repeatedly → uncleared source (stone, diverticulum); different organisms → reinoculation. RBUS with post-void imaging.
  • New symptoms: culture, more fluid and irrigation; self-start antibiotics after culture for rapid deteriorators. > 4 infections a year → investigate.

Long-Term Risks

Malignancy

  • Congenital neurogenic bladder raises urothelial cancer risk, with or without bowel; also exstrophy, immunosuppression, inflammation, stones, haematuria–dysuria syndrome.
  • Ileocystoplasty ~1.2 %; gastrocystoplasty 2.8–10.3 % (2–10-fold). Mixed histology; latency 10–20 years; nearly always fatal.
  • Annual screening cystoscopy is not cost-effective or accurate. Cystoscopy for symptoms (pain, haematuria, frequent UTI) or a filling defect.

Stones

  • Up to 40 %, more in substitution; 2 % with dependent, complete drainage. Daily high-volume flushes reduce them. Many adults use 10–12 Fr catheters, too small to irrigate.

Channels and Stomas

  • Mitrofanoff revision 30–50 %, mainly stenosis (skin level) and leakage; weight gain and tortuosity add difficulty. Examine in the patient's usual position (e.g. wheelchair).
  • Skin-level stenosis: indwelling catheter for a few days or a night-time "L-stent" (Fig. 65.6: the patient's catheter, bent and taped into a Mitrofanoff or Malone antegrade continence enema (MACE) channel to dilate it). Then excision with advancement flap or oral mucosa.
  • Conduit stoma complications 18–35 %. Digital examination localises stenosis relative to fascia. Retention → loopogram; hernia planning → CT; nephrostomy or equivocal loopogram → antegrade nephrostogram.
  • Appliance leakage: stoma nurses first; they may avoid surgery and mark the revision site.
  • Metabolic: acidosis (alkalosis with gastric segments) and B12: annual bloods.
  • Abdominal wall: SB, BEEC and Eagle–Barrett predispose to hernias; high recurrence, so involve wall specialists. In multiply operated patients, almost any procedure carries a 91.5 % complication risk (mostly minor).
  • Change in reservoir function: shorter dry interval, new enuresis, or new continence (hostile bladder behind a tighter outlet) → urodynamics ± voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG). No routine adult urodynamics; use for upper-tract change, incontinence or hypercontinence, escalating UTI.
Key Points
  • Treat symptomatic bacteriuria early. Collaborate on complex problems.
  • Changes in continence require investigation.

Concept — In a reconstructed tract, change is the signal. A new pattern of continence, infection or dilatation prompts investigation; stable bacteriuria does not.

Progression to ESRD and Renal Replacement

  • CAKUT: 34–43 % of childhood ESRD. PUV: 23 % in ESRD by median age 31.
  • SB patients are transplanted less often (P < 0.001) despite comparable survival on renal replacement therapy (RRT): complexity, transplant teams' uncertainty about the bladder, patients "worn out".
  • Psychosocial: patients lost in childhood reappear with severe renal failure; family guilt, blame and denial; more depression (a driver of non-adherence); carer distress.

Management of Polyuria

  • Most marked in PUV: up to one-quarter lose function after puberty; up to 75 % have instability, myogenic failure or poor compliance after adolescence; polyuria may produce a "VURD" kidney (VUR with renal dysplasia).
  • Double voiding; mainstay: overnight indwelling catheter. Native nephrectomy is discouraged: residual urine eases dialysis and blood pressure. Overactivity: behavioural → anticholinergic or β3 agonist → botulinum toxin → augmentation.
  • Before any surgery, plan future graft sites so incisions, ports and channels leave room.
  • Bladder re-evaluation at listing is mandatory. Small, poorly compliant → augment before graft. Myogenic failure → reliable emptying (Mitrofanoff): inconsistent catheterisation causes graft failure.

Native Kidneys and Peritoneal Dialysis (PD)

  • Native nephrectomy indications: recurrent pyelonephritis, recurrent stones, malignant hypertension, chronic flank pain (reflux), severe nephrotic syndrome. Pyelonephritis often starts after transplant (Fig. 65.8: ileal conduit, native emphysematous pyelitis with reflux into the native ureter; nephroureterectomy). Massive reflux: remove the whole collecting system, leaving no orphan stump.
  • PD relative contraindications: dense adhesions, VPS. Channels and stomas raise infection concern (small SB series: no excess, underpowered). VPS problem on PD → non-peritoneal shunt drainage. Poor sensation delays peritonitis diagnosis: act early on cloudy effluent.

Transplant Considerations

  • Transplant is the preferred RRT. Neuropathic bladder work-up: cystoscopy and video urodynamics (or urodynamics plus VCUG). Patient must show willingness to do bladder self-care.
  • Pyelonephritis 0.49 vs 0.31–0.44 episodes per patient-year; graft survival 92.3 % at 5 years, similar to general recipients.
