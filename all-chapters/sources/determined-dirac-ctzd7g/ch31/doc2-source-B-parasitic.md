### PARASITIC INFECTIONS OF THE UROGENITAL TRACT

- **Parasites are seen rarely in non-endemic practice, but the urologist must recognise them** to diagnose and treat them correctly.
- **Parasites relevant to urology:** **UG schistosomiasis, filariasis, amoebiasis, enterobiasis and echinococcosis**.

#### Schistosomiasis

- **More than 200 million people are infected with *Schistosoma* species.**

**THE THREE MEDICALLY IMPORTANT SCHISTOSOMES**

| Species | Distribution | Principal target | Snail host |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***S. haematobium*** | **Africa, Arabian Peninsula** | **Urogenital tract** | ***Bulinus*** |
| *S. mansoni* | Africa, Arabian Peninsula, **South America** | Liver, GI tract | *Biomphalaria* |
| *S. japonicum* | **China, South-East Asia** | Liver, GI tract | *Oncomelania* |

- **UG schistosomiasis is the *S. haematobium* disease.** It involves a complex life cycle, a disease with many manifestations, and close ties to the environment.
- **It has co-evolved with humans and non-human primates for millennia**, so even ancient civilisations recognised the syndrome.

##### History

- **Schistosome antigens have been found in Egyptian mummies (c. 3500 BCE)**, and ***S. haematobium* eggs** in the tissues of later mummies.
- **The Egyptians named it "A-a-a disease."** The hieroglyph shows a **penis dripping blood-stained urine**.
- **Theodor Bilharz (1852, Cairo autopsies)** found worms in the **mesenteric veins** and linked them to **eggs in urine and stool**. This is the origin of the term "bilharzia".

##### Biology and Life Cycle

- **The human cycle follows a sequence:**
  - **Cercariae penetrate skin (even intact skin)** in contact with infested fresh water. Cercariae live about **1 day** and lose infectivity **within hours** of release from the snail.
  - They **shed their tails** over about **90–120 minutes** → become **schistosomulae**.
  - Schistosomulae migrate through blood or lymph **to the lungs** (several weeks) → through the venous circulation **to the liver sinusoids**, where they **begin feeding on blood**.
  - **Adults migrate to the vesical and pelvic venous plexus** and live on average **3–5 years**. Development from cercaria to adult takes **80–110 days**.
  - **Pairing:** the **male holds the female in the gynaecophoric canal**, helps her pump blood and **chemically stimulates egg-laying**.
- **Eggs:**
  - **Fecundity** is very high: hundreds to thousands of eggs per female per day.
  - ***S. haematobium* eggs are oval, about 140 µm long, with a terminal spine** (Fig. 31.9).
  - Eggs must **cross the endothelium and the bladder wall** to reach the urine. Some pass through the gut wall into faeces.
  - **Fewer than half of eggs are excreted.** **Eggs retained in tissue drive the pathology.**
  - Eggs survive about **20 days if kept wet**.
- **The environmental cycle follows a sequence:**
  - **Contact with fresh water and light** → eggs hatch → **ciliated miracidia**, which live about **6 hours**.
  - Miracidia penetrate ***Bulinus*** snails. *Bulinus* prefers **slow-flowing water** and tolerates **low oxygen**.
  - **Primary sporocyst → 20–40 daughter sporocysts → 200–400 cercariae per sporocyst**, released into water.
  - **The prepatent period depends on temperature. No cercariae are shed below 15 °C or above 35 °C.**

**FIG. 31.8** Life cycle of a schistosome. *Source: King CH, Schistosomiasis, in Tropical Infectious Diseases, 2006.* The figure adds the following points not in the text:
- Cercariae are forked-tailed (about 500 µm) and cause **"swimmer's itch"** on penetration.
- Adults are **1–3 cm long** and pair for life.
- **Eggs measure 70–170 µm** across species; miracidia hatch within 1–3 hours.
- **Each species has its own venous destination and snail:**
  - ***S. haematobium* → vesical venous plexus**: haematuria, bacterial UTI, scarring, calcification, **SCC**.
  - ***S. mansoni* and *S. japonicum* → mesenteric venules of bowel and rectum**: **Katayama fever (especially *S. japonicum*)**, presinusoidal granulomas, **Symmers pipestem periportal fibrosis**, portal hypertension.
  - All species occasionally cause **embolic egg granulomas in the brain or spinal cord**.

##### Epidemiology

- **Distribution follows the snail host:** **most of sub-Saharan Africa**, parts of **North Africa and the Middle East**.
- **Europe:**
  - **Corsica, 2013:** the **first autochthonous transmission in Europe for over a century**. **Further cases in 2015** suggest a new endemic focus.
  - Some infections were ***S. bovis* or *S. haematobium–S. bovis* hybrids**.
- **Burden:**
  - More than 200 million infected, **mostly with the UG form**. Newer estimates reach **440 million**.
  - **Up to 150,000 deaths a year from *S. haematobium* obstructive renal failure alone.**
  - In sub-Saharan Africa, tens of millions have haematuria and dysuria at any time, with **major bladder pathology and hydronephrosis in millions**.
- **Individual risk depends on the type and duration of freshwater contact:**
  - **Rural women and children:** domestic chores (laundry, dishwashing).
  - **Children:** play and swimming.
  - **Adults:** fishing, car washing, agriculture with **water-intensive crops (rice, sugar cane)**.
  - **Prevalence is highly focal**, because transmission depends on water.
- **Dams and irrigation increase transmission.** They create **year-round slow-flowing habitats for snails** and draw in **denser farming populations**.

**DAMS AND SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| Dam | Decade | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Aswan Low Dam, Egypt** | **1930s** | Flood irrigation converted to perennial irrigation → prevalence rose from **< 11% to > 75%** in some regions |
| **Aswan High Dam, Egypt** | 1960s | Similar rise |
| **Diama Dam, West Africa** | 1980s | Similar rise |

- **Highest prevalence:** communities with **poor sanitation and no clean running water**.
- **Children aged 5–15 carry the heaviest worm burden.** It is unclear whether this reflects exposure or susceptibility.
- **Schistosomiasis and poverty reinforce each other:**
  - **The snail hosts live in tropical and subtropical developing countries.**
  - **Poor sanitation** puts eggs into surface water.
  - **No safe water supply** means prolonged contact with water.
  - **Chronic infection impairs growth, learning and productivity**, which perpetuates poverty.

*Concept —* **Schistosomiasis is an environmental disease.** Transmission needs human excreta in fresh water, a particular snail and prolonged contact with water. Every feature of its epidemiology (focality, children, dams, poverty) is one of those three links, and so is every method of control.



##### Pathogenesis and Pathology

- **Cercarial penetration** is aided by secreted **proteases**, which trigger cellular and humoral responses. However, the parasite **modulates host immunity from first contact**, so brief penetration causes **only local skin inflammation**. **Repeated exposure → hypersensitivity → maculopapular rash.**
- **Immune evasion by the tegument:** the maturing schistosomulum forms a **double lipid bilayer surface (the tegument)**, which allows it to survive for years. Mechanisms include:
  - **Host antigen mimicry**
  - **Continuous membrane turnover**
  - **Immunomodulatory proteins and proteases**
  - **Evasive biophysical properties of the surface**
  - **Modulation of surface-antigen expression**
- **Most knowledge comes from autopsy studies and animal models.**

**WORM VS EGG — THE GOVERNING CONTRAST**

| Adult worm | Egg |
|---|---|
| **Immunologically near-silent** (tegumental evasion) | **The main immunopathological trigger** |
| Lives in the venous plexus | Deposited in the **walls of the bladder and pelvic organs** |
| Causes little disease directly | **Granuloma → fibrosis → obstruction of blood or urine flow** |

- **The egg granuloma:**
  - A **mixed infiltrate of eosinophils, plasma cells and lymphocytes**.
  - **Continuous egg-laying means granulomas of every stage coexist.** Eggs deposited in clusters form **composite, coalescing granulomas**.
  - Grossly, these form **bulky, hyperaemic, polypoid masses** projecting into the bladder (Fig. 31.10).
- **Modifiers of severity:** **host genetics, in utero sensitisation, and co-infection**.
- ***S. haematobium* eggs rapidly induce type 2 inflammatory genes and suppress urothelial barrier genes.** This suggests that **host and parasite share the aim of expelling eggs** across a temporarily weakened urothelium.

**FIG. 31.10** IVU showing **scalloping of the bladder and right lower ureter** by schistosomal polypoid lesions.

- **Ectopic egg deposition:**
  - **Portal tract:** a heavy load of eggs swept to the liver blocks **presinusoidal capillaries** → granulomas → **portal hypertension**. Embolised eggs cause **granulomatous, fibrotic portal tracts** and **dilated portosystemic collaterals** → **pipestem (Symmers) fibrosis** → **hepatosplenomegaly**. Susceptibility depends on the individual immune response.
  - **Lung:** worm pairs migrate to the pulmonary vessels, **only in very severe infection** and after pelvic disease is established → **pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale**.
- **Naturally acquired immunity exists.** Some people stay **egg-negative for ≥ 5 years** despite continued exposure and no treatment.

**RESISTANT VS CHRONICALLY INFECTED**

| Resistant to reinfection | Chronically infected |
|---|---|
| **Mixed T-helper 1 (Th1) + Th2 response** | **Th2 response only** |
| Protective **IgE** active | **IgG4 blocks protective IgE** (antibodies to worm and egg antigens) |

- **IgE to worm antigens rises with age**, which suggests immune-mediated resistance. However, **water contact also falls with age**. Analyses that control for exposure still suggest that **age contributes independently**.

- **Calcification and obstructive uropathy follow a causal chain:**
  - **Eggs that are not expelled calcify** (bladder, ureters).
  - This gives **reduced urinary tract compliance → higher upper-tract pressure → stasis, hydroureter, hydronephrosis**.
  - **The extent of calcification on imaging roughly reflects the tissue burden of calcified eggs.**

**LEVEL OF SCHISTOSOMAL URETERIC OBSTRUCTION**

| Level | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Ureteric meatus | ~1% |
| Intramural (interstitial) ureter | 10–30% |
| **Juxtavesical ureter** | **20–60%** |
| Lower third of ureter | 15–50% |
| Contiguous combination | 30–60% |

- **The pattern:** obstruction is **overwhelmingly distal**, from the juxtavesical ureter to the lower third. The meatus itself is rarely the site.

**THREE PATTERNS OF SCHISTOSOMAL HYDROURETER**

| | Segmental (cylindrical or fusiform) | Tonic | Atonic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Share of obstructive uropathy** | **About one-quarter** (**80% in the lower ureter**) | **Up to 30%** | The remainder |
| **Wall** | Dilatation **above concentric muscle replaced by fibrosis and sandy patches** | **Thick-walled, trabeculated; marked muscular hypertrophy**; tortuous | **Thin-walled**; **atrophic, fibrotic muscle**; very tortuous |
| **Peristalsis** | — | **Impaired** | **Absent** |
| **Extent** | Segmental | **Whole ureter above the lesion** (functional stenosis) | Markedly dilated |
| **Hydronephrosis** | **Rarely significant** | **Significant, but reversible** if obstruction is relieved | — |

- **The logic of the table:** tonic = **hypertrophied muscle fighting an obstruction** (reversible). Atonic = **muscle that has failed** (fibrotic, aperistaltic). The trap is to assume that every dilated ureter is irreversibly damaged.

- **Hydroureter precedes hydronephrosis.** Untreated hydronephrosis progresses: **pelvic dilatation → medullary atrophy → medullary effacement → cortical atrophy**. This explains why **tubular function (concentrating ability) is lost before glomerular function**.
- **Bacterial UTI is commoner in chronic infection.** Possible reasons are **bacteria adhering to the worm tegument**, **stasis** and **immunomodulation**.
- **Bladder cancer:**
  - ***S. haematobium* has the strongest link to cancer of any helminth**, with a particular link to **SCC**. Evidence comes from epidemiology (especially **Egypt**) and experimental models.
  - **Risk rises with the duration and severity of infection.**
- **Female genital schistosomiasis (FGS):**
  - Egg sequestration causes **fibrotic nodules ("sandy patches")** in the **uterus, cervix and lower genital tract** (Fig. 31.11).
  - Its mechanism is poorly understood, apart from **increased vascularity of the genital mucosa** around eggs.
- **Male genital schistosomiasis (MGS):**
  - **Cause:** **ectopic eggs** in the **testis, epididymis, spermatic cord, scrotum and penis**.
  - **Poorly characterised**, partly because **transrectal US is needed** to assess the internal sex organs.
  - **Pathology:** **neoplastic growth around lesions** with fibrosis and calcification.
  - **Presentation:** **genital disfigurement, haemospermia, haematuria, infertility, genital pain**. A minority have sexual dysfunction or **urethral discharge mistaken for a sexually transmitted infection**.
  - **Infertility is thought to be reversible** (from epididymitis and funiculitis). **Azoospermia** with **eosinophilic interstitial orchitis** can occur.
  - **Diagnosis: eggs in the ejaculate on microscopy.** However:
    - **Symptoms predict disease poorly.**
    - **Semen egg counts vary between samples** and **correlate poorly with urine counts**.
    - The effect of abstinence is unknown.
  - **Hydrocele and testicular calcification** have been reported, but the causal link is uncertain.
  - **Raised PSA** suggests frequent prostatic involvement. **Serum testosterone is lower** in men with *S. haematobium* or *S. mansoni* infection (mechanism unknown).
  - **MGS, like FGS, may increase susceptibility to HIV.**
  - **Treatment: praziquantel.**
- **Bladder sequelae follow a progression:**
  - **Urothelial hyperplasia → squamous metaplasia → dysplasia → urothelial carcinoma or SCC**. **Bladder cancer is the final pathological sequel.**

**SCHISTOSOMAL VS CONVENTIONAL BLADDER CANCER**

| | Schistosomal bladder cancer | Conventional (non-endemic) bladder cancer |
|---|---|---|
| **Age at onset** | **Early: 40–50 years** | Older |
| **Histology** | **SCC 60–90%**; **adenocarcinoma 5–15%** | Predominantly urothelial (transitional cell) |
| **Differentiation** | **> 40% of SCCs well differentiated, verrucous, with good prognosis** | — |
| **Site** | **Posterior wall about half**; lateral wall about 30% | — |
| **Growth** | **Exophytic about two-thirds**; the rest ulcerative, endophytic | — |

- **Mass drug administration (MDA) in Egypt reduced bladder neoplasms** and caused a **shift from SCC to transitional cell carcinoma**. This is evidence of cause.
- **Smoking**, which is common in endemic regions, may act **synergistically**. However, some autopsy series from the same regions show **similar cancer rates without schistosomiasis**.
- **Carcinogenesis:**
  - **The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC, WHO) classifies *S. haematobium* as a Group 1 ("carcinogenic to humans") agent.**
  - **Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)** rises in the bladder soon after egg exposure. It may promote tumour vasculogenesis and progression.
  - **Proposed pathway:** papillomas merge with basal transitional epithelium → **benign fibroepithelial papillary growths** → repeated inflammation and fibrosis → **clonal, precancerous lesions including squamous metaplasia**.
  - Egg exposure alters **carcinogenesis-related signalling pathways** (mouse bladder).
  - **Co-infecting bacteria convert nitrates to nitrosamines**, which **alkylate DNA and protein**. This leads to mutations in genes such as **p53**.

*Concept —* **In schistosomiasis the worm is tolerated and the egg is not.** Eggs held in the wall give granuloma, then fibrosis and calcification, then obstruction, and eventually metaplasia and cancer. Every urological sequel is the egg's damage accumulating over time.

##### Clinical Manifestations

**ACUTE VS CHRONIC SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| | Acute | Chronic |
|---|---|---|
| **Cause** | Response to **cercarial penetration** and to **migration and maturation** of the worms | **Immune response to prolonged egg-laying** over years |
| **Who** | Often **travellers**, during **primary infection** | **Long-term residents of endemic areas**: repeated reinfection, high worm burden |
| **Outcome** | Self-limiting | **Organ damage** |

- **Schistosomes cannot multiply in the human host.** After a single exposure, **adult worms die of old age within 3–5 years** even without treatment, which limits pathology in visitors.

###### Acute Schistosomiasis

- **Cercarial dermatitis:** an **itchy maculopapular rash within 1–2 days** of penetration. It **often resolves before the traveller returns home**, which makes diagnosis harder.
- **Katayama fever (acute schistosomiasis):**
  - **2–8 weeks after primary infection**, in some patients (many are asymptomatic).
  - **Named after the Katayama Valley, Japan.**
  - Commonest with heavy ***S. japonicum*** infection, less common with *S. mansoni*, **rare with *S. haematobium***.
  - **Features:** **fever, dry cough, fatigue, headache, diarrhoea, eosinophilia**, neck pain, urticaria.
  - **Rare in residents of endemic areas.**
  - **Non-specific**, so it is often missed or mistaken for **malaria or enteric fever**.
- **Because acute infection can be silent, anyone exposed to potentially infested water should be considered for testing and treatment.**

###### Chronic Schistosomiasis

- **Egg-laying into the bladder begins 8–12 weeks after infection.** It may present with **painless, recurrent haematuria**, dysuria or frequency.
  - In highly endemic cultures, **male haematuria is regarded as a sign of puberty**, and it can be heavy enough to cause **anaemia**. **Proteinuria** is common.
  - **Haematuria is consistent and specific enough to be used as a primary diagnostic test in endemic areas.** In **travellers returning home it is often not recognised as schistosomal**.
- **Ureteric disease:** eggs in the ureter → granulomas, polyps, ulcers → **impaired peristalsis of the pelvis and ureter** → **hydronephrosis, hydroureter, obstruction and vesicoureteric reflux**.
  - **Short-term infection: renal function can recover with anthelmintic treatment.**
  - **Late or severe disease: ureteric repair or urinary diversion may be needed.**
- **FGS** occurs in **one-third to three-quarters of women infected with *S. haematobium*.** Eggs lodge in the **fallopian tubes, cervix, vagina, vulva, ovaries and uterus**.
  - **Friable sandy patches bleed on contact** (examination, intercourse).
  - **Dyspareunia, pelvic pain, bleeding, discharge, frequency and infertility** mimic UTI and sexually transmitted infection → **misdiagnosed and untreated**.
- **Male genital involvement:**
  - Heavy egg loads in the **ejaculatory ducts and seminal vesicles**. **Blood or eggs can appear in the ejaculate before eggs appear in urine.**
  - Presents as a **testicular mass or scrotal pain**.
  - **Egg burden is higher in the epididymis, ovaries and tubes than in the testis, uterus and vagina.**

**STAGES OF CHRONIC URINARY SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| Stage | Egg activity | Clinical picture |
|---|---|---|
| **Active** | Eggs being laid; haematuria | Polypoid lesions, haematuria |
| **Late chronic active** | **Tissue egg burden peaks** | **"Schistosomal contracted bladder"**: chronic suprapubic and pelvic pain, urgency, frequency, incontinence. **Trigone normal or mildly hyperaemic**; **rest of the detrusor thickened and indurated**. **Functional capacity as low as 50 mL** |
| **Quiescent** | Egg-laying and excretion fall; symptoms fade (**> 30% of light infections become asymptomatic**) | **Silent obstructive uropathy continues**: fibrosis replaces polyps → **insidious severe hydroureteronephrosis** |
| **Chronic inactive** | **No viable eggs** in urine or tissue | Sequelae of the reaction to **calcified dead eggs**. **40–60% of patients with schistosomal obstructive uropathy first reach a urologist at this end stage.** Poorly functioning or **non-functioning kidneys in asymptomatic people** |

- **The trap in this table:** **a normal urine egg count does not exclude severe disease.** The most damaged kidneys belong to patients in the inactive stage, who have no eggs and often no symptoms.

- **Bacterial superinfection:**
  - **About half** of patients with obstructive uropathy develop superimposed bacterial UTI, caused by the **usual uropathogens**.
  - ***Salmonella* association:** chronic or recurrent *Salmonella* UTI with **intermittent bacteraemia**. The bacteriuria may be **spill-over from the bloodstream**. ***Salmonella* shelters in the apical folds of the worm tegument**, protected from host defences and antibiotics.
  - **Treat both infections.** **Antibiotics alone do not clear it.**
- **Bladder ulcers:**
  - **Acute ulcers are rare.** They form in the active stage when **necrotic polyps slough**.
  - **Chronic ulcers are commoner** and are a **late sequel of heavy infection**. They cause **constant burning, intense suprapubic and pelvic pain**, and usually **gross haematuria and pyuria**.
- **Eosinophilia:**
  - **Very common in acute infection**, and **low-grade in chronic infection**.
  - It is neither sensitive nor specific, but it is **a clue to a parasitic infection**.
- **Exceptions to the usual sequence:** ectopic **pulmonary schistosomiasis, neuroschistosomiasis and FGS**.

**BLADDER — TB VS SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| | TB | Schistosomiasis |
|---|---|---|
| **Pathogenesis** | Descending infection from the kidney | **Egg deposition in the bladder wall** |
| **Trigone** | **Spared** (dome most affected) | **Normal or mildly hyperaemic** (rest of the detrusor indurated) |
| **Contracted bladder** | **Thimble bladder < 20 mL** | Functional capacity **as low as 50 mL** |
| **Ureteric orifice** | **Golf-hole** | **Golf-hole** (dilated) or stenotic |
| **Ureteric calcification** | **Intraluminal**, in a **non-dilated** ureter (forms a cast) | **Intramural**, in a **dilated** ureter |
| **Malignancy** | SCC of the pelvis after metaplasia (rare) | **SCC 60–90% of schistosomal bladder cancers** |

*Concept —* **Chronic schistosomiasis dissociates symptoms from damage.** Egg-laying and symptoms decline over the years while fibrosis goes on obstructing the tract. The worst kidneys are therefore found in quiet, egg-negative patients in the inactive stage.



##### Diagnosis

- **Gold standard for active infection: *S. haematobium* eggs in urine or stool.** Eggs do not appear until egg-laying begins, **8–12 weeks after infection**.
- **Urine sampling:**
  - **Egg excretion peaks at noon**, so **collect urine between 09:00 and 15:00**.
  - **Concentrate the urine** to detect light infections.
- **Eggs absent but suspicion high, with serology showing exposure → tissue biopsy:**
  - **Rectal snip biopsy first**, before bladder biopsy. **Eggs are common in rectal mucosa**, and the **risks of bladder biopsy (infection, perforation) are avoided**.
  - **A squash preparation between glass slides is better than histology.** It is **more sensitive and shows whether eggs are viable**.
- **Suspected FGS:** biopsy of **vulval, vaginal or cervical lesions** can reveal eggs.
- **Indirect markers:** **visual or dipstick haematuria and urine turbidity**. These are **less sensitive and specific**, and are used mainly in **control and elimination campaigns** in combination with other tests.

**DIAGNOSTIC TESTS FOR UG SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| Test | Indicates | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Urine or stool egg microscopy** | **Active infection (gold standard)** | Direct proof | Negative in the first **8–12 weeks**; insensitive in light infection |
| **Rectal snip / bladder biopsy (squash preparation)** | Active or past infection | **Egg viability** | Invasive |
| **Serology: FAST-ELISA + Western blot (CDC)** | **Exposure** | **> 90% sensitive and specific**; positive from **4–6 weeks** | **Cannot distinguish acute, chronic or cured**; titres persist after cure |
| **Circulating anodic and cathodic antigens (CAA, CCA), serum or urine** | **Active infection only** (released only by **live adult worms**) | **Quantitative** (measures intensity); **point-of-care urine CCA strip** | **CCA strip has failed to detect *S. haematobium*** (performs well for *S. mansoni*); expensive |
| **PCR (urine, stool, vaginal lavage for FGS)** | Infection | **Most sensitive method** | Needs trained staff, solvents and kits; **impractical in the field**; useful for **surveillance in low-transmission settings** |
| **Haematuria dipstick** | Indirect | Cheap, used in campaigns | Low sensitivity and specificity |

- **What the table is for:** it sorts tests by **what a positive result means**. **Only eggs and CAA/CCA prove active infection.** **Serology proves exposure only.** The trap is to use serology to judge cure.

- **Imaging:**

**IMAGING IN UG SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| Modality | Findings and use |
|---|---|
| **US** | **Bladder and ureteric wall thickening, polyps, hydroureter, hydronephrosis, calcification**, even **bladder carcinoma** |
| **Plain film** | Urinary tract calcification. **A calcified bladder resembling a "fetal head in the pelvis" is characteristic of chronic disease.** The prostate, seminal vesicles, posterior urethra, distal ureters and occasionally the colon can also calcify |
| **IVU** | **Earliest sign: striations of the ureters and renal pelvis.** Also hydronephrosis, hydroureter, non-function, stenosis and **polypoid filling defects**. **Delayed films** are needed in severe obstruction; **post-void views** show bladder neck obstruction. **IVU + fluoroscopy distinguishes tonic from atonic ureters** and shows non-stenotic immobile ureters |

| **Cystourethroscopy ± retrograde pyelography** | Mucosal lesions (Fig. 31.13); ureteric anatomy and drainage |

- **Globally, most cases go undetected and untreated**, because diagnosis relies on **direct egg detection** rather than more sensitive methods. Better accessible diagnostics are needed for control.

##### Treatment

###### Medical Management

- **Praziquantel (PZQ) is the only WHO-recommended drug.** Dependence on one drug risks resistance, but PZQ is **effective, widely available and of low toxicity**, so there has been little incentive to develop alternatives.
- **Dose for *S. haematobium*: two doses of 20 mg/kg on the same day, 6–8 hours apart**, or **a single dose of 40 mg/kg**.
- **Katayama fever: add corticosteroids.**
- **Efficacy (egg reduction and cure rate): 60–90%.** Even without cure, the **worm burden falls substantially**, which reduces further sequelae.
- **Follow-up:** **urine and stool egg counts** and **serial UG ultrasound**. **Repeat the course** if infection persists.
- **Tolerability:**
  - **Side effects** (abdominal pain, nausea, headache, dizziness) are **mild**, appear **within 3–4 hours** and settle spontaneously.
  - The **tablets are large and bitter**, which is difficult for children. A paediatric formulation is in development.
- **Pregnancy:**
  - **FDA Pregnancy Category B** (safety based on animal data only), so many programmes **exclude pregnant and lactating women**.
  - However, **adverse effects have rarely been reported despite millions of pregnant women treated**.
- **PZQ is weak against schistosomulae:**
  - Cure rates are lower where **transmission and reinfection** are high.
  - **PZQ cannot abort infection soon after exposure.**
  - **Repeat doses several weeks apart** catch juveniles that were missed and have since matured.
- **Resistance:**
  - **Large studies suggest little resistance in endemic areas.**
  - PZQ failures have been reported in **returning travellers and military personnel**.
  - **Protection against resistance:** alternative drugs and **environmental control (snail control, sanitation)**.

**PRAZIQUANTEL VS ARTEMISININ DERIVATIVES**

| | Praziquantel | Artemether / artesunate |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage targeted** | **Adult worms** (weak against schistosomulae) | **Schistosomulae** |
| **Role** | **Treatment of established infection** | **Chemoprophylaxis** (**90–97% effective**); **poor treatment** for established infection |
| **Combination** | PZQ + artemisinin → **lower infection rates than PZQ alone** (useful for MDA in high-transmission areas) | |
| **Caution** | — | **Do not use widely in malaria co-endemic areas**, because of the risk of **artemisinin-resistant malaria** |

- **What the pair tests:** the two drugs are **complementary by life-cycle stage**. The combination covers both juvenile and adult worms. The limit on it is **malaria**, not schistosomiasis.

###### Surgical Management

- **Medical therapy first.** PZQ is effective and easy to give, and **early disease can reverse**.
- **Surgery is for:**
  - Complications that have **not responded to adequate medical treatment** after a reasonable follow-up period.
  - Situations needing **immediate intervention**, for example **severe bladder haemorrhage**, a common cause of urgent surgery.
- **Bladder outlet:**
  - **Prostatitis and prostatic enlargement are uncommon.** **Autopsies show no anatomical outlet obstruction.**
  - However, **cystoscopic and urodynamic findings and raised post-void residual volumes show *functional* outlet obstruction**, which occasionally needs surgery in severe inactive disease.
- **Scrotum:** **induration, pain and enlargement from schistosomal epididymitis** can lead to **surgery for a suspected testicular tumour**.
- **Irreversibly contracted bladder:** **vesical denervation, urinary diversion, ileocystoplasty or hydrodistension**. **Always combine with chemotherapy.**
- **Chronic deep bladder ulcer → partial cystectomy.** Fulguration **rarely relieves symptoms or heals the ulcer**.
- **Urothelial hyperplasia** accompanies **severe disease**. **Dysplasia and metaplasia** accompany **schistosomal bladder cancer**. Cancer is managed surgically (see the bladder cancer chapters).
- **Obstructive uropathy is the commonest sequel.** Hydroureter and hydronephrosis track the **intensity of infection**.

**URETERIC OBSTRUCTION — ACTIVE VS LATE DISEASE**

| Active infection | Late chronic / inactive disease |
|---|---|
| Obstruction is caused by **concentric or hemi-concentric polypoid lesions** that "girdle" the intramural and adjacent extravesical ureter | **Anatomical stenosis (± calculi)** — found in **up to 80%** of obstructed ureters |
| **Responds to medical treatment alone.** **Renal function recovers within 1–2 months of PZQ.** Chemotherapy also **prevents** obstruction despite reinfection | **Less responsive to chemotherapy**, so **surgery** is needed for persistent stenosis |

**SURGERY FOR RESIDUAL SCHISTOSOMAL URETERIC STENOSIS**

| Lesion | Procedure | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Anatomical stenosis | **Balloon dilatation** | Effective, but **mechanical dilatation often restenoses** |
| **Meatus, intramural ureter, vesicoureteric junction, distal ureter** | **Reimplantation with a functional valve: variants of the Politano–Leadbetter operation** | Highly effective in some; **restenosis can occur** |
| **Long or multifocal lesions** (too little ureter left for reimplantation or ureteroureterostomy) | **Boari flap; ileal conduit; suprapubic intravesical ureterostomy** (a peritoneal dialysis catheter bypasses the segment into the bladder); **ileal ureter** | **Keep the ileal segment isoperistaltic** |
| **Isolated meatal stenosis** | **Meatoplasty** | — |
| **Unreconstructable ureter** | **Long-term nephrostomy** | — |

##### Prognosis

- **Most patients have mild infection and a good prognosis.** Morbidity and mortality depend on the **intensity of infection** and on **polymorphisms in immune-response genes**.

**PROGNOSIS BY PREVALENCE SETTING**

| Low-prevalence region (e.g. Nigeria) | Egypt at 50% prevalence |
|---|---|
| **Essentially no related mortality**; little obstructive uropathy | Schistosomiasis **contributed to death in 10%** of infected people |
| | **Severe disease: mortality approached 50% within 2–5 years** |

**TWO DEATH PROFILES**

| Death from obstructive uropathy | Death from pyelonephritis or urothelial cancer |
|---|---|
| **Patients in their 20s** with **heavy egg burdens** | **Patients over 40** |
| **Bilateral end-stage hydronephrosis**, or unilateral with contralateral non-schistosomal end-stage renal disease | Reflects **time- and intensity-related** pathology |

- **PZQ has transformed prognosis.** In **children, obstructive polyps usually resolve completely within 2–6 weeks** of treatment.
- **Chronic obstruction from sandy patches and fibrosis:** the prognosis is **less certain**. Some people tolerate advanced uropathy with little decline in renal function.
- **Obstructive uropathy, stones, outlet obstruction and bacterial cystitis all predispose to pyelonephritis.** **Bacterial superinfection can be life-threatening. Treat it promptly and aggressively.**
- **Bladder cancer:** the prognosis depends on the tumour's aggressiveness.

##### Prevention and Control

- **Travellers: avoid contact with fresh water** (streams, rivers, ponds, lakes). **Fast-flowing water can still transmit.**
- **Cercariae are killed by:**
  - **Heating water to > 125 °F (about 52 °C) for 5 minutes**
  - **Chlorination**
  - **Letting water stand for > 2 days, free of snails**
- **PZQ was introduced in the late 1970s**, with **mass distribution from the 1980s and 1990s**. Treatment is now **simple and cheap, but control remains hard**.
- **The WHO strategy is periodic, usually annual, targeted MDA with PZQ**, focused on **reducing morbidity**. **Without safe water, cycles of infection, treatment and reinfection** make **more frequent dosing** necessary.
- **Cost:** **under US$0.50 per person** in campaigns, but at scale this can still exceed national budgets. **Donated PZQ** from companies and foundations helps.

**BREAKING TRANSMISSION — THREE CONTROL LEVERS**

| Lever | Mechanism | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Sanitation + MDA** | Reduce **egg input** into water | Must cut egg input by **about 90%** before transmission falls substantially, because **asexual multiplication in the snail amplifies** the parasite |
| **Health education** | Reduce **human water contact** | — |
| **Snail control** (molluscicides, biological control by predators or competitors) | Reduce intermediate hosts; theoretically gives **proportional reductions in transmission**; some regard it as the **best single measure** | **Adult worms live for years**, so snail control alone must continue for many years |

- **Integrated campaigns** (treat people + separate people and their waste from water + control snails) are the most promising.
- **Water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) programmes** are back at the centre of control, with wider benefits.
- **Vaccine:** **none effective yet**.
- **Unlike malaria, lymphatic filariasis and onchocerciasis, global prevalence has not fallen.** However, schistosomiasis has been **eliminated in 10 countries: Iran, Japan, Lebanon, Malaysia, Martinique, Montserrat, Morocco, Thailand, Tunisia, Turkey**.
- **65th World Health Assembly, May 2012: resolution WHA65.21** called for intensified control and **elimination campaigns**. This marks a **shift from controlling morbidity to elimination**.

**KEY POINTS: SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

- ***S. haematobium* worms survive in the host for years to decades.** A careful **travel and social history** identifies exposure, links it to UG symptoms and guides testing.
- **PZQ in early-stage UG schistosomiasis can reverse the inflammatory lesions, including fibrosis**, caused by the host response to eggs in tissue.
- **Gold standard for active infection: eggs in urine, stool, or bladder or rectal biopsy.** **Serology and PCR are highly sensitive but may not distinguish active from resolved infection**, and they are **impractical in endemic regions**.

*Concept —* **Schistosomal management follows the stage of the lesion.** Egg-driven polypoid disease is reversible with PZQ, so treat medically first. Fibrotic stenosis is not reversible and needs surgery. Transmission is held up by snails and sanitation, so drugs alone reduce morbidity but cannot eliminate the disease.



#### Filariasis

- **Filariae are vector-borne tissue nematodes.** Human pathogens include the agents of **lymphatic filariasis (LF)**, ***Onchocerca volvulus*** and ***Loa loa***.
- **LF is caused by the mosquito-borne *Wuchereria bancrofti*, *Brugia malayi* and *Brugia timori*.** The spectrum runs from **acute lymphatic inflammation to chronic lymphatic dilatation**, with **hydrocele, lymphoedema and elephantiasis**.

##### Organisms

- **Transmission and development follow a sequence:**
  - **A mosquito bite deposits third-stage (infective) larvae.**
  - The larvae migrate to the **central lymphatics** and mature over **6–9 months** into adults.
  - **Adults (about 20–100 mm × 0.2 mm)** live in the **afferent lymphatics**, especially of the **lower limbs (inguinal, iliac, para-aortic)**. ***W. bancrofti* alone** also lives in the **male genitalia (epididymis, spermatic cord, testis)**. **Adult lifespan is 5–7 years.**
  - **Females release microfilariae (about 200 × 10 µm)**.
  - **Periodicity:** microfilaraemia is **nocturnal (peaks at midnight)** in most areas, to match mosquito biting. It is **diurnal in parts of the Pacific**.
  - **In the mosquito, microfilariae develop into infective larvae over 10–14 days.**
- ***Wolbachia***: an **obligate rickettsia-like endosymbiont** of *W. bancrofti* and *Brugia*, needed for embryogenesis. **Doxycycline kills it**, which **reduces microfilarial release and suppresses larval moulting**.

##### Epidemiology

- **About 68 million people have LF**, a large fall since the early 2000s.

**LYMPHATIC FILARIAE — DISTRIBUTION AND GENITAL INVOLVEMENT**

| Species | Share and distribution | Genital disease | Limb pattern | Circulating antigen test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ***W. bancrofti*** | **> 90% of infections**: sub-Saharan Africa, South and South-East Asia, western Pacific. **Americas: only Haiti, Dominican Republic, Guyana and Brazil** | **Almost exclusively this species; very common** | **Whole limb** | **Yes (ELISA, ICT card)** |
| ***B. malayi*** | **Asia and Pacific islands** (e.g. Indonesia, Philippines) | Uncommon | **Leg below the knee** | **None** (antibody dipstick available) |
| ***B. timori*** | **South-eastern Indonesia only** | Uncommon | Below the knee | None |

- **The trap:** **genital filariasis means *W. bancrofti***. Brugian infection spares the genitals and affects the limb below the knee.

- **Vectors:** ***Anopheles*** (rural Africa, Pacific), ***Culex*** (urban areas, especially India), ***Aedes aegypti*** (some Pacific islands), and others.
- **Transmission is inefficient.** **Obstructive lymphatic disease needs repeated infection over many years**, so it is seen mainly in **long-term residents**.
- **Prevalence rises through childhood to the third or fourth decade**, then plateaus as adult worms accumulate. **Lymphoedema and genital disease are rare before age 10.**
- **About one-third of infected people have overt disease.** Clinical disease is **more likely in India, Papua New Guinea and Africa** and less likely in the Americas.

##### Pathology and Clinical Manifestations

- **Immune phases:**
  - **Early (larvae, young adults):** **pro-inflammatory, mixed Th1 and Th2**. Filaria-specific **IgE rises**, and **eosinophils kill microfilariae**.
  - **With microfilaraemia:** T-cell responses are **down-regulated** by **IL-10, IgG4 blocking antibody and antigen-specific suppressor T cells**.
  - **Some people remain uninfected despite long exposure.** Whether protective immunity develops is unclear.
- **Damage is cumulative** (progressive scarring and lymphatic obstruction). **Treatment does not readily reverse it but can halt progression** in active infection.
- **LF is rarely fatal but causes the third-highest loss of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) among parasitic infections.**
- **How lymphoedema develops:**
  - **Parasite factors dilate the lymphatics** → **secondary bacterial infection** and **inflammation against dying worms** (and ***Wolbachia***, which is pro-inflammatory).
  - Histology: **granulomas around worms**, ± **tissue eosinophils** (Fig. 31.15).
  - **A vicious cycle follows:** acute attack → worse lymphoedema → more infection → worse lymphoedema.
  - It ends in **obliterated, scarred lymphatics**, with **elephantiasis or hydrocele** as the end stage.

###### Subclinical Infection

- **Most infected people have few symptoms, even with heavy microfilaraemia.** Yet **almost all patent infections cause subclinical disease**: **dilated lymphatics, scrotal lymphangiectasia, microscopic haematuria, proteinuria**. **Eosinophilia is very common.**

###### Acute Adenolymphangitis

- **Acute adenolymphangitis (ADL) is often the first manifestation:** **fever, lymphadenitis, lymphangitis and oedema** lasting days to a week.

**FILARIAL VS BACTERIAL LYMPHANGITIS**

| Filarial (ADL) | Bacterial |
|---|---|
| **Retrograde**: spreads **peripherally**, away from the node | Ascending: spreads **centrally** |

- **Genital lymphatics are affected almost only by *W. bancrofti*:**
  - **Funiculitis, epididymitis, scrotal pain and tenderness.**
  - **Lymph scrotum:** ruptured lymphatic vesicles on the scrotal skin → **whitish discharge** and **secondary bacterial infection**.
- **Dermatolymphangioadenitis (DLA):**
  - **Fever, chills, myalgia, headache.**
  - **Oedematous inflammatory plaques**, hyperpigmentation, vesicles and ulcers, often at a **skin injury**.
  - Inflammation spreads **proximally**. It is thought to be **bacterial**.

###### Lymphoedema

- **Limb oedema is the commonest chronic manifestation.** The **lower limb is affected more than the upper**, usually **asymmetrically**.
- **Bancroftian filariasis affects the whole limb; brugian affects the leg below the knee.**
- The skin may **exude serous fluid**. **The breast can be involved in women.**

###### Genitourinary Manifestations

- **Male genital disease is very common in bancroftian filariasis and uncommon in brugian.** Female genital involvement is not well characterised and appears uncommon.
- **Genital disease is not usually seen before the teenage years.**
- **Acute painful episodes of (usually unilateral) epididymitis or funiculitis** with fever and malaise last several days. They are **one of the commonest consequences** of bancroftian infection.

**Funiculoepididymitis**
- **Palpable, cord-like swelling with oedema.** Usually **self-limiting**, but **recurrence and chronic lymphoedema are common**.
- **Sterility and orchitis are rare, because the testis is usually spared**.
- **Often mistaken for malignancy**, which leads to unnecessary surgery, **including orchidectomy**.
- **A varicocele can complicate the inflammation**, adding pain and swelling.
- **Bacterial superinfection is rare but severe**, with **exquisite pain and septic thrombophlebitis**.

**Hydrocele**
- **The commonest result of chronic male genital disease.** It can be **very large** (Fig. 31.16).
- **In endemic areas, filarial and non-filarial hydroceles are hard to tell apart.** **Parasites are rarely found in the fluid.**
- **Features that suggest LF:**
  - **Nodules in the cord or epididymis** plus **residence in or travel to an endemic area**.
  - A **thick fibrous tunica**, especially with **cholesterol or calcium deposits**.
- **Hydroceles are painless** unless complicated by acute epididymitis or funiculitis. The scrotal skin may be **thickened and brawny, oozing lymph**.
- **Superinfection:** **rare in filarial hydrocele**; **common with elephantiasis and lymph scrotum**.

**Scrotal and penile elephantiasis**
- **Mild scrotal oedema** is common early in infection or with a hydrocele. **Penile oedema is unusual.**
- **Massive scrotal or penile enlargement appears late**, mainly where access to care is poor.
- **Genital elephantiasis rarely has a cause other than LF.**

**Chyluria**
- **Damaged GU lymphatics leak lymph into the urine**, causing **heavy fat and protein loss** and possible **malnutrition**. It is rare.
- **It occurs earlier in the disease than genital elephantiasis**, is **usually intermittent**, and can **remit spontaneously**.

###### Tropical Pulmonary Eosinophilia

- **Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia (TPE):** **paroxysmal, mainly nocturnal cough and wheeze**, fever, lymphadenopathy, **high-grade eosinophilia and raised IgE**.
- **Cause:** an **allergic response to microfilarial antigens**. It is **commonest in South and South-East Asia**.
- **Chest radiograph:** normal to diffuse reticulonodular shadowing. **Lung function:** restrictive (sometimes obstructive). **Biopsy:** eosinophilic interstitial pneumonitis.
- **Microfilaraemia is usually absent.** This is the trap: a negative blood smear does not exclude TPE.

##### Diagnosis

- **Epidemiological context matters:** in endemic areas, lymphoedema or male genital disease is **more likely to be LF**, provided other causes of secondary oedema are excluded.
- **Differential diagnosis of funiculoepididymitis: TB, *S. haematobium* and gonorrhoea.** **Non-filarial hydrocele is common everywhere**, but in endemic areas hydroceles are **more frequent and appear at a younger age**.

**DIAGNOSTIC TESTS FOR LYMPHATIC FILARIASIS**

| Test | Detail | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Adult worm on histology** | **Definitive** | **Insensitive**; worms lie deep in lymphatics |
| **Ultrasound of lymphatics** | **"Filarial dance sign"**: characteristic movement of live adults; **≥ 80% sensitive** in some settings | Operator-dependent |
| **Plain radiograph** | Calcifications, suggestive in context | Non-specific |
| **Giemsa-stained blood smear** | **Microfilariae. Time the sample to local periodicity (usually midnight)** | **Positive in only a minority** of infected people |
| **Circulating antigen (ELISA; immunochromatographic card test, ICT)** | ***W. bancrofti* only.** The **Alere Filariasis Test Strip is more sensitive** in the field than the older **BinaxNOW** ICT | **None exists for *Brugia*** |
| **PCR (blood)** | *W. bancrofti* and *B. malayi*; **very sensitive** | Not widely available |
| **Antibody** | Traditionally **poorly specific**. **IgG4** is less cross-reactive, so more specific; species-specific antigens help; **dipstick for *Brugia*** | Cannot confirm active infection |
| **Lymphoscintigraphy** | **Reliably shows lymphatic abnormality** and documents extent | **Does not distinguish LF from other lymphatic disease** |

- **"Burned-out" infection** (after treatment, or years after leaving an endemic area): **lasting lymphoedema or genital damage**, with **negative microfilariae and negative antigen**. **Negative tests do not exclude LF**, and these patients are **usually antibody positive**.

##### Treatment

- **Treat all patients with microfilaraemia, with or without symptoms**, because almost all have subclinical disease.

**ANTIFILARIAL DRUGS — TARGETS, DOSES AND TOXICITY**

| Drug | Target | Regimen (LF unless stated) | Signature caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Diethylcarbamazine (DEC)** | **Microfilaricidal**; **only modest activity against adults** | **Drug of choice for active LF**: **2 mg/kg orally three times daily**. **A 1-day course is as effective as the traditional 12-day course**. **TPE: 2–3 weeks**. In the United States **available only through the CDC** | **Contraindicated where onchocerciasis or *Loa loa* may coexist** (West and Central Africa) unless excluded. Reactions: fever, chills, arthralgia and myalgia, headache, nausea and vomiting; in heavy infection **painful nodules, lymphadenitis and epididymitis** from **dying worms and *Wolbachia*** |
| **Albendazole** | **Microfilaricidal and macrofilaricidal** | **400 mg twice daily for 21 days** | Few side effects in single-dose MDA regimens |
| **Ivermectin** | **Mostly microfilaricidal** | **150–400 µg/kg once** | Side effects similar to DEC; **caution if *Loa loa* is possible** |
| **Doxycycline** | **Anti-*Wolbachia***; some **macrofilaricidal** activity; **sterilises adults** | **200 mg daily for 4–8 weeks** (6 weeks is reasonable in the United States) | **Not in pregnancy or young children**; the long course is hard to deliver in resource-poor settings |

- **What the table is for:** **most antifilarial drugs kill microfilariae, not adults**. That is why control needs **years of repeated dosing** until the adult worms die of old age.
- **Doxycycline is the exception.** It **improves lymphoedema and hydrocele even without active infection**, which suggests a benefit **beyond its anti-*Wolbachia* action**.
- **The trap:** **DEC in a patient with onchocerciasis or loiasis can cause severe reactions**, including blindness in onchocerciasis.

- **Chronic lymphoedema:** control morbidity with **prevention of secondary bacterial infection, hygiene, elastic stockings, elevation and physiotherapy**. **Give antiparasitic drugs only if infection is active.**
- **Surgery:**
  - **Lymphatic–venous and nodal–venous anastomoses** have reduced leg swelling, and **reconstruction** has helped genital disease. The **long-term results are unknown**.
  - **Genital elephantiasis rarely suits surgery.** **Lymphadenectomy can worsen lymphatic drainage.**
  - **Funiculoepididymitis:** **decompression or excision of filarial nodules** to preserve the testis and cord. If recurrent, painful, deforming or complicated by vascular involvement → **more radical surgery**.
  - **Hydrocele:**
    - **Aspiration gives immediate relief** but **recurs** without medical and definitive surgical treatment.
    - **Hydrocelectomy for large or symptomatic hydroceles.** **Excision of the intact sac is the procedure of choice**; **inversion with partial excision** is an alternative.
    - **Suture or excise leaking or dilated lymphatics.**
    - **Small, non-enlarging hydroceles do not need surgery.**
  - **Reconstruction of the scrotum or vulva** with removal of redundant tissue helps selected patients.

##### Prevention and Control

- **Personal protection:** avoid mosquitoes; use **long-lasting insecticide-treated bednets (LLINs)**, which are proven for control and elimination.
- **Removing microfilariae from a community interrupts transmission**, because **the mosquito needs microfilaraemia to transmit**. However, **drugs do not kill all adult worms**, so **intermittent dosing must continue for years** until the adults die of senescence.
  - This works for ***W. bancrofti***. It is **harder for *Brugia***, which has **animal reservoirs**.
- **MDA: single annual doses of albendazole plus either ivermectin or DEC**, which have a sustained microfilaricidal effect:
  - **Africa: albendazole + ivermectin** (DEC is avoided because onchocerciasis and *Loa* are co-endemic).
  - **Elsewhere: albendazole + DEC.**
  - **MDA is the main reason global prevalence has fallen.** **About 19 million hydroceles have been prevented since 2000**, halving LF hydroceles.
- **Triple therapy (DEC + albendazole + ivermectin):** small studies show **deeper, longer suppression of microfilariae** than dual therapy, **with no extra serious adverse effects**.

##### Onchocerciasis

- **River blindness.** Caused by ***O. volvulus***, transmitted by ***Simulium* black flies**.
  - **99% of cases are in Africa**, with foci in **Brazil, Venezuela and Yemen**.
  - **About 17 million infected**, about half the 2000 figure, thanks to **MDA**.
  - Transmission is **inefficient and focal**.
- **Adults live up to 15 years (mean 9–10) in subcutaneous nodules.** Microfilariae migrate through the **skin and eye**. Adults carry ***Wolbachia***.
- **Clinical features:** **dermatitis and pruritus, keratitis, chorioretinitis** → **blindness from corneal scarring** after many years.
- **Urological relevance:** late **"hanging groin"** or **scrotal elephantiasis** from recurrent lymphadenitis and loss of skin elasticity. **Histology:** atrophic, fibrotic inguinal nodes with subcutaneous oedema and fibrosis. Occasionally **massive inguinal lymphadenopathy**.
- **Diagnosis:** **skin snips** for microfilariae; **adult worms in nodules**; **microfilariae in the anterior chamber on slit lamp**. Serological tests are less developed than for LF.
- ***Onchocerca lupi*** (a parasite of dogs): a **rare human pathogen**. **Six United States residents since 2013** (five of them children), all in the **south-west**, with nodules containing adult worms (spine, skin and soft tissue, extraocular muscle).
- **Treatment:**
  - **Ivermectin is the treatment of choice: 150 µg/kg once, repeated every 6–12 months until asymptomatic.**
  - It acts mainly on microfilariae, but **repeated doses shorten adult lifespan by about half or more**.
  - **Adverse effects:** fever, rash, dizziness, pruritus, myalgia, arthralgia, lymphadenopathy, caused by **dying filariae and *Wolbachia***.
  - **Caution if *Loa* co-infection is possible.**
  - **Follow with doxycycline 200 mg/day for 6 weeks.** It **kills or shortens the life of most adult females and sterilises the rest**, clearing or greatly reducing microfilariae.
  - **DEC is contraindicated.** It can cause **blindness and systemic toxicity** from the inflammatory response to dying parasites.

##### Loiasis

- ***Loa loa*:** **Central and West Africa**, transmitted by ***Chrysops* flies**.
  - **Adults migrate through subcutaneous tissue.** **Microfilariae circulate by day (diurnal).**
  - ***Loa* has no *Wolbachia***, so **doxycycline has no role**.
- **Clinical features:**
  - **Most have asymptomatic eosinophilia.**
  - Some have urticaria, **migratory subcutaneous swellings**, and **"eye worm"** (adult crossing the conjunctiva).
  - **Haematuria and proteinuria in about 30%.** **Lymphadenitis and hydrocele are rare.**
- **Treatment:**
  - **DEC 2–3 mg/kg orally three times daily for 14–21 days** is effective. Multiple courses may be needed.
  - **Treatment reactions:** pruritus, arthralgia, migratory swellings, fever, eye worms, diarrhoea, **renal failure**.
  - **Risk of treatment-induced encephalopathy with high microfilaraemia (> 2500–8000 microfilariae/mL), especially with ivermectin.** **Pre-treatment apheresis** may reduce it.
  - **Albendazole 200 mg twice daily for 3 weeks** carries a **lower encephalopathy risk than DEC** and may be **safer with high parasitaemia**.

**THE THREE FILARIAL INFECTIONS COMPARED**

| | Lymphatic filariasis | Onchocerciasis | Loiasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Organism** | *W. bancrofti*, *B. malayi*, *B. timori* | *O. volvulus* | *L. loa* |
| **Vector** | **Mosquito** (*Anopheles*, *Culex*, *Aedes*) | ***Simulium* black fly** | ***Chrysops* fly** |
| **Adult site** | **Lymphatics** (± male genitalia, *W. bancrofti*) | **Subcutaneous nodules** | **Migrating subcutaneously** |
| **Microfilarial periodicity** | **Nocturnal** (diurnal in parts of the Pacific) | In skin and eye | **Diurnal** |
| ***Wolbachia*** | **Yes** | **Yes** | **No** |
| **Urological disease** | **Hydrocele, funiculoepididymitis, elephantiasis, chyluria** | **Hanging groin, scrotal elephantiasis** | **Haematuria and proteinuria (about 30%)**; rarely hydrocele |
| **Drug of choice** | **DEC** (Africa: ivermectin + albendazole in MDA) | **Ivermectin** → doxycycline | **DEC** (albendazole if high load) |
| **Contraindicated** | DEC where *Onchocerca* or *Loa* is co-endemic | **DEC** | **Ivermectin (and DEC) with high microfilaraemia** → encephalopathy |

*Concept —* **Filarial treatment depends on which filaria is present.** Drugs kill microfilariae rather than adults, so the killing is what causes harm: DEC blinds in onchocerciasis, and DEC or ivermectin can cause encephalopathy in heavy *Loa* infection. *Wolbachia* is present in LF and *Onchocerca* but absent in *Loa*, which is why doxycycline is useful in the first two and not the third.



#### Other Non-filarial Genitourinary Parasites

##### Echinococcosis

- ***Echinococcus granulosus*** is a **cestode (tapeworm)** that causes **cystic echinococcosis (hydatid disease)**.
- **Transmission:** **ingesting eggs in contaminated food or water**, or **contact with infected dogs**. **Prevalence is high in pastoral communities**: South America, the Mediterranean littoral, Eastern Europe, the Middle East, East Africa, Central Asia, China, Russia and Australia.
- **Cyst sites:**
  - Usually the **liver**, less often the **lung**.
  - **The kidney is the third commonest site (about 2–3% of cases).**
- **Natural history:**
  - Cysts are **asymptomatic at first** and grow about **1–2 cm per year** → **pain or a palpable mass**.
  - **Hydatiduria and renal colic occur in a minority.**
  - **Renal function is usually preserved.**
- **Imaging:** a **thick-walled, fluid-filled spherical cyst, often with a calcified wall**. The appearance **defines the stage**, which guides management.
- **Serology is adjunctive only** (sensitivity 60–90%).
- **Treatment:**
  - **Puncture, aspiration, injection, re-aspiration (PAIR)** is suitable for **liver cysts, but not for lung or kidney cysts**. For these the only options are **surgical resection or antiparasitic drugs**.
  - **Albendazole 400 mg twice daily for 1–6 months** is the recommended medical treatment.
  - **Surgical excision** is indicated for some lesions because of size or location.
  - **Cyst rupture can cause anaphylaxis.**
  - **Perioperative PZQ + albendazole** may reduce **secondary seeding**.

##### Enterobiasis

- ***Enterobius vermicularis* (pinworm)** occurs **worldwide**, in both temperate and tropical countries.
- **Life cycle:** worms live in the **proximal colon** → migrate to the **perianal skin to lay eggs**, which become **infective after 6 hours**. Spread is **person to person** (faecal–oral, hands, fomites).
- **Clinical features:**
  - **Usually asymptomatic.** **Perianal pruritus** can be severe.
  - **Ectopic migration in women:** through the **vagina, uterus and tubes into the peritoneum**. Dead worms and eggs cause **granulomas and adhesions**, leading to **vulval and cervical granulomas, salpingitis, oophoritis, tubo-ovarian abscess, appendicitis, peritonitis**.
  - **In men:** rare epididymal involvement and inguinal hernia.
- **Treatment:** **single-dose albendazole 400 mg or mebendazole 100 mg** (highly effective). Alternative: **ivermectin 200 µg/kg once**.
  - **Treat household and close contacts.**
  - **Repeat after 2 weeks** because of **reinfection and autoinfection**.

##### Amoebiasis

- ***Entamoeba histolytica*:** a **protozoan** spread by the **faecal–oral route**, commonest in the tropics.
- **Most infected people are asymptomatic.** **About 10% develop disease in other organs, including the kidney.** **Cutaneous amoebiasis** causes **painful perianal and genital ulcers**.
- **Treatment:**
  - **Tissue phase:** **tinidazole 2 g/day for 3–5 days** or **metronidazole 750 mg three times daily for 10 days**.
  - **Then a luminal agent:** **paromomycin 8–12 mg/kg three times daily for 7 days** or **iodoquinol 650 mg three times daily for 20 days**.

##### Trichomoniasis

- ***Trichomonas vaginalis*** is a common **sexually transmitted protozoan**. It is covered in **Chapter 29**.

**KEY POINTS: LYMPHATIC FILARIASIS**

- **LF is caused by three species of tissue nematode transmitted by mosquitoes.**
- **LF is endemic in much of the tropics but transmitted inefficiently.** Clinically apparent disease is usually seen only in **long-term residents** of endemic countries.
- **Diagnosis:** blood smears, antibody and antigen assays, imaging and clinical assessment.
- **Treatment of active LF is DEC.** **Ivermectin and albendazole are also active, and all three are used in mass chemotherapy.**
