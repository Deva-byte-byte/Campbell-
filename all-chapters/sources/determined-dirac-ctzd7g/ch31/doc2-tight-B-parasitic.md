### PARASITIC INFECTIONS OF THE UROGENITAL TRACT

- **Parasites are rare in non-endemic practice, but the urologist must recognise them** to diagnose and treat correctly.
- **Relevant parasites:** **UG schistosomiasis, filariasis, amoebiasis, enterobiasis and echinococcosis**.

#### Schistosomiasis

- **More than 200 million people are infected with *Schistosoma* species.**

**THE THREE MEDICALLY IMPORTANT SCHISTOSOMES**

| Species | Distribution | Principal target | Snail host |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***S. haematobium*** | **Africa, Arabian Peninsula** | **Urogenital tract** | ***Bulinus*** |
| *S. mansoni* | Africa, Arabian Peninsula, **South America** | Liver, GI tract | *Biomphalaria* |
| *S. japonicum* | **China, South-East Asia** | Liver, GI tract | *Oncomelania* |

- **UG schistosomiasis is the *S. haematobium* disease**: a complex life cycle, many manifestations and close ties to the environment.
- **It has co-evolved with humans and non-human primates for millennia**, so ancient civilisations recognised it.

##### History

- **Schistosome antigens in Egyptian mummies (c. 3500 BCE)**; ***S. haematobium* eggs** in later mummies.
- **The Egyptians called it "A-a-a disease"**; the hieroglyph shows a **penis dripping blood-stained urine**.
- **Theodor Bilharz (1852, Cairo autopsies)** found worms in the **mesenteric veins** and linked them to **eggs in urine and stool** — hence "bilharzia".

##### Biology and Life Cycle

- **Human cycle:**
  - **Cercariae penetrate skin (even intact)** in infested fresh water. They live about **1 day** and lose infectivity **within hours** of leaving the snail.
  - They **shed their tails** over **90–120 minutes** → **schistosomulae**.
  - Schistosomulae migrate via blood or lymph **to the lungs** (several weeks) → venous circulation → **liver sinusoids**, where they **begin feeding on blood**.
  - **Adults migrate to the vesical and pelvic venous plexus** and live on average **3–5 years**. Cercaria to adult takes **80–110 days**.
  - **Pairing:** the **male holds the female in the gynaecophoric canal**, helps her pump blood and **chemically stimulates egg-laying**.
- **Eggs:**
  - **Fecundity:** hundreds to thousands of eggs per female per day.
  - ***S. haematobium* eggs: oval, about 140 µm, terminal spine** (Fig. 31.9).
  - Eggs must **cross the endothelium and bladder wall** to reach urine; some cross the gut wall into faeces.
  - **Fewer than half are excreted**; **eggs retained in tissue drive the pathology.**
  - Eggs survive about **20 days if kept wet**.
- **Environmental cycle:**
  - **Fresh water and light** → eggs hatch → **ciliated miracidia** (live about **6 hours**).
  - Miracidia penetrate ***Bulinus*** snails, which prefer **slow-flowing water** and tolerate **low oxygen**.
  - **Primary sporocyst → 20–40 daughter sporocysts → 200–400 cercariae each**, released into water.
  - **The prepatent period is temperature-dependent; no cercariae are shed below 15 °C or above 35 °C.**

- Cercariae are forked-tailed (about 500 µm) and cause **"swimmer's itch"** on penetration.
- Adults are **1–3 cm long** and pair for life.
- **Eggs measure 70–170 µm** across species; miracidia hatch within 1–3 hours.
- **Each species has its own venous destination and snail:**
  - ***S. haematobium* → vesical venous plexus**: haematuria, bacterial UTI, scarring, calcification, **SCC**.
  - ***S. mansoni* and *S. japonicum* → mesenteric venules of bowel and rectum**: **Katayama fever (especially *S. japonicum*)**, presinusoidal granulomas, **Symmers pipestem periportal fibrosis**, portal hypertension.
  - All species occasionally cause **embolic egg granulomas in the brain or spinal cord**.

**FIG. 31.9** Micrographs of *S. haematobium* eggs showing the **characteristic terminal spine**. *Source: Ray et al., PLoS Negl Trop Dis, 2012.*

##### Epidemiology

- **Distribution follows the snail:** **most of sub-Saharan Africa**, parts of **North Africa and the Middle East**.
- **Europe — Corsica, 2013:** the **first autochthonous transmission in Europe for over a century**; **further cases in 2015** suggest a new endemic focus. Some were ***S. bovis* or *S. haematobium–S. bovis* hybrids**.
- **Burden:**
  - **Mostly the UG form**; newer estimates reach **440 million** infected.
  - **Up to 150,000 deaths a year from *S. haematobium* obstructive renal failure alone.**
  - In sub-Saharan Africa, tens of millions have haematuria and dysuria at any time; **millions have major bladder pathology and hydronephrosis**.
- **Individual risk depends on the type and duration of freshwater contact:**
  - **Rural women and children:** domestic chores (laundry, dishwashing).
  - **Children:** play and swimming.
  - **Adults:** fishing, car washing, agriculture with **water-intensive crops (rice, sugar cane)**.
  - **Prevalence is highly focal**, because transmission depends on water.
- **Dams and irrigation increase transmission** by creating **year-round slow-flowing snail habitats** and drawing **denser farming populations**.

**DAMS AND SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| Dam | Decade | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Aswan Low Dam, Egypt** | **1930s** | Flood → perennial irrigation; prevalence rose from **< 11% to > 75%** in some regions |
| **Aswan High Dam, Egypt** | 1960s | Similar rise |
| **Diama Dam, West Africa** | 1980s | Similar rise |

- **Highest prevalence:** **poor sanitation and no clean running water**.
- **Children aged 5–15 carry the heaviest worm burden**; whether from exposure or susceptibility is unclear.
- **Schistosomiasis and poverty reinforce each other:** the **snail hosts live in tropical and subtropical developing countries**; **poor sanitation** puts eggs into surface water; **no safe water supply** prolongs water contact; and **chronic infection impairs growth, learning and productivity**, perpetuating poverty.

*Concept —* **Schistosomiasis is an environmental disease.** Transmission needs human excreta in fresh water, a particular snail and prolonged contact with water. Every feature of its epidemiology (focality, children, dams, poverty) is one of those three links, and so is every method of control.



##### Pathogenesis and Pathology

- **Cercarial penetration** is aided by secreted **proteases**, which trigger cellular and humoral responses; but the parasite **modulates host immunity from first contact**, so brief penetration causes **only local skin inflammation**. **Repeated exposure → hypersensitivity → maculopapular rash.**
- **Immune evasion by the tegument:** the maturing schistosomulum forms a **double lipid bilayer surface (the tegument)** that lets it survive for years, via **host antigen mimicry**, **continuous membrane turnover**, **immunomodulatory proteins and proteases**, **evasive biophysical surface properties** and **modulation of surface-antigen expression**.
- **Most knowledge comes from autopsy studies and animal models.**

**WORM VS EGG — THE GOVERNING CONTRAST**

| Adult worm | Egg |
|---|---|
| **Immunologically near-silent** (tegumental evasion) | **The main immunopathological trigger** |
| Lives in the venous plexus | Deposited in the **walls of the bladder and pelvic organs** |
| Causes little disease directly | **Granuloma → fibrosis → obstruction of blood or urine flow** |

- **The egg granuloma:** a **mixed infiltrate of eosinophils, plasma cells and lymphocytes**. **Continuous egg-laying means granulomas of every stage coexist**; clustered eggs form **composite, coalescing granulomas**, grossly **bulky, hyperaemic, polypoid masses** projecting into the bladder (Fig. 31.10).
- **Severity modifiers:** **host genetics, in utero sensitisation, co-infection**.
- ***S. haematobium* eggs rapidly induce type 2 inflammatory genes and suppress urothelial barrier genes**, suggesting that **host and parasite share the aim of expelling eggs** across a temporarily weakened urothelium.

**FIG. 31.10** IVU showing **scalloping of the bladder and right lower ureter** by schistosomal polypoid lesions.

- **Ectopic egg deposition:**
  - **Portal tract:** heavy egg loads swept to the liver block **presinusoidal capillaries** → granulomas → **portal hypertension**. Embolised eggs give **granulomatous, fibrotic portal tracts** and **dilated portosystemic collaterals** → **pipestem (Symmers) fibrosis** → **hepatosplenomegaly**. Susceptibility depends on the individual immune response.
  - **Lung:** worm pairs reach the pulmonary vessels **only in very severe infection**, after pelvic disease is established → **pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale**.
- **Naturally acquired immunity exists:** some stay **egg-negative for ≥ 5 years** despite continued exposure and no treatment.

**RESISTANT VS CHRONICALLY INFECTED**

| Resistant to reinfection | Chronically infected |
|---|---|
| **Mixed T-helper 1 (Th1) + Th2 response** | **Th2 response only** |
| Protective **IgE** active | **IgG4 blocks protective IgE** (antibodies to worm and egg antigens) |

- **IgE to worm antigens rises with age**, suggesting immune resistance — but **water contact also falls with age**. Exposure-controlled analyses still suggest **age contributes independently**.

- **Calcification and obstructive uropathy — the causal chain:**
  - **Unexpelled eggs calcify** (bladder, ureters).
  - → **reduced urinary tract compliance → higher upper-tract pressure → stasis, hydroureter, hydronephrosis**.
  - **Calcification extent on imaging roughly reflects the tissue burden of calcified eggs.**

**LEVEL OF SCHISTOSOMAL URETERIC OBSTRUCTION**

| Level | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Ureteric meatus | ~1% |
| Intramural (interstitial) ureter | 10–30% |
| **Juxtavesical ureter** | **20–60%** |
| Lower third of ureter | 15–50% |
| Contiguous combination | 30–60% |

- **The pattern:** obstruction is **overwhelmingly distal** (juxtavesical to lower third); the meatus itself is rarely the site.

**THREE PATTERNS OF SCHISTOSOMAL HYDROURETER**

| | Segmental (cylindrical or fusiform) | Tonic | Atonic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Share of obstructive uropathy** | **About one-quarter** (**80% in the lower ureter**) | **Up to 30%** | The remainder |
| **Wall** | Dilatation **above concentric muscle replaced by fibrosis and sandy patches** | **Thick-walled, trabeculated; marked muscular hypertrophy**; tortuous | **Thin-walled**; **atrophic, fibrotic muscle**; very tortuous |
| **Peristalsis** | — | **Impaired** | **Absent** |
| **Extent** | Segmental | **Whole ureter above the lesion** (functional stenosis) | Markedly dilated |
| **Hydronephrosis** | **Rarely significant** | **Significant, but reversible** if obstruction is relieved | — |

- **The logic:** tonic = **hypertrophied muscle fighting an obstruction** (reversible); atonic = **muscle that has failed** (fibrotic, aperistaltic). The trap is assuming every dilated ureter is irreversibly damaged.

- **Hydroureter precedes hydronephrosis.** Untreated hydronephrosis progresses **pelvic dilatation → medullary atrophy → medullary effacement → cortical atrophy**, which is why **tubular function (concentrating ability) is lost before glomerular function**.
- **Bacterial UTI is commoner in chronic infection**, possibly from **bacteria adhering to the worm tegument**, **stasis** and **immunomodulation**.
- **Bladder cancer:** ***S. haematobium* has the strongest cancer link of any helminth**, particularly to **SCC**, on epidemiological (especially **Egypt**) and experimental evidence. **Risk rises with duration and severity of infection.**
- **Female genital schistosomiasis (FGS):** egg sequestration causes **fibrotic nodules ("sandy patches")** in the **uterus, cervix and lower genital tract** (Fig. 31.11). The mechanism is poorly understood beyond **increased vascularity of the genital mucosa** around eggs.
- **Male genital schistosomiasis (MGS):**
  - **Cause:** **ectopic eggs** in the **testis, epididymis, spermatic cord, scrotum and penis**.
  - **Poorly characterised**, partly because **transrectal US is needed** to assess the internal sex organs.
  - **Pathology:** **neoplastic growth around lesions** with fibrosis and calcification.
  - **Presentation:** **genital disfigurement, haemospermia, haematuria, infertility, genital pain**; a minority have sexual dysfunction or **urethral discharge mistaken for a sexually transmitted infection**.
  - **Infertility is thought reversible** (epididymitis, funiculitis); **azoospermia** with **eosinophilic interstitial orchitis** can occur.
  - **Diagnosis: eggs in the ejaculate on microscopy.** But **symptoms predict disease poorly**, **semen egg counts vary between samples** and **correlate poorly with urine counts**, and the effect of abstinence is unknown.
  - **Hydrocele and testicular calcification** are reported; causal link uncertain.
  - **Raised PSA** suggests frequent prostatic involvement. **Serum testosterone is lower** with *S. haematobium* or *S. mansoni* infection (mechanism unknown).
  - **MGS, like FGS, may increase HIV susceptibility.**
  - **Treatment: praziquantel.**
- **Bladder sequelae:** **urothelial hyperplasia → squamous metaplasia → dysplasia → urothelial carcinoma or SCC**. **Bladder cancer is the final pathological sequel.**

**SCHISTOSOMAL VS CONVENTIONAL BLADDER CANCER**

| | Schistosomal bladder cancer | Conventional (non-endemic) bladder cancer |
|---|---|---|
| **Age at onset** | **Early: 40–50 years** | Older |
| **Histology** | **SCC 60–90%**; **adenocarcinoma 5–15%** | Predominantly urothelial (transitional cell) |
| **Differentiation** | **> 40% of SCCs well differentiated, verrucous, good prognosis** | — |
| **Site** | **Posterior wall about half**; lateral wall about 30% | — |
| **Growth** | **Exophytic about two-thirds**; rest ulcerative, endophytic | — |

- **Mass drug administration (MDA) in Egypt reduced bladder neoplasms** and **shifted SCC to transitional cell carcinoma** — evidence of cause.
- **Smoking**, common in endemic regions, may act **synergistically**; yet some autopsy series there show **similar cancer rates without schistosomiasis**.
- **Carcinogenesis:**
  - **The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC, WHO) classifies *S. haematobium* as Group 1 ("carcinogenic to humans").**
  - **Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)** rises in the bladder soon after egg exposure and may promote tumour vasculogenesis and progression.
  - **Proposed pathway:** papillomas merge with basal transitional epithelium → **benign fibroepithelial papillary growths** → repeated inflammation and fibrosis → **clonal, precancerous lesions including squamous metaplasia**.
  - Egg exposure alters **carcinogenesis-related signalling pathways** (mouse bladder).
  - **Co-infecting bacteria convert nitrates to nitrosamines**, which **alkylate DNA and protein**, causing mutations in genes such as **p53**.

*Concept —* **In schistosomiasis the worm is tolerated and the egg is not.** Eggs held in the wall give granuloma, then fibrosis and calcification, then obstruction, and eventually metaplasia and cancer. Every urological sequel is the egg's damage accumulating over time.

##### Clinical Manifestations

**ACUTE VS CHRONIC SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| | Acute | Chronic |
|---|---|---|
| **Cause** | Response to **cercarial penetration** and worm **migration and maturation** | **Immune response to prolonged egg-laying** over years |
| **Who** | Often **travellers**, in **primary infection** | **Long-term endemic residents**: repeated reinfection, high worm burden |
| **Outcome** | Self-limiting | **Organ damage** |

- **Schistosomes cannot multiply in the human host**, so after a single exposure **adults die of old age within 3–5 years** even untreated, limiting pathology in visitors.

###### Acute Schistosomiasis

- **Cercarial dermatitis:** **itchy maculopapular rash within 1–2 days** of penetration; it **often resolves before the traveller returns home**, hindering diagnosis.
- **Katayama fever (acute schistosomiasis):**
  - **2–8 weeks after primary infection** in some patients (many are asymptomatic).
  - **Named after the Katayama Valley, Japan.**
  - Commonest with heavy ***S. japonicum***, less common with *S. mansoni*, **rare with *S. haematobium***.
  - **Features:** **fever, dry cough, fatigue, headache, diarrhoea, eosinophilia**, neck pain, urticaria.
  - **Rare in endemic residents.**
  - **Non-specific**: often missed or mistaken for **malaria or enteric fever**.
- **Because acute infection can be silent, anyone exposed to potentially infested water should be considered for testing and treatment.**

###### Chronic Schistosomiasis

- **Egg-laying into the bladder begins 8–12 weeks after infection**, presenting with **painless, recurrent haematuria**, dysuria or frequency.
  - In highly endemic cultures **male haematuria is seen as a sign of puberty**; it can cause **anaemia**. **Proteinuria** is common.
  - **Haematuria is consistent and specific enough to serve as a primary diagnostic test in endemic areas**, but in **returning travellers is often not recognised as schistosomal**.
- **Ureteric disease:** ureteric eggs → granulomas, polyps, ulcers → **impaired pelvic and ureteric peristalsis** → **hydronephrosis, hydroureter, obstruction and vesicoureteric reflux**.
  - **Short-term infection: renal function can recover with anthelmintics.**
  - **Late or severe disease: ureteric repair or urinary diversion may be needed.**
- **FGS** affects **one-third to three-quarters of women infected with *S. haematobium***; eggs lodge in the **fallopian tubes, cervix, vagina, vulva, ovaries and uterus**.
  - **Friable sandy patches bleed on contact** (examination, intercourse).
  - **Dyspareunia, pelvic pain, bleeding, discharge, frequency and infertility** mimic UTI and sexually transmitted infection → **misdiagnosed and untreated**.
- **Male genital involvement:**
  - Heavy egg loads in the **ejaculatory ducts and seminal vesicles**; **blood or eggs can appear in the ejaculate before eggs appear in urine.**
  - Presents as a **testicular mass or scrotal pain**.
  - **Egg burden is higher in the epididymis, ovaries and tubes than in the testis, uterus and vagina.**

**STAGES OF CHRONIC URINARY SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| Stage | Egg activity | Clinical picture |
|---|---|---|
| **Active** | Eggs being laid; haematuria | Polypoid lesions, haematuria |
| **Late chronic active** | **Tissue egg burden peaks** | **"Schistosomal contracted bladder"**: chronic suprapubic and pelvic pain, urgency, frequency, incontinence. **Trigone normal or mildly hyperaemic**; **rest of detrusor thickened, indurated**. **Functional capacity as low as 50 mL** |
| **Quiescent** | Egg-laying and excretion fall; symptoms fade (**> 30% of light infections become asymptomatic**) | **Silent obstructive uropathy continues**: fibrosis replaces polyps → **insidious severe hydroureteronephrosis** |
| **Chronic inactive** | **No viable eggs** in urine or tissue | Sequelae of reaction to **calcified dead eggs**. **40–60% with schistosomal obstructive uropathy first reach a urologist at this end stage.** Poorly or **non-functioning kidneys in asymptomatic people** |

- **The trap:** **a normal urine egg count does not exclude severe disease.** The most damaged kidneys belong to inactive-stage patients, who have no eggs and often no symptoms.

- **Bacterial superinfection:**
  - **About half** with obstructive uropathy develop superimposed UTI from the **usual uropathogens**.
  - ***Salmonella* association:** chronic or recurrent *Salmonella* UTI with **intermittent bacteraemia**; bacteriuria may be **spill-over from the bloodstream**. ***Salmonella* shelters in the apical folds of the worm tegument**, protected from host defences and antibiotics.
  - **Treat both infections**; **antibiotics alone do not clear it.**
- **Bladder ulcers:**
  - **Acute ulcers are rare**, forming in the active stage when **necrotic polyps slough**.
  - **Chronic ulcers are commoner**, a **late sequel of heavy infection**, with **constant burning, intense suprapubic and pelvic pain**, usually **gross haematuria and pyuria**.
- **Eosinophilia:** **very common in acute**, **low-grade in chronic infection**; neither sensitive nor specific, but **a clue to parasitic infection**.
- **Exceptions to the usual sequence:** ectopic **pulmonary schistosomiasis, neuroschistosomiasis and FGS**.

**BLADDER — TB VS SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| | TB | Schistosomiasis |
|---|---|---|
| **Pathogenesis** | Descending infection from the kidney | **Egg deposition in the bladder wall** |
| **Trigone** | **Spared** (dome most affected) | **Normal or mildly hyperaemic** (rest of detrusor indurated) |
| **Contracted bladder** | **Thimble bladder < 20 mL** | Functional capacity **as low as 50 mL** |
| **Ureteric orifice** | **Golf-hole** | **Golf-hole** (dilated) or stenotic |
| **Ureteric calcification** | **Intraluminal**, **non-dilated** ureter (forms a cast) | **Intramural**, **dilated** ureter |
| **Malignancy** | SCC of the pelvis after metaplasia (rare) | **SCC 60–90% of schistosomal bladder cancers** |

*Concept —* **Chronic schistosomiasis dissociates symptoms from damage.** Egg-laying and symptoms decline over the years while fibrosis goes on obstructing the tract. The worst kidneys are therefore found in quiet, egg-negative patients in the inactive stage.



##### Diagnosis

- **Gold standard for active infection: *S. haematobium* eggs in urine or stool** (see table for timing).
- **Urine sampling:** **egg excretion peaks at noon**, so **collect between 09:00 and 15:00**; **concentrate the urine** to detect light infection.
- **No eggs but high suspicion, with serology showing exposure → tissue biopsy:**
  - **Rectal snip first**, before bladder biopsy: **eggs are common in rectal mucosa** and the **risks of bladder biopsy (infection, perforation) are avoided**.
  - **A squash preparation between glass slides beats histology**: **more sensitive and shows egg viability**.
- **Suspected FGS:** biopsy **vulval, vaginal or cervical lesions** for eggs.
- **Indirect markers** (**visual or dipstick haematuria, urine turbidity**) are used mainly in **control and elimination campaigns**, combined with other tests.

**DIAGNOSTIC TESTS FOR UG SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| Test | Indicates | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Urine or stool egg microscopy** | **Active infection (gold standard)** | Direct proof | Negative in the first **8–12 weeks**; insensitive in light infection |
| **Rectal snip / bladder biopsy (squash preparation)** | Active or past infection | **Egg viability** | Invasive |
| **Serology: FAST-ELISA + Western blot (CDC)** | **Exposure** | **> 90% sensitive and specific**; positive from **4–6 weeks** | **Cannot distinguish acute, chronic or cured**; titres persist after cure |
| **Circulating anodic and cathodic antigens (CAA, CCA), serum or urine** | **Active infection only** (released only by **live adult worms**) | **Quantitative** (intensity); **point-of-care urine CCA strip** | **CCA strip has failed to detect *S. haematobium*** (good for *S. mansoni*); expensive |
| **PCR (urine, stool, vaginal lavage for FGS)** | Infection | **Most sensitive method** | Needs trained staff, solvents, kits; **impractical in the field**; useful for **surveillance in low-transmission settings** |
| **Haematuria dipstick** | Indirect | Cheap, used in campaigns | Low sensitivity and specificity |

- **What the table is for:** sorting tests by **what a positive means**. **Only eggs and CAA/CCA prove active infection**; **serology proves exposure only**. The trap is using serology to judge cure.

- **Imaging:**

**IMAGING IN UG SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

| Modality | Findings and use |
|---|---|
| **US** | **Bladder and ureteric wall thickening, polyps, hydroureter, hydronephrosis, calcification**, even **bladder carcinoma** |
| **Plain film** | Urinary tract calcification. **Calcified bladder like a "fetal head in the pelvis" is characteristic of chronic disease.** Prostate, seminal vesicles, posterior urethra, distal ureters and occasionally colon can also calcify |
| **IVU** | **Earliest sign: striations of ureters and renal pelvis.** Also hydronephrosis, hydroureter, non-function, stenosis, **polypoid filling defects**. **Delayed films** in severe obstruction; **post-void views** show bladder neck obstruction. **IVU + fluoroscopy distinguishes tonic from atonic ureters** and shows non-stenotic immobile ureters |
| **CT** | **Obstruction plus colonic and urinary tract calcification** — an advantage over IVU |
| **MRI** | **No proven superiority**; not routine |
| **Voiding cystourethrography** | **Vesicoureteric reflux in about 25% of infected ureters** |
| **Cystourethroscopy ± retrograde pyelography** | Mucosal lesions (Fig. 31.13); ureteric anatomy and drainage |

- **Globally, most cases go undetected and untreated** because diagnosis relies on **direct egg detection** rather than more sensitive methods; better accessible diagnostics are needed for control.

##### Treatment

###### Medical Management

- **Praziquantel (PZQ) is the only WHO-recommended drug.** Single-drug dependence risks resistance, but PZQ is **effective, widely available and of low toxicity**, so there is little incentive for alternatives.
- **Dose for *S. haematobium*: 20 mg/kg twice on the same day, 6–8 hours apart**, or **40 mg/kg once**.
- **Katayama fever: add corticosteroids.**
- **Efficacy (egg reduction and cure rate): 60–90%.** Even without cure, **worm burden falls substantially**, reducing further sequelae.
- **Follow-up:** **urine and stool egg counts** and **serial UG ultrasound**; **repeat the course** if infection persists.
- **Tolerability:** **mild side effects** (abdominal pain, nausea, headache, dizziness) **within 3–4 hours**, settling spontaneously. **Large, bitter tablets** are difficult for children; a paediatric formulation is in development.
- **Pregnancy:** **FDA Category B** (animal safety data only), so many programmes **exclude pregnant and lactating women** — yet **adverse effects are rarely reported despite millions of pregnant women treated**.
- **Weak activity against schistosomulae** means lower cure rates where **transmission and reinfection** are high, and **PZQ cannot abort infection soon after exposure**. **Repeat doses several weeks apart** catch juveniles missed earlier that have since matured.
- **Resistance:** **little in endemic areas in large studies**; failures reported in **returning travellers and military personnel**. **Protection:** alternative drugs and **environmental control (snail control, sanitation)**.

**PRAZIQUANTEL VS ARTEMISININ DERIVATIVES**

| | Praziquantel | Artemether / artesunate |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage targeted** | **Adult worms** (weak against schistosomulae) | **Schistosomulae** |
| **Role** | **Treatment of established infection** | **Chemoprophylaxis** (**90–97% effective**); **poor treatment** of established infection |
| **Combination** | PZQ + artemisinin → **lower infection rates than PZQ alone** (useful for MDA in high-transmission areas) | |
| **Caution** | — | **Avoid wide use in malaria co-endemic areas** (risk of **artemisinin-resistant malaria**) |

- **What the pair tests:** the drugs are **complementary by life-cycle stage**; together they cover juvenile and adult worms. The limit is **malaria**, not schistosomiasis.

###### Surgical Management

- **Medical therapy first:** PZQ is effective and easy to give, and **early disease can reverse**.
- **Surgery is for** complications **unresponsive to adequate medical treatment** after reasonable follow-up, or those needing **immediate intervention** — e.g. **severe bladder haemorrhage**, a common cause of urgent surgery.
- **Bladder outlet:** **prostatitis and prostatic enlargement are uncommon** and **autopsies show no anatomical outlet obstruction**; but **cystoscopic and urodynamic findings and raised post-void residuals show *functional* outlet obstruction**, occasionally needing surgery in severe inactive disease.
- **Scrotum:** **induration, pain and enlargement from schistosomal epididymitis** can prompt **surgery for suspected testicular tumour**.
- **Irreversibly contracted bladder:** **vesical denervation, urinary diversion, ileocystoplasty or hydrodistension**. **Always combine with chemotherapy.**
- **Chronic deep bladder ulcer → partial cystectomy**; fulguration **rarely relieves symptoms or heals the ulcer**.
- **Urothelial hyperplasia** accompanies **severe disease**; **dysplasia and metaplasia** accompany **schistosomal bladder cancer**, which is managed surgically (see bladder cancer chapters).
- **Obstructive uropathy is the commonest sequel**; hydroureter and hydronephrosis track **infection intensity**.

**URETERIC OBSTRUCTION — ACTIVE VS LATE DISEASE**

| Active infection | Late chronic / inactive disease |
|---|---|
| **Concentric or hemi-concentric polypoid lesions** "girdle" the intramural and adjacent extravesical ureter | **Anatomical stenosis (± calculi)** in **up to 80%** of obstructed ureters |
| **Responds to medical treatment alone.** **Renal function recovers within 1–2 months of PZQ.** Chemotherapy also **prevents** obstruction despite reinfection | **Less responsive to chemotherapy**, so persistent stenosis needs **surgery** |

**SURGERY FOR RESIDUAL SCHISTOSOMAL URETERIC STENOSIS**

| Lesion | Procedure | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Anatomical stenosis | **Balloon dilatation** | Effective, but **mechanical dilatation often restenoses** |
| **Meatus, intramural ureter, vesicoureteric junction, distal ureter** | **Reimplantation with a functional valve: Politano–Leadbetter variants** | Highly effective in some; **restenosis can occur** |
| **Long or multifocal lesions** (too little ureter for reimplantation or ureteroureterostomy) | **Boari flap; ileal conduit; suprapubic intravesical ureterostomy** (peritoneal dialysis catheter bypasses the segment into the bladder); **ileal ureter** | **Keep the ileal segment isoperistaltic** |
| **Isolated meatal stenosis** | **Meatoplasty** | — |
| **Unreconstructable ureter** | **Long-term nephrostomy** | — |

##### Prognosis

- **Most have mild infection and a good prognosis.** Morbidity and mortality depend on **infection intensity** and **immune-response gene polymorphisms**.

**PROGNOSIS BY PREVALENCE SETTING**

| Low-prevalence region (e.g. Nigeria) | Egypt at 50% prevalence |
|---|---|
| **Essentially no related mortality**; little obstructive uropathy | Schistosomiasis **contributed to death in 10%** of infected |
| | **Severe disease: mortality approached 50% within 2–5 years** |

**TWO DEATH PROFILES**

| Death from obstructive uropathy | Death from pyelonephritis or urothelial cancer |
|---|---|
| **Patients in their 20s** with **heavy egg burdens** | **Patients over 40** |
| **Bilateral end-stage hydronephrosis**, or unilateral with contralateral non-schistosomal end-stage renal disease | Reflects **time- and intensity-related** pathology |

- **PZQ has transformed prognosis:** in **children, obstructive polyps usually resolve completely within 2–6 weeks**.
- **Chronic obstruction from sandy patches and fibrosis:** prognosis **less certain**; some tolerate advanced uropathy with little renal decline.
- **Obstructive uropathy, stones, outlet obstruction and bacterial cystitis all predispose to pyelonephritis.** **Bacterial superinfection can be life-threatening — treat promptly and aggressively.**
- **Bladder cancer:** prognosis depends on tumour aggressiveness.

##### Prevention and Control

- **Travellers: avoid fresh water** (streams, rivers, ponds, lakes); **fast-flowing water can still transmit.**
- **Cercariae are killed by** **heating water to > 125 °F (about 52 °C) for 5 minutes**, **chlorination**, or **standing water > 2 days, free of snails**.
- **PZQ was introduced in the late 1970s**, with **mass distribution from the 1980s and 1990s**; treatment is **simple and cheap, but control remains hard**.
- **WHO strategy: periodic, usually annual, targeted MDA with PZQ**, aimed at **reducing morbidity**. **Without safe water, cycles of infection, treatment and reinfection** demand **more frequent dosing**.
- **Cost:** **under US$0.50 per person** in campaigns, yet at scale this can exceed national budgets; **donated PZQ** from companies and foundations helps.

**BREAKING TRANSMISSION — THREE CONTROL LEVERS**

| Lever | Mechanism | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Sanitation + MDA** | Reduce **egg input** into water | Egg input must fall **about 90%** before transmission falls substantially, because **asexual multiplication in the snail amplifies** the parasite |
| **Health education** | Reduce **human water contact** | — |
| **Snail control** (molluscicides, biological control by predators or competitors) | Fewer intermediate hosts; theoretically **proportional reductions in transmission**; some call it the **best single measure** | **Adult worms live for years**, so snail control alone must continue for many years |

- **Integrated campaigns** (treat people + separate people and their waste from water + control snails) are most promising.
- **Water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) programmes** are back at the centre of control, with wider benefits.
- **Vaccine:** **none effective yet**.
- **Unlike malaria, lymphatic filariasis and onchocerciasis, global prevalence has not fallen**, but schistosomiasis is **eliminated in 10 countries: Iran, Japan, Lebanon, Malaysia, Martinique, Montserrat, Morocco, Thailand, Tunisia, Turkey**.
- **65th World Health Assembly, May 2012: resolution WHA65.21** called for intensified control and **elimination campaigns** — a **shift from morbidity control to elimination**.

**KEY POINTS: SCHISTOSOMIASIS**

- ***S. haematobium* worms survive in the host for years to decades.** A careful **travel and social history** identifies exposure, links it to UG symptoms and guides testing.
- **PZQ in early-stage UG schistosomiasis can reverse the inflammatory lesions, including fibrosis**, caused by the host response to eggs in tissue.
- **Gold standard for active infection: eggs in urine, stool, or bladder or rectal biopsy.** **Serology and PCR are highly sensitive but may not distinguish active from resolved infection**, and they are **impractical in endemic regions**.

</div>

*Concept —* **Schistosomal management follows the stage of the lesion.** Egg-driven polypoid disease is reversible with PZQ, so treat medically first. Fibrotic stenosis is not reversible and needs surgery. Transmission is held up by snails and sanitation, so drugs alone reduce morbidity but cannot eliminate the disease.



#### Filariasis

- **Filariae are vector-borne tissue nematodes.** Human pathogens include the agents of **lymphatic filariasis (LF)**, ***Onchocerca volvulus*** and ***Loa loa***.
- **LF is caused by the mosquito-borne *Wuchereria bancrofti*, *Brugia malayi* and *Brugia timori***, spanning **acute lymphatic inflammation to chronic lymphatic dilatation**, with **hydrocele, lymphoedema and elephantiasis**.

##### Organisms

- **Transmission and development:**
  - **A mosquito bite deposits third-stage (infective) larvae**, which migrate to the **central lymphatics** and mature over **6–9 months**.
  - **Adults (about 20–100 mm × 0.2 mm)** live in the **afferent lymphatics**, especially of the **lower limbs (inguinal, iliac, para-aortic)**; ***W. bancrofti* alone** also in the **male genitalia (epididymis, spermatic cord, testis)**. **Adult lifespan 5–7 years.**
  - **Females release microfilariae (about 200 × 10 µm)**.
  - **Periodicity:** microfilaraemia is **nocturnal (peaks at midnight)** in most areas, matching mosquito biting; **diurnal in parts of the Pacific**.
  - **In the mosquito, microfilariae become infective larvae over 10–14 days.**
- ***Wolbachia***: an **obligate rickettsia-like endosymbiont** of *W. bancrofti* and *Brugia*, needed for embryogenesis. **Doxycycline kills it**, **reducing microfilarial release and suppressing larval moulting**.

##### Epidemiology

- **About 68 million people have LF**, a large fall since the early 2000s.

**LYMPHATIC FILARIAE — DISTRIBUTION AND GENITAL INVOLVEMENT**

| Species | Share and distribution | Genital disease | Limb pattern | Circulating antigen test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ***W. bancrofti*** | **> 90% of infections**: sub-Saharan Africa, South and South-East Asia, western Pacific. **Americas: only Haiti, Dominican Republic, Guyana, Brazil** | **Almost exclusively this species; very common** | **Whole limb** | **Yes (ELISA, ICT card)** |
| ***B. malayi*** | **Asia and Pacific islands** (e.g. Indonesia, Philippines) | Uncommon | **Leg below the knee** | **None** (antibody dipstick available) |
| ***B. timori*** | **South-eastern Indonesia only** | Uncommon | Below the knee | None |

- **The trap:** **genital filariasis means *W. bancrofti***; Brugian infection spares the genitals and affects the limb below the knee.

- **Vectors:** ***Anopheles*** (rural Africa, Pacific), ***Culex*** (urban areas, especially India), ***Aedes aegypti*** (some Pacific islands), and others.
- **Transmission is inefficient**, so **obstructive lymphatic disease needs repeated infection over many years** and is seen mainly in **long-term residents**.
- **Prevalence rises through childhood to the third or fourth decade**, then plateaus as adult worms accumulate. **Lymphoedema and genital disease are rare before age 10.**
- **About one-third of the infected have overt disease**, **more likely in India, Papua New Guinea and Africa**, less in the Americas.

##### Pathology and Clinical Manifestations

- **Immune phases:**
  - **Early (larvae, young adults):** **pro-inflammatory, mixed Th1 and Th2**; filaria-specific **IgE rises** and **eosinophils kill microfilariae**.
  - **With microfilaraemia:** T-cell responses **down-regulated** by **IL-10, IgG4 blocking antibody and antigen-specific suppressor T cells**.
  - **Some stay uninfected despite long exposure**; whether protective immunity develops is unclear.
- **Damage is cumulative** (progressive scarring, lymphatic obstruction). **Treatment does not readily reverse it but can halt progression** in active infection.
- **LF is rarely fatal but causes the third-highest loss of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) among parasitic infections.**
- **How lymphoedema develops:**
  - **Parasite factors dilate lymphatics** → **secondary bacterial infection** and **inflammation against dying worms** (and pro-inflammatory ***Wolbachia***).
  - Histology: **granulomas around worms**, ± **tissue eosinophils** (Fig. 31.15).
  - **Vicious cycle:** acute attack → worse lymphoedema → more infection → worse lymphoedema.
  - End result: **obliterated, scarred lymphatics**, with **elephantiasis or hydrocele**.

###### Subclinical Infection

- **Most infected people have few symptoms, even with heavy microfilaraemia**, yet **almost all patent infections cause subclinical disease**: **dilated lymphatics, scrotal lymphangiectasia, microscopic haematuria, proteinuria**. **Eosinophilia is very common.**

###### Acute Adenolymphangitis

- **Acute adenolymphangitis (ADL) is often the first manifestation:** **fever, lymphadenitis, lymphangitis and oedema** for days to a week.

**FILARIAL VS BACTERIAL LYMPHANGITIS**

| Filarial (ADL) | Bacterial |
|---|---|
| **Retrograde**: spreads **peripherally**, away from the node | Ascending: spreads **centrally** |

- **Genital lymphatics (*W. bancrofti*):** **funiculitis, epididymitis, scrotal pain and tenderness**; **lymph scrotum** — ruptured scrotal lymphatic vesicles → **whitish discharge** and **secondary bacterial infection**.
- **Dermatolymphangioadenitis (DLA):** **fever, chills, myalgia, headache**; **oedematous inflammatory plaques**, hyperpigmentation, vesicles and ulcers, often at a **skin injury**. Inflammation spreads **proximally**; thought to be **bacterial**.

###### Lymphoedema

- **Limb oedema is the commonest chronic manifestation**: **lower limb more than upper**, usually **asymmetric** (limb pattern by species in the table above).
- The skin may **exude serous fluid**. **The breast can be involved in women.**

###### Genitourinary Manifestations

- **Male genital disease** (frequency by species in the table above); female genital involvement is poorly characterised and appears uncommon.
- **Genital disease is not usually seen before the teenage years.**
- **Acute painful (usually unilateral) epididymitis or funiculitis** with fever and malaise lasts several days — **one of the commonest consequences** of bancroftian infection.

**Funiculoepididymitis**
- **Palpable, cord-like swelling with oedema.** Usually **self-limiting**, but **recurrence and chronic lymphoedema are common**.
- **Sterility and orchitis are rare, because the testis is usually spared**.
- **Often mistaken for malignancy** → unnecessary surgery, **including orchidectomy**.
- **A varicocele can complicate the inflammation**, adding pain and swelling.
- **Bacterial superinfection is rare but severe**: **exquisite pain, septic thrombophlebitis**.

**Hydrocele**
- **The commonest result of chronic male genital disease**; can be **very large** (Fig. 31.16).
- **In endemic areas filarial and non-filarial hydroceles are hard to distinguish**; **parasites are rarely found in the fluid.**
- **Suggesting LF:** **cord or epididymal nodules** plus **residence in or travel to an endemic area**; a **thick fibrous tunica**, especially with **cholesterol or calcium deposits**.
- **Painless** unless complicated by acute epididymitis or funiculitis. Scrotal skin may be **thickened, brawny, oozing lymph**.
- **Superinfection:** **rare in filarial hydrocele**; **common with elephantiasis and lymph scrotum**.

**Scrotal and penile elephantiasis**
- **Mild scrotal oedema** is common early or with hydrocele; **penile oedema is unusual.**
- **Massive scrotal or penile enlargement appears late**, mainly where care is poorly accessible.
- **Genital elephantiasis rarely has a cause other than LF.**

**Chyluria**
- **Damaged GU lymphatics leak lymph into urine** → **heavy fat and protein loss**, possible **malnutrition**. Rare.
- **Occurs earlier than genital elephantiasis**, is **usually intermittent**, and can **remit spontaneously**.

###### Tropical Pulmonary Eosinophilia

- **Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia (TPE):** **paroxysmal, mainly nocturnal cough and wheeze**, fever, lymphadenopathy, **high-grade eosinophilia, raised IgE**.
- **Cause:** **allergic response to microfilarial antigens**; **commonest in South and South-East Asia**.
- **Chest radiograph:** normal to diffuse reticulonodular shadowing. **Lung function:** restrictive (sometimes obstructive). **Biopsy:** eosinophilic interstitial pneumonitis.
- **Microfilaraemia is usually absent** — the trap: a negative blood smear does not exclude TPE.

##### Diagnosis

- **Epidemiological context:** in endemic areas lymphoedema or male genital disease is **more likely LF**, once other causes of secondary oedema are excluded.
- **Differential of funiculoepididymitis: TB, *S. haematobium*, gonorrhoea.** **Non-filarial hydrocele is common everywhere**, but in endemic areas hydroceles are **more frequent and appear younger**.

**DIAGNOSTIC TESTS FOR LYMPHATIC FILARIASIS**

| Test | Detail | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Adult worm on histology** | **Definitive** | **Insensitive**; worms lie deep in lymphatics |
| **Ultrasound of lymphatics** | **"Filarial dance sign"**: characteristic movement of live adults; **≥ 80% sensitive** in some settings | Operator-dependent |
| **Plain radiograph** | Calcifications, suggestive in context | Non-specific |
| **Giemsa-stained blood smear** | **Microfilariae. Time sample to local periodicity (usually midnight)** | **Positive in only a minority** of infected |
| **Circulating antigen (ELISA; immunochromatographic card test, ICT)** | ***W. bancrofti* only.** **Alere Filariasis Test Strip more sensitive** in the field than older **BinaxNOW** ICT | **None exists for *Brugia*** |
| **PCR (blood)** | *W. bancrofti* and *B. malayi*; **very sensitive** | Not widely available |
| **Antibody** | Traditionally **poorly specific**. **IgG4** less cross-reactive, so more specific; species-specific antigens help; **dipstick for *Brugia*** | Cannot confirm active infection |
| **Lymphoscintigraphy** | **Reliably shows lymphatic abnormality** and extent | **Does not distinguish LF from other lymphatic disease** |

- **"Burned-out" infection** (after treatment, or years after leaving an endemic area): **lasting lymphoedema or genital damage** with **negative microfilariae and antigen**. **Negative tests do not exclude LF**; these patients are **usually antibody positive**.

##### Treatment

- **Treat all patients with microfilaraemia, symptomatic or not**, because almost all have subclinical disease.

**ANTIFILARIAL DRUGS — TARGETS, DOSES AND TOXICITY**

| Drug | Target | Regimen (LF unless stated) | Signature caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Diethylcarbamazine (DEC)** | **Microfilaricidal**; **only modest activity against adults** | **Drug of choice for active LF**: **2 mg/kg orally three times daily**. **1-day course as effective as the traditional 12-day course**. **TPE: 2–3 weeks**. In the United States **available only through the CDC** | **Contraindicated where onchocerciasis or *Loa loa* may coexist** (West and Central Africa) unless excluded. Reactions: fever, chills, arthralgia, myalgia, headache, nausea, vomiting; in heavy infection **painful nodules, lymphadenitis, epididymitis** from **dying worms and *Wolbachia*** |
| **Albendazole** | **Microfilaricidal and macrofilaricidal** | **400 mg twice daily for 21 days** | Few side effects in single-dose MDA regimens |
| **Ivermectin** | **Mostly microfilaricidal** | **150–400 µg/kg once** | Side effects similar to DEC; **caution if *Loa loa* possible** |
| **Doxycycline** | **Anti-*Wolbachia***; some **macrofilaricidal** activity; **sterilises adults** | **200 mg daily for 4–8 weeks** (6 weeks reasonable in the United States) | **Not in pregnancy or young children**; long course hard to deliver in resource-poor settings |

- **What the table is for:** **most antifilarials kill microfilariae, not adults** — hence control needs **years of repeated dosing** until adults die of old age.
- **Doxycycline is the exception**: it **improves lymphoedema and hydrocele even without active infection**, suggesting benefit **beyond its anti-*Wolbachia* action**.
- **The trap:** **DEC with onchocerciasis or loiasis can cause severe reactions**, including blindness in onchocerciasis.

- **Chronic lymphoedema:** control morbidity by **preventing secondary bacterial infection, hygiene, elastic stockings, elevation, physiotherapy**. **Antiparasitics only if infection is active.**
- **Surgery:**
  - **Lymphatic–venous and nodal–venous anastomoses** have reduced leg swelling, and **reconstruction** has helped genital disease; **long-term results unknown**.
  - **Genital elephantiasis rarely suits surgery**; **lymphadenectomy can worsen lymphatic drainage.**
  - **Funiculoepididymitis:** **decompress or excise filarial nodules** to preserve testis and cord; if recurrent, painful, deforming or with vascular involvement → **more radical surgery**.
  - **Hydrocele:**
    - **Aspiration gives immediate relief** but **recurs** without medical and definitive surgical treatment.
    - **Hydrocelectomy for large or symptomatic hydroceles**: **excision of the intact sac is the procedure of choice**; **inversion with partial excision** is an alternative.
    - **Suture or excise leaking or dilated lymphatics.**
    - **Small, non-enlarging hydroceles need no surgery.**
  - **Scrotal or vulval reconstruction** with removal of redundant tissue helps selected patients.

##### Prevention and Control

- **Personal protection:** avoid mosquitoes; **long-lasting insecticide-treated bednets (LLINs)** are proven for control and elimination.
- **Clearing microfilariae from a community interrupts transmission**, because **the mosquito needs microfilaraemia to transmit**. But **drugs do not kill all adults**, so **intermittent dosing must continue for years** until adults die of senescence. This works for ***W. bancrofti*** but is **harder for *Brugia***, which has **animal reservoirs**.
- **MDA: single annual doses of albendazole plus ivermectin or DEC** (sustained microfilaricidal effect):
  - **Africa: albendazole + ivermectin** (DEC avoided because onchocerciasis and *Loa* are co-endemic).
  - **Elsewhere: albendazole + DEC.**
  - **MDA is the main reason global prevalence has fallen**; **about 19 million hydroceles prevented since 2000**, halving LF hydroceles.
- **Triple therapy (DEC + albendazole + ivermectin):** small studies show **deeper, longer microfilarial suppression** than dual therapy, **with no extra serious adverse effects**.

##### Onchocerciasis

- **River blindness**, caused by ***O. volvulus*** (vector in the comparison table).
  - **99% of cases in Africa**; foci in **Brazil, Venezuela and Yemen**.
  - **About 17 million infected**, about half the 2000 figure, thanks to **MDA**.
  - Transmission is **inefficient and focal**.
- **Adults live up to 15 years (mean 9–10) in subcutaneous nodules**; microfilariae migrate through **skin and eye**.
- **Clinical features:** **dermatitis and pruritus, keratitis, chorioretinitis** → **blindness from corneal scarring** after many years.
- **Urological relevance:** late **"hanging groin"** or **scrotal elephantiasis** from recurrent lymphadenitis and lost skin elasticity. **Histology:** atrophic, fibrotic inguinal nodes with subcutaneous oedema and fibrosis. Occasionally **massive inguinal lymphadenopathy**.
- **Diagnosis:** **skin snips** for microfilariae; **adult worms in nodules**; **microfilariae in the anterior chamber on slit lamp**. Serology is less developed than for LF.
- ***Onchocerca lupi*** (a dog parasite): a **rare human pathogen** — **six United States residents since 2013** (five children), all in the **south-west**, with nodules containing adult worms (spine, skin and soft tissue, extraocular muscle).
- **Treatment:**
  - **Ivermectin 150 µg/kg once, repeated every 6–12 months until asymptomatic.**
  - It acts mainly on microfilariae, but **repeated doses shorten adult lifespan by about half or more**.
  - **Adverse effects:** fever, rash, dizziness, pruritus, myalgia, arthralgia, lymphadenopathy, from **dying filariae and *Wolbachia***.
  - **Caution if *Loa* co-infection is possible.**
  - **Then doxycycline 200 mg/day for 6 weeks**: **kills or shortens the life of most adult females and sterilises the rest**, clearing or greatly reducing microfilariae.
  - **DEC** causes **blindness and systemic toxicity** from inflammation against dying parasites.

##### Loiasis

- ***Loa loa*:** **Central and West Africa**, transmitted by ***Chrysops* flies**. **Adults migrate through subcutaneous tissue.**
- **Clinical features:**
  - **Most have asymptomatic eosinophilia.**
  - Some have urticaria, **migratory subcutaneous swellings** and **"eye worm"** (adult crossing the conjunctiva).
  - **Haematuria and proteinuria in about 30%**; **lymphadenitis and hydrocele rare.**
- **Treatment:**
  - **DEC 2–3 mg/kg orally three times daily for 14–21 days** is effective; multiple courses may be needed.
  - **Treatment reactions:** pruritus, arthralgia, migratory swellings, fever, eye worms, diarrhoea, **renal failure**.
  - **Encephalopathy risk with high microfilaraemia (> 2500–8000 microfilariae/mL), especially with ivermectin**; **pre-treatment apheresis** may reduce it.
  - **Albendazole 200 mg twice daily for 3 weeks** has a **lower encephalopathy risk than DEC** and may be **safer with high parasitaemia**.

**THE THREE FILARIAL INFECTIONS COMPARED**

| | Lymphatic filariasis | Onchocerciasis | Loiasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Organism** | *W. bancrofti*, *B. malayi*, *B. timori* | *O. volvulus* | *L. loa* |
| **Vector** | **Mosquito** (*Anopheles*, *Culex*, *Aedes*) | ***Simulium* black fly** | ***Chrysops* fly** |
| **Adult site** | **Lymphatics** (± male genitalia, *W. bancrofti*) | **Subcutaneous nodules** | **Migrating subcutaneously** |
| **Microfilarial periodicity** | **Nocturnal** (diurnal in parts of the Pacific) | In skin and eye | **Diurnal** |
| ***Wolbachia*** | **Yes** | **Yes** | **No** |
| **Urological disease** | **Hydrocele, funiculoepididymitis, elephantiasis, chyluria** | **Hanging groin, scrotal elephantiasis** | **Haematuria and proteinuria (about 30%)**; rarely hydrocele |
| **Drug of choice** | **DEC** (Africa: ivermectin + albendazole in MDA) | **Ivermectin** → doxycycline | **DEC** (albendazole if high load) |
| **Contraindicated** | DEC where *Onchocerca* or *Loa* is co-endemic | **DEC** | **Ivermectin (and DEC) with high microfilaraemia** → encephalopathy |

*Concept —* **Filarial treatment depends on which filaria is present.** Drugs kill microfilariae rather than adults, so the killing is what causes harm: DEC blinds in onchocerciasis, and DEC or ivermectin can cause encephalopathy in heavy *Loa* infection. *Wolbachia* is present in LF and *Onchocerca* but absent in *Loa*, which is why doxycycline is useful in the first two and not the third.



#### Other Non-filarial Genitourinary Parasites

##### Echinococcosis

- ***Echinococcus granulosus***, a **cestode (tapeworm)**, causes **cystic echinococcosis (hydatid disease)**.
- **Transmission:** **ingested eggs in contaminated food or water**, or **contact with infected dogs**. **High prevalence in pastoral communities**: South America, the Mediterranean littoral, Eastern Europe, the Middle East, East Africa, Central Asia, China, Russia, Australia.
- **Cyst sites:** usually **liver**, less often **lung**; **kidney third** (see table).
- **Natural history:** **initially asymptomatic**, growing **1–2 cm per year** → **pain or palpable mass**. **Hydatiduria and renal colic in a minority.** **Renal function usually preserved.**
- **Imaging:** **thick-walled, fluid-filled spherical cyst, often with a calcified wall**; the appearance **defines the stage**, which guides management.
- **Serology is adjunctive only** (sensitivity 60–90%).
- **Treatment:**
  - **Puncture, aspiration, injection, re-aspiration (PAIR)** suits **liver cysts, not lung or kidney cysts**, which have only **surgical resection or antiparasitic drugs**.
  - **Albendazole** (regimen in table) is the recommended medical treatment; **surgical excision** for some lesions because of size or location.
  - **Cyst rupture can cause anaphylaxis.**
  - **Perioperative PZQ + albendazole** may reduce **secondary seeding**.

##### Enterobiasis

- ***Enterobius vermicularis* (pinworm)** occurs **worldwide**, temperate and tropical.
- **Life cycle:** worms live in the **proximal colon** → migrate to **perianal skin to lay eggs**, **infective after 6 hours**. Spread **person to person** (faecal–oral, hands, fomites).
- **Clinical features:**
  - **Usually asymptomatic**; **perianal pruritus** can be severe.
  - **Ectopic migration in women** via **vagina, uterus and tubes into the peritoneum**: dead worms and eggs cause **granulomas and adhesions** → **vulval and cervical granulomas, salpingitis, oophoritis, tubo-ovarian abscess, appendicitis, peritonitis**.
  - **Men:** rare epididymal involvement and inguinal hernia.
- **Treatment:** single-dose albendazole or mebendazole (**highly effective**; doses in table); alternative **ivermectin 200 µg/kg once**. **Treat household and close contacts**; the **2-week repeat** covers **reinfection and autoinfection**.

##### Amoebiasis

- ***Entamoeba histolytica*:** a **protozoan** spread **faecal–orally**, commonest in the tropics.
- **Most infected are asymptomatic**; **about 10% develop disease in other organs, including the kidney.** **Cutaneous amoebiasis** causes **painful perianal and genital ulcers**.
- **Treatment:**
  - **Tissue phase:** **tinidazole 2 g/day for 3–5 days** or **metronidazole 750 mg three times daily for 10 days**.
  - **Then a luminal agent:** **paromomycin 8–12 mg/kg three times daily for 7 days** or **iodoquinol 650 mg three times daily for 20 days**.

##### Trichomoniasis

- ***Trichomonas vaginalis***, a common **sexually transmitted protozoan**, is covered in **Chapter 29**.

**OTHER NON-FILARIAL GU PARASITES — SUMMARY**

| Parasite | Type | Transmission | GU manifestation | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ***E. granulosus*** | **Cestode** | Eggs from dogs, food, water | **Renal hydatid cyst** (third site, about 2–3%); hydatiduria | **Albendazole 400 mg twice daily for 1–6 months ± surgery**; **no PAIR for the kidney** |
| ***E. vermicularis*** | Nematode | Person to person, faecal–oral | Ectopic female genital tract and pelvic granulomas; rarely epididymis | **Albendazole 400 mg or mebendazole 100 mg once; repeat at 2 weeks; treat contacts** |
| ***E. histolytica*** | **Protozoan** | Faecal–oral | Renal disease; **painful genital and perianal ulcers** | **Tinidazole or metronidazole, then paromomycin or iodoquinol** |
| ***T. vaginalis*** | Protozoan | **Sexual** | — | See Chapter 29 |

**KEY POINTS: LYMPHATIC FILARIASIS**

- **LF is caused by three species of tissue nematode transmitted by mosquitoes.**
- **LF is endemic in much of the tropics but transmitted inefficiently.** Clinically apparent disease is usually seen only in **long-term residents** of endemic countries.
- **Diagnosis:** blood smears, antibody and antigen assays, imaging and clinical assessment.
- **Treatment of active LF is DEC.** **Ivermectin and albendazole are also active, and all three are used in mass chemotherapy.**
