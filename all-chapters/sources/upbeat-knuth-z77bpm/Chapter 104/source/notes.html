Chapter 104 — Pathophysiology and Classification of Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction

  • The lower urinary tract (LUT) works as one interrelated unit whose adult purpose is low-pressure storage with continence and complete voluntary expulsion, also at low pressure.
  • Normal sensation: a gradually rising, deferrable sense of fullness up to a volume that prevents frequency — without pain or urgency (a sudden compelling desire to void that is difficult to defer).
  • The adult LUT is under voluntary neural control — unlike other autonomically innervated viscera, which are regulated solely by involuntary mechanisms.
  • The micturition cycle is taught as two discrete phases — filling/storage and emptying/voiding — operating in an on–off fashion: inhibit voiding reflex + activate storage reflexes ⇄ inhibit storage reflexes + activate voiding reflex.

Bladder Response During Filling

  • Physiological filling → almost no pressure change; once the wall unfolds, high compliance (Δ volume/Δ pressure) reflects elastic and viscoelastic properties.
    • Elasticity — wall constituents stretch without any rise in tension.
    • Viscoelasticity — stretch raises tension, which then decays (“stress relaxation”) when filling slows; set by the extracellular matrix (elastic fibres, collagen).
  • Smooth muscle may make continuous asynchronous length adjustments (never synchronous, pressure-raising activity); actomyosin cross-bridge regulation may set compliance. The bladder appears “relaxed”; the urothelium expands yet keeps its barrier.
  • A possible non-neurogenic active storage component: an unidentified urothelial relaxing factor; urothelial nitric oxide (NO) may also inhibit afferents.
  • Passive compliance = viscoelastic stroma (collagen, elastin) + urodynamically non-contractile detrusor.
  • Klevmark: the pressure rise on cystometry is an artefact of supraphysiological filling; physiologically, none until capacity.
  • ↑ collagen → ↓ compliance: chronic inflammation, bladder outlet obstruction (BOO), neurological decentralisation, injury; hypertrophic detrusor is less elastic and makes more collagen. Once established, collagen-driven low compliance is generally drug-unresponsive.
  • Neural storage reflexes (clear in animals, indirect in humans): mainly sympathetic inhibition of cholinergic ganglionic transmission, plus:
    • α1-adrenergic stimulation at the smooth sphincter → ↑ outlet resistance.
    • β3-adrenergic (inhibitory) stimulation of the bladder body → ↓ wall tension.
    • Pudendal afferents from the striated sphincter directly inhibit sacral detrusor motor neurons.
    • Other inhibitory transmitters: glycine, γ-aminobutyric acid, opioids, purines, noradrenergic system.
    • Distension releases urothelial factors (acetylcholine, adenosine triphosphate, NO, prostaglandins, peptides) that modulate contractility.
  • Current urodynamics cannot measure true detrusor wall tension.

Outlet Response During Filling

  • Proximal urethral pressure rises gradually during filling, tracking efferent pudendal firing and sphincter electromyographic activity — the guarding reflex (spinal somatic). A sympathetic smooth-sphincter contribution is plausible but very hard to prove.
  • Urethral pressure = active muscle plus passive elastic, collagenous and vascular components; wall tension compresses a submucosal “filler material” — the softer, the less pressure continence needs.
  • Mucosal seal mechanism: the lumen must close watertight — a greased rubber tube closes with less pressure; scarred or atrophic mucosa seals poorly.
  • Oestrogen: shared urogenital sinus origin, urethral receptors, menopausal urinary incontinence (UI) — yet oestrogen for postmenopausal UI remains controversial.

Voiding With a Normal Bladder Contraction

  • Trigger: rising intravesical pressure producing the sensation of distension.
  • Parasympathetic outflow (pelvic nerve) is sacral, but the coordinating centre is the rostral brainstem, modulated by cortex.
  • Sequence: inhibit somatic efferents to striated sphincter + all spinal sympathetic storage reflexes → parasympathetic pelvic-nerve efferents → coordinated contraction of bulk detrusor.
  • Outlet resistance falls by: continence reflexes ceasing; active smooth-sphincter relaxation via a non-adrenergic non-cholinergic (NANC) mechanism, probably NO; funnelling — longitudinal smooth-muscle continuity of base and proximal urethra shortens and widens the urethra.
  • Reflexes from contraction and urethral flow reinforce emptying; supraspinal inputs give conscious control.

Urinary Continence During Abdominal Pressure Increases

  • Why does coughing not open the outlet? (1) No coordinated contraction is triggered — raised intravesical pressure ≠ emptying ability; (2) urethral entry needs a neurally mediated contraction plus bladder neck conformational change.
  • Key protection: at least equal pressure transmission to the proximal urethra (and female mid-urethra) — Enhorning (1961). Its failure invariably accompanies effort-related UI in both sexes.
  • Tanagho (1978): closure pressure rises with abdominal pressure — first direct evidence of an active reflex component beyond passive transmission.

ABNORMALITIES OF FILLING/STORAGE AND EMPTYING/VOIDING: OVERVIEW OF PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

  • Excluding psychological causes, every failure to fill, store or empty is due to the bladder, the outlet, or both — the basis of the Wein functional classification.
  • Some patients have combined storage and emptying failure — e.g. suprasacral spinal cord injury: DO + striated sphincter dyssynergia; or DO during filling with detrusor underactivity during emptying.
  • Failure is usually relative. The scheme expands (Box 104.2), but the simple form avoids argument when mechanism is uncertain, and files every test (Table 104.1) and treatment (Boxes 104.3–104.4) by phase and site.

Filling and Storage Failure

  • Results from bladder overactivity (involuntary contraction and/or ↓ compliance), ↓ outlet resistance, heightened or altered sensation, or a combination.

Bladder Overactivity

  • Expressed as phasic involuntary contractions, low compliance, or both.
  • Involuntary contractions: neurological disease, BOO, stress urinary incontinence (SUI) (urine entering the proximal urethra may trigger one), ageing, or idiopathic; micromotions may drive afferents to DO. Also ↑ afferent input (inflammation) or ↑ sensitivity: urothelial excitatory transmitters → DO or heightened sensation.
  • Urgency urinary incontinence (UUI) → assume DO. Urgency alone suggests DO, often not shown on urodynamic studies (UDS); urodynamic DO may be asymptomatic.
  • Overactive bladder (OAB) — International Continence Society (ICS): urgency ± UUI, usually with frequency and nocturia. Possible mechanisms:
    1. ↓ suprapontine inhibition
    2. Damaged spinal axonal paths
    3. Damaged peripheral axonal paths
    4. Loss of peripheral inhibition
    5. Enhanced excitatory transmission in the micturition reflex
    6. ↑ LUT afferent input
    7. Idiopathic
  • ↓ pelvic floor stimulation may contribute; pelvic floor contraction engages the frontal-cortex voluntary inhibition reflex — basis of bladder training.

Filling and Storage Failure Resulting From Altered Sensation

  • Storage failure without overactivity — inflammation, irritation, hypersensitivity, pain (chemical, psychological, idiopathic); classically interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS). ↑ afferent activity gives DO, urgency without DO, premature fullness alone, or pain on filling.

Filling and Storage Failure From Remodeling of the Bladder Leading to Decreased Compliance

  • ↓ compliance: neurological injury, usually sacral or infrasacral, or anything destroying wall elasticity, e.g. collagen from long-standing BOO.

Outlet Underactivity

  • ↓ outlet resistance from damage to sphincter innervation or structure, or to female outlet support: neurological disease, surgical/mechanical trauma, ageing.

Contemporary view: most female effort UI = varying mix of support loss and ISD. Incontinence can be due to ISD alone, never to hypermobility alone — some ISD must exist.
  • DeLancey’s hammock hypothesis: SUI with hypermobility requires an unstable suburethral supportive layer. Firm layer → abdominal pressure compresses the urethra; lax/mobile layer → compression fails.
  • SUI = pelvic floor muscle, nerve or connective tissue damage; the female urethra is supported by levator ani via anterior vaginal wall endopelvic fascia. Loss of bladder neck closure causes UI even with normal support.
  • Continence was once placed solely at the bladder neck, but in about half of continent females urine enters the urethra on straining; their continence point (highest pressure transmission) is the mid-urethra.
  • Urethral instability: episodic urethral pressure falls without bladder or abdominal change; rarely demonstrable; probably the urethral half of a reflex in a bladder that does not measurably contract.
  • Males: categories theoretically parallel, but male sphincteric incontinence is not associated with hypermobility — it resembles female intrinsic sphincter dysfunction. Almost no data on male urethral instability.
  • Treatment of storage failure: inhibit contractility, ↓ sensory output, mechanically ↑ capacity, or ↑ outlet resistance (continuously or only during abdominal pressure rises).

Emptying and Voiding Failure

  • Results from ↓ bladder contractility (magnitude, coordination or duration), ↑ outlet resistance, or both.

Bladder Underactivity

  • Temporary or permanent failure of the neuromuscular mechanisms that initiate and sustain a detrusor contraction.
  • In the neurologically normal: reflex inhibition from ↑ pelvic/perineal afferent input, or psychogenic.
  • Iatrogenic: colorectal, urological and gynaecological surgery.
  • Myogenic: overdistension; central or peripheral drugs; severe infection; fibrosis.
  • Diabetes mellitus: combined neuromuscular and vascular mechanism.

Outlet Overactivity or Obstruction

  • ICS bladder outflow obstruction = reduced flow and/or raised post-void residual (PVR) with raised detrusor pressure; much commoner in males; usually anatomic, sometimes functional (sphincter fails to relax or contracts).
  • Hallmark of BOO, whatever the cause: high detrusor pressure producing low flow.

ABNORMALITIES OF PAIN: CHRONIC PELVIC PAIN SYNDROME

  • Hallmark of IC/BPS is pain, but frequency and urgency are very common.
  • Classic urgency is “a compelling need to urinate which is difficult to postpone”; IC/BPS urgency is qualitatively different — driven by pain, pressure or discomfort that worsens with filling.
  • May present with a single symptom, then progresses to the full constellation (pain, nocturia, urgency/frequency) over about two years.

Central Sensitization

  • Definition: ↑ responsiveness of central nociceptive neurons to normal or subthreshold afferent input. Implicated in fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, headache, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) — and recently theorised in OAB urgency and frequency.

Abnormalities of Nocturia

  • Nocturia: waking one or more times during intended sleep to void; variable bother; from LUT dysfunction and/or other medical conditions.

Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction

  • ICS definition of NLUTD: abnormal or difficult bladder/urethral (± prostatic) function in the context of a clinically confirmed relevant neurological disorder.
  • Symptoms are related to storage, voiding and post-voiding; diagnoses combine symptoms with signs, often urodynamic.

Classification by Function

  • Weighs symptoms and UDS together to set the timing and order of treatment.
  • Combination deficits must be recognised. Classic example — T10 paraplegia after spinal shock: storage failure (DO) + emptying failure (striated sphincter dyssynergia). Designate one deficit primary, treat it, address the other secondarily:
    • Circumvent emptying (external collection device) → treat as primary storage failure; or
    • Abolish DO (drugs or surgery) → treat resulting emptying failure with clean intermittent catheterisation.
  • Other combinations: impaired contractility or overactivity with sphincter dysfunction; BOO with DO; BOO with sphincter malfunction; DO with impaired contractility.

  • Examination shows SUI (urethral leak synchronous with effort) and anterior vaginal wall prolapse, which may accompany UI or mask it by kinking the urethra; pelvic floor function is graded vaginally or rectally.

  • Storage and voiding described separately, each for bladder and urethra. DO: involuntary filling contractions, spontaneous or provoked — neurogenic with a relevant neurological condition, else idiopathic.
