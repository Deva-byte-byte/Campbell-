Chapter 157 — Diagnosis and Staging of Prostate Cancer

Presentation

  • Early prostate cancer is rarely symptomatic; symptoms imply locally advanced or metastatic disease.
  • Locally advanced: obstructive urinary symptoms, ureteric obstruction (pain, renal failure), haematuria, reduced ejaculate volume, rarely erectile dysfunction.
  • Metastatic: bone pain, pathological fracture, lower-limb oedema; less often malignant retroperitoneal fibrosis, paraneoplastic syndromes, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), paralysis from spinal cord compression.
  • Such presentations are now uncommon because of screening with prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and digital rectal examination (DRE); diagnosis is usually histological, by transrectal ultrasound (TRUS)-guided needle biopsy, before symptoms.
  • Staging aims to define disease extent and risk for management and prognosis, using PSA, DRE, biopsy grade and volume, and imaging (locoregional extent, exclusion of metastases).

Diagnosis

Screening

General Concepts of Screening

  • Screening tests healthy asymptomatic populations; diagnosis identifies disease in those with signs or symptoms.
  • Goal: better outcomes through earlier treatment — but screening carries harm from overdiagnosis and overtreatment; early diagnosis is not invariably beneficial.
  • Screening reduces advanced disease at diagnosis and, in more recent data, prostate cancer mortality; the benefit–harm balance remains contested and depends on the individual's characteristics and preferences.
  • Since widespread PSA testing (early 1990s): prostate cancer mortality has fallen by more than 40% and the proportion of advanced-stage disease at diagnosis by about 75% (Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results [SEER] data); modelling attributes a substantial share of the mortality fall to screening.
  • Randomised trials comparing screened with unscreened populations are the highest level of evidence.

Concept — Screening reduces mortality but at the price of overdiagnosis; every recommendation is an attempt to shift that balance by selecting age, interval and patient preference, not by denying the benefit.

Diagnostic Modalities

Digital Rectal Examination

  • Before PSA, DRE was the sole detection tool; it has only fair reproducibility, even among experienced examiners, and misses many early cancers.
  • Limited screening value at PSA <3.0 ng/mL; some evidence that DRE does not reduce mortality, and some bodies advise against it as a routine screen.
  • Drawbacks: blood-only testing lets reflex tests run on the same sample; DRE needs a separate attendance (cost, logistics) and deters some from screening.
  • Nonetheless most urologists combine PSA and DRE — an abnormal DRE carries real cancer risk and the examination is simple; many nomograms incorporate DRE.

Prostate-Specific Antigen

  • Human kallikrein gene family serine protease; secreted into seminal fluid at milligram-per-millilitre concentrations; circulates bound (complexed) and unbound (free), both measurable by US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved assays.

Factors influencing PSA.

  • Varies with age, race and prostate volume; Black people without cancer have higher PSA than White people.
  • PSA rises about 4% per millilitre of prostate volume; volume explains about 30% and age about 5% of PSA variance.
  • Androgen-dependent: detectable from puberty as luteinising hormone and testosterone rise; hypogonadal men may have falsely low PSA that masks cancer.
  • Assay differences between serial measurements cause pseudo-acceleration or pseudo-deceleration; genetic factors may justify genetically adjusted PSA.
  • Prostate disease is the single most important determinant — cancer, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), prostatitis; PSA is organ-specific, not cancer-specific, and not all prostate disease raises it.
  • Mechanism: disruption of prostatic architecture lets PSA reach the circulation — disease or manipulation (massage, biopsy, transurethral resection).
  • DRE causes only a slight rise, within assay error — rarely a false positive.
  • Ejaculation: conflicting data; repeat after 48 hours' abstinence when PSA is minimally raised.

Clinical use for diagnosis.

  • FDA approved PSA for early detection in 1994; initial assays measured free PSA (fPSA) plus PSA complexed (cPSA) to α1-antichymotrypsin — together total PSA (tPSA).
  • PSA testing increases detection and detects cancers more likely to be organ-confined.
  • Future cancer risk and present biopsy yield both rise incrementally with PSA, across its full range (Table 157.1).
  • Baseline PSA predicts later detection; the Malmö Preventive Project showed PSA in the 40s predicts metastasis and death >25 years later.

Triggers for prostate biopsy.

  • PSA threshold for biopsy is controversial; dichotomising PSA into "normal" and "abnormal" discards information below the cut-off — the Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial (PCPT) showed risk is continuous, and some advise against calling PSA "elevated" or "abnormal".
Higher thresholdLower threshold
Risks missing significant cancers during the window for cureMore unnecessary biopsies and overdiagnosis
  • 4 ng/mL — original FDA upper limit of normal; many clinicians adopted 2.5–3 ng/mL; most ERSPC centres used 3 ng/mL; a higher cut-off (e.g. 10 ng/mL) >70 years has been suggested to reduce harm.
  • Repeat a suspicious PSA before biopsy — physiological fluctuation causes false positives.
  • Choosing Wisely: no empirical antibiotics for a raised PSA without urinary symptoms.
  • Combine PSA with family history, race and DRE.

  • Contrast: a systematic review found pre-treatment PSAV adds nothing to PSA; other studies show kinetics add predictive value for overall and high-risk disease.

Multiplex tests.

  • Prostate Health Index (phi) — FDA-approved; tPSA + fPSA + [−2]proPSA; outperforms tPSA and %fPSA for cancer and high-grade disease in prospective multicentre trials.
  • 4Kscore — tPSA + fPSA + iPSA + hK2; predicts clinically significant cancer.

Staging

General Concepts of Staging

  • Clinical staging uses pre-treatment parameters to predict extent for prognosis and treatment choice: DRE (clinical T stage), PSA and derivatives, needle biopsy features, imaging.

Clinical Versus Pathological Staging

Clinical stagePathological stage
Pre-treatment: DRE, PSA, biopsy, imagingAfter prostatectomy: histology of prostate, seminal vesicles, pelvic nodes (if dissected)
Less accurateMore accurate; better outcome predictor
  • Biochemical recurrence–free and cancer-specific survival are both inversely related to pathological stage.
  • Pathological predictors after radical prostatectomy: tumour grade, surgical margin status, extracapsular disease, seminal vesicle invasion, pelvic lymph node involvement.

Classifications

  • Whitmore–Jewett staging is now historical; clinical staging uses tumour, node, metastasis (TNM), adopted by the American Joint Committee on Cancer in 1975.
  • Current version combines TNM + PSA + grade group 1–5 into prognostic stage groups.

Prediction of Tumour Extent

Prostate-Specific Antigen

  • PSA correlates directly with pathological stage and extent (its relation to volume is disputed) but cannot predict extent for an individual: overlap between stages, variable BPH contribution, and poorly differentiated tumours make less PSA per gram.
  • Organ-confined disease by PSA: about 80% at <4; 66% at 4–10; <50% at >10 ng/mL.
  • Nodal involvement: about 20% at PSA >20; 75% at >50 ng/mL.
  • fPSA, hK2, proPSA, PSAD, PSAV and newer tests have been studied as grade and extent predictors; prostatic acid phosphatase has been virtually abandoned because PSA correlates better.

Digital Rectal Examination

  • Defines palpability and clinical T stage; an abnormal DRE raises risk of grade group 4–5 in screened men.
  • Poor sensitivity and reproducibility → both over- and under-staging; poor sensitivity with moderate specificity for organ-confined disease; useful only in combination.

Prostate Needle Biopsy

  • Histological grade is the most important biopsy finding; Gleason is the most used system (1966).
  • Gleason sequence: low-power assessment → grade 1–5 for predominant pattern + grade for second most common pattern → score 2–10.
  • Tertiary pattern (2005 consensus): on biopsy, 3+4 or 4+3 with tertiary 5 → recorded as 3+5 or 4+5.
  • Scores 2–5 are no longer assigned; lowest in practice is 6 — confusing for patients and a barrier to active surveillance, hence grade groups.

Combined Use of Pretreatment Parameters

  • Nomograms and algorithms combining T stage (DRE), PSA and Gleason score/grade group predict extent and long-term outcome better than any single parameter.

Imaging

  • Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI): T2-weighted plus functional diffusion-weighted and dynamic contrast-enhanced sequences → better locoregional characterisation; used for pre-treatment planning and active surveillance candidacy.
  • MRI–TRUS fusion enables targeted biopsy; AUA and Society of Abdominal Radiology: strongly consider high-quality MRI after a prior negative biopsy; suspicious MRI lesion → image-guided targeted biopsy.
  • The chapter's key points go further: MRI is recommended before prostate biopsy (improves detection of clinically significant cancer).
  • Risk-based staging: weigh pre-test probability of metastasis against test sensitivity and specificity.

  • Nodal disease is rare in screened populations — staging imaging is overused.
  • Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) PET: 68Ga PSMA-11 and piflufolastat F-18 (18F-DCFPyL) are FDA-approved for initial staging at high risk of metastasis; more sensitive and specific than conventional imaging for nodal and distant disease; evidence of clinical benefit when conventional imaging is negative remains limited.

Concept — Imaging yield follows pre-test probability: the risk group chooses the scan, which is why low-risk staging scans are wasteful and why the most sensitive tracer is reserved for high-risk disease.

Pelvic Lymphadenectomy

  • No consistent oncological benefit from pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND); its value is staging — refines risk, informs counselling, guides selective secondary therapy.
  • Risk-stratified: perform at radical prostatectomy when the nomogram-predicted probability of nodal disease exceeds a guideline threshold (thresholds vary); balance against PLND morbidity.

Concept — Staging is prediction under uncertainty: no single parameter suffices, so parameters are combined — into risk groups, nomograms and genomic scores — and the predicted risk then decides imaging and nodal dissection.

Key Points: Diagnosis and Staging of Prostate Cancer

  • PSA screening remains controversial; adults at periodic health examinations should be told of the test and decide on an informed basis.
  • PSA, DRE, age, race and family history together predict cancer risk; prostate disease is the main determinant of PSA.
  • PSA testing detects more, and more organ-confined, cancers; future risk and biopsy yield rise incrementally with PSA.
  • Most with a raised PSA should have repeat PSA and/or serum/urine biomarkers before biopsy.
  • MRI improves detection of clinically significant cancer and is recommended before biopsy.
  • Every diagnosed cancer needs a risk group, which directs metastatic imaging and treatment options.
  • Survival is inversely related to pathological stage; post-prostatectomy predictors: grade, margins, extracapsular disease, seminal vesicle invasion, nodes.
