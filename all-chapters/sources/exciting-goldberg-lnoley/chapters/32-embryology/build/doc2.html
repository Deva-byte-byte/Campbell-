  • Two interrelated systems. The urogenital system has a urinary and a reproductive part, most closely linked early in development.
  • Timing. Development begins in the first trimester and is mostly complete by the end of the second trimester.
  • Carnegie collection. Documents human embryos from fertilisation to the indifferent stage, at about 8–9 weeks' gestation.
  • Sexual divergence. After 8–9 weeks, androgens drive male and female urogenital structures apart.
  • Clinical relevance. Normal development is the basis for understanding congenital anomalies and the first step in planning reconstruction.

DEVELOPMENT OF THE EXTERNAL GENITALIA

Genetic and Hormonal Control

  • Indifferent stage. At 8–9 weeks the external genitalia consist of an indifferent genital tubercle.
  • The gonad comes first. External genital differentiation depends on prior gonadal differentiation.
  • SRY. Gonadal sex is set by the SRY (sex-determining region Y) region on the short arm of the Y chromosome, whose downstream cascade directs testicular development.
  • Accessory-structure genes lie on the X chromosome and the autosomes, not only the Y.
  • Leydig cells appear at about 8 weeks and begin secreting testosterone.
  • Targets. Testosterone and the more potent dihydrotestosterone (DHT) masculinise the genital tubercle, the Wolffian duct and the urogenital sinus (UGS).
  • Type 2 5α-reductase converts testosterone to DHT, is expressed in the UGS and genital tubercle, and is required for normal penile and prostatic development.

Masculinisation Sequence

  • Order: increased anogenital distance (first sign) → elongation of the genital tubercle → formation of the urethral plate within it.
  • The genital tubercle is the precursor of both penis and clitoris and contains all three germ layers.

GERM-LAYER ORIGIN OF THE GENITAL TUBERCLE

Germ layer Derivative
Ectoderm Skin of the phallus and the prepuce
Mesoderm Corporal bodies (erectile tissue)
Endoderm Urethral plate → penile urethra, to near the distal tip of the future glans
  • Pattern. Each layer gives one tissue compartment; the urethra is endodermal along its whole length — the basis of the modern view of the glanular urethra.

Penile Urethra — the "Double Zipper"

  • Opening zipper (canalisation). The urethral plate canalises proximal → distal into a wide, diamond-shaped urethral groove bounded by the urethral folds.
    • Driven by cell proliferation (Ki67-positive); no apoptosis (caspase-3-negative).
  • Closing zipper (fusion). The medial epithelial edges of the urethral folds fuse in the ventral midline → tubular penile urethra and median penile raphe.
    • A dense vascular and neural network lies at the fusion site.
  • Timetable:
    • 11–12 weeks: coronal sulcus separates glans from shaft.
    • 16 weeks: urethral folds completely fused along the shaft.
    • 18 weeks: penile and urethral development essentially complete.
    • 20 weeks: normal fetal ventral curvature (chordee) has resolved.

  • Common ground. On either view, the meatus marks the ectoderm–endoderm (skin–urethral epithelium) junction.

Prepuce

  • Tied to the urethra. The prepuce forms with the glanular urethra and depends on normal urethral development.
  • Sequence:
    1. About 8 weeks: low preputial folds appear on each side of the shaft.
    2. They join dorsally as a flat preputial ridge at the corona; ventrally the ridge is blocked by the not-yet-formed glanular urethra.
    3. The folds extend distally over the glans and ventrally to fuse in the midline.
    4. An epithelial preputial lamina separates glans mesenchyme from preputial mesenchyme.
    5. Postnatally the lamina delaminates → preputial space → retractable prepuce.
  • The prepuce is richly innervated.
  • Proof of the urethral link. In hypospadias, preputial tissue is absent ventrally and excessive dorsally (dorsal hooded foreskin).

Penile Innervation

  • Origin. At week 5, neural crest cells give rise to the peripheral and autonomic nervous systems.
  • Somatic supply: S2–S4 via the pudendal nerve → over the sacrospinous ligament → through Alcock's (pudendal) canal → through the transverse perineal muscle → onto the dorsum as the dorsal nerve of the penis.
  • Autonomic supply from the vesical and prostatic plexuses: sympathetic L1–L2, parasympathetic S2–S4.
  • Cavernous nerves leave the pelvis between the transverse perineal muscles and the membranous urethra, then pass beneath the pubic arch to each corpus cavernosum.
  • Dorsal nerves: bundled at 11 and 1 o'clock; fan out around the tunica as far as the junction of the corpora cavernosa with the corpus spongiosum; leave a nerve-free zone at 12 o'clock.

Concept — The penile urethra is built by two mechanisms — fusion in the shaft, canalisation in the glans — and the prepuce depends on both. So the junction between mechanisms predicts the commonest site of hypospadias, and failed ventral urethral closure predicts the hooded foreskin.

Clinical Correlation: Hypospadias

  • Incidence: about 1 in 250 newborn males.
  • Definition — four components: (1) ectopic urethral meatus; (2) incomplete prepuce (dorsal hooded foreskin); (3) ventral skin deficiency; (4) possibly penile curvature.
  • Commonest meatal site: junction of penile shaft and glans, matching the switch from fold fusion to direct canalisation.
  • Anatomy. The hypospadiac penis is normal apart from the urethra, spongiosum, overlying skin and ventral prepuce.
  • Arrest, not deformation. Abortive urethral and spongiosal elements (epithelium, connective tissue, smooth muscle, vessels) lie in adjacent tissue, so hypospadias is an arrest of development, not a deformation anomaly.
  • Aetiology:
    • Unknown in the overwhelming majority.
    • Working hypothesis: genetic susceptibility plus maternal exposure to endocrine disruptors.
    • A known genetic defect is rare; example: type 2 5α-reductase deficiency → severe hypospadias, because the enzyme is expressed at the fold-fusion site and is needed to fuse the groove edges.

Clinical Correlation: Correction of Penile Curvature

  • Nerve map. Along the shaft the nerves run on the tunica albuginea and surround the corpora ventrally up to the spongiosal junction; from the glans to the crural separation at the pubic rami they are bundled tightly at 11 and 1 o'clock.
  • Plication sutures at 12 o'clock — suitable for dorsal plication in mild-to-moderate curvature because it is:
    • the nerve-free zone;
    • where the tunica albuginea is thickest, so the suture anchors well.
  • Scope: curvature with hypospadias and congenital curvature without hypospadias.

Clinical Correlation: Duplicated Urethra

  • Rare; both hypospadiac and epispadiac forms.
  • Embryology — longitudinal splitting of the urethral plate: complete duplication = split from the bladder distally; Y-duplication = split along the shaft of the genital tubercle.
  • Signalling: Sonic hedgehog (Shh) appears to control the process.

Concept — All three correlations apply one developmental map: hypospadias is arrested development at the fusion–canalisation boundary, the safe plication site is the 12 o'clock nerve-free zone, and duplication is abnormal splitting of the single endodermal plate.

DEVELOPMENT OF THE FEMALE EXTERNAL GENITALIA

  • The clitoris forms like the penis, except that no urethra is incorporated.
  • Vestibular plate (homologue of the urethral plate) canalises (opening zipper) into a wide, diamond-shaped vestibular groove.
  • Canalisation is androgen-independent — it occurs in both sexes.
  • Fusion is the androgen-dependent step. The vestibular folds (homologues of the urethral folds) do not fuse, so the groove stays open.
  • Glans clitoris caps the narrowed paired corporal bodies; where these fuse they form a midline septum, starting ventrally and running about halfway into the glans.
  • Innervation mirrors the penis: large bundles densest dorsally; nerve-free zone at 12 o'clock; nerves extend ventrolaterally around the tunica; the glans is supplied by perforating branches of the dorsal nerves at the dorsal corporoglandular junction; glanular nerve density is lowest ventrally, beside the glans septum.

Clinical Correlation: Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia

  • Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) is autosomal recessive, impairs cortisol synthesis, and causes varying virilisation of 46,XX fetuses → genital atypia at birth.
  • Differential: can be mistaken for hypospadias with bilateral non-palpable testes.
  • Mechanism: the vestibular groove closes to a varying degree, forming a clitoral-shaft urethra anatomically indistinguishable from a male urethra.
  • Most severe form: penis-like clitoris with terminal meatus, glans and foreskin.
  • Only distinguishing features: no palpable gonads and excess pigmentation.

Concept — External genital sex comes down to one androgen-dependent step, fusion of the folds; canalisation is common to both sexes. So androgen excess in a female (CAH) closes the vestibular groove into a male-type urethra, and androgen deficiency in a male leaves it open.

KEY POINTS: EXTERNAL GENITALIA

  • Androgens start differentiation: the ambisexual genital tubercle begins to become a penis at 8 weeks.
  • Two-step penile urethra: opening zipper (plate canalisation) → closing zipper (fold fusion).
  • The epithelium of the entire male urethra comes from the urogenital sinus.
  • The clitoris is analogous to the penis but has no urethra.
  • The hypospadiac penis is otherwise normal — only urethra, spongiosum, overlying skin and ventral prepuce are abnormal.
  • 12 o'clock nerve-free zone on both penile and clitoral tunica — the site for dorsal plication in mild-to-moderate curvature.

DEVELOPMENT OF THE HUMAN PROSTATE

Onset, Evidence Base and Stages

  • First sign. At 10 weeks, prostatic buds emerge from urogenital sinus epithelium (UGE) caudal to the bladder base.
  • Evidence base. Human fetal data are sparse; mechanism and endocrinology come mainly from animal studies — Jost and Price established androgen dependence, later confirmed in humans.
  • Six stages, the same in human and mouse: prebud UGS → solid epithelial buds from UGE → bud elongation and branching → canalisation of solid ducts → differentiation into luminal and basal cells → secretory cytodifferentiation.
  • Endodermal origin. The prostate develops below the bladder from FOXA1-positive UGE (FOXA1 marks the endodermal lineage).

Verumontanum — a Uniquely Human Feature

  • What it is: a cranio-caudally elongated hillock on the dorsal wall of the UGS.
  • Opening onto its apex: the Wolffian (mesonephric) ducts and the fused Müllerian ducts (prostatic utricle).
  • Significance: the interface between mesodermal epithelium (Wolffian ducts, utricle) and endodermal UGE.

Budding and Branching

  • Timing: buds at 10 weeks (reported range 9.5–10 weeks, because fetal age is hard to estimate).
  • Site: most buds emerge in the gutters lateral to the verumontanum — as in the adult, where the ducts open lateral to the verumontanum.
  • Budding is stochastic (mouse data, apparently true in humans): cords of UGE grow in, then regress. Evidence: fetal ventral buds are not represented in the adult gland.
  • Branching. From about 11 weeks, growth and branching follow a fixed spatial pattern that sets up the adult zonal anatomy.
  • Canalisation runs proximal (urethral end) → distal (ductal tips), together with luminal and basal cell differentiation.

  • What it shows: precursor epithelium (prebud UGE and solid ducts) co-expresses basal and luminal markers.
  • The rule: on differentiation, future luminal cells keep luminal markers and lose basal ones; future basal cells do the reverse.
  • The trap: TP63 and KRT5/14 are basal; KRT8/18 are luminal; KRT19 and GSTpi go with the basal set in the adult despite being present in every precursor.

Stromal Differentiation

  • Urogenital sinus mesenchyme (UGM) differentiates into smooth muscle, the main component of adult prostatic stroma.
  • Direction: ventral first, then lateral and dorsal.
  • At 19 weeks: ductal branching is most intense near the capsule, where smooth muscle is most abundant.

Endocrinology

  • Androgens are essential. Fetal testicular androgens act through the nuclear androgen receptor (AR), activated by testosterone or DHT.
  • Evidence: no prostate when the AR is non-functional (mice and humans); the female UGS forms a prostate when exposed to androgen.
  • DHT is critical, although testosterone binds the AR directly: DHT is made within the UGS by type 2 5α-reductase (5αR2), mainly in the prostatic mesenchyme.
  • Evidence for 5αR2: 5αR2 deficiency → rudimentary prostate (and abnormal male external genitalia); mice lacking both 5αR1 and 5αR2 have smaller prostates and seminal vesicles.
  • 5αR1's role is unclear — the 5αR1 gene is normal in 5αR2-deficient patients.

Mesenchymal–Epithelial Interactions

  • Androgens act through the mesenchyme. Androgen triggers the process via the AR, but UGM induces and specifies prostatic identity in the epithelium.
  • Instructive induction: UGM can induce prostate even in embryonic or adult bladder epithelium (rodent and human).
  • Two-way interaction: prostatic epithelium is needed for UGM to become smooth muscle.

Oestrogen Effects

  • Oestrogens disrupt prostatic development; the acute effect of exogenous oestrogen (e.g. diethylstilbestrol, DES) is squamous metaplasia.
  • Receptor: ESR1 (oestrogen receptor α) — metaplasia occurs in wild-type and ESR2 (oestrogen receptor β)-knockout mice, not in ESR1-null mice.

The same two-compartment rule explains why DHT is made in the stroma, why stroma can impose prostatic identity even on bladder epithelium, and why oestrogen-induced metaplasia needs receptor in both compartments.

Development of the Human Seminal Vesicle

  • Literature is minimal — only two human papers.
  • Origin: a diverticulum of the Wolffian duct at 10–12 weeks.
  • Growth: elongates and folds back → hook-like duct, with extensive side ducts by months 4–5.
  • Autonomic innervation of the muscle coat detectable by 13 weeks.
  • Pathology: malformation and malignancy extremely rare; the main malformation is a seminal vesicle cyst.

DEVELOPMENT OF THE GONADS

The Indifferent Gonad

  • Testes make sperm, ovaries oocytes, but development begins identically in both sexes.
  • Germinal ridge forms at 4 weeks on the medial side of the urogenital ridge (which contains the mesonephric duct and tubules).
  • Four components: coelomic epithelium of the germinal ridge; underlying mesenchyme; adjacent mesonephros; primordial germ cells (PGCs).
  • Shape. Gonads are macroscopically identical at 7–8 weeks; thereafter testes become spherical and ovaries stay oblong (and longer).

Primordial Germ Cells and Sex Cords

  • PGC migration: yolk sac wall, week 3 → dorsal mesentery of the hindgut → gonadal ridges.
  • Primary sex cords. In week 6, surface epithelium invades the ridge mesenchyme; in humans these do not form the seminiferous cords, which appear to come from the mesonephric tubules.
  • First sex-specific histology: testicular cords at about 6 weeks; ovary at about 8 weeks, when it segregates into a cortical zone (ovigerous cords) and a medullary zone.
  • Origin of the ovarian zones — competing views: two successive proliferations of germinal epithelium, or ovigerous cords derived from mesonephric tubules.

Testicular Formation

  • SRY-dependent step. Under the SRY gene product, medullary cells coalesce into seminiferous cords of Sertoli cells enclosing the PGCs — the testicular medulla is a zone of mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition.
  • Sertoli cells differentiate only if SRY protein is present; without it the gonad becomes an ovary by default.
  • Direct Sertoli–germ cell contact soon after PGC arrival is thought key to normal male gamete development.
  • Cords canalise into seminiferous tubules.
  • Ducts: the rete testis develops with the cords and at the hilum joins 5–12 residual mesonephric tubules, which become the efferent ductules to the epididymal head. The full connection (tubules → rete → efferent ductules → epididymis) is not established until the third month.
  • Epididymis and vas deferens derive from the Wolffian (mesonephric) duct.
  • Shape change from oblong to round reduces contact with the mesonephros; a thick tunica albuginea forms beneath the surface mesothelium.
Müllerian-Inhibiting Substance
  • Müllerian-inhibiting substance (MIS; anti-Müllerian hormone, AMH) is a glycoprotein from Sertoli cells that makes the paramesonephric (Müllerian) ducts regress between weeks 8 and 10.
  • Male Müllerian remnants: appendix testis (small epithelial cyst at the upper pole) and prostatic utricle (pouch at the apex of the verumontanum).
  • In females MIS is absent, so the Müllerian ducts persist and form most of the female internal genitalia.
Leydig Cells and Wolffian Derivatives
  • Leydig cells differentiate from genital-ridge mesenchyme in response to SRY and begin testosterone production at 8 weeks.
  • Control of testosterone secretion: placental chorionic gonadotrophin early → fetal pituitary gonadotrophins later.
  • Weeks 8–12: testosterone turns the Wolffian ducts into paired epididymis, vas deferens and seminal vesicle.
  • Remnants: cranial Wolffian duct → appendix epididymis; degenerating mesonephric tubules near the lower pole → paradidymis.

Ovarian Formation

  • Why the ovary is the default: no Y chromosome → no SRY → no Sertoli-cell cords, no early MIS, no Leydig cells, no androgen → female development ensues.
  • First ovary-specific event: cortex–medulla segregation at about 8 weeks; the cortex fills with anastomosing cortical cords.
  • Origin of cortical cords disputed — mesonephric tubules, or gonadal-ridge epithelial-like cells at or near the surface that become somatic ovarian cells. Either way the cords break into clusters that the germ cells enter.
  • Second ovary-specific event: germ cell nests at 10–11 weeks — clusters of 2–4 germ cells joined by intercellular bridges; nests are clonal (one nest may arise from a single PGC); they enlarge by proliferation, then break up by germ-cell apoptosis.

Folliculogenesis — sequence:

  1. FOXL2-positive somatic interstitial cells surround single germ cells → primordial follicle (oocyte with a single layer of squamous follicular cells).
  2. About 20 weeks: follicular cells become cuboidal → primary follicle.
  3. At term: granulosa proliferation → secondary follicle with multiple granulosa layers.
  4. Postnatally: antral follicles (fluid-filled cavity) appear, and the vascular theca organises around the follicle — it supports the follicle, nourishes the avascular granulosa and synthesises sex steroids.
  • Meiotic arrest. Oogonia enlarge and enter meiosis I as primary oocytes in fetal life, then arrest until puberty; thereafter individual oocytes resume under the monthly gonadotrophin surge.

  • The traps: the vagina sits under the UGS, not the Müllerian duct — consistent with the UGE origin of vaginal epithelium (see Müllerian Structures); the appendix testis is Müllerian, whereas the appendix epididymis is Wolffian.

Gonadal Descent

Starting Position and the Two Ligaments
  • Start. The urogenital ridge is identical in both sexes at 7–8 weeks; the undifferentiated gonad lies beside the kidney in the upper lumbar region, held cranially by the cranial suspensory ligament (CSL) and caudally by a ligament that becomes the gubernaculum.
  • Weeks 10–15: the testis reaches the future inguinal region as the abdominal cavity enlarges; the ovary descends only into the pelvis.
  • Male: androgen causes the CSL to regress, and the gubernaculum swells into a plump ligament anchoring the testis at the groin while the kidney moves cranially (tethering first observed in the 1700s).
  • Female: the CSL persists and keeps the ovary near the kidney; the gubernaculum stays rudimentary.
Transabdominal Phase
  • Swelling reaction (gubernacular outgrowth): the gubernaculum enlarges caudally.
  • Shortening of the gubernacular cord: the cord is taken up into the enlarging bulb, positioning the testis over the inguinal ring so that abdominal pressure can push it out.
  • Cord transection misdirects the testis → contralateral inguinal canal or aberrant intra-abdominal position.
  • Intra-abdominal pressure: controversial in this phase, important in the inguinal and scrotal phases.
Inguinoscrotal Phase
  • From the seventh month the gubernaculum bulges beyond the external ring toward the scrotum, while being hollowed out by the processus vaginalis (a peritoneal diverticulum), letting the testis leave the abdomen.
  • The gubernaculum migrates 3–5 cm to the scrotum; the processus vaginalis lengthens with it.
  • Driving force: intra-abdominal pressure, transmitted directly through the processus lumen and indirectly through the gubernacular cord.
  • Direction: controlled by androgen acting mostly indirectly via the genitofemoral nerve, which releases calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP).
  • After descent: the gubernacular bulb is resorbed; the processus vaginalis closes, then involutes completely.

INSL3
  • Insulin-like peptide 3, a Leydig-cell product identified in 1993, structurally related to relaxin and insulin; expressed in fetal and adult Leydig cells according to their differentiation.
  • Insl3-null mice: intra-abdominal cryptorchidism with no other reproductive defect — the phenotype of classic human cryptorchidism; early orchidopexy restores fertility potential in these mice.
  • Androgen's overall role in descent remains unclear — defective androgen production or metabolism gives variable cryptorchidism.
Ovarian Descent
  • A female gubernaculum exists, from the lower ovarian pole to the labioscrotal folds, later passing through a fully formed inguinal canal.
  • It does not shorten but anchors the ovary in the pelvis → descent in the third month onto the broad ligament.
  • Week 7: the gubernaculum attaches to the Müllerian ducts; as they fuse into the uterovaginal canal it is tethered to the uterus and divided into the ovarian ligament (ovary → uterus) and the round ligament (uterus → inguinal canal → labia majora).
  • Female processus vaginalis is normally obliterated; if patent → indirect inguinal hernia.

Concept — Descent is a tug between two ligaments followed by a migration: androgen removes the cranial restraint (CSL), INSL3 builds the caudal anchor (gubernaculum), and androgen via CGRP steers the inguinoscrotal migration — so the site of an undescended testis tells you which step failed.

Clinical Correlation: Cryptorchidism

  • Incidence: about 3% of newborn males; hydrocele and hernia are even more common.

Clinical Correlation: Streak Gonad

  • Three genotypes: 45,X (Turner syndrome); 46,XX gonadal dysgenesis; 46,XY gonadal dysgenesis.
  • Both testicular hormones absent: no testosterone → Wolffian ducts regress; no MIS → Müllerian ducts form tubes, uterus, cervix and upper vagina.
  • Phenotype: female-like internal and external genitalia.
  • Treatment: hormone replacement for secondary sexual characteristics (the streak is non-functional); in 46,XY, risk of gonadoblastoma and later malignancy → streak gonads typically removed.

Clinical Correlation: Ovo-testicular Syndrome

  • Ovotestis: a gonad with both testicular and ovarian tissue; extremely rare.
  • Both tissues can function hormonally before and after birth; female fertility with normal oocytes is possible, but functioning adult spermatogenesis has not been reported.
  • Ducts follow the ipsilateral gonad: Müllerian structures on a side without testicular tissue; Wolffian structures on a side with functioning testicular tissue.
  • Genitalia: often atypical.

Clinical Correlation: Persistent Müllerian Duct Syndrome

  • A 46,XY male with a uterus and fallopian tubes (hernia uteri inguinale).
  • Usual cause: abnormal MIS receptor → no MIS action → female internal genitalia with a relatively normal male phenotype.
  • Typical presentation: at exploration for bilateral abdominal testes.
  • Alternative cause: immature testis, with MIS production out of phase with the Müllerian ducts' sensitivity window, normally 9–13 weeks.

KEY POINTS: GONADAL DEVELOPMENT

  • SRY makes the indifferent gonad of the ambisexual embryo differentiate into a testis.
  • Leydig-cell androgens drive male genital differentiation, including the prostate.
  • Sertoli-cell MIS makes the Müllerian structures (uterus and fallopian tubes) degenerate.
  • Both gonads reach the pelvis by the third month; the testis enters the scrotum at about the seventh month, guided by the gubernaculum.

Concept — Every correlation here follows from two independent testicular signals acting on two independent duct systems: remove both (streak gonad), block one (PMDS), give them unilaterally (ovotestis) or add androgen without MIS (CAH), and the phenotype can be predicted.

MÜLLERIAN STRUCTURES (FEMALE INTERNAL GENITALIA)

  • Males lack Müllerian structures because of testicular MIS.
  • Females have no testosterone, so the Wolffian ducts resorb; the Müllerian ducts persist and form the uterine body, cervix and fallopian (uterine) tubes.
  • In whole mounts the uterine body, cervix and vagina cannot be told apart; the uterus enlarges with gestational age.

Development of the Human Female Reproductive Tract

Müllerian duct formation — sequence:

  1. Start of week 5: coelomic epithelium invaginates on the lateral surface of the urogenital ridge; the infoldings become the tubal ostia.
  2. The paired Müllerian (paramesonephric) ducts grow caudally using the Wolffian ducts as guidewires — the Wolffian duct is essential for Müllerian migration to the UGS.
  3. The relationship changes along their length: cranially separated by mesenchyme → further down basement membranes in contact → at the tip epithelia in direct contact. Cell crossing between ducts was once suggested, but mouse lineage tracing does not support it.
  4. Weeks 7–8: the Müllerian ducts lie between the two Wolffian ducts as they approach the UGS, along a route with two gentle curves (horizontal and vertical parts); their contact point is the Müllerian tubercle, a poorly defined term of limited value.
  5. Week 8: the ducts fuse in the midline → uterovaginal canal, with a midline epithelial septum.
  6. Week 9: the septum disappears.
  7. Female Wolffian ducts degenerate, leaving epoöphoron and paroöphoron (broad ligament) and Gartner's duct (vaginal wall).
Uterine Tube
  • Origin: the paired cranial, unfused Müllerian ducts; fimbriae from the irregular ostia.
  • At 8 weeks: narrow, with a circular epithelial profile.
  • Later: mucosa becomes highly folded, most in the infundibulum and ampulla; mesenchyme divides into inner stroma and outer smooth muscle.
Uterine Corpus and Cervix
  • Origin: the cranial uterovaginal canal; the caudal canal takes part in vaginal development.
  • Septum anomalies: the midline septum marks the fusion zone and is normally eliminated; failure to regress → obstruction or duplication.
  • Corpus epithelium stays simple columnar and forms the uterine and cervical glands.
  • Cervix: endocervix simple columnar and glandular; near the external os the Müllerian epithelium becomes stratified squamous, which also covers the exocervix.
  • Mesenchyme forms the endometrial stroma, surrounded by myometrium.
Vagina — Origin of the Epithelium
  • The debate: contributions from the UGS, Müllerian ducts or Wolffian ducts, alone or in combination.

Sequence of vaginal development (PAX2/FOXA1 evidence):

  1. Weeks 8–9: Müllerian ducts and uterovaginal canal PAX2-positive.
  2. Caudal proliferation of the uterovaginal canal → solid vaginal plate, abutting the FOXA1-positive sinovaginal bulbs (UGS-derived).
  3. 12 weeks: plate continuous with the uterine end; mostly PAX2-positive; FOXA1 limited to caudal plate, urethra and bladder.
  4. Weeks 14–21: plate lengthens and FOXA1-positive UGE progressively replaces PAX2-positive Müllerian epithelium.
  5. Canalisation starts at about 15 weeks, mostly complete by 18–19 weeks: fornices defined, exocervix projects into the vault; FOXA1 has reached mid-vagina, PAX2 runs continuously from tube to upper vagina.
  6. 21 weeks: epithelium multilayered and glycogenated (likely endogenous oestrogen); FOXA1 from introitus to cervix; PAX2 over tube, uterus and cervix; markers switch abruptly at the vaginal–cervical junction.
  • Conclusions: the entire human vaginal epithelium is UGE-derived; the hymen is FOXA1-positive and presumed UGE-derived.

Clinical Correlation: Vaginal Agenesis and Imperforate Hymen

  • Transverse vaginal septum: failure of the vaginal canal to canalise → atresia or obstruction; about 1 in 80,000 females; typically at the junction of the middle and upper thirds.
  • Imperforate hymen: failure of the inferior end to open; the commonest cause of vaginal obstruction.

Clinical Correlation: Uterus Didelphys

  • Uterus didelphys: failure of fusion of the caudal Müllerian ducts.
  • Unicornuate uterus: one Müllerian duct fails to develop.

Clinical Correlation: Obstructed Hemi-Vagina and Unilateral Renal Anomalies

  • Obstructed hemi-vagina and ipsilateral renal anomaly (OHVIRA): a rare combined Müllerian and urinary tract anomaly from abnormal development of both Müllerian and Wolffian ducts.
  • Presentation: usually after menarche with cyclic abdominal pain and vaginal discharge; sometimes acutely with an acute abdomen or tubo-ovarian abscess.
  • The renal anomaly is not always agenesis — ipsilateral dysplastic kidneys or ectopic ureters are reported.
  • Kidney often not seen on imaging because of dysplasia and atrophy from abnormal (not absent) Wolffian duct formation — not true agenesis.

  • Imperforate hymen is the commonest cause of vaginal obstruction.

BLADDER: URETERAL DEVELOPMENT

  • Shared wall structure of bladder and ureter: urothelium-lined lumen; lamina propria of extracellular matrix with disorganised smooth muscle; outer thick smooth muscle bundles; serosa.
  • Sex differences are small — ureters and bladder develop alike and are of similar size, except that in males the prostate develops below the ureteric insertion, so the prostatic urethra is larger than the female homologue.
  • The ureterovesical junction inserts into the trigone identically in both sexes.

Formation of Urogenital Sinus

  • Weeks 5–6: the urorectal septum divides the cloaca into the dorsal anorectal canal and the ventral UGS (classic observations of Rathke and Tourneux).
  • Anorectal malformations are thought to arise from the cloacal membrane, not the urorectal septum.
  • The UGS and its diverticulum, the allantois, form bladder and urethra; the allantois is the precursor of the urachal remnant, and the early bladder is continuous with it.
  • The Wolffian ducts join the cloaca just before it divides; their entry point is the landmark between the cephalad vesicourethral canal (→ bladder and pelvic urethra) and the caudal UGS (→ phallic urethra in males; urethra and vaginal vestibule in females).

Formation of Trigone

  • Weeks 5–6: the common excretory ducts (mesonephric ducts distal to the ureteric bud origin) dilate and join the UGS.
  • Apoptosis lets the ureters disconnect from the mesonephric ducts and fuse with the bladder.
