Chapter 31 — Tuberculosis and Parasitic Infections of the Genitourinary Tract

Urogenital Tuberculosis

  • Tuberculosis (TB) can affect any organ, including the urogenital (UG) tract; untreated UG TB causes irreparable damage — renal failure and infertility — so TB belongs in the differential of UG disorders.
  • Called the second “great imitator” (after syphilis): it mimics many diseases and delays correct diagnosis.
  • As TB becomes rarer in industrialised nations, diagnosis increasingly depends on clinical recognition and a high index of suspicion.
  • “Urogenital TB” is now preferred to “genitourinary TB” because the urinary tract is affected far more often than the genital tract.

History

  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis coevolved with humans; its progenitor (M. prototuberculosis) may have infected early hominids >3 million years ago. Evidence runs from a Homo erectus skeleton, through Neolithic DNA, to Egyptian mummies (c. 3000 BCE).
  • TB reached epidemic proportions in the 18th–19th centuries in Europe and North America (“consumption”, up to a quarter of deaths in the Industrial Age).
  • 24 March 1882: Robert Koch presented the first isolation of the tubercle bacillus. This date is now World Tuberculosis Day.

  • TB is caused by the acid-fast Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC):

  • “M. tuberculosis” is often used loosely for the whole complex. The human-infecting species cause clinically indistinguishable disease but differ in drug susceptibility: M. bovis is innately resistant to pyrazinamide.

Epidemiology

  • The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that one-quarter of the world’s population has latent MTBC infection. In 2021 there were 10.6 million new active cases and 1.6 million deaths; mortality has fallen 45% since 1990.
  • Obstacles to control: human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) in sub-Saharan Africa, rising obesity and diabetes, and multidrug and extensive drug resistance.
  • United States: about 2.5 cases per 100,000 (2022). Incidence peaked in 1992 and has declined since. There was a COVID-19 dip in 2020, back to prepandemic levels by 2022. Incidence is 16-fold higher in non–US-born persons.
  • UG involvement: 15–20% of pulmonary TB patients in developing countries vs 2–10% in developed countries; >90% of UG TB occurs in developing countries.
  • Rank among extrapulmonary sites: second after lymph nodes in the developing world; third after pleural and lymphatic in the US (27% of extrapulmonary cases).
  • UG TB is about two-thirds male and mainly a disease of adults, although it has been reported from age 2.

Infection, Host Immune Response, and Transmission

  • Entry by inhalation of cough-generated aerosols; direct soft-tissue inoculation is rare. The course then runs: alveolar macrophage phagocytosis → either killed and cleared (no infection, no adaptive response) or replication inside macrophages → spread via lymphatics to hilar nodes → haematogenous seeding of distant organs. This can happen during the up to 12 weeks before cellular immunity is detectable.
  • Granuloma formation: infected macrophages secrete interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-12, IL-1β and tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). Foamy macrophages, epithelioid cells and multinucleated giant cells (Langhans cells) form the centre, with a lymphocyte cuff.
  • T cells secrete IL-2, TNF-α and, above all, interferon-γ (IFN-γ), which maintains the granuloma and drives macrophage killing. Where killing fails, the granuloma sequesters dormant bacilli.

Clinical Manifestations and Pathological Features

  • Symptoms are nonspecific. Patients are often treated repeatedly for bacterial infection, or investigated for malignancy, before TB is considered. Renal TB can be silent until it reaches the bladder.
  • Constitutional symptoms (fever, weight loss, night sweats, malaise) occur in <20%. Presentations: dysuria alone up to 50%, storage symptoms 50%, haematuria and flank pain 33%. Renal colic <10%, from passage of necrotic papillae, clots, stones or caseous material. A minority are asymptomatic.
  • Hallmark laboratory finding: sterile pyuria and/or haematuria (>90% in developing countries).

Kidney

  • 80% of UG TB is renal; infection is progressive and destructive.
  • Collateral renal damage without imaging change: in pulmonary TB with renal failure, biopsy may show TB granulomatous interstitial nephritis, which can reverse with treatment. Immune-complex glomerulonephritis and secondary amyloidosis also occur.
  • Miliary TB: massive haematogenous seeding gives innumerable ~3-mm pale granulomas (“millet seeds”) through cortex and medulla. It carries high mortality but usually spares renal function.
  • Localised renal TB progresses as: bacilli lodge in periglomerular capillaries → parenchymal granulomas coalesce → caseation → cavities → abscess, chronic pyelonephritis, parenchymal and papillary necrosis, flank sinus tracts. Costovertebral angle tenderness may be present. Calyces become inflamed and then calcify → distortion, dilatation, stenosis.

Ureter

  • Always descending from the kidney. Mural granulomas and descending infected calculi cause scarring and strictures, commonest distally at the vesicoureteric junction.
  • Pan-ureteric strictures give a “beaded corkscrew” ureter. Distortion causes both obstruction and reflux, and obstruction is an important cause of renal failure. Spontaneous ureteric perforation above a stricture has been reported once.

Bladder

  • Disease begins near the ureteric orifices and spreads by lymphatics as a patchy cystitis, with ulceration where granulomas coalesce. The dome is most affected; trigone and neck usually spared.
  • Contracture after about 1 year of chronic inflammation. Frequency, urgency, pain and dysuria become prominent at capacity <100 mL; the “thimble” bladder is <20 mL. It is a late complication, commoner in developing countries (diagnosed later). Rarely, spontaneous perforation presents as an acute abdomen, often recognised only at laparotomy.

Epididymis, Vas Deferens, Testes, and Scrotum

  • Epididymis is the second commonest UG site of haematogenous seeding after the kidney. It is involved in 10–55% of UG TB and is bilateral in about one-third.
  • Disease starts in the more vascular globus minor (tail) → fibrous luminal obliteration → nodular, swollen or hard epididymis. A posterior scrotal sinus tract develops in up to 50%. Spread to the vas gives a thickened “beaded” vas.
  • Isolated testicular or epididymal TB is rare; the testis is usually involved by extension from the epididymis. Hard masses mimic testicular tumour. Hydrocele in about 5%.

Urethra and Penis

  • Urethra is relatively resistant (1.9–4.5%). It is usually involved with prostatic TB and can present with urethroscrotal fistulae; isolated urethral TB is very rare.
  • Primary penile TB is exceedingly rare. It follows a sequence: inflamed papule or keratotic plaque (lupus vulgaris) → ulceration → cavernosal spread → pea-sized nodules (painless and hard, or fungating). It mimics malignancy; fibrosis causes deformity and erectile dysfunction.

Concept — Every UG organ follows the same arc (granuloma → caseation → fibrosis and calcification), so early disease mimics infection or tumour and late disease presents as obstruction, contraction or infertility. The damage is cicatricial, and much of it outlasts the infection.

Diagnosis

  • Primary goal: isolate MTBC in culture for drug susceptibility testing. If cultures and DNA tests are negative, caseating granulomas on tissue support the diagnosis. Failing both, diagnosis rests on consistent findings, probable exposure and response to empirical treatment.
  • Up to 20% of UG TB coexists with pulmonary TB, so always look for pulmonary disease.

Culture

  • Gold standard: urine acid-fast bacilli (AFB) culture. Collect first-void urine (most concentrated) on 3–5 consecutive days and culture immediately, because urinary acidity retards growth. Sensitivity is up to 80–90% when done this way but as low as 10% in real-world practice (sporadic shedding).
  • Ziehl-Neelsen staining of urine: sensitivity <50%. Always culture any biopsy or surgical tissue.

Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests

  • Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) give results in 1–2 days and help when bacillary load is low. Urine contains amplification inhibitors that lower sensitivity. Performance depends on specimen, target sequence and protocol.
  • GeneXpert MTB/RIF (since 2010): self-contained and automated; detects MTBC and rifampicin resistance simultaneously. Because >90% of rifampicin-resistant strains are also isoniazid (INH)-resistant, rifampicin resistance is a surrogate for multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) (resistance to INH and rifampicin).
  • Systematic reviews in urinary TB show NAAT sensitivity 70–100% (small, heterogeneous studies). Pooled GeneXpert values in urine against AFB culture: sensitivity 83–95%, specificity 79–99%. NAAT also performs well on epididymal tissue.
  • US position: NAAT is an adjunct and does not replace culture, and must not be used to exclude extrapulmonary TB. WHO recommends GeneXpert in the initial work-up of all TB, although cost limits its use in developing countries.
  • NAAT cannot monitor treatment: nucleic acid from dead organisms keeps the test positive.

Urine Lipoarabinomannan Assay

  • Lipoarabinomannan (LAM) is a cell-wall glycolipid found in urine, sputum and serum. The lateral flow urine assay (LF-LAM) is point-of-care, needs no laboratory equipment and gives a result in <30 minutes.
  • In persons living with HIV (PLHIV), sensitivity is low but specificity high (meta-analysis). WHO (2019) recommends it for PLHIV with TB symptoms, advanced HIV illness, or CD4 <200 cells/mm³. In the US its use is mainly academic.

Histopathology

  • Used when urine cultures are negative. Caseating granulomas in the right clinical context establish the diagnosis even if mycobacteria are not seen. Histology is consistent with TB in only a minority of UG TB cases.
  • Fine-needle aspiration cytology of epididymal nodules is diagnostic in about two-thirds. Adding NAAT to tissue increases sensitivity.

Screening Tests

  • Tuberculin skin test (TST) and IFN-γ release assays (IGRAs) cannot distinguish latent infection from active disease: a positive test does not rule TB in, and a negative test does not rule it out. Their role is screening for infection.
  • IGRA is preferred for persons ≥5 years old born in endemic countries; TST is an acceptable alternative.

Tuberculin skin test (purified protein derivative, Mantoux test). Tuberculin is injected intradermally into the volar forearm and read at 48–72 hours, when delayed-type hypersensitivity induration appears in primed individuals. Radiography

  • The choice of test depends on disease location and symptoms. Imaging is often the first clue that TB is the cause.

Plain radiography.

  • The kidney-ureter-bladder (KUB) film shows calcification in 24–44% (Fig. 31.1). Progression runs: faint punctate parenchymal calcification → globular calcification (tuberculous mass) → triangular ring-like calcification (papillary necrosis) → “cement” or “putty” kidney in fibrotic autonephrectomy. Calcific rims outlining each lobe form a lobar pattern that is pathognomonic of end-stage renal TB.
  • Infected calculi form odd shapes in a deformed pelvis; an upward-arrowhead stone suggests a “hiked-up” pelvis contracted by scarring.
  • Ureteric calcification in TB is intraluminal (vs mural in schistosomiasis). Bladder wall calcification is uncommon except in late contraction. Prostate and seminal vesicle calcification in about 10%. Look for vertebral erosion and psoas cold-abscess calcification.
  • Every patient evaluated for UG TB needs a chest radiograph to exclude infectious pulmonary TB.

Intravenous urography.

  • Intravenous urography (IVU) was formerly the gold standard for early renal TB and is still preferred first-line in some regions. It has largely been replaced by computed tomography (CT), CT urography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and ultrasound (US).
  • Signs of early disease: loss of calyceal sharpness and edge irregularity; “moth-eaten” calyces; filling defects (tuberculoma rupture, papillary necrosis); medullary cavities communicating with the collecting system; the “phantom calyx”, where a stenosed calyx or infundibulum fails to opacify.
  • Ureter: a rigid, straight, aperistaltic “pipestem” ureter or a “beaded corkscrew” ureter. Both are highly suggestive of TB, especially with renal or bladder change. IVU also shows nonfunction (autonephrectomy) and a contracted bladder (Fig. 31.2). A perinephric abscess is suggested by restricted renal movement with breathing or by ureteric displacement.
  • Commonest IVU findings are obstructive: calyceal obliteration (Fig. 31.3), infundibular narrowing, hydrocalycosis, segmental or total hydronephrosis, hydroureter (Fig. 31.4). Dilated, distorted calyces form a cloverleaf pattern. Vesicoureteric obstruction comes from cystitis or distal-third stricture (Fig. 31.5).
  • Kerr’s kink: a “hiked-up” renal pelvis with sharp angulation of the pelviureteric junction (PUJ).

Computed tomography with urography.

  • The most-used modality in developed countries. It shows calcification, scarring and obstruction; multidetector scanners detect lesions of 3–4 mm; contrast phases assess function.
  • Small granulomas are hypoattenuating with little or no enhancement. Large granulomas mimic renal cell carcinoma (mass-like, peripheral enhancement).
  • CT is more sensitive than KUB for calcification and collecting-system thickening, and best for complicated or extensive disease: perinephric and psoas abscess, nodes, vertebrae, spleen, liver, and prostate or seminal vesicle enlargement, cavitation, abscess and calcification (Fig. 31.6: a right kidney hydronephrotic from infundibular stenosis but still functioning, beside an end-stage atrophic calcified left kidney).
  • Disadvantages: higher radiation dose than IVU; less sensitive than IVU for minimal urothelial thickening and subtle papillary necrosis, so IVU may still be preferred for early disease in resource-constrained settings. Advantages of contrast CT: shows parenchyma, oedema and hypoperfusion.

Retrograde and antegrade pyelography. Largely replaced by CT urography. Still useful when renal insufficiency or contrast allergy precludes IVU or CT, and to show whether cavities are obstructive and whether they communicate with the collecting system.

Ultrasonography

  • Limited role: nonspecific findings and poorer definition than CT. Useful in children and pregnancy (no radiation) and cheaper.
  • Shows scrotal disease (diffuse enlargement, hypoechoic areas, complex collections, hydrocele) and, transrectally, thickened prostate and vesicles. Also shows renal abscesses and cavities: septated cystic lesions suggest chronic infection; calcification is echogenic with shadowing. Restricted renal movement suggests perinephric or psoas abscess. Shows ascites, lymphadenopathy and omental caking.
  • Key use: monitoring hydronephrosis during medical treatment, because healing fibrosis can worsen obstruction.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

  • Used less than contrast CT but useful in children and pregnancy. Findings are comparable to CT with better tissue resolution; it can detect a single granuloma.
  • Small lesions are hypointense on T1 and T2; larger lesions show central T2 hyperintensity (central cellularity). Large lesions may be indistinguishable from malignancy.
  • Magnetic resonance urography (MRU) is more sensitive than IVU for urothelial thickening and caliectasis. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) separates hydronephrosis from pyonephrosis; cine and dynamic MRU assess peristalsis. Apparent diffusion coefficient falls with fibrosis, which allows staging and monitoring of treatment.
  • Caution with gadolinium in renal failure (nephrogenic systemic fibrosis).

Cystoscopy and Ureteroscopy

  • Limited diagnostic role; findings are nonspecific: hyperaemia, erosion, ulceration, granulomatous masses, irregular orifices. Ulcers can mimic malignancy.
  • A “golf-hole” ureteric orifice suggests TB and should prompt upper-tract imaging or endoscopy (Fig. 31.7).
  • Biopsy when possible, especially if malignancy is possible. Positive urine culture is sufficient but slow. In culture-negative patients, bladder biopsy is 19–52% sensitive.

Concept — Culture is the gold standard because only culture yields susceptibility data and tracks treatment response. Every faster test (NAAT, LAM, histology, immunological assays) trades away one of those properties. Imaging finds the cicatricial pattern, not the organism.

Treatment

  • Before antimicrobials, treatment was sanatorium rest and, for severe UG disease, extirpative surgery. Streptomycin (1944), INH (1952) and the rifamycins (1957) replaced both.
  • Most patients, even with MDR-TB, are now treated medically as outpatients. Surgery serves diagnosis or as an adjunct in advanced disease.

Medical Therapy

  • Multidrug therapy is mandatory because:
    • bacilli occupy different microenvironments with different metabolic and replication rates;
    • drugs differ: bactericidal vs bacteriostatic, fast vs slow replicators, tissue penetration and optimal pH;
    • combinations prevent emergence of resistance.
  • Start with INH, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol. Baseline tests: platelets, liver and renal function; HIV test in all; hepatitis B and C and diabetes screening as appropriate. Tailor to susceptibilities. Directly observed therapy (DOT) ensures adherence and limits resistance.

Corticosteroids

  • The rationale is to limit immune-mediated tissue destruction and scarring; their role is not fully defined.
  • Strongly recommended in TB meningitis (mortality benefit). Sometimes used in severe pulmonary TB with paradoxical worsening on treatment, and in pericarditis to prevent constriction.
  • In UG TB, use to prevent ureteric strictures and bladder contraction is anecdotal only (no trials). A meta-analysis across pulmonary, meningeal, pleural, pericardial and peritoneal TB found that steroids reduced mortality whichever organ was affected.

Surgical Therapy

  • About 55% of UG TB patients need surgery at some point, more often as disease advances.
  • Indications:
    • relieve obstruction or drain infection;
    • remove a nonfunctioning infected kidney resisting cure;
    • treat medically resistant hypertension from a functionally excluded kidney;
    • reconstruct the urinary tract (now over half of TB operations);
    • drain unresponsive abscesses by aspiration, percutaneous or open drainage.
  • Optimal timing: 4–6 weeks after starting medical therapy, so that inflammation settles, bacillary load falls and lesions stabilise.

Procedures to relieve obstruction. Emergency drainage for uraemia or sepsis. Renal failure usually reflects bilateral obstruction or obstruction of a functionally solitary kidney. Early stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN) limits loss of renal function and preserves reconstructive options. Stepwise approach:

  • retrograde double-J stent (preferred), which succeeds in only about 41% → antegrade internalised or externalised stent via percutaneous puncture → PCN until definitive management (more than one may be needed because of strictures) → nephrectomy if unsalvageable.
  • PCN must be followed by correction of the cause: removing it alone risks a tuberculous cutaneous fistula (less likely under effective therapy).
  • Avoid high contrast-injection pressures, which risk dissemination.

Ureteropelvic and ureteral surgery. Stent PUJ and ureteric strictures temporarily to let renal function recover. Upper and mid-ureteric strictures are rare and may suit endourology; lower strictures are commoner and often need open surgery. Decision factors: stricture length and degree, whether a guidewire passes, blood supply and renal function.

Endoscopic management. TB strictures have mucosal ischaemia and dense fibrosis, so success rates from other aetiologies do not transfer. Best results: short strictures with a residual lumen and good renal function. Strictures forming on treatment and stented early may stabilise without further intervention. Retrograde or antegrade balloon dilatation of ureter, PUJ, vesicoureteric junction or infundibulum is usually followed by a stent; failure is high and repeat procedures common.

  • Image all ureteric strictures during follow-up (US or IVU), especially endoscopically treated ones, because healing fibrosis may worsen them. Add steroids if they deteriorate.
  • Failure to improve or progression after 6 weeks of medical therapy → open surgery.

Open surgical options. Long, complex strictures need open repair, made harder by fibrosis, inelasticity and poor vascularity. PUJ repair is harder in TB than in congenital stenosis.

  • Allograft TB is rare; it usually presents within 6 months of transplantation (range up to 7 years), reflecting immunosuppression or donor-derived TB.

Concept — Host immunity shapes the disease. Where immunity is blunted (HIV, transplant), there is less fibrosis and fewer strictures but more dissemination and death. The regimen problem shifts from sterilisation to rifamycin interactions.

Key points: Tuberculosis
  • Untreated UG TB causes irreparable damage (renal failure, infertility); consider TB in the differential of UG disorders.
  • Symptoms are nonspecific, the course indolent and constitutional symptoms infrequent, so a high index of suspicion is needed.
  • Gold standard: positive AFB culture of urine or tissue. NAATs or granulomas on histopathology often allow the diagnosis.
  • Optimal timing of surgery is 4–6 weeks after starting medical therapy.

Parasitic Infections of the Urogenital Tract

  • These are rare in nonendemic practice, but urologists must know them to diagnose and treat. The relevant infections are UG schistosomiasis, filariasis, amoebiasis, enterobiasis and echinococcosis.

History

  • Schistosome antigens are found in Egyptian mummies (c. 3500 BCE), and S. haematobium eggs in later ones. The Egyptians called it “A-a-a disease”, drawn as a penis dripping bloody urine.
  • Theodor Bilharz, Cairo, 1852: found worms in the mesenteric veins and linked them to eggs in urine and stool.

Biology and Life Cycle

  • Human cycle: cercariae penetrate even intact skin in infested fresh water (“swimmers’ itch”) → shed forked tail over 90–120 min → schistosomulae → blood or lymph to the lungs (several weeks) → liver sinusoids (begin blood feeding, mature) → mate → migrate to the vesical and pelvic venous plexus. Cercaria to adult takes 80–110 days.
  • Pairing: the male clasps the female in the gynaecophoric canal, helps her feed and chemically stimulates egg-laying.
  • Eggs: hundreds to thousands per female per day; S. haematobium eggs are ovoid, about 140 µm, with a terminal spine (Fig. 31.9). They must cross the endothelium and bladder wall into urine; some pass in faeces. Fewer than half are excreted; retained eggs drive the pathology.
  • Environmental cycle: eggs survive about 20 days if wet → hatch in fresh water and light → ciliated miracidia (lifespan ~6 h) penetrate the snail (Bulinus tolerates slow, low-oxygen water) → primary sporocyst → 20–40 daughter sporocysts → 200–400 cercariae each → released into water. No cercarial shedding below 15 °C or above 35 °C.
  • Cercarial lifespan and infectivity: average about 1 day in the text (1–4 days in Fig. 31.8); penetration success falls within hours of shedding.
  • Adult lifespan: average 3–5 years (Fig. 31.8: 3–30 years; survival for years to decades).

Epidemiology

  • Endemic across sub-Saharan Africa, parts of North Africa and the Middle East, limited by the tropical needs of the parasite and its snails.
  • Corsica, 2013: the first autochthonous European transmission in over a century, with further cases in 2015 suggesting an endemic focus. Some infections were due to S. bovis or S. haematobium–S. bovis hybrids.
  • Most of the >200 million (up to 440 million on newer estimates) have the UG form; up to 150,000 deaths a year from obstructive renal failure alone.
  • Risk depends on the nature and duration of freshwater contact: domestic chores (rural women and children), play and swimming, fishing, car-washing, and water-intensive agriculture (rice, sugar cane). Prevalence is highly focal.
  • Dams and irrigation increase transmission: they create year-round, slow-flowing snail habitat and denser farming populations. After the Aswan Low Dam (1930s), prevalence rose from <11% to >75% in places; the Aswan High Dam (1960s) and Diama Dam (1980s) did the same.
  • Prevalence is highest where there is no clean running water or sanitation. Peak burden is at age 5–15 (whether from exposure or susceptibility is unclear).
  • Link with poverty:
    • snail hosts live in tropical developing countries;
    • inadequate sanitation lets eggs reach water;
    • lack of safe water prolongs contact;
    • infection itself impairs growth, learning and productivity, perpetuating poverty.

Pathogenesis and Pathology

  • Cercarial penetration uses proteases but provokes little beyond local skin inflammation (early immunomodulation). Repeated exposure → hypersensitisation and maculopapular rash.
  • The tegument is a double lipid bilayer that permits years of survival. Immune evasion uses host antigen mimicry, membrane turnover, immunomodulatory proteins and proteases, and altered surface antigen expression.
  • Disease is driven by eggs, not worms. Oviposition in bladder and pelvic organs causes granulomas (eosinophils, plasma cells, lymphocytes) and fibrosis. All granuloma stages coexist; clustered eggs form composite granulomas. These appear as bulky, hyperaemic polypoid masses (Fig. 31.10: IVU scalloping of bladder and lower ureter by polyps).
  • Disease severity is also modified by host genetics, in utero sensitisation and coinfection. Eggs induce type 2 inflammation genes and suppress urothelial barrier genes: host and parasite “share” the goal of expelling eggs across a temporarily weakened urothelium.
  • Ectopic egg deposition:
    • portal: presinusoidal obstruction → portal hypertension; pipestem (Symmers) fibrosis → hepatosplenomegaly;
    • pulmonary: only in very severe infection → pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale.

Clinical Manifestations

  • Acute disease is the response to penetration and migration. Chronic disease is the immune response to years of oviposition, so it is confined to long-term residents with repeated reinfection and heavy burdens.
  • Schistosomes cannot replicate in humans, so a single-exposure traveller’s worms die of senescence within 3–5 years, limiting pathology even without treatment.

Treatment

Medical management.

  • Praziquantel (PZQ) is the only WHO-recommended drug. Single-drug dependence risks resistance, but PZQ is effective, available and low in toxicity.
  • Dose for S. haematobium: 20 mg/kg orally twice on the same day, 6–8 h apart (or 40 mg/kg once). Add corticosteroids for Katayama fever.
  • Efficacy (egg reduction and cure) 60–90%; uncured patients still have much lower worm burdens. Monitor with urine and stool egg counts and serial US; repeat courses if infection persists.
  • Side effects (abdominal pain, nausea, headache, dizziness) are mild, occur within 3–4 h and resolve. Tablets are large and bitter (hard for children); a paediatric formulation is in development.
  • Pregnancy: US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Category B (animal data). Many programmes exclude pregnant and lactating women, yet few adverse effects have been reported among millions treated.
  • PZQ is less active against schistosomulae than adults. It therefore cannot abort infection soon after exposure, which partly explains lower cure rates where transmission is high. Repeat doses weeks apart catch worms that have since matured.
  • Resistance is debated: large endemic studies show little, but failures occur in returning travellers and military personnel. Environmental control may reduce reliance on PZQ.

Prevention and Control

  • Travellers: avoid fresh water. Fast-flowing water can still be infested. Heating to ≥125 °F (about 52 °C) for 5 min, chlorination, or standing >2 days away from snails kills cercariae.
  • PZQ (late 1970s; MDA from the 1980s–90s) made treatment simple and cheap, but control remains difficult. WHO strategy has been periodic (typically annual) targeted MDA for morbidity control. Without safe water, reinfection forces more frequent dosing.
  • Cost is typically <US$0.50 per person but still burdensome at scale; donated PZQ supports campaigns.
  • Asexual amplification in snails means egg input must fall by nearly 90% before transmission falls substantially. Snail reduction lowers transmission proportionally, but adult worms persist for years, so snail control alone must run for years.
  • Integrated control offers the most promise: treat people, separate people and excreta from water, and control snails. Other measures: molluscicides, biological control (snail predators and competitors), WASH (water, sanitation and hygiene). There is no effective vaccine yet.
  • Unlike malaria and lymphatic filariasis, global prevalence has not fallen. Nevertheless it has been eliminated in 10 countries (Iran, Japan, Lebanon, Malaysia, Martinique, Montserrat, Morocco, Thailand, Tunisia, Turkey).
  • WHA65.21 (65th World Health Assembly, May 2012): marks the shift from morbidity control to elimination.

Concept — PZQ controls morbidity but not transmission, because the snail amplifies every egg that reaches water. Durable gains need water, sanitation and snail control alongside drugs.

Key points: Schistosomiasis
  • S. haematobium survives years to decades; a careful travel and social history identifies exposure and guides testing.
  • PZQ in early UG schistosomiasis can reverse inflammatory lesions, including fibrosis, caused by the host response to eggs.
  • Gold standard for active infection: eggs in urine, stool, or bladder or rectal biopsy. Serology and PCR are highly sensitive but may not distinguish active from resolved infection and are impractical in endemic regions.

Filariasis

  • Filariae are vector-borne tissue nematodes: the agents of lymphatic filariasis (LF), Onchocerca volvulus and Loa loa.
  • LF is caused by mosquito-borne Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi and B. timori. It ranges from acute lymphatic inflammation to chronic dilatation with hydrocele, lymphoedema and elephantiasis.

Organisms

  • Lifecycle: infective third-stage larvae enter by mosquito bite → migrate to central lymphatics → mature over 6–9 months into adults (about 20–100 mm × 0.2 mm; microfilariae about 200 × 10 µm, Fig. 31.14). Adults live about 5–7 years.
  • Adult site: afferent lymphatics, especially of the lower limb (inguinal, iliac, para-aortic), and, for W. bancrofti only, male genitalia (epididymis, cord, testis).
  • Microfilaraemia peaks around midnight in most areas, matching vector biting; in parts of the Pacific W. bancrofti periodicity is diurnal. In the mosquito, microfilariae become infective larvae in 10–14 days.
  • Wolbachia: an obligate endosymbiont needed for embryogenesis. Doxycycline kills it, reducing microfilarial release and larval moulting.

Epidemiology

  • About 68 million infected, much reduced since the early 2000s. W. bancrofti causes >90%: sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia, western Pacific; in the Americas only Haiti, Dominican Republic, Guyana, Brazil. B. malayi: Asia and Pacific islands. B. timori: southeastern Indonesia only.
  • Vectors: Anopheles (rural Africa, Pacific), Culex (urban, especially India), Aedes aegypti (some Pacific islands).
  • Transmission is inefficient, so obstructive disease is seen mainly in long-term residents with years of reinfection. Prevalence rises to the third or fourth decade, then plateaus. Lymphoedema and genital disease are rare before age 10. About one-third of infected people have overt disease; clinical disease is likelier in India, Papua New Guinea and Africa than in the Americas.

Pathology and Clinical Manifestations

  • Early response is proinflammatory (Th1 and Th2, rising filaria-specific IgE, eosinophil killing of microfilariae). With microfilaraemia, T-cell responses fall (IL-10, IgG4 blocking antibodies, suppressor T cells). Some people stay uninfected despite long exposure.
  • Damage is cumulative (scarring, lymphatic obstruction). Drugs prevent progression but do not readily reverse damage. LF is rarely fatal but third among parasitic infections for disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost.
  • Lymphoedema cycle: parasite factors cause the initial lymphatic dilatation; secondary bacterial infection, reactions to dying worms and Wolbachia add to it. Lesions range from nodular inflammation to suppuration (granulomas around worms ± eosinophils; Fig. 31.15). The vicious cycle runs: acute attack → worse lymphoedema → more infection → worse lymphoedema. The end state is obliterated, scarred lymphatics → elephantiasis or hydrocele.

Subclinical infection. Most are clinically silent even with heavy microfilaraemia, yet virtually all patent infections have subclinical disease (dilated lymphatics, scrotal lymphangiectasia, microscopic haematuria, proteinuria). Eosinophilia is common.

Acute adenolymphangitis (ADL). Often the first manifestation: fever, lymphadenitis, lymphangitis and oedema lasting days to a week. Lymphangitis is retrograde (spreads peripherally), unlike bacterial lymphangitis. Limbs are involved in both bancroftian and brugian disease, but genital lymphatics almost only in W. bancrofti: funiculitis, epididymitis, scrotal pain, lymph scrotum (ruptured scrotal lymph vesicles, whitish discharge, secondary infection).

Dermatolymphangioadenitis (DLA). Fever, chills, myalgia and headache, with oedematous inflammatory plaques, hyperpigmentation, vesicles and ulcers at a skin-injury site. It spreads proximally and is thought to be bacterial.

Genitourinary manifestations. Female genital involvement is poorly defined and probably uncommon. Genital disease usually begins at least in the teens. Acute (usually unilateral) epididymitis or funiculitis with fever and malaise lasting days is one of the commonest consequences of bancroftian filariasis.

Funiculoepididymitis. Palpable cord-like swellings and oedema. Usually self-limiting, but recurrence and chronic lymphoedema are common. Sterility and orchitis are rare because the testis is usually spared. It is often mistaken for malignancy, leading to surgery including orchidectomy. Varicocele can add pain and swelling. Bacterial superinfection is rare but severe (exquisite pain, septic thrombophlebitis).

Hydroceles. The chronic male genital lesion, sometimes huge (Fig. 31.16). Filarial and nonfilarial hydroceles are hard to tell apart and parasites are rarely found in the fluid. Pointers to LF: hydrocele with cord or epididymal nodules plus endemic exposure; a thick fibrous tunica with cholesterol or calcium. Usually painless unless complicated. Scrotal skin may be thick and brawny with oozing lymph. Superinfection is rare with hydrocele alone but common with elephantiasis and lymph scrotum.

Scrotal and penile elephantiasis. Mild scrotal oedema is common early; penile oedema is unusual. Massive enlargement is late, mostly where care is poor. Genital elephantiasis rarely has any cause other than LF.

Chyluria. Damaged GU lymphatics leak lymph into urine → massive fat and protein loss and possible malnutrition. It occurs earlier than genital elephantiasis, is intermittent and may remit.

Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia (TPE). An allergic response to microfilarial antigens (South and Southeast Asia): nocturnal paroxysmal cough and wheeze, fever, adenopathy, high-grade eosinophilia, raised IgE. Chest film ranges from normal to reticulonodular; lung function is restrictive (± obstructive); biopsy shows eosinophilic interstitial pneumonitis. Microfilaraemia is usually absent.

Diagnosis

  • In endemic residents, lymphoedema and male genital disease are more likely to be LF if other causes of oedema are absent. Differential for funiculoepididymitis: TB, S. haematobium, gonorrhoea. Nonfilarial hydrocele is common everywhere, but filarial hydrocele occurs earlier and more often in endemic areas.

Prevention and Control

  • Personal protection and long-lasting insecticide-treated bed nets (LLINs) are valuable for control and elimination.
  • Transmission needs patent microfilaraemia, so clearing microfilariae interrupts it. Drugs do not kill all adults, so MDA must continue for years until adults die of senescence. This works for W. bancrofti but is harder for Brugia (animal reservoirs).
  • MDA gives single annual doses: albendazole + ivermectin in Africa; albendazole + DEC elsewhere. It is the main reason for falling prevalence and has prevented about 19 million hydroceles since 2000, halving LF hydroceles. Triple therapy (DEC + albendazole + ivermectin) suppresses microfilariae more deeply and for longer, without extra serious adverse effects (small studies).

Onchocerciasis (river blindness).

  • 99% of cases are in Africa, with foci in Brazil, Venezuela and Yemen. About 17 million are infected (down about 55% since 2000 through MDA). Transmission is inefficient and focal.
  • Adults live up to 15 years in subcutaneous nodules (mean 9–10).
  • Clinical: dermatitis and pruritus, keratitis, chorioretinitis → blindness from corneal scarring. Urological relevance: “hanging groin” and scrotal elephantiasis from recurrent lymphadenitis and loss of skin elasticity (atrophic, fibrotic inguinal nodes), and occasionally massive inguinal lymphadenopathy.
  • Diagnosis: skin snips, adults in nodules, or microfilariae in the anterior chamber on slit lamp. Serology is less developed than for LF.
  • O. lupi (a canine parasite) is a rare human pathogen: six US cases since 2013, mostly children in the Southwest, with nodules in spine, soft tissue and extraocular muscle.
  • Treatment: ivermectin 150 µg/kg every 6–12 months until asymptomatic, then 6 weeks of doxycycline 200 mg daily, which kills or sterilises most adult females. DEC is contraindicated.

Loiasis.

  • Central and West Africa; adults migrate subcutaneously.
  • Usually asymptomatic eosinophilia. Otherwise urticaria, migratory subcutaneous swellings and eye worm crossing the conjunctiva. Haematuria and proteinuria in 30%; lymphadenitis and hydrocele are rare.
  • DEC is effective (may need several courses) but causes pruritus, arthralgia, swellings, fever, eye worm, diarrhoea and renal failure.
  • Microfilaraemia >2500–8000/mL → risk of treatment-associated encephalopathy (especially with ivermectin). Pretreatment apheresis may help, and albendazole is safer.

Concept — Filarial disease is cumulative lymphatic injury. Drugs clear microfilariae and stop progression, but scarred lymphatics stay, so treatment of established disease is hygiene and surgery. Drug choice is dictated by what dying parasites will provoke: never DEC in onchocerciasis, and caution with ivermectin or DEC where Loa is present.

Key points: Lymphatic filariasis
  • LF is caused by three species of tissue nematodes transmitted by mosquitoes.
  • LF is endemic across much of the tropics but transmitted inefficiently; clinical disease is usually confined to long-term residents.
  • Diagnosis is by blood smear, antibody or antigen assays, and radiological and clinical means.
  • Active LF is treated with DEC; ivermectin and albendazole are also active, and all three are used in mass chemotherapy programmes.
