Chapter 42 — Bladder Anomalies in Children

  • ICD-10-CM (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification) classifies birth defects as structural or functional; bladder anomalies span both.
  • Structural: exstrophy, duplication, agenesis, urachal anomalies, diverticular disease. Functional: usually secondary — bladder outlet obstruction, neurogenic dysfunction. Both: the bladder of prune-belly syndrome (PBS).
  • Focus is the less common congenital anomalies and rare acquired paediatric conditions; embryology and prenatal imaging matter because many congenital anomalies are now first detected prenatally.

Congenital Bladder Anomalies

Embryology of the Urinary Bladder

  • Weeks 4–6: the urorectal septum divides the endodermal cloaca → ventral urogenital sinus + dorsal rectum.
  • Cranial urogenital sinus (continuous with the allantois) → bladder and pelvic urethra. Caudal urogenital sinus → phallic urethra (male) / distal vagina (female).
  • Male urethra = pelvic + phallic parts; entire female urethra = pelvic part of the urogenital sinus only.
  • Allantois: extraembryonic outpouching of the yolk sac, joining the cranioventral cloaca (future bladder).
  • Week 10: definitive nephrons start urine production — the earliest point at which lower urinary tract anomalies can show.
  • Months 4–5: bladder descends into the pelvis; the allantoic duct does not grow with the embryo, so it elongates and narrows → obliterates to a fibrous cord, the urachus → median umbilical ligament (bladder apex to umbilicus).

Concept — the urachus is one tube obliterated by descent and elongation; every urachal anomaly is a failure of that single process at a different level.

Normal and Abnormal Antenatal Sonographic Findings of the Bladder

  • Normal = absence of abnormal findings; criteria for abnormality are broad, and physiological bladder cycling must be excluded first.
  • Elliptical anechoic pelvic structure, bordered laterally by the umbilical arteries, anteriorly by the pubic bones, posteriorly by the rectosigmoid; wall ≤3 mm, echogenicity like other pelvic structures.
  • Visible in about half at 10 weeks (onset of urine production), rising to almost all by 13 weeks; transvaginal is superior to transabdominal scanning (seen in the large majority at 12 weeks).
  • Fetal bladder empties every 15–20 minutes → if not seen, a repeat scan in the same sitting is mandatory.
  • First-trimester diameter ≤6–8 mm.
  • Fetal sex: unreliable before 14 weeks; base on visualising the testes, not the phallus.
  • Amniotic fluid: until 16 WGA (weeks' gestational age) mainly placental transudate, thereafter predominantly fetal urine — so it reflects fetal urine output only after 16 weeks.
  • Umbilical cord: two arteries, one vein, no fluid-filled urachus.

Concept — the fetal bladder is a cycling organ, so one image can neither prove absence nor confirm dilation; timing — emptying every 15–20 minutes, amniotic fluid becoming urine at 16 weeks, sex reliable from 14 weeks — governs interpretation.

Nondilated or Absent Bladder

  • A non-visualised bladder is absent, malformed, or present but not filling — a normal bladder may not fill because of renal dysfunction or ectopic drainage; renal development and amniotic fluid discriminate.
  • Absent bladder is diagnosed only after a repeat scan 15–20 minutes later.

Bladder Agenesis

  • Mechanism: the hindgut is usually normal, so cloacal division is regular → either atrophy of the cranial urogenital sinus or failure to incorporate the mesonephric ducts and ureters into the trigone.
  • Associations: neurological, orthopaedic and urogenital — renal dysplasia or agenesis; absent prostate, seminal vesicles, penis, vagina.
  • Incidence about 1:600,000; few reported cases, a minority live-born; significant female majority.
  • Compatible with life only if the ureters drain ectopically — into müllerian structures (female) or the rectum (male).
  • Confirm with antegrade or retrograde contrast; MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) or CT (computed tomography) for anatomy before surgery. Preserve renal function by ureterosigmoidostomy or external stoma.

Bladder Hypoplasia

  • The bladder forms and is visible throughout pregnancy but never reaches adequate capacity because of inadequate filling or storage in fetal life.
  • Some grow once the cause is corrected; later augmentation is often required.

Cloacal and Bladder Exstrophy

  • Bladder template only → suspected when regular bladder filling is absent.
  • Covered cloacal exstrophy: omphalocele, pelvic diastasis and genital anomalies with bladder filling; may become exstrophic near birth.
  • Many cases still missed prenatally, though detection is improving; fetal MRI adds anatomical detail for counselling.

Dilated Bladder and Congenital Megacystis

Megacystis

  • Originally attributed to bladder neck obstruction causing massive bilateral VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) and a thin wall — but bladder neck surgery did not change outcome.
  • Re-examination showed normal urethras and complete emptying on VCUG (voiding cystourethrography): reflux is the cause, not the consequence, of dilation, through continuous recycling of urine between upper tracts and bladder.
  • Modern usage: megacystis = any prenatally dilated bladder, cause-neutral. Like hydronephrosis, a finding, not a diagnosis — harder to judge because the bladder is normally distended at times. Rare (about 0.06% of unselected fetuses).
  • First trimester (10–14 WGA): normal controls all had sagittal length <10% of CRL (crown–rump length), i.e. ≤6 mm; established megacystis had a ratio ≥13% and diameter ≥8 mm.
  • Beyond the first trimester definitions are mostly subjective; later studies focus on a disease such as PUV (posterior urethral valves) or PBS, where size is one finding among several. Bladder cycling is the marker of normal function.
  • The formula (15–40 WGA) is SL (sagittal length, mm) = GA (gestational age, weeks) − 5, 95% CI (confidence interval) ±7; the two tiers allow more specific prenatal counselling.

Aneuploidy.

  • About one-fifth of fetuses with megacystis are aneuploid, mostly trisomy 13 or 18 — more than maternal age and nuchal translucency predict.
  • Outcome uniformly poor: most terminated, the rest did not survive to term; high termination limits urological outcome data.

Resolution by size and timing (euploid fetuses).

  • First-trimester longitudinal diameter 7–15 mm: the large majority resolve spontaneously; the remainder have obstructive uropathy. >15 mm: none resolve — all obstructive uropathy.
  • In a national multicentre fetal-medicine database, overall resolution was a minority, but early presentation resolved about three times as often as late; early with ≤12 mm was a sensitive predictor of resolution (most resolved). Limitation: population selected by high termination; genetics not captured.

Megacystis Microcolon Intestinal Hypoperistalsis Syndrome

  • About 1% of megacystis but important for its morbidity. Prenatally, megacystis is often the only finding; gastrointestinal (GI) findings are rarely seen, but amniotic fluid digestive enzymes are abnormal in most if sampled.
  • Visceral smooth-muscle dysfunction of GI and urinary tracts; ACTG2 mutations (actin smooth-muscle filament).
  • Survival: historically about 20%, improving to about 55% in cases from 2004–2011.
  • Postnatal: abdominal distension, bilious vomiting, failure to pass meconium — bowel obstruction with an enlarged bladder = evaluate as MMIHS. Exclude Hirschsprung disease by colonic biopsy showing ganglion cells; contrast shows a hypomotile small bowel and a small colon.
  • Enteric care: ileostomy, gastrostomy, TPN (total parenteral nutrition); TPN complications — bloodstream infection and liver dysfunction — are the leading causes of death; small-intestinal transplantation is promising.
  • Urological care: morbidity mostly secondary to enteric disease → short-term CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) or vesicostomy for retention → once enterically stable, urodynamics → most need lifelong CIC and lifelong urological follow-up, as in PUV or neurogenic bladder. Normal micturition in older patients is reported but uncommon.

Concept — megacystis is a sign whose meaning is set by size, gestation at detection and timing of resolution, with karyotype and amniotic fluid as the overriding qualifiers; small, early, early-resolving is benign — large, late or persistent signals obstruction, and normal fluid with megacystis in a female points away from obstruction towards MMIHS.

Bladder Duplication

  • Complete or incomplete; sagittal or coronal. Complete sagittal duplication is commonest.
  • Rarely one bladder lacks a urethra → complete obstruction → ipsilateral renal dysplasia. Continence varies.
  • External genital duplication in up to 90%; lower GI duplication in up to 42%. Duplicated vagina ± separate unicornuate uterus; each duplicated penis has its own urethra. VUR, renal ectopia and dysplasia are common.
  • Non-urological anomalies are more frequent in sagittal than coronal duplication: GI malformations, spinal duplication, spina bifida, urogenital–GI fistulae.
  • Exstrophy variant: exstrophic bladder and urethra plus a normal closed intra-abdominal bladder.
  • Presentation: often neonatal (GI or genital anomalies); many later with recurrent infection or incontinence.
  • Work-up: karyotype, ultrasound, videourodynamics, genitogram, cross-sectional imaging; VCUG and renal nuclear scans for VUR and function. Final plan often deferred to endoscopy and surgical exploration.

Congenital Diverticula

  • Mucosa herniates between detrusor fibre defects; neck width = size of the muscle defect.
  • Low incidence (about 1.7% of symptomatic children imaged); true incidence higher because asymptomatic diverticula go undiscovered.
  • Detectable prenatally, but mostly found postnatally during work-up for infection, haematuria, incontinence or obstruction.
  • Ultrasound shows large ones; VCUG is the gold standard (shows VUR and post-void retention); nuclear scan for function and obstruction if hydroureteronephrosis.
  • Dynamic — may not appear on every study; repeat imaging if suspicion persists.

Paraureteral Congenital Diverticula

  • By far the commonest congenital diverticula; Hutch divided them into primary and secondary (secondary are acquired — see Acquired Diverticula).
  • Primary: herniation through the ureteral hiatus, between the intravesical ureter and the roof of the hiatus, most likely from a congenitally deficient bladder wall. An isolated Waldeyer sheath defect is unlikely because they are usually unilateral.
  • Small and asymptomatic → conservative. With VUR → many recommend excision.
  • Females resolve (diverticulum with VUR) spontaneously more often than males — attributed to higher male voiding pressures even without obstruction.
  • A primary paraureteral diverticulum does not preclude endoscopic dextranomer/hyaluronic acid (Deflux) injection for VUR.

Posterolateral and Multiple Congenital Diverticula

  • Associated with connective tissue disorders — Ehlers–Danlos, Williams, Menkes — occasionally non-syndromic.
  • Can become massive, often larger than the bladder (Fig. 42.5: isolated posterolateral diverticulum in a newborn, larger than the bladder).
  • Histology: an attenuated, lax muscle wall rather than classic herniation through a defect, without obstruction.
  • Presents with recurrent UTI (urinary tract infection) from stasis (Fig. 42.4: post-void residual in the diverticulum) and stones.
  • Asymptomatic → close observation. Symptomatic → resection (open or laparoscopic) eliminates UTI and often improves voiding.
  • Recurrence and new diverticula are common in syndromic patients → weigh against quality of life; vesicostomy or suprapubic drainage may be preferred. Postoperative urodynamics to detect obstruction or high-pressure voiding that would hasten recurrence.

Concept — diverticular behaviour follows the wall and the pressure: a focal defect at normal pressure gives a single intermittent primary diverticulum, generalised laxity gives giant recurrent syndromic ones, and raised pressure gives multiple secondary diverticula — which is why the outlet is treated before the diverticulum.

Key Points: Congenital Bladder Anomalies

  • Persistent megacystis with oligohydramnios → LUTO; megacystis resolving before delivery may have no postnatal sequelae.
  • MMIHS may show only megacystis prenatally.
  • Nondilated anomalies such as exstrophy are the most severe congenital urological malformations; amniotic fluid is usually normal in exstrophy.
  • Bladder agenesis is compatible with life only with ectopic ureteric drainage.
  • Connective tissue disorders → multiple diverticula, which may need treatment for stasis-related infection or stones.

Urachal Anomalies

  • Symptomatic remnant → complete excision with a bladder cuff, open or laparoscopic; simple, low morbidity.
  • The asymptomatic incidental remnant is the dilemma: urachal adenocarcinoma is unreported in children, but in adults carries high mortality and much more complex surgery.

Patent Urachus

  • Non-descent of the bladder or, more commonly, failure of the epithelial canal to obliterate.
  • Fetal obstruction theory is doubtful: patency is often absent in severely obstructed fetal bladders, and only a minority (up to 14%) have confirmed in-utero obstruction → obliteration appears independent of distension.
  • Retubularisation of an obliterated urachus, rather than primary patency, may explain umbilical urine drainage — supported by umbilical urinary fistulae in obstruction acquired later in life.
  • Neonatal umbilical drainage, enlarged or oedematous umbilicus, delayed cord-stump healing. Organisms: Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Enterococcus, Citrobacter, rarely Proteus.
  • Diagnosis: fluid-filled canal on longitudinal ultrasound, or contrast on retrograde fistulogram or VCUG (Fig. 42.7: contrast pooling in the umbilicus); MRI depends on bladder filling. Differentiate from a patent omphalomesenteric duct (coexistence rare).
  • Infected or abscessed: drain under antibiotics → complete excision with a bladder cuff once settled; remove all anomalous tissue to prevent recurrence, stones and adenocarcinoma.

Open excision: balloon catheter distends bladder → transverse or midline infraumbilical incision (infants: small transverse subumbilical, as the dome is still high) → feeding tube into the urachus for identification → circumscribe urachus at the umbilicus, preserving the umbilicus → longitudinal rectus fascia incision, muscles spread, dome identified → urachus isolated on a vessel loop → extraperitoneal dissection to the umbilicus, umbilical portion delivered and removed → bladder cuff including the insertion excised with electrocautery → two-layer watertight closure → catheter overnight.

  • Laparoscopic excision is feasible even under 6 months and safe at all ages; series report no complications or recurrences and short operating times.
  • Ports: three-port — camera midline between umbilicus and xiphoid, working ports in each upper quadrant; or camera in the left lower abdomen with 2-mm ports left lower and upper for a better view of the umbilical end. Robotic and single-port approaches are described.

Urachal Sinus

  • Obliterated at the bladder, open at the umbilicus; the caudal urachus is filled with desquamated epithelium.
  • Mimic: persistent omphalomesenteric duct (a Meckel diverticulum connected to the umbilicus) — the sinugram cannot separate them, since neither shows a bladder or bowel connection; both need complete excision; omphalomesenteric remnants may show gastric or small-bowel mucosa on histology.

Urachal Cyst

  • Usually distal; commoner in adults than children; contents of desquamated epithelium; S. aureus commonest when infected.
  • Infected: umbilical abscess, bladder infection, lower abdominal pain, voiding symptoms, painful mass. Ultrasound shows the cyst between anterior abdominal wall and peritoneum; CT or MRI if extensive.
  • Unrecognised → perforation into bladder or peritoneum → peritonitis, enteric fistula. Drain → complete excision.

Urachal Diverticulum

  • Usually asymptomatic, incidental; wide mouth drains well even if enlarged by obstruction. Intervene only if a narrow neck causes stones or UTI.

Key Points: Urachal Anomalies

  • Usually detected postnatally because of umbilical drainage.
  • Infected remnant → antibiotics, then surgical excision.
  • May be confused with a persistent omphalomesenteric duct preoperatively.
  • Discuss excision of an asymptomatic incidental remnant with the family, given potential adult malignant transformation.

Concept — every urachal anomaly is the same tube obliterated to a different level, so the level of residual patency predicts the communication, the symptom and the diagnostic test; treatment converges on complete excision with a bladder cuff, the only live debate being the asymptomatic incidental remnant.

Concept — congenital bladder anomalies are read through function before form — whether the bladder fills, empties and holds its wall — and both the prenatal picture and the postnatal treatment follow from those three questions.

Acquired Bladder Anomalies in Children

Noninflammatory Acquired Conditions

Acquired Diverticula

  • Result of infravesical obstruction, which is treated first; once outlet resistance is normal, remodelling may make diverticulectomy unnecessary.
  • Symptomatic → excise, with ipsilateral ureteric reimplantation if the ureter lies near or within the diverticulum; intravesical or extravesical, open or laparoscopic/robotic.
  • Secondary paraureteral (Hutch): from infravesical obstruction or neurogenic bladder — raised pressure forces mucosa between muscle fibres, usually one of many "pop-off" mechanisms; also from muscle weakened by infection or a defect after bladder surgery.

Progression (both paraureteral types): enlarging diverticulum damages the Waldeyer sheath → intravesical ureter pulled out of its anchored position → ureterovesical junction dysfunction → eventually ureteric obstruction → renal dysplasia reported.

  • Vicious circle: a large paraureteral or posterolateral diverticulum compresses the bladder neck or posterior urethra → outlet obstruction → diverticulum fills and expands → complete retention; seen with the congenital diverticular syndromes, leading to surgery.

Bladder Haemangiomas

  • Benign vascular tumours, mostly with Klippel–Trénaunay or Sturge–Weber syndrome; usually solitary, <3 cm, can be multifocal; present with gross haematuria ± irritative symptoms.

Fibroepithelial Polyps

  • Rare in the bladder; haematuria, irritative symptoms; outlet or ureteric obstruction reported. Second commonest paediatric bladder tumour after rhabdomyosarcoma.

Inflammatory Acquired Conditions

Nephrogenic Adenoma and Cystitis Cystica

  • Nephrogenic adenoma: rare benign tumour, mostly adult; in children a reaction to infection, stones, trauma or surgery. Presents with haematuria or irritative symptoms; diagnosis by cystoscopy and biopsy. No malignant change reported in children; long-term resolution reported with ibuprofen and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole after resection.
  • Malignant potential: in a young adult with post-traumatic neurogenic bladder, repeatedly resected nephrogenic adenoma became adenocarcinoma → radical cystectomy.
  • In biopsy series most had bladder augmentation with recurrent stones and infection; immunohistochemistry indicates origin from distal renal tubular cells.
  • Cystitis cystica: fluid-filled cystic masses from chronic inflammation. Cystitis glandularis: rarer in children; metaplasia to mucus-secreting epithelium; premalignant potential poorly supported.

Eosinophilic Cystitis

  • Sporadic case reports; cause unclear — immunological disease and allergy suggested. Histology: eosinophils throughout all layers of the wall.
  • Dysuria, haematuria, suprapubic pain, retention (Fig. 42.8: mass on ultrasound in a teenage boy with severe dysuria and gross haematuria; biopsy showed eosinophilic infiltrate, no malignancy).
  • Transurethral resection with corticosteroids, antihistamines or antibiotics most successful across ages; a small series treated with all three combined succeeded in every case.

Haemorrhagic Cystitis

  • Almost always related to treatment of childhood malignancy, especially haematological.
  • Commonest risk factor: cyclophosphamide or ifosfamide, exacerbated by BK virus, CMV (cytomegalovirus) and adenovirus in the immunocompromised child; pelvic radiotherapy also causes it.
  • Irrigation is difficult and traumatic in children (small catheters only) → better results with an early-detection protocol, catheter avoidance and oral pentosan polysulfate sodium.

Key Points: Acquired Bladder Anomalies in Children

  • Acquired diverticula can cause obstruction or stasis with recurrent UTI.
  • Routine cystoscopy after resection of nephrogenic adenoma — 80% recurrence in children.
  • Cystitis cystica can mimic tumour; best treated with long-term antibiotic prophylaxis.
  • Eosinophilic cystitis has no typical appearance and needs biopsy.
  • Cyclophosphamide causes haemorrhagic cystitis; mesna is given simultaneously for prevention, and oral pentosan polysulfate shows promise.

Concept — acquired lesions divide by what drives them — pressure (diverticula: treat the outlet first), vascular or stromal growth (haemangioma, polyp: local ablation or resection), and chronic irritation or toxicity (inflammatory lesions: remove the stimulus, biopsy because appearance misleads, and survey for recurrence).
