## Instalment 1 — Chapter Preamble, Neural Tube Defects (Classification, Epidemiology, Risk Factors, Pathogenesis, Perinatal Concerns, MOMS), Classification of Neuropathic LUT Dysfunction, Tables 46.1–46.2

*Source pp 900–902.*

### CHAPTER OPENING (unheaded preamble)

- **Definition:** neuromuscular (neurogenic, neuropathic) lower urinary tract (LUT) dysfunction is **bladder and/or urinary sphincter dysfunction secondary to aberrant innervation**.
- **Causes may be congenital or acquired, central or peripheral, transient or permanent** (Table 46.1).
- **Commonest congenital cause: neural tube defect (NTD)**, used near-synonymously with spina bifida (SB).
- **Survival has transformed:**
  - before 1960, **fewer than 10% survived infancy**;
  - by the mid-1990s, **more than 85% survived infancy**;
  - first-year mortality is now **about 5%**;
  - overall **survival at 10 years is about 90%**.
- **The most important urological intervention in this population was clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC)**, introduced by **Lapides (1971)**, to prevent upper-tract deterioration.
- **Long-term mortality rises with the level of the neurological deficit.** The **strongest risk factors for SB infant mortality are preterm birth (relative risk [RR] about 4.5) and low birth weight (RR about 4.8)**.
- **As survival lengthens, renal failure becomes a leading cause of morbidity and mortality.** This is why the **urinary tract is monitored for life, from infancy onward**.
- **About 10% of deaths in SB are urological.** This is the index of the urologist's role.
- **The urologist has two remits: protecting and managing the urinary tract, and enabling satisfactory sexual function.**

#### TABLE 46.1 Causes of Neuromuscular Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction

| Congenital | Acquired |
|---|---|
| Neural tube defect | Extensive pelvic surgery |
| Occult NTD: lipomeningocele and other spinal dysraphisms | Central nervous system (CNS) insults: cerebral palsy; brain tumours, infarcts, encephalopathies |
| Sacral agenesis | Spinal cord insults: trauma; transverse myelitis |
| Anorectal malformations | — |

- **What the table is for:** it is the **skeleton for the whole chapter**. NTD is covered first, then every other cause, grouped by brain, spinal cord and pelvis.
- **Trap:** **anorectal malformation is listed as congenital, but the LUT dysfunction usually comes from an associated spinal cord lesion.** It is not usually caused by the pull-through operation itself.

### NEURAL TUBE DEFECTS

#### Background and Classification

- **Mechanism:** NTDs arise from **abnormal development of the spinal canal and spinal cord**.
- **NTDs are classified along three axes: location, severity and the structures involved.**

**NTD Classification — Location × Severity**

| Type | Location | Open / closed | Defining feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Myelomeningocele** | Spinal | **Open** | **Cord, nerve roots and meninges protrude**. It is the **most severe and the most common open NTD**, usually **lumbosacral** (Fig. 46.1, Table 46.2) |
| Meningocele | Spinal | Open (source) | **Meninges only** protrude; cord and roots stay in the canal |
| **SB occulta** | Spinal | Closed | **Mildest form; up to 15% of the general population**. It is skin-covered, rarely symptomatic, and **usually a non-pathological variant** |
| **Sacral agenesis (caudal regression syndrome)** | Spinal | Closed | **One or more sacral vertebral bodies are absent** (Fig. 46.2). Severity varies; it **may coexist with cord tethering** |
| Anencephaly | Cranial | Open | Most of the brain and skull are absent; very limited survival |
| Encephalocele | Cranial | Open | A sac of brain and meninges herniates through a skull defect |

- **Open versus closed:**
  - an **open NTD** leaves **neural tissue exposed** to the environment;
  - a **closed NTD** is **skin-covered**.
- **Trap:** the source places meningocele among the open NTDs in the anatomical list, but lists it as closed in the severity list (see flags).
- **Detection:** NTDs are identified **prenatally by maternal serum α-fetoprotein and ultrasound**. Early detection allows counselling and delivery planning.

#### Epidemiology

- **Timing of neural tube closure:**
  - spinal cord and vertebral column formation **begins around day 18** of gestation;
  - closure proceeds **caudally from the cephalad end**;
  - closure is **complete by day 35**.
- **The mechanism of dysraphism is unknown**; the cause is multifactorial.
- **Worldwide prevalence is about 2 per 1000 births**, disproportionately in **developing countries**.
- **In the USA, SB birth prevalence has plateaued at about 30 per 100,000.** Non-Hispanic Black children carry the lowest risk.
- **Prevalence varies geographically and over time.**

#### Risk Factors for the Development of Neural Tube Defects

- **Folate is the dominant factor.** Risk rises with **low maternal folate, impaired folate-mediated pathways, or anti-folate antibodies**.
- **Folic acid supplementation in randomised trials reduces NTD prevalence by about 50–70%.**
- **US Public Health Service (1992): 400 µg folic acid daily** for all women of child-bearing age.
  - **Why all women:** the **neural tube closes before most women know they are pregnant**.
- **Optimal timing: at least 4 weeks before conception and through the first month of pregnancy.**
- **Adherence is poor; only about one-third supplement as advised.**
  - This led to **mandatory fortification of flour and pasta (late 1990s)**, which reduced NTD prevalence by **about 20–50%**.
- **Genetic component:**
  - **Concordance is higher in monozygotic than dizygotic twins.**
  - **Recurrence risk is about 5% after one affected pregnancy, but does not exceed 10% after two.** This argues **against a monogenic cause**.
  - More than 240 genes are implicated in mouse models, but **no clinically actionable human gene** has been identified.
- **Other maternal risk factors:**
  - **age at either extreme**;
  - **obesity; diabetes; fever or influenza; caffeine**;
  - occupational exposures; polycyclic hydrocarbons; pesticides (endosulfan, DDT, DDE);
  - low education or socioeconomic status; passive smoking; periconceptional stress; low weight gain;
  - **folate antagonists: valproic acid, carbamazepine**;
  - prior miscarriage or birth defect; higher birth order.

#### Pathogenesis

- **Almost all infants with an open NTD have an Arnold–Chiari type II malformation.** Its components are:
  - hindbrain herniation;
  - brainstem abnormalities;
  - low-lying venous sinuses;
  - a small posterior fossa.
- **Chiari II has wider effects:**
  - it is associated with **hydrocephalus**;
  - it affects motor, cranial-nerve and cognitive function;
  - it carries **significant mortality even after surgery**.
- **Hydrocephalus treatment:**
  - traditional: **ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt**;
  - alternative: **endoscopic third ventriculostomy with choroid plexus cauterisation**, which controls hydrocephalus in **more than 70%**, **avoids a shunt**, and gives **equivalent neurocognitive outcomes**.
- **The myelomeningocele lesion is variable.** It depends on which neural elements have everted into the sac.
- **The bony level predicts the neurological level poorly:**
  - the two differ by **one to three vertebrae in either direction**;
  - LUT function **cannot be predicted from the bony defect or from lower-limb function**.

*Perinatal Concerns.*

- **Antenatal ultrasound shows the injury is progressive in utero:**
  - **lower-limb movement is lost**;
  - **hindbrain herniation and hydrocephalus worsen**.
- **Animal models:** **prenatal coverage preserves neurological function** and limits hindbrain herniation.
- **"Two-hit" hypothesis:**
  - **hit 1:** failure of neural tube formation;
  - **hit 2:** ongoing mechanical and chemical injury to the exposed tissue in utero.
  - It follows that **in-utero repair should improve outcome**.

**Management of Myelomeningocele Study (MOMS)** — landmark randomised trial

- **Design:** **prenatal repair before 26 weeks** versus standard **postnatal repair**.
- **Benefits of prenatal repair:**
  - **Primary outcome** (fetal or neonatal death, or need for a cerebrospinal fluid shunt) was **reduced, RR 0.70**.
  - **Yet 40% of the prenatal group still needed a shunt.** Neuromotor gain and reversal of hindbrain herniation were not universal.
  - **Second primary outcome** (a cognitive and motor composite at 30 months) was **better after prenatal repair**.
- **Neutral results:**
  - **no maternal deaths**;
  - **no difference in fetal or neonatal deaths**.
- **Costs of prenatal repair:**
  - **more obstetric complications:** oligohydramnios, chorioamniotic separation, placental abruption, transfusion at delivery, spontaneous membrane rupture;
  - **one-third had uterine-scar dehiscence or a very thin scar** at delivery;
  - **preterm birth: mean 34.1 versus 37.3 weeks; 13% delivered before 30 weeks** (none in the postnatal group);
  - **more procedures for delayed cord tethering**, **more respiratory distress syndrome**, **lower birth weight**.
- **In the ten years since MOMS, refined open and fetoscopic techniques have reduced maternal and fetal risk.**

|---|---|

| Low thoracic | 5% |

- **The pattern:** **about two-thirds are lumbosacral or sacral** (47% + 20% = 67%), and **over 90% including lumbar**. High lesions are rare.
- **Trap:** a **high bony level does not mean the sacral cord is spared, and a low level does not mean it is involved**. The bony level is unreliable (see Pathogenesis).

**KEY POINTS: NEURAL TUBE DEFECTS**
- **NTD is the commonest cause** of neuropathic LUT dysfunction in children; **myelomeningocele is both the most severe and the most common type**.
- **Folic acid is the key preventive measure**, taken at least 4 weeks before conception and through the first month of pregnancy.
- **LUT effects cannot be predicted** from the bony defect, the neurological level or lower-limb function.
- **About 10% of SB deaths are urological.**

*Concept —* **The spinal lesion in SB is both unpredictable and progressive: the bony level misstates the neurological level, and the exposed cord continues to be injured in utero.** Every later principle follows: **urodynamic definition rather than anatomical inference, early and repeated study, and lifelong surveillance.**

### NEUROPATHIC LOWER URINARY TRACT DYSFUNCTION

#### Classification

- **Normal bladder function:**
  - **storage at low pressure**;
  - **emptying by a detrusor contraction coordinated with a fall in outlet resistance**.
- **Denervation of the bladder and/or external urethral sphincter disrupts this.** Dysfunction is classified as **storage, emptying, or both**.
- **Terminology (International Children's Continence Society [ICCS], 2016):**
  - **overactive bladder** replaces "hyperreflexic";
  - **underactive bladder** replaces "hyporeflexic/areflexic".

**Neurogenic Detrusor Overactivity versus Neurogenic Underactive Bladder**

| Neurogenic detrusor overactivity (NDO) | Neurogenic underactive bladder |
|---|---|
| **Involuntary, frequent detrusor contractions** | **Impaired stimulation of the detrusor**, so contraction is ineffective |
| If sensate → **sudden strong urge and frequency** | **Incomplete emptying, retention** |
| **Storage volume reduced** by small capacity and high storage pressure, the result of **pathological detrusor remodelling** (multifactorial; mechanism not fully understood) | **Incontinence (overflow) and urinary tract infection (UTI)** |

- **Incontinence occurs with or without intact bladder sensation.**
- **Low outlet resistance also compromises storage.** It arises from an **untoned bladder neck and/or a denervated external sphincter**.

*Concept —* **Every neuropathic LUT pattern reduces to two components, the detrusor (overactive or underactive) and the outlet (competent, incompetent or dyssynergic).** Their **combination sets both the continence problem and the renal risk**.

### Assessment — overview

- **Goals of assessment:**
  - **preserve renal function**;
  - **achieve continence**;
  - **prevent UTI**;
  - **enable sexual function**.
  - Each depends on **reliable, precise and timely testing**.
- **Four bodies publish guidance:**
  - **ICCS (2012)**;
  - **British Association of Paediatric Urologists (BAPU, 2016)**;
  - **Spina Bifida Association (SBA, 2018)**;
  - **European Association of Urology (EAU, 2022)**.
  - They are **broadly in agreement despite minor variation**.

- **What the table is for:** it sets out the **baseline and surveillance schedule** for the neurogenic bladder.
- **The pattern:**
  - **ICCS, SBA and EAU converge on an early study within the first 3 months and serial ultrasound through the first 2 years.**
  - **BAPU is the outlier**, being the most selective about urodynamics.
- **The traps:**
  - **Only ICCS and BAPU positively recommend cystography.** EAU calls it "may be helpful"; SBA does not address it.
  - **Baseline creatinine is recommended by three bodies (ICCS, SBA, EAU); BAPU is silent.**
  - **Vitamin B12 surveillance begins 2 years after bowel reconstruction.** Time is needed for terminal-ileal loss to deplete stores.

### History and Physical Examination

- **In a child without obvious NTD stigmata, the symptoms overlap with non-neurogenic LUT dysfunction:**
  - incontinence;
  - LUT symptoms (LUTS);
  - UTI.
- **No feature is specific.** The **red flags** are:
  - **worsening incontinence or LUTS**;
  - **recurrent febrile UTI**;
  - **upper-tract change on imaging**.
- **Examine every child's spine and do a lower-limb neurological examination.**
- **Cutaneous markers are present in about 90% of occult spinal dysraphisms** (Fig. 46.3):
  - hair tuft;
  - dermal vascular malformation;
  - sacral dimple;
  - abnormal gluteal cleft.
- **Mild sacral agenesis:**
  - the child **may walk normally**;
  - the telltale signs are a **short gluteal cleft and flattened buttocks** (Fig. 46.4).
- **Assess sexual function in adolescents and young adults.** (See Sexual Function.)

### Urodynamics

- **Urodynamics are the primary test driving management.**
- **Landmark study — McGuire (1981):** a **detrusor leak point pressure (DLPP) above 40 cm H₂O is associated with VUR and upper-tract change**.
- **Impaired compliance also predicts renal injury.** Compliance = volume ÷ end-filling pressure.
- **No precise pressure threshold for renal injury is known.** The principle is established: **high storage pressure damages the kidneys**.

**Timing of urodynamics by condition**

| Setting | When |
|---|---|
| Open NTD | **Once the child can be transported and lie supine**, usually **within the first few months**. It is often impractical before first discharge. **Safe from about 3 months of age** |
| Closed NTD or spinal cord tumour | **Before neurosurgery and about 3–4 months after** |
| SCI | **Defer until spinal shock resolves**, usually **3–4 months** |

- **Newborn myelomeningocele urodynamics:**
  - **most have detrusor contractions**, including many with **upper lumbar or thoracic lesions in whom the sacral cord is spared**;
  - **over a third are acontractile**; compliance during filling may be good or poor.
- **Interpretation varies and interrater reliability is poor.**

#### Detrusor–Sphincter Dyssynergia

- **Definition:** DSD (also called neurogenic detrusor–sphincter dysfunction) is **lack of coordination between the detrusor and the external urethral sphincter**.
- **Why it matters:** failure of the sphincter to relax during a voiding or overactive contraction is thought to **drive pathological bladder remodelling**. **Accurate diagnosis is therefore essential.**
- **Electromyography (EMG) is the best test for sphincter function and coordination.** Bladder-neck appearance on fluoroscopy is **unreliable**.

**Surface Patch EMG versus Direct Concentric Needle EMG**

| Surface (patch) EMG | Direct concentric needle EMG |
|---|---|
| **Commonest method**, electrodes on the perineum | **Reliable and objective**; placed alongside the standard UDS catheters (Fig. 46.5) |
| **Reliability challenged by UMPIRE:** patch-EMG DSD was not supported by the other urodynamic elements, so **DSD was removed from the revised "high-risk" category** | Needs **specialised training or a neurologist collaborator** |
| Shows coordination only | Also shows **innervation: acute versus chronic denervation** |

- **Why needle EMG helps with suspected re-tethering:** spine MRI is **often ambiguous because of postoperative scarring** (Fig. 46.6). Needle EMG provides **correlative evidence** for the neurosurgeon.
- **Newborn myelomeningocele, needle EMG:**
  - **intact sacral reflex arc in about 40%**;
  - **partial denervation in about a quarter**;
  - **complete loss of sacral cord function in about a third**.
  - This is the **baseline for later comparison**.

**Urodynamic Risk Categories for Renal Deterioration — earlier scheme versus UMPIRE revision**

| Category | Definition (earlier scheme) | UMPIRE revision |
|---|---|---|
| **Hostile** | **End-filling pressure or DLPP ≥40 cm H₂O, or NDO with DSD** | **DSD removed** from the high-risk definition (patch EMG not supported) |
| **Intermediate** | **NDO, reduced compliance, end-filling pressure or DLPP 25–39 cm H₂O** | — |
| **Abnormal but safe** | **End-filling pressure or DLPP <25 cm H₂O** | — |
| **Normal** | Normal capacity; **compliance >15 mL/cm H₂O**; **no NDO; no DSD; minimal post-void residual** | — |

- **UMPIRE** is the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Urologic and Renal Protocol for the Newborn and Young Child with SB. It is a landmark prospective protocol.

*Future directions.*

- **Needed:** non-invasive, home or ambulatory urodynamics.
- **Promising:** machine-learning interpretation.
- **Both depend on standardised, reliable data, which do not yet exist.**

### Imaging

*Ultrasound.*

- **Renal and bladder ultrasound is essential.** It is **non-invasive**, gives real-time imaging, and **tracks renal size, growth and urinary tract dilatation**. Dilatation predicts renal health.
- **Ultrasound is unreliable for:**
  - **renal scarring**;
  - **VUR**;
  - **bladder wall thickness as a marker of neuropathic bladder**.
- **Every guideline recommends a baseline and interval scans** (Table 46.3).
- **Timing of the first scan:**
  - NTD: **before newborn discharge**;
  - acquired causes (for example SCI): **soon after the injury**.
- **UMPIRE baseline ultrasound:**
  - **56% normal**;
  - **40% mild hydronephrosis**;
  - **4% moderate to severe**.
- **Ultrasound also measures residual urine**, which informs the need for CIC.
- **Frequency:** **usually yearly**; some guidelines scan **6-monthly for the first 2 years, then yearly**.
- **Spinal ultrasound works in the first few months, before the posterior arches ossify.** It detects occult dysraphism (Fig. 46.7).

*Dimercaptosuccinic Acid Renal Scintigraphy.*

- **Technetium-99m DMSA is the most sensitive test for renal cortical defects.**
- **It is used inconsistently** because of isotope availability and expertise.
  - **In UMPIRE only about one-third had a baseline DMSA**, and **about 8% of these had defects**.
- **The EAU text recommends a baseline DMSA** to allow **precise longitudinal assessment of renal damage**. (See flags on Table 46.3.)

*Cystography.*

- **Contrast imaging of the bladder and urethra is a fundamental requirement.**
  - **Ideal: video UDS**, which **links the anatomy to the pressures**;
  - otherwise **VCUG** (Fig. 46.8).
- **Its main purpose is to detect VUR.** This matters especially when there is:
  - **on ultrasound:** hydronephrosis, ureteric dilatation, renal size or contour discrepancy, or bladder wall thickening;
  - **on UDS:** detrusor overactivity, poor compliance, high leak point pressure, or DSD.
- **UMPIRE: only 7% of newborns with myelomeningocele have grade III–V VUR** on screening cystography.
- **High-grade VUR acts as a pressure "pop-off" and makes urodynamic pressures unreliable** (Fig. 46.8).

*What the baseline battery achieves.*

- It provides a **baseline** of upper and lower tract, cord and CNS.
- It allows **comparison over time**, so that **deterioration or progressive denervation** is detected early.
- It **identifies infants at risk** (poor compliance, overactivity, DSD) **before** the upper tract changes, so **prophylactic treatment can start early**.
- It supports **counselling of parents**.

### Laboratory Studies

**Serum Creatinine versus Serum Cystatin C as a GFR Marker in SB**

| Serum creatinine | Serum cystatin C |
|---|---|
| **Made from the non-enzymatic breakdown of muscle creatine** | **13-kDa cysteine proteinase inhibitor made at a constant rate by all nucleated cells** |
| **Filtered and also secreted** | **Freely filtered, not secreted, almost entirely catabolised in the proximal tubule**, so the level reflects GFR |
| Level depends on age, sex, height and **above all muscle mass** | **Independent of muscle mass** |
| **Low muscle mass in SB → normal creatinine despite a falling GFR** | **Correlates better with measured GFR than the Schwartz formula** or other small proteins |
| **Cheap, easy, consistent: baseline recommended by three guidelines** | **Limitations:** less reliable in **early** impairment; not widely available; **affected by inflammation and protein catabolism** |

- **Gold standard for glomerular filtration rate (GFR):** a **nuclear-medicine clearance study**. It is invasive and involves radiation.
- **The need:** a serum marker that tracks GFR in this at-risk population.
- **Cystatin C-based estimate:** log(GFR) = 1.962 + [1.123 × log(1/cystatin C)].
- **In adults, cystatin C predicts end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and death better than creatinine** (meta-analysis).
- **Bottom line:**
  - **cystatin C is superior to creatinine** in SB;
  - if it is unavailable, or if mild impairment is suspected → **formal clearance study**;
  - **baseline creatinine is still recommended**.

**KEY POINTS: NEUROPATHIC LUT DYSFUNCTION: ASSESSMENT**
- **Suspect a neuropathic cause** with **worsening incontinence or LUTS, recurrent febrile UTI, or upper-tract change**.
- **Urodynamics drive management**, but **interrater reliability is poor**.
- **DSD is thought to be a primary driver of pathological remodelling.**
- **SB ultrasound:** **56% normal, 40% mild dilatation, 4% moderate to severe**.
- **Cystography is fundamental**, ideally by video UDS. **About 7% of newborns with SB have dilating VUR.**
- **Baseline serum creatinine is recommended.**

*Concept —* **Assessment is built on one causal chain: high storage pressure → upper-tract injury.** Each test measures a different link:
- **urodynamics** measure the pressure;
- **EMG** shows the outlet that generates it;
- **cystography** shows the reflux that transmits it;
- **ultrasound and DMSA** show the damage;
- **cystatin C** exposes what low muscle mass hides from creatinine.

## Instalment 3 — Management Goals, CIC, Pharmacotherapy (Table 46.5: Antimuscarinics, β3 Agonists, Propiverine, Sympathomimetics), Early Medical Intervention and Its Effects on Bladder, UTI, Upper Tract, VUR, Surgery and Renal Outcome

*Source pp 908–912.*

### Management

- **Goals, in order of priority:**
  1. **preserve renal function** (first and foremost);
  2. achieve continence;
  3. avoid UTI;
  4. enable sexual function and fertility.

#### Medical Management

*Clean Intermittent Catheterization.*

- **CIC is the single most important non-surgical tool.** It was first described by **Lapides (1971)**.
- **How it works:** regular emptying **prevents injurious overdistension** and **bypasses DSD**.
- **Timing differs between guidelines** (Table 46.4). **The authors favour early CIC in appropriate patients** because the renal and bladder benefits are well established.
- **Families report that starting CIC in infancy makes it easier to learn and integrate.**
- **If CIC alone fails** to improve hydronephrosis, increase capacity or reduce UTI → **overnight indwelling catheter drainage** has some success.

*Pharmacotherapy.*

- **Main drug groups:** **antimuscarinics, β3 agonists and sympathomimetics** (Table 46.5).
- **Antimuscarinics are first-line for detrusor overactivity and/or hypertonicity.**
- **Receptor pharmacology:**
  - the detrusor carries **M2 and M3 receptors**;
  - **M2 are more numerous**;
  - **M3 mediate the contraction** caused by acetylcholine.
- **Response is good at first**, but **side effects and waning efficacy erode long-term adherence**.
- **Effects of antimuscarinics:**
  - **increased capacity**;
  - **increased volume at first contraction**;
  - **fewer incontinence episodes**;
  - **fewer catheterisations** (Fig. 46.9).
- **Common side effects:** **dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, facial flushing, dizziness, headache**.
- **Cognition:** a **prospective randomised double-blind trial showed no adverse effect on attention or memory**.

**Antimuscarinics — Distinguishing Features and Signature Adverse Effects**

| Drug | Distinguishing feature | Signature adverse or practical issue |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxybutynin** | **Commonest in children**; the only one with **oral, transdermal and intravesical** routes | Oral: classic anticholinergic effects. **Patch: skin irritation, stopped by about 20%.** **Intravesical:** avoids **first-pass metabolism**, so fewer side effects; low-level evidence; **stopped because of inconvenience (crushing tablets) more often than because of side effects** |
| **Tolterodine** | Lowers both non-voiding activity and voiding amplitude | **Minimal CNS effects (poor brain penetration)**; **raised capacity under 10 years, fewer incontinence episodes over 12 months** |
| **Fesoterodine** | **Prodrug** with the **same active metabolite (5-hydroxymethyl tolterodine)** | **Less pharmacokinetic variability and fewer adverse events than tolterodine.** Activation **does not depend on the cytochrome P450 pathway** that activates tolterodine |
| **Trospium** | **Quaternary amine**; **half-life about 8 hours versus 2 hours for oxybutynin** | **Markedly fewer CNS adverse events** (minimal M1 affinity); adverse events uncommon |
| **Solifenacin** | **Once daily**; **licensed from 2 years**; **suspension** | Mild side effects common, moderate uncommon, **few stop** |
| **Propiverine** | **Mixed antimuscarinic and calcium antagonist** (see flags) | Urodynamic improvement **greater than with oxybutynin** in comparative studies |

- **β3 agonists (mirabegron, vibegron):**
  - **well tolerated with minimal side effects**;
  - **can cause hypertension**;
  - mirabegron **alone or with an antimuscarinic** gives respectable efficacy in idiopathic and neuropathic overactive bladder;
  - **first-line mirabegron may save costs.**
- **Propiverine mechanism:**
  - **it reduces calcium entry into the detrusor cell**, which **limits calcium–calmodulin-dependent activation of the contractile apparatus** (actomyosin ATPase);
  - it also has antimuscarinic action;
  - result: **improved LUTS and, above all, urodynamic parameters**, **greater than with oxybutynin**;
  - in a small prospective series, capacity and detrusor pressure improved substantially and incontinence improved in about half.

*Sympathomimetic Agents.*

- **Indication:** **incontinence from inadequate urethral resistance.**
- **Mechanism:** **ephedrine** or **pseudoephedrine** act on the **bladder neck, where α-adrenergic receptor density is highest**. This **raises outlet resistance** (Fig. 46.10).
- **Response:**
  - **only a minority respond, and responders cannot be predicted**;
  - when they work, the effect is **dramatic**, with **dryness between catheterisations**.
- **Adverse effects:**
  - **dizziness, nausea, nervousness, insomnia, anorexia, headache, mood change**;
  - **urinary retention in children who void volitionally**.

*Early Medical Intervention.*

- **Definition:** **early intervention = CIC plus antimuscarinic.**
- **Indication:** **proactive treatment of high-risk dynamics**:
  - **DSD**;
  - **poor compliance**;
  - **high outlet pressure**;
  - **outflow obstruction**.
- **History:** the concept was introduced by **Bauer (1986)**. It split the field into two camps:
  - **proactive**;
  - **"wait and see"**, intervening only after upper-tract change.
- **Why the proactive camp prevailed:**
  - the **risk to the upper tract from a hostile bladder was confirmed**;
  - **oxybutynin was shown to benefit the detrusor at cellular and muscarinic level**.
- **Current position: all paediatric urology societies now endorse proactive management of a hostile bladder on neonatal urodynamics.**

**Early Intervention versus Expectant ("Wait and See") Management**

| Outcome | Expectant | Early CIC plus antimuscarinic |
|---|---|---|
| **Bladder dynamics** | Hostile dynamics persist | **Overactive contractions abolished or peak pressure lowered; filling pressure at capacity falls** with poor compliance; minimal side effects, none from CIC |
| **Continence** | — | **Improved**; up to **about 44% dry with preserved upper tracts at 6 years** |
| **UTI** | **About 50% by 15 months, about 80% by 15 years**; recurrence common | **About 0.3 infections per year** |
| **Hydroureteronephrosis** | **18–80%** (72% with dyssynergia) | **0–15%** |
| **VUR** | **Up to 50% by age 9** | **Much less common** |
| **Urinary diversion / augmentation** | **Up to about 11% diverted** | **18–24% fewer augmentations**; no renal-protective surgery in one early-CIC cohort |
| **Renal scarring** | **27–50%** | **4–12%** |
| **ESRD** | **About 30%** | **0–1.6%** |

- **Limit:** early intervention **gives no advantage to children who empty spontaneously**.
- **Baseline renal damage at birth:**
  - one study reported **renal impairment in about 7%** and **DMSA abnormality in 30%** of newborns with SB;
  - **UMPIRE found only 8% with cortical defects**;
  - **either way, neuropathic LUT dysfunction harms the kidneys**.

**KEY POINTS: NEUROPATHIC LOWER URINARY TRACT: MANAGEMENT**
- **CIC is the single most important non-surgical tool.**
- **Antimuscarinics are first-line** for overactivity or hypertonicity, **with no adverse cognitive effect** (randomised double-blind trial).
- **Early intervention (CIC plus antimuscarinic) is recommended by all paediatric urology societies** for hostile dynamics in neonates.

*Concept —* **Early intervention works because it attacks the cause (storage pressure and failure to empty) before the consequence (reflux, dilatation, scarring, renal failure).** Every row of the comparison is the same pressure–drainage mechanism measured at a different end-organ. The **one exception, the child who empties spontaneously, proves the rule**: that bladder has no pressure problem to prevent.

## Instalment 4 — Surgical Management Overview, Intradetrusor Botulinum Toxin, Management of VUR (Credé, Antireflux Surgery, Augmentation, Endoscopic Injection), Sexual Function (Psychosocial, Precocious Puberty, Contraception, Erectile, Ejaculatory and Reproductive Function)

*Source pp 912–915.*

### Surgical Management

- **When medical management fails, surgery aims to:**
  - improve bladder dynamics;
  - achieve continence;
  - facilitate CIC.
- **Augmentation, bladder-neck procedures and continent catheterisable channels are covered in the reconstruction chapter.** Here: **botulinum toxin** and **management of VUR**.

#### Botulinum Toxin

- **Agent:** intradetrusor **botulinum toxin A (BTA)**, a neurotoxin from ***Clostridium botulinum*** (Gram-positive).
- **Mechanism:**
  - **it inhibits acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction**, causing paralysis;
  - it also **inhibits release of ATP and substance P**;
  - it **down-regulates purinergic and capsaicin receptors on bladder afferents**;
  - it **reduces M2, M3, P2X2 and P2X3 expression**.
  - It therefore acts on **both the efferent (motor) and afferent (sensory) arms**.
- **History:**
  - **first used in adults with SCI (Dykstra and Sidi, 1990)**;
  - **first used in children with neuropathic bladder in 2002**.

*Technique (as a sequence).*

- **General anaesthesia → rigid cystoscopy → about 10 U/kg (max total 360 U) → about 30 injection sites → trigone spared.**

*Outcomes.*

- **Continence:** **65–87%** of children who fail CIC plus drugs become continent.
- **Urodynamics:**
  - **lower maximum detrusor pressure**;
  - **higher maximum cystometric capacity**;
  - **improved compliance**.
- **Duration of effect:** **6 to about 10.5 months**.
- **Minimum age:** commonly **2 years**, which matches the **FDA approval age for cerebral palsy**.
- **Satisfaction:** patient and carer satisfaction **>70%**.
- **Stopping antimuscarinics:** **about 25% stop after the first injection** and a further **about 10% after the second**.
- **Avoiding augmentation:** **close to 90%** of children who failed first-line treatment avoided augmentation.
- **Side effects are infrequent:**
  - retention;
  - UTI;
  - haematuria;
  - **transient muscle weakness**;
  - dysuria;
  - pain.

*Repeat injection.*

- **Repeat injections stay effective, and the interval between them lengthens.**
- **Lowering the dose (300 → 200 U) led to relapse**, which was reversed by returning to the original dose.
- **Serial injections improve urodynamics further. There is no tachyphylaxis, no neutralising-antibody effect and no progressive fibrosis.**
  - **Histology:** there is no more oedema, inflammation or fibrosis than in toxin-naive bladders, and **fibrosis actually falls** with repeat injections.

*Poor response.*

- **Predicted by pre-existing poor compliance.**
- **Complete non-response has been linked to anti-BTA antibodies**, although another study found no link between the number of injections, antibodies and response.

*Sphincter injection.*

- **Injecting the external sphincter as well as the detrusor** (in a cohort with DSD in more than 90%) **significantly improved post-void residual**. Constipation and incontinence trended better (not significant).
- **Preliminary**: needs confirmation.

- **Bottom line:** **intradetrusor BTA improves continence and urodynamics, may avoid reconstruction after failed conservative treatment, and serial injection causes neither fibrosis nor loss of effect.**

#### Management of Vesicoureteral Reflux in Neuropathic Dysfunction of the Lower Urinary Tract

- **Prevalence:** **dilating VUR (grades III–V) is present in 7% of neonates with SB** (UMPIRE).
- **It almost always accompanies hostile dynamics:**
  - poor compliance;
  - overactivity;
  - DSD.
  - **VUR is rare without them.**
- **Untreated, reflux accumulates: 30–40% by age 5.**

**Treatment by pattern (International Reflux Classification)**

| Situation | Management |
|---|---|
| **Grade I–III**, voids spontaneously **or** complete lesion with little outlet resistance **and empties completely** | **Antibiotic prophylaxis alone** |
| **Grade IV–V** | **CIC to ensure complete emptying** |
| **Cannot empty spontaneously, any grade** | **CIC** |
| **Poor compliance, with or without hydroureteronephrosis** | **Add an antimuscarinic** to lower intravesical pressure and decompress the upper tracts |

- **With the LUT managed this way, VUR resolves in 30–55%.**
- **Bacteriuria is common on CIC (up to about half)** but **harmless unless VUR is high-grade**. Symptomatic infection and scarring are rare with low-grade reflux.

**Credé Manoeuvre — Reactive versus Denervated External Sphincter** (Fig. 46.11)

| Reactive sphincter | Denervated, non-reactive sphincter |
|---|---|
| **Credé → reflex rise in sphincter EMG → higher urethral resistance → high voiding pressure** | **No EMG rise, no rise in resistance or voiding pressure** |
| **Worsens VUR and its "water-hammer" effect on the kidneys: avoid** | **Credé not harmful** |

- **Rule: avoid Credé voiding in children with VUR**, especially if the sphincter is reactive.

*Antireflux surgery.*

- **Indications are much the same as for children with a normal LUT:**
  - **recurrent febrile UTI despite adequate antibiotics and proper catheterisation**;
  - **persistent hydroureteronephrosis despite effective emptying and lowered pressure**;
  - **severe reflux with an anatomical abnormality of the ureterovesical junction**.
- **Jeffs (1976):** antireflux surgery **succeeds in neuropathic bladders if complete emptying is ensured**.
  - **Before this, reimplantation results were so poor that diversion was favoured.**
  - **Since CIC, success approaches 95%.**
- **Unilateral VUR: operate on one side only.** Contralateral VUR does not arise after surgery.
- **At augmentation cystoplasty:**
  - **low-grade VUR:** acceptable resolution **without reimplantation**;
  - **dilating VUR (≥ grade III):** augmentation alone leaves **persistent reflux in 20–47%**, so **reimplant at the same operation**.

**Open Reimplantation versus Endoscopic Injection in Neuropathic Bladder**

| Open ureteroneocystostomy | Endoscopic injection |
|---|---|
| **Success about 84–96%** | **Success about 61–73%** in head-to-head comparisons. Series report **73% after one injection and 91% after two**; **about 95% when combined with intradetrusor BTA** |
| **Durable** | **Durability questioned: about 40% recur at a mean of 4.5 years** |
| Standard | **Reasonable alternative**; success reportedly **similar to that in normal bladders** |

- **Where success figures differ:**
  - single-arm series of endoscopic treatment report **73% (one injection) and 91% (two)**;
  - **comparative studies against open surgery report 61–72.5%**;
  - the difference lies in the **denominator and study design** (selected single-arm series versus head-to-head cohorts). **Both are recorded.**

**KEY POINTS: NEUROPATHIC LUT DYSFUNCTION — SURGICAL MANAGEMENT**
- **Surgery is used when medical management fails**: to improve dynamics, achieve continence and facilitate CIC.
- **Intradetrusor BTA improves continence and urodynamics**, may **avoid reconstruction**, and **serial injection does not cause fibrosis or loss of effect**.
- **Dilating VUR affects 7% of newborns with SB. Avoid Credé.**
- **Antireflux indications are the same as in a normal LUT.**
- **Reimplant dilating VUR at the time of augmentation.**

*Concept —* **Reflux in the neuropathic bladder is a consequence of the bladder, not a primary ureteric defect.** Treat the bladder first (emptying and pressure) and much reflux resolves. Surgery succeeds only on a bladder that has been made safe. **This is why Jeffs' insight, and the arrival of CIC, turned reimplantation from failure to about 95% success.**

### SEXUAL FUNCTION

- **Sexual function is an increasingly prominent issue as more patients with SB reach adulthood.** Nearly **three-quarters of adults with SB report interest in sexual activity**.

*Psychosocial factors.*

- **Parents and clinicians avoid discussing sexuality.**
- **Adolescents with SB are socially isolated** and **less likely to learn about sex from peers**.
- **Dependence for daily living**, and **parental overprotection limit autonomy**.
- **Men with SB who live independently report better erectile function and satisfaction.**
- **Parental permissiveness and age-appropriate treatment support self-esteem.**
- **Dissatisfaction is highest for partnership and sex life** (more than half of young adults).

*Central precocious puberty.*

- **Central precocious puberty (CPP) is more common in SB:**
  - **approaching 50% in girls**;
  - **10–30% in boys**.
- **Likely cause:** **hydrocephalus disturbing the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis**, together with **Chiari II**.

*Education and contraception.*

- **Offer SB-specific sexual education.**
- **Latex-free condoms are mandatory** for latex allergy or latex avoidance.
- **Combined oestrogen–progestin contraception → thromboembolic risk with reduced mobility.**
- **Intrauterine device → unsafe** (pelvic infection).

*Determinants of sexual function.*

- **Sexual function is inversely proportional to the level of neurological dysfunction.**
  - **Lowest lesions** → most likely to form partnerships.
- **Less sexual activity with more incontinence and greater disability.**
- **Older age → more likely to have a partner.**
- **No hydrocephalus → more likely to be sexually active.** The effect is **stronger in women**.
- **Women are more often sexually active, but are at risk of sexual abuse.** Unwanted sexual attention and touching are reported by about a third of women.
- **Teenagers:**
  - a **minority (about a third)** have had sexual encounters;
  - **almost all wish to marry and have children**;
  - **most desire sexual contact**.
- **Selection bias:** studies **under-represent the severely disabled or intellectually impaired**, so they **overstate function**.

**Sexual and Reproductive Function — Males versus Females with SB**

| Males | Females |
|---|---|
| **Sacral cord often involved → erection and ejaculation affected** | **Reproductive function is hormonally controlled → not affected** |
| **Erection:** older series (highly selected) **about 70–92%**; more recent **only about 40% normal**. **Ambulation predicts erectile function** | **Sexual dysfunction (Female Sexual Function Index [FSFI]): about 55%** (**89%** in a combined International Index of Erectile Function [IIEF] and FSFI study) |
| **Ejaculation about 40–75%**; **retrograde ejaculation** in some | **Continent women score better on lubrication and pain**; VP shunt, mobility and CIC made no difference |
| **Lesion at S1 or below → usually normal reproductive function**; **above S1 → only about 50% adequate** | **70–80% conceive and have an uneventful pregnancy.** **Late-pregnancy incontinence** and **caesarean delivery** are common |
| **Fathering children: about 17–39%** (plus about 25% with a good prognosis) | — |
| **Infertility:** ED, **poor semen quality, Sertoli-cell-only histology** | — |
| **IIEF, age ≥18: about 75% have ED**; function relates to maintaining erection and a **sacral root lesion** | **Arousal correlates with physical and mental quality of life** |
| **Intact pelvic parasympathetic reflex activity** is a positive correlate | — |

- **Both sexes are sexually dysfunctional more often and more severely than people with other causes, despite similar desire.**
- **IIEF may miss the range of experience in young men with SB.** A **disease-specific instrument** is needed.

*Offspring risk.*

- **3.7% risk of SB in offspring of an affected parent.** Higher if the **mother** is affected.
- **Both parents affected → about 15%.**
- **Women with SB: folic acid 4–5 mg daily before contemplating pregnancy.** Compare **400 µg for the general population**.

*Treatment.*

- **Sildenafil improves erectile function in about 80%** of men with myelomeningocele. This comes from a **randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trial**.
- **Penile sensation restoration:** **microsurgical ilioinguinal-to-dorsal penile nerve transfer** (in men with normal groin sensation).
  - Gives **better ipsilateral glans sensation, sexual function and satisfaction** in small series.
  - **Long-term data are awaited.**

**KEY POINTS: SEXUAL FUNCTION**
- **Sexuality is an increasingly prominent issue as more patients reach adulthood.**
- **CPP risk is increased.**
- **Sexual function is inversely proportional to the neurological level.**
- **70–80% of women conceive and have an uneventful pregnancy; caesarean is more common.**
- **3.7% risk of SB in offspring.**
- **75% of men aged ≥18 have ED; sildenafil is effective.**
- **55.5% of women have sexual dysfunction.**

*Concept —* **The sexes diverge because of what the sacral cord controls.**
- **Male sexual and reproductive function (erection, ejaculation) is neurally mediated through the sacral cord, which SB usually involves.**
- **Female reproductive function is hormonal and spared.**
- **So in men the lesion level sets function (S1 is the watershed), whereas in women continence and psychosocial factors dominate.**

### OTHER CONDITIONS ASSOCIATED WITH NEUROPATHIC LOWER URINARY TRACT DYSFUNCTION

- **Grouping:** conditions of the **brain, spinal cord and pelvis**, whether congenital or acquired.

#### Conditions of the Brain

##### Cerebral Palsy

- **Cerebral palsy (CP) is the commonest physical disability of childhood.**
  - **About 3.3 per 1000** eight-year-olds in the USA;
  - varies by region; commoner in Black and White than in Hispanic children;
  - **male : female about 1.2 : 1**.
- **Definition:** a disorder of **movement and posture** from a **non-progressive disturbance of the fetal or infant brain**. Sensation, cognition, communication and behaviour may also be affected.
- **Gross Motor Function Classification System — Expanded and Revised (GMFCS–E&R, 2007):**
  - based on **self-initiated movement** (sitting, transfers, mobility);
  - **five levels**, from **1 (walks without limitation)** to **5 (transported in a manual wheelchair)**;
  - age-banded descriptors.

*Continence.*

- **Children with CP usually become continent, but later than their peers.**
  - **Daytime continence comes first, then night-time within about a year.**
  - Overall, **14–34%** are continent (varies by series).
- **Median age at continence:**
  - **high intellect with diplegia or hemiplegia: about 3.6–4.1 years**;
  - **low intellect with tetraplegia: about 10–13 years**.
- **Mobility (GMFCS level) mostly does not predict continence**, but LUT dysfunction is **commoner in non-walkers** (about three-quarters versus one-quarter).
- **LUTS increase with age.**

*Symptoms.*

- **Urinary symptoms range 16–94%.** **Commoner in girls.**
- **Commonest symptom: incontinence** (most series **35–45%**).
- **Urgency and frequency** are also reported.
- **Monosymptomatic enuresis 3–13%.**
- **UTI 5–57%.**
- **Constipation 33–66%.**

*Upper tract.*

- **Upper-tract deterioration is uncommon: under 5%** (range 0.5–12%). It is mainly hydronephrosis; also asymmetric renal size, VUR and microlithiasis.
- **Risk factors:**
  - **increasing age**;
  - **clinical DSD** (retention, interrupted voiding, hesitancy);
  - **recurrent UTI**.

*Urodynamics.*

- **Normal in about one-third.**
- **Detrusor overactivity about 30–61%.**
- Underactivity uncommon (about 6%).
- **Reduced capacity 42–93%.**
- **DSD about 12%.**
- Raised post-void residual about 13%.
- **Uroflow is usually bell-shaped**; staccato, intermittent, plateau and tower patterns are less common.

##### Tumors/Infarcts/Encephalopathies

- **Presentation with CNS tumour:** **incontinence and retention** are commonest. UTI, straining, hydronephrosis and urgency also occur.
- **New bladder symptoms may herald tumour progression or recurrence.**
- **Urodynamics:**
  - poor compliance, inability to void, high voiding pressure, overactivity, raised residual, abnormal sphincter;
  - **no correlation with tumour location** (intracranial versus extracranial, suprasacral versus sacral).
- **Other brain conditions are poorly studied.** In **newborn hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE):**
  - **lower voided volume**;
  - **less arousal with voiding**;
  - **higher post-void residual and voiding frequency**.

#### Conditions of the Spinal Cord

##### Trauma

- **Paediatric SCI is rare:**
  - **Sweden: about 2.6 per million children per year**;
  - **USA:** spinal-injury admissions **about 170 per million** under 21 years, **SCI about 24 per million**.
- **Incidence rises with age.** **SCI is about six times commoner in adolescents than in children** (Fig. 46.12).
- **Sex:** **boys more often, but in young children the sexes are equal**.
- **Causes:**
  - **motor vehicle or bicycle accidents (about a quarter to a half)**;
  - falls from a height;
  - gunshot;
  - diving or sport.
- **Cause by age:**
  - **infants:** motor vehicle;
  - **toddlers and children:** falls;
  - **adolescents:** sport.
- **Iatrogenic causes:**
  - scoliosis, kyphosis or intraspinal surgery;
  - congenital aortic surgery;
  - **ligation of a patent ductus arteriosus**.
- **Neonatal cause: hyperextension during high forceps delivery.**
- **Why the child's cord is vulnerable** (hypermobility → **ischaemic necrosis**):
  - **horizontally oriented facet joints**, which predispose to anteroposterior subluxation;
  - **late development of paraspinal muscle and ligament support**;
  - **a relatively heavy head**, which puts the **fulcrum of maximal flexion in the upper cervical spine**.

**Spinal Shock versus Recovered Phase after SCI**

| Acute phase (spinal shock) | After recovery |
|---|---|
| **Underactive bladder, non-reactive sphincter** | **Detrusor contractility and sphincter reactivity return** as cord oedema settles |
| Needle EMG may still record **normal-appearing potentials** | **Suprasacral lesion** with disrupted reticulospinal pathways → **overactive detrusor plus DSD** |
| — | **Cauda equina lesion → little or no return** of bladder or sphincter function |

- **Sacral sensation and peripheral reflexes do not predict final LUT function.**
- **Thoracic lesion, long term:** **overactivity with DSD, high voiding pressure, eventual hydronephrosis and VUR**.
  - Typical pattern: **good compliance for part of filling, then small, ineffective, C-fibre-mediated rhythmic contractions** with a **waxing and waning sphincter**.
  - **Some leakage, but no effective emptying.**

*Autonomic dysreflexia.*

- **Lesion level: at or above T6** (upper thoracic or cervical).
- **Hallmark: severe hypertension.** It can cause **stroke, seizures or death**.
- **Why T6:** a lesion at or above T6 removes supraspinal control of the **splanchnic sympathetic outflow**. A benign stimulus below the level, such as **bladder filling or contraction**, then triggers an **exaggerated, unopposed sympathetic (α1-mediated) response**.
- **Rule: monitor blood pressure and have α-blockers available during cystography and urodynamics.**

##### Transverse Myelitis

- **Transverse myelitis (TM) is an immune-mediated inflammatory myelopathy.**
- **Epidemiology:**
  - **about 1400 new US cases a year, 28% in children**;
  - **incidence peaks at 10–19 years** and again in the **third decade**;
  - **males = females**.
- **Diagnostic criteria:**
  - **sensory, motor or autonomic dysfunction attributable to the cord**;
  - **bilateral** signs or symptoms;
  - **a defined sensory level**;
  - **inflammation**, shown by **cerebrospinal fluid pleocytosis or increased T2 signal on MRI** (Fig. 46.13).
- **Presentation:** **sudden back pain or leg weakness → rapid paralysis → often urinary retention**.
  - **Bladder dysfunction usually follows the motor deficit** (sometimes simultaneous).
  - **Bowel dysfunction** is also likely at presentation.
- **Spinal shock may last up to 6 weeks**, then progresses to **spasticity** if recovery is incomplete.
- **The cord segment involved determines the motor deficit.**
- **Acute treatment:** **steroids, plasma exchange, intravenous immunoglobulin**.
- **Recovery:** starts at **2 weeks to 3 months**; **most occurs in the first 6 months**; may continue **up to 2 years**.
- **Commonest urinary presentation: retention, in >95% during the acute phase.**

**Transverse Myelitis — Acute versus Post-Shock Urodynamics**

| Acute phase | After spinal shock resolves |
|---|---|
| **Areflexia or underactivity about 70%** | **Overactivity 59–90%** |
| Overactivity about 13% | **Reduced compliance about 47%** |
| Reduced compliance about 30% | **DSD 17–80%** |
| DSD about 20% | **DLPP >40 cm H₂O in 12–33%** |
| — | **Underactivity rare** |

*Outcomes.*

- **About one-third void spontaneously after the acute phase.**
- **57–73% need CIC.**
- **Antimuscarinics are needed in 14–64%.**
- **BTA or augmentation** for refractory small capacity, poor compliance or overactivity.
- **Persistent bowel dysfunction in up to about three-quarters**, so **bowel management is usually needed**.
- **Motor and bladder recovery move together** (in the larger studies):
  - **full motor recovery → full LUT recovery**;
  - **partial motor recovery → voids but needs an antimuscarinic**;
  - **no recovery or wheelchair-dependent → CIC with or without an antimuscarinic**.
  - Some smaller studies found no correlation.
- **Serious complications are infrequent:**
  - UTI, VUR, hydroureteronephrosis;
  - **chronic renal insufficiency**;
  - **bladder calculi and diverticula**.
- **Late CIC → poorer compliance and upper-tract deterioration** (about 5%).
- **Neither neurological examination, symptoms nor ambulation predicts upper-tract risk.** This is why **urodynamics are needed**.
- **Largest paediatric series:**
  - **intact lower-limb reflexes at presentation and complete neurological recovery → better volitional voiding**;
  - **but more than half on CIC at presentation still need it at 1 year**.

#### Conditions of the Pelvis

##### Anorectal Malformation

- **Anorectal malformation (ARM):**
  - a spectrum of the distal anus and rectum, with urinary and genital involvement;
  - **about 1 in 5000 live births**;
  - classified by the **International (Krickenbeck) Classification**, by **fistula site** plus rare or regional variants.
- **Urinary tract anomalies occur in 26–52%.** The commonest are **renal agenesis (mainly left) and VUR**.

**High versus Low Anorectal Malformation**

| High (supralevator fistula) | Low (infralevator) |
|---|---|
| **Urinary anomaly about 70%** | **About 35%** |
| **Spinal bony anomaly about 48–54%** | **About 15–27%** |
| **Cord anomaly more frequent** (overall 18–50%, rising with lesion height) | Less frequent |

- **Anomaly rates overall:**
  - **boys have more urinary anomalies than girls** (about 50% versus 29%);
  - spinal bony anomalies overall **30–44%**.
- **Cord anomalies:**
  - **tethered cord, thickened or fatty filum terminale, lipoma** (Fig. 46.14);
  - **neuropathic LUT dysfunction is frequent when the cord is abnormal and rare when it is normal**;
  - it usually shows as **incontinence discovered when toilet training fails**.
- **Cutaneous markers are rarely, if ever, present.** Early detection therefore needs **spinal ultrasound before 3 months of age**.

*Syndromic ARM.*

| Syndrome | Components |
|---|---|
| **VACTERL** | **V**ertebral, **A**nal atresia, **C**ardiac, **T**racheo-**E**sophageal fistula, **R**enal, **L**imb |
| **MURCS** | **MÜ**llerian duct aplasia, **R**enal aplasia, **C**ervicothoracic **S**omite dysplasia |
| **OEIS** | **O**mphalocele, **E**xstrophy, **I**mperforate anus, **S**pinal defects |
| **Currarino** | **ARM + sacrococcygeal defect + presacral mass** |

- **Currarino syndrome:**
  - **tethered cord in more than 80%**;
  - **about 80% have postoperative voiding complaints**;
  - upper-tract changes are not uncommon;
  - **untethering has unpredictable urodynamic results**.

*Urodynamics.*

- **Commonest pattern: upper motor neuron — overactivity and/or DSD.**
- **Lower motor neuron pattern also occurs:** underactive detrusor and denervated sphincter.
- **Other findings:** **leak point pressure >40 cm H₂O**, poor compliance, small capacity.
- **Normal urodynamics do not exclude a vertebral anomaly or SB.**

*Aetiology.*

- **ARM:** multifactorial with a genetic component. Associations:
  - **paternal smoking**;
  - **maternal obesity**;
  - **maternal pregestational or gestational diabetes**.
- **The neuropathic bladder in ARM stems mainly from the associated cord anomaly, not from surgery.**
  - **Peña's posterior sagittal anorectoplasty (PSARP, 1986) spares the pelvic nerves and does not cause neuropathic LUT dysfunction.**
  - **18–35% of children with ARM have neuropathic LUT dysfunction.**
  - **Nearly all have abnormal urodynamics before PSARP**, usually with a cord anomaly.

*When to do urodynamics in ARM.*

- **Only if there is:**
  - **a spinal bony anomaly**;
  - **a cord defect**; or
  - **telltale signs on VCUG or ultrasound**.
- **Timing (sequence):** **early infancy, before definitive surgery** (to establish the true incidence) → **after the pull-through** (to detect surgical change) → **repeat, or do for the first time, if secondary urinary or faecal incontinence develops**.
- **An abnormal infant study leads either to cord surgery or to watchful waiting** for progression.
- **ESRD is rare, just under 2%.**
- **If neuropathic, follow up with annual ultrasound plus repeat urodynamics or imaging** (Table 46.3).

##### Pelvic Surgery

- **Pelvic operations that harm LUT function:**
  - **sacrococcygeal teratoma resection**;
  - **pelvic rhabdomyosarcoma and other pelvic tumours**;
  - **Hirschsprung disease surgery**.

**Pelvic Nerve Injury → LUT Pattern**

| Nerve injured | Autonomic loss | LUT result |
|---|---|---|
| **Pelvic splanchnic nerves** | **Parasympathetic** | **Underactive bladder** |
| **Hypogastric nerves** | **Sympathetic** | **Loss of compliance**; **bladder neck fails to respond to rises in abdominal pressure** |
| **Pelvic plexus** | Mixed | **Mixed patterns** |

**Pelvic Surgery — Three Conditions Compared**

| | Sacrococcygeal teratoma | Pelvic rhabdomyosarcoma / pelvic tumours | Hirschsprung disease |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rate of LUT dysfunction** | **35–45% neuropathic** after resection | **Normal function in only 27–40%** after treatment | **Up to 45% unstable detrusor, often resolving after resection**; **transient retention up to 7%**; long-term **incontinence 0–6%** |
| **Presentation** | **UTI, incomplete emptying** | **Nocturnal enuresis, daytime incontinence, urgency, suprapubic pain on filling, UTI** | Usually asymptomatic unless function altered |
| **Urodynamics** | Normal about 28%; **overactivity 57–81%**; underactivity 14–27%; poor compliance; DSD 18–38% | **Markedly reduced functional capacity**; overactivity; underactivity; DSD | **About one-third normal**; **two-thirds increased capacity**; **high residual 55–78%**; poor compliance rare |
| **Upper tract** | **Scarring or reduced GFR about 25%; VUR 25–50%; hydronephrosis 30–43%** | **Abnormal renal function about one-third; hydronephrosis about 15%; reconstruction or diversion about 20%** | — |
| **Special feature** | **Tethered cord may later contribute** | **Past: cystectomy; now bladder preservation.** **Radiotherapy → high risk** | **Surgical approach alters bladder outcome** |
| **Screening** | **Urodynamics before and after surgery in all** (high risk) | **Frequency–volume chart for all survivors**, especially after radiotherapy; **ultrasound and UDS only if dysfunction** | **Clinical screening and ultrasound before and after surgery for all**; **UDS before surgery if symptomatic**; **UDS 6 months after surgery if symptomatic** (lets neurapraxia resolve) |
| **Long-term** | Annual ultrasound and clinical review if abnormal | Same | Same |

- **Universal rule:** after any pelvic surgery, **children who are asymptomatic and/or have normal postoperative investigations need no long-term follow-up unless symptoms arise.**

**Other Conditions at a Glance**

| Condition | Dominant UDS pattern | Upper-tract risk | Key surveillance point |
|---|---|---|---|
| CP | Overactivity, small capacity; **one-third normal** | **Low (<5%)** | Risk rises with age, DSD symptoms, recurrent UTI |
| CNS tumour | Mixed; **no correlation with location** | Variable | **New symptoms may mean recurrence** |
| SCI | Shock: underactive → **suprasacral: overactivity with DSD**; cauda equina: no return | **High** (thoracic) | **Autonomic dysreflexia at or above T6** |
| TM | Acute: **areflexia** → later **overactivity, DSD, poor compliance** | Low (about 5%), higher with late CIC | Examination does not predict risk; **urodynamics essential** |
| ARM | **Upper motor neuron (overactivity with DSD)**; lower motor neuron possible | ESRD about 2% | **Spinal ultrasound before 3 months**; UDS before and after pull-through |
| Pelvic surgery | Depends on the nerve injured | **High after teratoma or rhabdomyosarcoma** | Selective, as per the table above |

**KEY POINTS: OTHER CONDITIONS ASSOCIATED WITH NEUROPATHIC LOWER URINARY TRACT DYSFUNCTION**
- **Children with CP usually achieve continence, but late**; upper-tract deterioration is **under 5%**.
- **Paediatric SCI is rare, and about six times commoner in adolescents than in children.**
- **Spinal shock: underactive bladder, non-reactive sphincter.**
- **Upper thoracic or cervical SCI → autonomic dysreflexia** with filling or contraction. **Monitor blood pressure and have α-blockers available** during cystography and urodynamics.
- **In TM, bladder dysfunction usually follows the motor deficit.** Intact reflexes and full recovery predict volitional voiding, but **more than half on CIC still need it at 1 year**.
- **ARM: cord anomalies in 18–50%.**
- **Pelvic rhabdomyosarcoma survivors, especially after radiotherapy, need careful upper and lower tract monitoring.**

*Concept —* **Across all the non-NTD causes, the level of the lesion predicts the pattern:**
- **suprapontine (CP, brain lesions) → overactivity with preserved coordination and low renal risk**;
- **suprasacral cord (SCI, TM, tethering) → overactivity with DSD and high renal risk**;
- **sacral or peripheral (cauda equina, pelvic surgery) → underactivity or denervation**.
- **In ARM and pelvic surgery, the cord or nerve lesion, not the visceral operation, is usually the culprit.**

  - **unstable detrusor up to 45%**, often resolving;
  - **transient retention up to 7%**;

- **Eponyms:**
  - **Krickenbeck:** ARM classification by fistula site;
  - **Peña:** PSARP, nerve-sparing, does not cause neuropathic bladder;
  - **Currarino:** triad above;
  - **VACTERL, MURCS, OEIS:** syndromic ARM.
- **When to do urodynamics:**
  - **ARM:** only with a bony spine anomaly, cord defect, or VCUG or ultrasound signs;
  - **sacrococcygeal teratoma:** all, before and after surgery;
  - **Hirschsprung:** only if symptomatic;
  - **rhabdomyosarcoma:** only if the frequency–volume chart shows dysfunction.
- **Classic traps:**
  - **PSARP causes the neuropathic bladder in ARM.** **The cord anomaly does.**
  - **No skin marker, so no occult dysraphism in ARM.** **Markers are usually absent; do a spinal ultrasound.**
  - **Normal ARM urodynamics exclude SB.** **They do not.**
  - **Sacral sensation predicts LUT recovery after SCI.** **It does not.**
  - **Examination or ambulation predicts upper-tract risk in TM.** **It does not.**
  - **Parasympathetic injury → poor compliance.** **No: it causes underactivity; sympathetic injury causes poor compliance.**
  - **Autonomic dysreflexia occurs with low thoracic lesions.** **It occurs at or above T6.**
  - **Routine urodynamics for all after Hirschsprung surgery.** **No: only if symptomatic, at 6 months.**
