# Chapter 46 — Neuromuscular Dysfunction of the Lower Urinary Tract in Children

- **Definition:** neuromuscular (neurogenic, neuropathic) lower urinary tract (LUT) dysfunction is **bladder and/or sphincter dysfunction from aberrant innervation**. It may be congenital or acquired, central or peripheral, transient or permanent.
- **Commonest congenital cause: neural tube defect (NTD)**, used almost synonymously with spina bifida (SB).
- **Survival has transformed:**
  - infant survival rose from **under 10% before 1960 to over 85% by the mid-1990s**;
  - **first-year mortality is now about 5%**;
  - **about 90% survive to 10 years**.
- **Pivotal urological advance: clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC), Lapides (1971)**. It prevents upper-tract deterioration.
- **Predictors of mortality:**
  - long-term mortality **rises with the level of neurological deficit**;
  - the **strongest infant risk factors are preterm birth (relative risk [RR] about 4.5) and low birth weight (RR about 4.8)**.
- **As survival lengthens, renal failure becomes a leading cause of morbidity and death.** This is why the **urinary tract needs lifelong surveillance from infancy**.
- **About 10% of deaths in SB are urological.**
- **The urologist protects the urinary tract and enables sexual function.**

| Congenital | Acquired |
|---|---|
| Neural tube defect | Extensive pelvic surgery |

- **The table is the chapter's skeleton:** NTD first, then the other causes grouped by brain, spinal cord and pelvis.
- **Trap: in anorectal malformation, the LUT dysfunction usually comes from an associated cord lesion, not from the operation.**

## NEURAL TUBE DEFECTS

### Background and Classification

- **NTDs are abnormal development of the spinal canal and cord.** They are classified by **location, severity (open or closed) and the structures involved**.

<p class="tcap">NTD Classification — Location and Severity</p>

| Type | Site | Open / closed | Defining feature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Myelomeningocele** | Spinal | **Open** | **Cord, roots and meninges protrude**; the **most severe and the most common open NTD**; usually **lumbosacral** |
| Meningocele | Spinal | Usually skin-covered | **Meninges only**; the cord stays in the canal |
| **SB occulta** | Spinal | Closed | **Mildest; up to 15% of the population**; skin-covered; **usually a non-pathological variant** |
| **Sacral agenesis (caudal regression)** | Spinal | Closed | **Absent sacral vertebrae**; **may coexist with tethering** |
| Anencephaly | Cranial | Open | Most of the brain and skull absent; very limited survival |
| Encephalocele | Cranial | Open | Brain and meninges herniate through a skull defect |

- **Open versus closed:**
  - an **open NTD exposes neural tissue**;
  - a **closed NTD is skin-covered**.
- **Prenatal detection:** **maternal α-fetoprotein and ultrasound**. This allows counselling and planning of delivery.

### Epidemiology

- **Spinal closure:**
  - begins **around day 18**;
  - proceeds **caudally from the cephalad end**;
  - is **complete by day 35**.
  - The mechanism of failure is **unknown and multifactorial**.
- **Worldwide prevalence is about 2 per 1000 births**, concentrated in **developing countries**.
- **In the USA it has plateaued at about 30 per 100,000**, lowest in non-Hispanic Black children. It varies by place and over time.

### Risk Factors for the Development of Neural Tube Defects

- **Folate is central.** Risk rises with **low folate, impaired folate pathways or anti-folate antibodies**.
- **Evidence for folic acid:**
  - **supplementation cuts NTDs by about 50–70%** (randomised trials);
  - **flour fortification (late 1990s) cuts them by about 20–50%**. Fortification was introduced because **only one-third of women supplement**.
- **US Public Health Service (1992): 400 µg daily for all women of child-bearing age.**
  - **Why all women:** the **tube closes before most women know they are pregnant**.
  - **Optimal timing: at least 4 weeks before conception and through the first month.**
- **Genetics:**
  - **monozygotic concordance is higher than dizygotic**;
  - **recurrence is 5% after one affected pregnancy and no more than 10% after two**, so **not monogenic**;
  - **no clinically actionable human gene** has been found.
- **Other maternal risk factors:**
  - **age at either extreme**;
  - **obesity, diabetes, fever or influenza, caffeine**;
  - occupational, hydrocarbon and pesticide exposure;
  - low socioeconomic status, passive smoking, periconceptional stress, low weight gain;
  - **folate antagonists (valproate, carbamazepine)**;
  - prior miscarriage or birth defect; higher birth order.

### Pathogenesis

- **Almost every open NTD has an Arnold–Chiari II malformation.**
  - **Components:** hindbrain herniation, brainstem anomalies, low venous sinuses, a small posterior fossa.
  - **Associated with:** hydrocephalus, and motor, cranial-nerve and cognitive deficits.
  - It **carries mortality even after surgery**.
- **Hydrocephalus treatment:**
  - traditional: **ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt**;
  - alternative: **endoscopic third ventriculostomy with choroid plexus cauterisation**, which controls it in **over 70% without a shunt**, with **equivalent neurocognitive outcome**.
- **The myelomeningocele lesion varies** with which neural elements evert into the sac.
- **The bony level differs from the neurological level by 1–3 vertebrae either way.** **LUT function cannot be predicted from the bony defect or from leg function.**

*Perinatal Concerns.*

- **The injury progresses in utero:** leg movement is lost and hindbrain herniation and hydrocephalus worsen.
- **Prenatal coverage preserves function** in animal models.
- **"Two-hit" theory:** failure of tube formation, then **ongoing mechanical and chemical injury to exposed tissue**. It follows that **in-utero repair should help**.

**Management of Myelomeningocele Study (MOMS)** — landmark randomised trial

- **Design:** **prenatal repair before 26 weeks** versus postnatal repair.
- **Benefits of prenatal repair:**
  - **fetal or neonatal death or shunt need: RR 0.70**, yet **40% still needed a shunt**;
  - **better cognitive and motor composite at 30 months**.
- **Neutral results:** **no maternal deaths**; **no difference in fetal or neonatal deaths**.
- **Costs of prenatal repair:**
  - **more obstetric complications:** oligohydramnios, chorioamniotic separation, abruption, transfusion, membrane rupture;
  - **one-third had uterine-scar dehiscence or thinning**;
  - **delivery at 34.1 versus 37.3 weeks; 13% before 30 weeks**;
  - more re-tethering procedures, more respiratory distress, lower birth weight.
- **Since MOMS, open and fetoscopic refinements have reduced maternal and fetal risk.**

| Cervical–high thoracic | Low thoracic | Lumbar | **Lumbosacral** | Sacral |

- **NTD is the commonest cause** of neuropathic LUT dysfunction in children; **myelomeningocele is the most severe and the most common type**.

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> The spinal lesion in SB is <b>both unpredictable and progressive</b>: the bony level misstates the neurological level, and the exposed cord keeps being injured in utero. ## NEUROPATHIC LOWER URINARY TRACT DYSFUNCTION

### Classification

- **Normal function:**
  - **storage at low pressure**;
  - **emptying by a detrusor contraction coordinated with a fall in outlet resistance**.
- **Denervation disrupts this, producing storage failure, emptying failure, or both.**
- **Terminology:** "**overactive**" replaces hyperreflexic; "**underactive**" replaces hypo- or areflexic.

<p class="tcap">Neurogenic Detrusor Overactivity versus Neurogenic Underactive Bladder</p>

| Neurogenic detrusor overactivity (NDO) | Neurogenic underactive bladder |
|---|---|
| **Involuntary, frequent contractions**; if sensate → **urgency and frequency** | **Impaired detrusor stimulation → ineffective contraction** |
| **Storage lost** through small capacity and high pressure, from **pathological detrusor remodelling** (multifactorial) | **Incomplete emptying, retention, overflow incontinence, urinary tract infection (UTI)** |

- **Incontinence occurs with or without sensation.**
- **Low outlet resistance also impairs storage.** It results from an **untoned bladder neck and/or a denervated external sphincter**.

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> Every neuropathic pattern reduces to <b>two components: the detrusor (overactive or underactive) and the outlet (competent, incompetent or dyssynergic)</b>. Their combination sets both the continence problem and the renal risk.</p>

### Assessment

- **Goals of assessment:** **preserve renal function, achieve continence, prevent UTI, enable sexual function**. Each depends on **reliable and timely testing**.
- **Four bodies publish guidance, broadly in agreement:**
  - **International Children's Continence Society (ICCS, 2012)**;
  - **British Association of Paediatric Urologists (BAPU, 2016)**;
  - **Spina Bifida Association (SBA, 2018)**;
  - **European Association of Urology (EAU, 2022)**.

<p class="tcap">TABLE 46.3 Assessment of Neurogenic LUT Dysfunction</p>

| Test | ICCS (2012) | BAPU (2016) | SBA (2018) | EAU (2022) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Urodynamics (UDS)** | After recovery from SB surgery; **before untethering**; **≥6 weeks after spinal cord injury (SCI)**; **yearly in toddler years**; **2–3 months after starting or changing an antimuscarinic** | **"Select few"** on clinical or radiological grounds; if stable on CIC, **only if indicated** | **Within 3 months**; **annually to 36 months**; repeat for UTI, upper-tract or continence change | **Within 3 months**; then annually as indicated |
| **Ultrasound** | Initial; **6, 12, 18, 24 months**; yearly or twice-yearly after the toddler years; yearly in adolescence; every 2 years after the growth spurt; every 3 years in adulthood | **Yearly** | After birth; **6, 12, 18, 24 months**; **annually after 24 months**; repeat for UTI or worse UDS | After birth; annually |
| **Dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA)** | — | — | — | **Baseline** |
| **Cystography (voiding cystourethrogram [VCUG] or video UDS)** | **If hydronephrosis, ureteric dilatation, renal size discrepancy, wall thickening or hostile dynamics**; repeat for vesicoureteric reflux (VUR), new dilatation, parenchymal loss or febrile UTI | **At initial evaluation** | — | **"May be helpful"** |

- **The pattern:**
  - **ICCS, SBA and EAU agree on an early study within 3 months and serial ultrasound through the first 2 years**;
  - **BAPU is the most selective**.
- **The traps:**
  - **only ICCS and BAPU positively recommend cystography**;
  - **baseline creatinine is recommended by three bodies, but not BAPU**;
  - **B12 surveillance starts 2 years after bowel reconstruction** (time for terminal-ileal loss to deplete stores).

#### History and Physical Examination

- **Without NTD stigmata, symptoms overlap with non-neurogenic dysfunction** (incontinence, LUT symptoms [LUTS], UTI).
- **Red flags:** **worsening incontinence or LUTS, recurrent febrile UTI, upper-tract change**.
- **Examine the spine and legs in every child.**
- **Cutaneous markers are present in about 90% of occult dysraphism:**
  - hair tuft;
  - dermal vascular malformation;
  - sacral dimple;
  - abnormal gluteal cleft.
- **Mild sacral agenesis:** the child **may walk**; look for a **short gluteal cleft and flat buttocks**.
- **Assess sexual function in adolescents.**

#### Urodynamics

- **Urodynamics are the primary test driving management.**
- **Landmark — McGuire (1981): detrusor leak point pressure (DLPP) >40 cm H₂O predicts VUR and upper-tract change.**
- **Poor compliance (volume ÷ end-filling pressure) also predicts renal injury.**
- **No exact injurious threshold is known**, but **high storage pressure harms the kidney**.

<p class="tcap">Timing of Urodynamics by Condition</p>

| Setting | When |
|---|---|
| Open NTD | **Once transportable and able to lie supine**; **usually within the first few months**; **safe from 3 months** |
| Closed NTD or cord tumour | **Before neurosurgery and 3–4 months after** |
| SCI | **After spinal shock resolves (about 3–4 months)** |

- **Newborn myelomeningocele:**
  - **most have detrusor contractions**, even with high lesions that spare the sacral cord;
  - **over a third are acontractile**, with good or poor compliance.
- **Interrater reliability is poor.**
- **Detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia (DSD):**
  - **definition:** **failure of the sphincter to relax during a voiding or overactive contraction**;
  - **significance:** a **presumed driver of pathological remodelling**, so accurate diagnosis matters;
  - **diagnosis: electromyography (EMG)**; bladder-neck fluoroscopy is **unreliable**.

<p class="tcap">Surface Patch EMG versus Direct Concentric Needle EMG</p>

| Surface patch EMG | Direct concentric needle EMG |
|---|---|
| **Commonest**; perineal electrodes | **Reliable and objective**; placed with the UDS catheters (**girls: 3 or 9 o'clock beside the meatus; boys: transperineal**) |
| **Unreliable in UMPIRE**: DSD not supported by other UDS elements → **removed from the "high-risk" category** | Needs **specialist training or a neurologist** |
| Coordination only | Also shows **acute versus chronic denervation**; **clarifies suspected re-tethering when postoperative scarring makes MRI equivocal** |

- **UMPIRE** is the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Urologic and Renal Protocol for the Newborn and Young Child with SB, a landmark prospective protocol.
- **Needle EMG in newborn myelomeningocele** gives a baseline for comparison:
  - **intact sacral arc about 40%**;
  - **partial denervation about 25%**;
  - **complete denervation about 35%**.

<p class="tcap">Urodynamic Risk Categories for Renal Deterioration</p>

| Category | Definition | UMPIRE revision |
|---|---|---|
| **Hostile** | **End-filling pressure or DLPP ≥40 cm H₂O, or NDO with DSD** | **DSD removed** |
| **Intermediate** | **NDO, reduced compliance, pressure 25–39 cm H₂O** | — |
| **Abnormal but safe** | **Pressure <25 cm H₂O** | — |
| **Normal** | Normal capacity; **compliance >15 mL/cm H₂O**; no NDO or DSD; minimal residual | — |

- **Needed:** **non-invasive or ambulatory urodynamics**, and **machine-learning interpretation**. Both depend on **standardised data that do not yet exist**.

#### Imaging

*Ultrasound.*

- **Ultrasound is non-invasive and tracks renal size, growth and dilatation**, which **predicts renal health**.
- **It is unreliable for scarring, VUR, and bladder wall thickness.**
- **Baseline scan:** **before newborn discharge**; **soon after injury** in acquired causes.
- **UMPIRE baseline:**
  - **56% normal**;
  - **40% mild hydronephrosis**;
  - **4% moderate to severe**.
- **It also measures residual urine**, which guides the need for CIC.
- **Interval: yearly, or 6-monthly for 2 years then yearly.**
- **Spinal ultrasound works before the posterior arches ossify (first few months)** to detect occult dysraphism.

*Dimercaptosuccinic Acid Renal Scintigraphy.*

- **Technetium-99m DMSA is the most sensitive test for cortical defects.**
- **It is used inconsistently** because of isotope availability and expertise.
  - **UMPIRE: only one-third scanned; about 8% had defects.**
- **EAU recommends a baseline DMSA** for longitudinal assessment.

*Cystography.*

- **Cystography is fundamental; ideally video UDS**, which links anatomy to pressure. **VCUG otherwise.**
- **Main purpose: VUR**, especially when there is:
  - **on ultrasound:** dilatation, renal discrepancy or wall thickening;
  - **on UDS:** overactivity, poor compliance, high leak point pressure or DSD.
- **UMPIRE: grade III–V VUR in only 7% of newborns.**
- **High-grade VUR acts as a pressure pop-off, so urodynamic pressures become unreliable.**
- **The baseline battery:**
  - gives **a reference** for later comparison;
  - **detects deterioration or progressive denervation early**;
  - **identifies at-risk infants before upper-tract change**, so **prophylaxis can start**;
  - **informs counselling**.

#### Laboratory Studies

<p class="tcap">Serum Creatinine versus Serum Cystatin C in SB</p>

| Serum creatinine | Serum cystatin C |
|---|---|
| **Made from muscle creatine**; filtered and **secreted** | **13-kDa cysteine proteinase inhibitor made at a constant rate by all nucleated cells**; **filtered, not secreted, catabolised in the proximal tubule** |
| Depends on age, sex, height and **above all muscle mass** | **Independent of muscle mass** |
| **Low muscle mass → normal creatinine despite a falling glomerular filtration rate (GFR)** | **Tracks GFR better than the Schwartz formula**; log(GFR) = 1.962 + [1.123 × log(1/cystatin C)] |
| **Cheap and consistent → baseline recommended** | **Less reliable in early impairment**; limited availability; **affected by inflammation and protein catabolism** |

- **Gold standard for GFR: a nuclear-medicine clearance study.** It is invasive and involves radiation.
- **In adults, cystatin C predicts end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and death better than creatinine.**
- **If cystatin C is unavailable, or mild impairment is suspected → clearance study.**

<p class="kpt">KEY POINTS: NEUROPATHIC LUT DYSFUNCTION: ASSESSMENT</p>

- **Suspect neuropathy** with **worsening incontinence or LUTS, recurrent febrile UTI or upper-tract change**.
- **Urodynamics drive management** despite **poor interrater reliability**.
- **DSD is a presumed primary driver of remodelling.**
- **SB ultrasound: 56% normal, 40% mild, 4% moderate to severe dilatation.**
- **Cystography is fundamental, ideally video UDS; about 7% have dilating VUR.**
- **Baseline creatinine is recommended.**
</div>

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> Assessment rests on one causal chain, <b>high storage pressure → upper-tract injury</b>. Each test measures a different link: urodynamics the pressure, EMG the outlet that generates it, cystography the reflux that transmits it, ultrasound and DMSA the damage, and cystatin C what low muscle mass hides from creatinine.</p>

### Management

- **Priorities, in order:** **kidneys first**, then continence, UTI avoidance, and sexual function and fertility.

|---|---|---|---|---|

- **The table is the treatment ladder:**
  - **CIC plus antimuscarinic**;
  - then **botulinum toxin**;
  - then **augmentation with or without outlet surgery and a channel**.
- **SBA and EAU favour early, proactive treatment; BAPU records no consensus.**
- **Traps:**
  - **no antibiotic prophylaxis on CIC**;
  - **vesicostomy is a bridge, not definitive**.

#### Medical Management

*Clean Intermittent Catheterisation.*

- **CIC is the single most important non-surgical tool.**
  - **Regular emptying prevents injurious overdistension.**
  - **CIC bypasses DSD.**
- **Early CIC is favoured.** **Families find it easier to learn and integrate CIC when it starts in infancy.**
- **If CIC fails** to relieve hydronephrosis, increase capacity or reduce UTI → **overnight indwelling drainage** can help.

*Pharmacotherapy.*

- **Antimuscarinics are first-line for overactivity or hypertonicity.**
- **Receptors:** the detrusor has **M2 (more numerous) and M3 (which mediates contraction)**.
- **Response is good at first, but side effects and waning efficacy erode adherence.**
- **Effects of antimuscarinics:**
  - **higher capacity**;
  - **higher volume at first contraction**;
  - **fewer incontinence episodes and catheterisations**.
- **Side effects:** **dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, flushing, dizziness, headache**.
- **A randomised double-blind trial found no effect on attention or memory.**

  - **four agents are licensed for children: oxybutynin, solifenacin, fesoterodine, mirabegron**;
  - each has a **child-friendly form or weight-banded dosing**.
- **The traps:**
  - **vibegron is not licensed in children**;
  - **sympathomimetics have no urinary licence**;
  - **oral oxybutynin has a 5 mg per-dose ceiling**.

<p class="tcap">Antimuscarinics — Distinguishing Features and Signature Adverse Effects</p>

| Drug | Distinguishing feature | Signature adverse or practical issue |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxybutynin** | **Commonest**; the only one with **oral, transdermal and intravesical** routes | **Patch: skin irritation, about 20% stop.** **Intravesical: no first-pass metabolism, fewer side effects**; low-level evidence; **more stop because of inconvenience (crushing tablets) than side effects** |
| **Tolterodine** | Reduces non-voiding activity and voiding amplitude | **Poor brain penetration → minimal CNS effects**; capacity rises under 10 years and incontinence falls over 12 months |
| **Fesoterodine** | **Prodrug** with **the same active metabolite (5-hydroxymethyl tolterodine)** | **Esterase activation, independent of cytochrome P450** → **less pharmacokinetic variability and fewer adverse events** than tolterodine |
| **Trospium** | **Quaternary amine**; **half-life about 8 hours versus 2 hours for oxybutynin** | **Crosses the blood–brain barrier poorly → few CNS effects**; adverse events uncommon |
| **Solifenacin** | **Once daily; from 2 years; suspension** | Mostly mild; **few stop** |
| **Propiverine** | **Mixed antimuscarinic and calcium antagonist** | **Urodynamic gain greater than with oxybutynin** |

- **β3 agonists:**
  - **well tolerated**;
  - **can cause hypertension**;
  - mirabegron, **alone or with an antimuscarinic**, is effective in idiopathic and neuropathic overactivity;
  - **first-line mirabegron may save costs**.
- **Propiverine:**
  - **reduces calcium entry**, so there is **less calcium–calmodulin activation of the contractile apparatus**;
  - **improves LUTS and above all urodynamics**.

*Sympathomimetic Agents.*

- **Indication: incontinence from low urethral resistance.**
- **Mechanism:** they act at the **bladder neck, where α-receptors are densest**, and raise outlet resistance.
- **Response:**
  - **only a minority respond, and responders are unpredictable**;
  - **responders can become dry between catheterisations**.
- **Adverse effects:**
  - **dizziness, nausea, nervousness, insomnia, anorexia, headache, mood change**;
  - **retention in children who void volitionally**.

*Early Medical Intervention.*

- **Definition: early intervention = CIC plus antimuscarinic.**
- **Indication:** **proactive treatment of high-risk dynamics**:
  - **DSD**;
  - **poor compliance**;
  - **high outlet pressure**;
  - **outflow obstruction**.
- **History:** **introduced by Bauer in the 1980s**. The field split into **proactive and "wait and see"** camps.
- **Why proactive management prevailed:**
  - **the upper-tract risk of a hostile bladder was confirmed**;
  - **oxybutynin benefits the detrusor at cellular and muscarinic level**.
- **All paediatric urology societies now endorse proactive treatment of a hostile neonatal bladder.**

<p class="tcap">Early Intervention versus Expectant ("Wait and See") Management</p>

| Outcome | Expectant | Early CIC plus antimuscarinic |
|---|---|---|
| **Bladder dynamics** | Hostile dynamics persist | **Contractions abolished or peak pressure lowered; filling pressure falls**; minimal side effects |
| **Continence** | — | **Improved; up to about 44% dry with normal upper tracts at 6 years** |
| **UTI** | **About 50% by 15 months, about 80% by 15 years**; recurrence common | **About 0.3 per year** |
| **Hydroureteronephrosis** | **18–80%** | **0–15%** |
| **VUR** | **Up to 50% by 9 years** | **Much less common** |
| **Diversion or augmentation** | **Diversion up to about 11%** | **18–24% fewer augmentations** |
| **Renal scarring** | **27–50%** | **4–12%** |
| **ESRD** | **About 30%** | **0–1.6%** |

- **Early intervention gives no benefit to the child who empties spontaneously.**
- **Renal damage at birth:** estimates range from **30%** to **8% (UMPIRE)** with DMSA defects. Either way, **neuropathic LUT dysfunction harms the kidney**.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
<p class="kpt">KEY POINTS: NEUROPATHIC LOWER URINARY TRACT: MANAGEMENT</p>

- **CIC is the single most important non-surgical tool.**
- **Antimuscarinics are first-line**, with **no adverse cognitive effect** (randomised double-blind trial).
- **All paediatric urology societies recommend early intervention (CIC plus antimuscarinic)** for hostile neonatal dynamics.
</div>

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> Early intervention works because it <b>attacks the cause (pressure and failure to empty) before the consequence (reflux, dilatation, scarring, renal failure)</b>. Each row of the comparison is the same mechanism measured at a different end-organ. The spontaneously emptying child, who gains nothing, proves the rule.</p>

#### Surgical Management

- **When medical treatment fails, surgery aims to:**
  - **improve dynamics**;
  - **achieve continence**;
  - **facilitate CIC**.
- **Augmentation, bladder-neck procedures and channels are covered in the reconstruction chapter.**

*Botulinum Toxin.*

- **Mechanism:**
  - **botulinum toxin A (BTA)**, from ***Clostridium botulinum*** (Gram-positive), **blocks acetylcholine release**;
  - it **also blocks ATP and substance P release**;
  - it **down-regulates purinergic and capsaicin receptors on afferents**;
  - it **reduces M2, M3, P2X2 and P2X3 expression**.
  - It therefore acts on **both the motor and sensory arms**.
- **History:** **first used in adult SCI (1990)**; **in children in 2002**.
- **Technique:** **general anaesthesia → rigid cystoscopy → about 10 U/kg (max 360 U) → about 30 sites → trigone spared.**
- **Outcomes after failed CIC plus drugs:**
  - **continence 65–87%**;
  - **lower detrusor pressure, higher capacity, better compliance**;
  - **effect lasts 6–10.5 months**;
  - usual **minimum age 2 years**, which matches the **FDA cerebral palsy licence**.
- **Wider benefits:**
  - **satisfaction >70%**;
  - **about 25% stop antimuscarinics after the first injection**, and about 10% more after the second;
  - **close to 90% avoid augmentation**.
- **Side effects are infrequent:** retention, UTI, haematuria, **transient weakness**, dysuria, pain.
- **Repeat injections:**
  - **stay effective, with lengthening intervals**;
  - **reducing the dose (300 → 200 U) caused relapse**, reversed by returning to the full dose;
  - **no tachyphylaxis, antibody effect or fibrosis**. **Histology shows fibrosis actually falls.**
- **Poor response:**
  - **predicted by pre-existing poor compliance**;
  - **complete non-response is linked to anti-toxin antibodies** (inconsistently).
- **Adding sphincter injection** (cohort with DSD in over 90%) **improved post-void residual**. This is **preliminary**.

*Management of Vesicoureteric Reflux in Neuropathic Dysfunction of the Lower Urinary Tract.*

- **Prevalence:** **dilating VUR (grades III–V) is present in 7% of SB neonates.**
- **It almost always accompanies hostile dynamics** (poor compliance, overactivity, DSD). It is **rare without them**.
- **Untreated, it reaches 30–40% by 5 years.**

<p class="tcap">Management of VUR by Pattern (International Classification)</p>

| Situation | Management |
|---|---|
| **Grade I–III, and empties completely** (voids spontaneously, or complete lesion with low outlet resistance) | **Antibiotic prophylaxis alone** |
| **Grade IV–V** | **CIC** |
| **Cannot empty, any grade** | **CIC** |
| **Poor compliance, with or without hydroureteronephrosis** | **Add an antimuscarinic** |

- **Managed this way, VUR resolves in 30–55%.**
- **Bacteriuria on CIC is common but harmless unless VUR is high-grade.**

<p class="tcap">Credé Manoeuvre — Reactive versus Denervated Sphincter</p>

| Reactive external sphincter | Denervated, non-reactive sphincter |
|---|---|
| **Credé → reflex rise in EMG and urethral resistance → high voiding pressure** | **No rise in EMG, resistance or pressure** |
| **Worsens VUR and its water-hammer effect: avoid** | **Not harmful** |

- **Antireflux surgery — indications (as for a normal bladder):**
  - **recurrent febrile UTI despite prophylaxis and proper CIC**;
  - **persistent hydroureteronephrosis despite effective emptying and lowered pressure**;
  - **severe VUR with an anatomical abnormality of the ureterovesical junction**.
- **Jeffs (1976): reimplantation succeeds if complete emptying is ensured.**
  - **Before this, diversion was preferred.**
  - **Since CIC, success is about 95%.**
- **Unilateral VUR → unilateral surgery.** Contralateral reflux does not follow.
- **At augmentation:**
  - **low-grade VUR resolves without reimplantation**;
  - **grade ≥III persists in 20–47% after augmentation alone**, so **reimplant at the same operation**.

<p class="tcap">Open Reimplantation versus Endoscopic Injection</p>

| Open ureteroneocystostomy | Endoscopic injection |
|---|---|
| **About 84–96% success** | **About 61–73% in comparative studies**; **73% (one) and 91% (two injections) in single-arm series**; **about 95% with intradetrusor BTA** |
| **Durable** | **About 40% recur at a mean of 4.5 years** |
| Standard | **Reasonable alternative**; results reportedly similar to those in normal bladders |

- **Why the endoscopic figures differ:** single-arm series use a **selected denominator**; head-to-head cohorts report lower success.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
<p class="kpt">KEY POINTS: NEUROPATHIC LUT DYSFUNCTION — SURGICAL MANAGEMENT</p>

- **Surgery is used when medical treatment fails**: to improve dynamics, achieve continence and facilitate CIC.
- **Intradetrusor BTA improves continence and UDS and may avoid reconstruction**; **serial injection causes neither fibrosis nor loss of effect**.
- **Dilating VUR is present in 7% of newborns with SB. Avoid Credé.**
- **Antireflux indications are as for a normal LUT.**
- **Reimplant dilating VUR during augmentation.**
</div>

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> Reflux in the neuropathic bladder is <b>a consequence of the bladder, not a primary ureteric defect</b>. Treat emptying and pressure first and much reflux resolves; surgery succeeds only on a bladder made safe. That is why Jeffs' insight and CIC turned reimplantation from failure to about 95% success.</p>

## SEXUAL FUNCTION

- **Sexual function matters increasingly as patients reach adulthood.** Nearly **three-quarters of adults with SB report sexual interest**.
- **Psychosocial barriers:**
  - **parents and clinicians avoid the topic**;
  - teenagers are **socially isolated** and **learn less from peers**;
  - **dependence and parental overprotection limit autonomy**.
- **Supports:**
  - **men living independently report better erections and satisfaction**;
  - **parental permissiveness and age-appropriate treatment support self-esteem**.
- **Dissatisfaction is highest for partnership and sex life** (more than half).
- **Central precocious puberty (CPP) is more common:**
  - **approaching 50% in girls**;
  - **10–30% in boys**.
  - **Likely cause:** **hydrocephalus disturbing the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis**, together with **Chiari II**.
- **Education and contraception:**
  - **offer SB-specific sexual education**;
  - **latex-free condoms are mandatory** with latex allergy or avoidance;
  - **combined oestrogen–progestin → thromboembolism** with reduced mobility;
  - **intrauterine device → unsafe** (pelvic infection).
- **Determinants of sexual function:**
  - **function is inversely proportional to the neurological level**; the **lowest lesions** form partnerships most often;
  - **more incontinence and disability → less activity**;
  - **older age → more partners**;
  - **no hydrocephalus → more activity**, especially in women.
- **Women are more often sexually active but face sexual abuse** (about a third report unwanted attention or touching).
- **Teenagers:**
  - **about a third have had sexual encounters**;
  - **almost all hope to marry and have children**;
  - **most desire sexual contact**.
- **Studies exclude the severely disabled, so they overstate function.**

<p class="tcap">Sexual and Reproductive Function — Males versus Females with SB</p>

| Males | Females |
|---|---|
| **Sacral cord often involved → erection and ejaculation affected** | **Hormonal control → reproductive function spared** |
| **Erection: about 70–92% in older selected series; only about 40% normal in a later cohort**; **ambulation predicts it** | **Sexual dysfunction about 55%** on the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI); **89%** in a combined FSFI and International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) study |
| **Ejaculation 40–75%**; some **retrograde** | **Continence → better lubrication and pain scores**; shunt, mobility and CIC made no difference |
| **S1 or lower → usually normal reproductive function; above S1 → about 50%** | **70–80% conceive with an uneventful pregnancy**; **late incontinence and caesarean delivery common** |
| **Fathering 17–39%** (plus about 25% with a good prognosis); **infertility from erectile dysfunction (ED), poor semen, Sertoli-cell-only testes** | **Arousal correlates with quality of life** |
| **IIEF, ≥18 years: about 75% ED**, linked to a **sacral root lesion**; **intact pelvic parasympathetic reflexes** are protective | — |

- **Both sexes have more severe dysfunction than other causes, with similar desire.**
- **IIEF may miss young men's experience**, so a **disease-specific tool is needed**.
- **Offspring risk:**
  - **3.7% if one parent has SB** (higher if the mother);
  - **about 15% if both**.
- **Folic acid for women with SB: 4–5 mg daily before contemplating pregnancy.**
- **Treatment:**
  - **sildenafil helps about 80%** of men with myelomeningocele (**randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trial**);
  - **ilioinguinal-to-dorsal penile nerve transfer** (men with normal groin sensation) improves glans sensation and satisfaction in small series; **long-term data awaited**.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
<p class="kpt">KEY POINTS: SEXUAL FUNCTION</p>

- **Sexuality matters increasingly as patients reach adulthood.**
- **CPP risk is increased.**
- **Function is inversely proportional to the neurological level.**
- **70–80% of women conceive with an uneventful pregnancy; caesarean is commoner.**
- **3.7% risk of SB in offspring.**
- **75% of men ≥18 have ED; sildenafil is effective.**
- **55.5% of women have sexual dysfunction.**
</div>

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> The sexes diverge on <b>what the sacral cord controls</b>. <b>Erection and ejaculation are sacral-neural and usually involved; female reproduction is hormonal and spared.</b> So in men the lesion level sets function, with S1 as the watershed, whereas in women continence and psychosocial factors dominate.</p>

## OTHER CONDITIONS ASSOCIATED WITH NEUROPATHIC LOWER URINARY TRACT DYSFUNCTION

- **Conditions are grouped by brain, spinal cord and pelvis**, whether congenital or acquired.

### Conditions of the Brain

#### Cerebral Palsy

- **Cerebral palsy (CP) is the commonest physical disability of childhood.**
  - **About 3.3 per 1000** eight-year-olds;
  - varies by region; commoner in Black and White than Hispanic children;
  - **male : female 1.2 : 1**.
- **Definition:** a disorder of **movement and posture from a non-progressive fetal or infant brain disturbance**. Sensation, cognition, communication and behaviour may also be affected.
- **Gross Motor Function Classification System — Expanded and Revised (GMFCS–E&R, 2007):**
  - based on **self-initiated movement**;
  - **five levels**, from **walks without limitation** to **transported in a manual wheelchair**;
  - age-banded.
- **Continence is usually achieved, but late:**
  - **daytime first, night-time within about a year**;
  - **14–34% continent** overall;
  - **median age 3.6–4.1 years** with good intellect and diplegia or hemiplegia; **10–13 years** with low intellect and tetraplegia.
- **GMFCS level mostly does not predict continence**, but **non-walkers have more LUT dysfunction** (about three-quarters versus one-quarter).
- **LUTS increase with age.**
- **Symptoms:**
  - **16–94%**, commoner in **girls**;
  - **incontinence commonest (mostly 35–45%)**;
  - urgency and frequency;
  - monosymptomatic enuresis 3–13%;
  - **UTI 5–57%**;
  - **constipation 33–66%**.
- **Upper-tract deterioration is under 5%** (0.5–12%). It is mainly hydronephrosis; also asymmetry, VUR and microlithiasis.
  - **Risk factors:** **age, DSD symptoms (retention, interrupted voiding, hesitancy), recurrent UTI**.
- **Urodynamics:**
  - **normal in one-third**;
  - **overactivity 30–61%**;
  - underactivity about 6%;
  - **small capacity 42–93%**;
  - **DSD about 12%**;
  - raised residual about 13%.
- **Uroflow is mostly bell-shaped**; less often staccato, intermittent, plateau or tower.

#### Tumours/Infarcts/Encephalopathies

- **CNS tumours present mainly with incontinence and retention.** UTI, straining, hydronephrosis and urgency also occur.
- **New bladder symptoms may herald tumour progression or recurrence.**
- **Urodynamics:**
  - poor compliance, inability to void, high voiding pressure, overactivity, raised residual, abnormal sphincter;
  - **no correlation with tumour site** (intracranial versus extracranial, suprasacral versus sacral).
- **Newborn hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE):**
  - **lower voided volume and arousal with voiding**;
  - **higher residual and voiding frequency**.

### Conditions of the Spinal Cord

#### Trauma

- **Paediatric SCI is rare:**
  - **Sweden about 2.6 per million per year**;
  - **USA:** spinal-injury admissions **about 170 per million** under 21 years, **SCI about 24 per million**.
- **Incidence rises with age**: about **six times higher in adolescents than in children**.
- **Sex:** **boys predominate, except in young children, where the sexes are equal**.
- **Causes:**
  - **motor vehicle or bicycle accidents (about a quarter to a half)**;
  - falls, gunshot, diving or sport.
- **Cause by age:**
  - **infants:** vehicle;
  - **toddlers and children:** falls;
  - **adolescents:** sport.
- **Iatrogenic:** spinal deformity surgery, aortic surgery, **ductus arteriosus ligation**.
- **Neonatal: hyperextension during high forceps delivery.**
- **Why the child's cord is vulnerable** (hypermobility → **ischaemic necrosis**):
  - **horizontal facet joints** → anteroposterior subluxation;
  - **late paraspinal muscle and ligament support**;
  - **a heavy head placing maximal flexion in the upper cervical spine**.

<p class="tcap">Spinal Shock versus Recovered Phase after SCI</p>

| Spinal shock | After recovery |
|---|---|
| **Underactive bladder, non-reactive sphincter**; needle EMG potentials may look normal | **Contractility and reactivity return** as oedema settles |
| — | **Suprasacral lesion (reticulospinal pathways cut) → overactivity with DSD** |
| — | **Cauda equina → little or no return** |

- **Sacral sensation and reflexes do not predict final LUT function.**
- **Thoracic lesion, long term:** **overactivity with DSD, high voiding pressure, then hydronephrosis and VUR**.
  - Typical pattern: **good compliance for part of filling, then small, C-fibre-mediated rhythmic contractions** with a **waxing and waning sphincter**.
  - **Leakage, but no effective emptying.**
- **Autonomic dysreflexia:**
  - **lesion at or above T6**;
  - **hallmark: severe hypertension**, which can cause **stroke, seizures or death**;
  - **mechanism:** loss of supraspinal control of splanchnic sympathetic outflow, so **bladder filling or contraction triggers an unopposed α1-mediated sympathetic surge**;
  - **rule: monitor blood pressure and have α-blockers available during cystography and UDS.**

#### Transverse Myelitis

- **Transverse myelitis (TM) is an immune-mediated inflammatory myelopathy.**
- **Epidemiology:**
  - **about 1400 US cases a year, 28% in children**;
  - **incidence peaks at 10–19 years** and in the **third decade**;
  - **sexes equal**.
- **Diagnostic criteria:**
  - **cord-attributable sensory, motor or autonomic dysfunction**;
  - **bilateral** signs;
  - **a defined sensory level**;
  - **inflammation (cerebrospinal fluid pleocytosis or increased T2 MRI signal)**.
- **Presentation:** **sudden back pain or leg weakness → rapid paralysis → retention**.
  - **Bladder dysfunction usually follows the motor deficit.**
  - **Bowel dysfunction** is likely.
- **Course:**
  - **spinal shock up to 6 weeks**, then spasticity if recovery is incomplete;
  - the **cord segment involved determines the motor deficit**.
- **Acute treatment:** **steroids, plasma exchange, intravenous immunoglobulin**.
- **Recovery:** begins at **2 weeks–3 months**; **mostly within 6 months**; may continue **up to 2 years**.
- **Retention in more than 95% during the acute phase.**

<p class="tcap">Transverse Myelitis — Acute versus Post-Shock Urodynamics</p>

| Acute phase | After spinal shock |
|---|---|
| **Areflexia or underactivity about 70%** | **Overactivity 59–90%** |
| Overactivity about 13% | **Poor compliance about 47%** |
| Poor compliance about 30% | **DSD 17–80%** |
| DSD about 20% | **DLPP >40 cm H₂O in 12–33%**; **underactivity rare** |

- **Outcomes:**
  - **about one-third void spontaneously**;
  - **57–73% need CIC**;
  - **antimuscarinics in 14–64%**;
  - **BTA or augmentation** if refractory;
  - **bowel dysfunction persists in up to about three-quarters**.
- **Motor and bladder recovery usually move together:**
  - **full → full**;
  - **partial → voids but needs an antimuscarinic**;
  - **none or wheelchair-dependent → CIC with or without an antimuscarinic**.
- **Complications are infrequent:**
  - UTI, VUR, hydroureteronephrosis;
  - **chronic renal insufficiency**;
  - **calculi, diverticula**.
- **Late CIC → poorer compliance and upper-tract deterioration** (about 5%).
- **Examination, symptoms and ambulation do not predict upper-tract risk.**
- **Largest series:**
  - **intact leg reflexes and full recovery → better volitional voiding**;
  - **but more than half on CIC still need it at 1 year**.

### Conditions of the Pelvis

#### Anorectal Malformation

- **Anorectal malformation (ARM):**
  - distal anorectal anomalies, with urinary and genital involvement;
  - **about 1 in 5000 live births**;
  - **Krickenbeck (International) Classification by fistula site**.
- **Urinary anomalies occur in 26–52%**, chiefly **left renal agenesis and VUR**. **Boys are affected more often than girls** (about 50% versus 29%).

<p class="tcap">High versus Low Anorectal Malformation</p>

| High (supralevator fistula) | Low (infralevator) |
|---|---|
| **Urinary anomaly about 70%** | **About 35%** |
| **Spinal bony anomaly 48–54%** | **15–27%** |
| **Cord anomaly more frequent** (overall 18–50%) | Less frequent |

- **Spinal bony anomalies overall: 30–44%.**
- **Cord anomalies:** **tethered cord, thick or fatty filum, lipoma**.
  - **Neuropathic LUT dysfunction is frequent with a cord anomaly, rare without one**;
  - it usually shows as **incontinence when toilet training fails**.
- **Skin markers are rarely present**, so **spinal ultrasound before 3 months** is needed.

<p class="tcap">Syndromic Anorectal Malformations</p>

| Syndrome | Components |
|---|---|
| **VACTERL** | **Vertebral, Anal atresia, Cardiac, Tracheo-oEsophageal fistula, Renal, Limb** |
| **MURCS** | **Müllerian duct aplasia, Renal aplasia, Cervicothoracic Somite dysplasia** |
| **OEIS** | **Omphalocele, Exstrophy, Imperforate anus, Spinal defects** |
| **Currarino** | **ARM + sacrococcygeal defect + presacral mass** |

- **Currarino syndrome:**
  - **tethered cord in more than 80%**;
  - **about 80% have postoperative voiding complaints**;
  - upper-tract changes are not uncommon;
  - **untethering has unpredictable effects on urodynamics**.
- **Urodynamics:**
  - **commonest: upper motor neuron pattern (overactivity and/or DSD)**;
  - lower motor neuron pattern possible;
  - **leak point pressure >40 cm H₂O**, poor compliance, small capacity;
  - **normal urodynamics do not exclude a vertebral anomaly or SB**.
- **ARM aetiology:**
  - multifactorial and partly genetic;
  - associations: **paternal smoking, maternal obesity and diabetes**.
- **The neuropathic bladder in ARM comes from the cord, not the surgery:**
  - **Peña's posterior sagittal anorectoplasty (PSARP) spares the pelvic nerves**;
  - **18–35% have neuropathic dysfunction**;
  - **nearly all abnormal before PSARP**, usually with a cord anomaly.
- **Urodynamics in ARM:**
  - **indications:** **spinal bony anomaly, cord defect, or VCUG or ultrasound signs**;
  - **timing:** **early infancy before definitive surgery → after the pull-through → again if secondary urinary or faecal incontinence develops**;
  - **an abnormal infant study → cord surgery or watchful waiting**.
- **ESRD just under 2%.**
- **If neuropathic → annual ultrasound plus repeat UDS or imaging** (Table 46.3).

#### Pelvic Surgery

- **Pelvic operations that harm LUT function:**
  - **sacrococcygeal teratoma resection**;
  - **pelvic rhabdomyosarcoma and other pelvic tumours**;
  - **Hirschsprung disease surgery**.

<p class="tcap">Pelvic Nerve Injury and LUT Pattern</p>

| Nerve injured | Autonomic loss | LUT result |
|---|---|---|
| **Pelvic splanchnic** | **Parasympathetic** | **Underactive bladder** |
| **Hypogastric** | **Sympathetic** | **Loss of compliance; bladder neck unresponsive to abdominal pressure** |
| **Pelvic plexus** | Mixed | **Mixed** |

<p class="tcap">Pelvic Surgery — Three Conditions Compared</p>

| | Sacrococcygeal teratoma | Pelvic rhabdomyosarcoma / tumours | Hirschsprung disease |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LUT dysfunction** | **35–45% neuropathic** | **Normal in only 27–40%** | **Unstable detrusor up to 45%, often resolving**; **transient retention up to 7%**; **incontinence 0–6%** |
| **Presentation** | **UTI, incomplete emptying** | **Enuresis, daytime incontinence, urgency, suprapubic pain on filling, UTI** | Usually silent |
| **Urodynamics** | Normal 28%; **overactivity 57–81%**; underactivity 14–27%; poor compliance; DSD 18–38% | **Markedly small capacity**; over- or underactivity; DSD | **One-third normal**; **two-thirds large capacity**; **high residual 55–78%**; poor compliance rare |
| **Upper tract** | **Scarring or low GFR about 25%; VUR 25–50%; hydronephrosis 30–43%** | **Abnormal renal function one-third; hydronephrosis 15%; reconstruction or diversion about 20%** | — |
| **Special point** | **Late tethering may contribute** | **Cystectomy then; bladder preservation now; radiotherapy → high risk** | **Surgical approach affects outcome** |
| **Screening** | **UDS before and after surgery in all** | **Frequency–volume chart for all survivors**; UDS and ultrasound only if abnormal | **Clinical screen and ultrasound before and after surgery for all**; **UDS before surgery if symptomatic, and 6 months after surgery if symptomatic** (neurapraxia resolves) |

- **Long-term follow-up in all three:** **annual ultrasound and review if UDS is abnormal or symptoms persist**.
- **Asymptomatic children with normal postoperative tests need no long-term follow-up unless symptoms arise.**

<p class="tcap">Other Conditions at a Glance</p>

| Condition | Dominant UDS pattern | Upper-tract risk | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| CP | Overactivity, small capacity; **one-third normal** | **Low (<5%)** | Risk rises with age, DSD symptoms and UTI |
| CNS tumour | Mixed; **no site correlation** | Variable | **New symptoms may mean recurrence** |
| SCI | Shock → **suprasacral: overactivity with DSD**; cauda equina: no return | **High (thoracic)** | **Autonomic dysreflexia at or above T6** |
| TM | Acute **areflexia** → **overactivity, DSD, poor compliance** | About 5%; higher with late CIC | **Examination does not predict risk; UDS needed** |
| ARM | **Upper motor neuron**; lower motor neuron possible | ESRD about 2% | **Spinal ultrasound before 3 months**; UDS before and after pull-through |
| Pelvic surgery | Depends on the nerve injured | **High after teratoma or rhabdomyosarcoma** | Screening as tabulated |

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
<p class="kpt">KEY POINTS: OTHER CONDITIONS ASSOCIATED WITH NEUROPATHIC LOWER URINARY TRACT DYSFUNCTION</p>

- **CP: continence is usually achieved, but late**; upper-tract deterioration is **under 5%**.
- **SCI is rare, and six times commoner in adolescents than in children.**
- **Spinal shock: underactive bladder, non-reactive sphincter.**
- **Upper thoracic or cervical SCI → autonomic dysreflexia.** **Monitor blood pressure and have α-blockers available** during cystography and UDS.
- **TM: bladder dysfunction usually follows the motor deficit.** Reflexes and full recovery predict voiding, but **more than half on CIC still need it at 1 year**.
- **ARM: cord anomalies in 18–50%.**
- **Monitor pelvic rhabdomyosarcoma survivors closely**, especially after radiotherapy.
</div>

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> Across the non-NTD causes, <b>the level of the lesion predicts the pattern</b>: suprapontine (CP, brain) gives overactivity with coordination and low renal risk; suprasacral cord (SCI, TM, tethering) gives overactivity with DSD and high renal risk; sacral or peripheral (cauda equina, pelvic surgery) gives underactivity or denervation. <b>In ARM and pelvic surgery, the cord or nerve lesion, not the visceral operation, is usually the culprit.</b></p>
