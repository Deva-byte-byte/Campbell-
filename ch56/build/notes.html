Chapter 56 — Hypospadias

  • Duckett (1995): “Hypospadiology” demands study of predecessors and current experts, and training in high-volume centres beats learning from one's own mistakes.
  • Progress continues to depend on meticulous documentation, multicentre collaboration and honest outcome reporting.

EMBRYOLOGY

  • Classic triad: dorsally hooded foreskin, proximal (ventral) meatus, ventral penile curvature, all from arrested penile development.
  • Indifferent stage: mesenchyme divides the cloacal membrane; cloacal folds merge into the genital tubercle. Anal and genital systems separate at 7 weeks. Urogenital folds and, laterally, labioscrotal folds flank the urogenital membrane.
  • Critical window: weeks 8–16, with urethral closure proceeding from the anus towards the distal glans.

  • Prepuce: folds appear at about 8 weeks, fuse dorsally first, then ventrally. Ventral fusion awaits urethral fold fusion (about 20 weeks), so an ectopic meatus comes with an incomplete foreskin in 95%.
  • Megameatus with intact prepuce (MIP) (5%): a wide distal meatus, normal foreskin, no severe curvature. The preputial and urethral processes can therefore dissociate.
  • Hormonal phases: a hormone-independent phase, then from week 8 a hormone-dependent one. SRY (sex-determining region Y) at 7–8 weeks → testis → testosterone → regression of female structures and tubercle elongation.
  • Leydig-cell testosterone → 5α-reductase type 2 → dihydrotestosterone (DHT) locally in the genitalia. Sonic hedgehog (Shh) drives tubercle and plate formation.
  • Androgen receptors lie in penile skin, inner prepuce, urethra and spongiosal stroma. Without receptor stimulation, scrotal and urethral folds fail to fuse, as in female development.

KEY POINTS

  • DHT at 8–12 weeks is the key mediator of penile development.
  • Male urethral closure is androgen-dependent, unlike female urethral formation.

Concept — Hypospadias is an arrest of the androgen-dependent closing zipper. The meatus stops where closure stopped, the prepuce cannot close ventrally over an open urethra, and the unfused ventral tissue tethers the shaft into curvature.

DIAGNOSIS OF HYPOSPADIAS

  • One of the commonest congenital anomalies: 1 in 150–300 male births. Diagnosis is clinical: ectopic ventral meatus, ventral curvature, dorsally hooded foreskin. The last two are not always present.
  • MIP (up to 5%) is hidden by an intact foreskin (Fig. 56.3). Retract the foreskin before every newborn circumcision. Previous circumcision did not harm later MIP repair, but avoid circumcision if there is curvature or ventral skin deficiency, because the foreskin may be needed.
  • Curvature without hypospadias (orthotopic meatus): defer circumcision until the curvature is repaired.
  • Severity by meatal position: distal 70–85%, proximal 10–25%. This oversimplifies: an apparently distal meatus may overlie hypoplastic tissue and absent spongiosum to the penoscrotal junction, with severe curvature (Fig. 56.5). Classify after degloving, by meatal position plus curvature.

  • The trap: the meatus is only one of three components. A coronal meatus with a flat, small glans and 45° curvature scores worse than its position suggests.

  • Proximal cases have more curvature, a shorter penis and less elastic tissue on elastography. Ultrasound can show the divergence of the spongiosum.
  • Associations: inguinal hernia or hydrocele 9–16%, not related to severity. Cryptorchidism about 7%, nearly 10% in proximal hypospadias.

Concept — The meatus is the least reliable marker of severity. The true phenotype (plate, spongiosum, curvature) appears only after degloving, and the hypospadias–cryptorchidism pair is the signal of a systemic androgen problem, not a local one.

ETIOLOGY OF HYPOSPADIAS

  • Multifactorial. Risk factors: prematurity, small for gestational age (<10th centile), intrauterine growth restriction. Prematurity (<37 weeks) and low birth weight cluster with proximal hypospadias plus cryptorchidism.
  • Placental insufficiency: low placental weight → about twofold hypospadias and 1.5-fold cryptorchidism. Assisted reproduction is an independent risk factor, even after adjusting for prematurity, low birth weight and twinning.
  • Familial: twin concordance 9–27%; first-degree relative affected in 9–15%. A Danish national registry showed maternal and paternal transmission and risk even in third-degree relatives.
  • No single gene: chromosomal abnormalities in up to 30%. Gene categories:
    • penile development: HOX, fibroblast growth factor (FGF)
    • gonadal development: WT1, SOX9, DMRT1, GATA4
    • androgen cascade: LH receptor, 5α-reductase, androgen receptor, testicular development
  • Nearly 200 syndromes include hypospadias, notably WAGR (Wilms tumour, aniridia, genitourinary anomalies, developmental delay), Denys–Drash (genital malformation, renal failure, high Wilms risk) and Smith–Lemli–Opitz (heart, lung, kidney, gut and genital malformations).
  • Incidence: reports of rises (up to 3% per year since the 1970s) are disputed, possibly reflecting inclusion of milder variants. A 40-year Swedish series shows a true rise in distal and proximal cases. Overall the rate is stable or increasing.
  • Testicular dysgenesis syndrome (TDS): poor semen quality, testicular cancer, cryptorchidism and hypospadias share a fetal exposure disrupting the androgen cascade, with genetic and environmental causes.
  • Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs): pesticides, hormones (contraceptives, diethylstilbestrol (DES)), phthalates (plastics, hairspray), phytoestrogens (vegetarian diet). They competitively inhibit the androgen receptor. The link is proven in animals but unproven in humans. One case-control study found risk from parental exposure to detergents, pesticides and cosmetics, highest when combined with occupational exposure.
  • Anogenital distance (AGD): centre of anus to the caudal penoscrotal junction. It is a marker of disrupted androgen action: shorter with EDC exposure, and linked to infertility, cryptorchidism and hypospadias. Shorter AGD correlates with greater severity.

KEY POINTS

  • Assisted reproduction and monozygotic twins raise risk.
  • Causes include placental insufficiency, androgen disruption (partly environmental) and genetics. AGD reflects androgen disruption and tracks severity.

Concept — Every aetiological thread (placenta, genes, chemicals, AGD) converges on insufficient androgen action during weeks 8–16. That is why hypospadias travels with cryptorchidism and poor spermatogenesis, and why the most severe cases need a DSD work-up.

SURGICAL ASSESSMENT

  • Goals: a straight erection of adequate length; a urethra to the tip for urine and semen; an acceptable cosmetic result. Achieve them with the fewest procedures and a durable result into adulthood.
  • A straight penis with a meatus distal enough to void standing may gain little from repair. Counsel honestly.
  • Timing: genital awareness begins at about 18 months. Repair before 12 months gives less anxiety, better psychosexual outcome and possibly fewer complications.
  • AAP Section on Urology (1996): elective repair at 6–12 months. Adult repair was once thought riskier, but recent series show similar success.
  • Anaesthesia: animal neurotoxicity data exist, but children with about 1 hour of general anaesthesia (hernia repair) showed no neurodevelopmental delay at 2 years. A single short exposure appears low-risk. Discuss deferring until the child can assent in mild cases. Unrepaired distal cases report urinary bother and concern about appearance.
  • History: previous surgery, urinary tract infection (UTI), voiding dysfunction, curvature with erection, asthma, bleeding disorders. Examination: stretched penile length (exclude micropenis), both groins (undescended testis), AGD (androgen insensitivity).
  • No routine voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) or renal ultrasound. Associated VUR, hydronephrosis and renal ectopia are of little significance. Screen with ultrasound only if there are other systemic anomalies.
  • Prostatic utricle: commoner in proximal cases. Large utricles risk stasis, UTI and difficult catheterisation. Endoscopic or robotic removal is possible, but most are asymptomatic and the vas is at risk, so intervention is rare.
  • Plan for staging before the day of surgery. If found at newborn circumcision, abort the circumcision if there is curvature or skin deficiency.

Preoperative Androgen Stimulation

  • Glans width <14 mm is associated with more complications, though this is disputed (technical error may matter more). Dihydrotestosterone, human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG) or testosterone enlarges the glans and penis, reducing glansplasty tension and giving more tissue.
  • Side effects: aggression, more erections, skin darkening, transient secondary sexual features, all resolving within 6 months. Theoretical concerns: more bleeding (angiogenesis) and impaired healing. Severe proximal cases may be partly androgen-insensitive and respond poorly.
  • Evidence: meta-analyses are inconclusive because the data are heterogeneous. Two randomised trials showed fewer complications with preoperative testosterone, and surveys show common use for proximal hypospadias or a small glans.
  • Authors' regimen: testosterone cypionate 2 mg/kg intramuscularly at 5 and 2 weeks before surgery if the glans width is <15 mm. This gives about 4 mm glans gain with two doses (2 mm with one), with fewer complications in formal urethroplasty.

Assessment and Management of Ventral Penile Curvature

  • Curvature (chordee) occurs with or without hypospadias. It is the main determinant of one-stage vs multistage repair. In adults, even 20–30° ventral curvature can impair intercourse and satisfaction.
  • Causes: tethered ventral skin, fibrous dysplastic dartos, a short urethra, corporal disproportion. Devine and Horton (1973) staged by dysplasia of Buck's fascia, dartos and spongiosum. A later four-group system: skin tethering · dysplastic Buck's and dartos fascia · corporal disproportion · congenitally short urethra.

Assessment: parents' history or photographs of erections → bougie à boule or catheter under the ventral tissue (shows urethral and skin quality) → circumscribe and deglove to the penoscrotal junction, excising dysplastic dartos → artificial erection (tourniquet at the base plus saline, Gittes–McLaughlin; or compress the corpora at the base; or prostaglandin). A tourniquet can hide basal curvature.

  • Grading: mild <30°, moderate 30–45°, severe ≥45°.
  • A goniometer reveals that “eyeballing” underestimates curvature, and apps can capture images. But goniometer interrater reliability is poor (an acute angle and an arc behave differently), so a reliable standard is still needed.

Algorithm (Fig. 56.9): deglove + artificial erection → straight → urethroplasty. Residual <30° → dorsal plication. >30° → divide the urethral plate → still >30° → ventral corporal lengthening. Poor ventral skin despite <30° → consider lengthening.

  • Evidence: in severe perineal hypospadias, recurrent curvature followed plication in about a fifth but no staged grafted repairs. In a 100-patient series, recurrence was 28% after plication vs 9% after grafting. Not dividing the plate is an independent risk factor.
  • Curvature may worsen at puberty, or represent under-correction. Correct it fully the first time, because persistent curvature needs multiple staged revisions.
  • Congenital penile curvature (severe chordee, normal urethra): disrupted tunical collagen and matrix. Algorithm: deglove → plicate → severe cases transect the urethra, graft and stage. Authors' alternative: mobilise the normal urethra in situ, corporotomy and graft in one operation (Fig. 56.13). Recurrence 2–15%, mostly after plication.
  • Dorsal or lateral curvature: usually multipoint plication; rarely a patch graft with urethral or neurovascular mobilisation. Plication fails more often for lateral, dorsal or spiral curves (thinner lateral tunica).

KEY POINTS

  • Curvature <30° → dorsal plication, without clinically apparent shortening.
  • Objective measurement is elusive, but curvature assessment drives technique and must be fully corrected.
  • Plate transection and/or ventral grafting are for moderate or severe curvature after degloving and dartos excision.
  • Under-corrected curvature causes major morbidity and repeated surgery.

KEY POINTS

  • Goals: normal voiding, sexual function and appearance.
  • Counselling weighs early anaesthesia against early-repair benefits.
  • No routine imaging in non-syndromic hypospadias.
  • Preoperative androgens transiently enlarge the penis; their effect on complications is controversial.
  • Examination findings to plan for: deficient ventral prepuce, downward glans tilt, deviated median raphe, ventral curvature, scrotal encroachment, scrotal cleft, penoscrotal transposition.
  • Regional block is important. Caudal block reduces anaesthetic and does not appear to increase complications.

Concept — Curvature, not meatal position, sets the operation. Mild curvature is shortened dorsally in one stage; severe curvature is lengthened ventrally and usually staged. Undercorrection is the error that keeps generating further surgery through puberty.

General Considerations of Surgical Repair

  • Age: repair under 6 months had more success in some studies but not others. A systematic review (over 16,000 patients) found fewer complications in younger children and recommended repair by 18 months. Surgery after 18 months carried no long-term emotional harm. Authors: start at 6 months, especially if staged, within the AAP range of 6–18 months. Adult repair outcomes were once worse; recent series show no excess.
  • Antibiotics: early studies counted asymptomatic bacteriuria. One randomised trial in TIP found fewer complicated UTIs and complications with continuous antibiotic prophylaxis (CAP); others found no effect, and some question preoperative antibiotics altogether. Infection is rare, so the question needs a multicentre trial. Authors' practice ranges from none to 3 days starting the day before stent removal.
  • Sutures: must hold until healed but dissolve before suture tracks epithelialise (fistula, sinus), with little inflammation. Rapidly absorbed chromic or polyglactin did better than slowly absorbed polydioxanone (PDS) in urethroplasty. Size: 6-0 to 8-0 in infants; 5-0 in adults.
  • Learning curve: higher surgeon volume and more years after fellowship → fewer complications.
  • Never use hair-bearing skin for urethroplasty: postpubertal hair → stones and recurrent UTI (Fig. 56.14).
  • Technique: gentle handling (fine forceps, skin hooks, stay sutures), magnification, generous flap mobilisation, and tension-free urethra, glans and skin. Tension → reduced capillary flow → ischaemia → fibrosis, contraction, infection and breakdown.
  • Closure technique is debated: epithelial inversion and two-layer closure have been favoured, but simple interrupted full-thickness closure has also done well.
  • Barrier layers of vascularised, non-epithelialised tissue over the neourethra reduce fistula (dartos interposition after TIP), provided they do not compress the glansplasty. Sources: de-epithelialised dorsal preputial dartos (Belman flap, harvested after rotating Byars flaps), de-epithelialised ventral shaft skin, tunica vaginalis (mobilised to the external ring to avoid tethering), spongiosum. Secure laterally so suture lines are offset.
  • Haemostasis: pinpoint cautery. Authors use an intermittent tourniquet, released every 30 minutes. Haematoma impairs flap and graft healing.
  • Dressing: a circumferential Tegaderm from glans to penoscrotal junction, including the glans (prevents swelling), with no constricting proximal ring. Or a “sandwich” dressing (stent between two Tegaderm layers) for 72 hours. Then petroleum jelly; warn parents about oedema and bruising.

KEY POINTS

  • Higher surgical volume → fewer complications.
  • Non-overlapping suture lines, tension-free closure and vascularised barrier layers improve success.

Concept — Hypospadias surgery succeeds or fails on blood supply. Every technical rule (fine instruments, no tension, offset suture lines, vascular barrier layers, quickly absorbed sutures, no haematoma) protects perfusion of thin, dysplastic tissue.

Distal Hypospadias Repair

  • More than 200 techniques exist, mostly variants. The tenets: correct curvature → advance the urethra (with or without urethroplasty) → glansplasty → skin closure ± preputioplasty.
  • Urethroplasty sources: local genital skin flaps; preputial or extragenital flaps or grafts; tubularisation of the plate.
  • TIP is used for over 90% of distal and about 80% of midshaft repairs.
  • Distal repair suits minimal curvature and favourable anatomy for single-stage repair; more severe variants are treated as proximal.

Comments on Proximal Hypospadias Repair

  • Historical series underestimated proximal complications by pooling mild and severe variants.
  • 25–75% need additional procedures after a planned two-stage repair, each adding further risk.
  • Distal hypospadias is corrected successfully in about 80%; the specialty's effort must now go into reducing complications in proximal hypospadias, where each additional procedure adds further risk.
  • Longer urethroplasty = more demanding: a long neourethra of uniform calibre is hard to build, and the neourethra may be inherently narrowed and unable to expand to propel the urine bolus.
  • Poiseuille's law: resistance to flow is proportional to tube length and inversely proportional to the radius to the fourth power. Small changes in calibre therefore have a large effect, and the longer the tube, the greater the stricture risk.
  • Failure of the neourethra to expand → raised resistance → fistula and/or diverticulum.
  • The glans is non-distensible (dense spongiosum). Even with uniform calibre, the fixed distal urethra causes turbulence and high proximal pressure → diverticulum.
  • The neourethra is a skin-lined tube without spongiosum, an independent risk for progressive distension.
  • Tissue dysplasia, the severity of curvature and skin shortage may impair healing, perfusion and growth with age. Each surgeon's numbers are falling, so multi-institutional collaboration is essential.

KEY POINTS

  • Proximal hypospadias has a higher complication rate than distal.
  • The trend is towards staged repair for severe hypospadias: curvature is corrected at stage one, urethroplasty at stage two. This gives supple ventral tissue for tubularisation and avoids a urethroplasty laid over a corporal graft in one stage.
  • 25–75% need additional procedures after a planned two-stage repair.

Concept — Proximal repairs fail for physical reasons: a long, rigid, spongiosum-free tube ending in a fixed glans. Length and stiffness raise resistance, and raised resistance produces fistulae, diverticula and strictures.

Postoperative Management

  • A careless postoperative course can ruin a good repair, yet quality evidence for specific regimens is lacking.
  • First 48 hours: urinary diversion plus a compressive dressing, which reduces stress on the neourethra, tamponades bleeding and limits swelling. The authors leave the dressing 72 hours, then apply petroleum jelly.
  • A soft, pliable urethral stent in complicated repairs reduces meatal stenosis and fistula. A suprapubic tube is used in older children and redo cases.
  • Diversion lasts 3–21 days, by complexity, urethroplasty length and preference. Longer stenting reduced complications in one study but is not universally accepted, and must be weighed against discomfort and UTI risk, which rises with each day. Short distal repairs have been done without a stent with no change in outcome.
  • Bladder spasm → high-pressure voiding around the stent → stress on the repair, so give oxybutynin until the stent is removed.
  • Long, stiff stents press on the ventral repair and risk glans dehiscence. A Foley catheter must be fixed in the midline to the anterior abdominal wall. Tape often slips and pulls the catheter laterally or ventrally, so the authors have abandoned Foley catheters because of glans dehiscence.
  • Analgesia: paracetamol is near-universal, and about half of urologists add non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Ibuprofen and ketorolac do not increase bleeding. Regular paracetamol plus ibuprofen minimises opioid use, tapering after 48–72 hours.

  • Duckett described “hypospadiology” as a humbling blend of art and science. The tissues are delicate and healing depends on fragile neovascularisation, threatened by oedema, infection and variable healing.
  • Complications have lacked standard definitions, which makes reporting inconsistent. The standardised set is in Table 56.1.

Urethrocutaneous Fistula

  • The commonest complication: just under 10% at short-term follow-up. It occurs most often at the original meatus or the coronal margin, from pinpoint to large.
  • Causes: ischaemia, oedema, infection, haematoma and especially distal obstruction (meatal stenosis or stricture → high pressure and turbulence). Technical causes: overlapping suture lines, inadequately inverted epithelium, poorly absorbable sutures. Risk rises with severity.
  • It is noticed as multiple streams, mostly within 2 years, or at toilet training.
  • Small early fistulae rarely close spontaneously. Repair at least 6–12 months after the primary operation, once tissues soften.

Assessment under anaesthesia: bougie à boule calibration for patency and irregular calibre → urethroscopy if irregular → “stress test”: catheter in the urethra, compress proximally and distally, inject saline or methylene blue to reveal hidden tracts.

Glans Dehiscence

  • Causes: catheter tension, poor technique, glans closure under tension, vascular compromise. The meatus retreats to a coronal or subcoronal position, in effect a breakdown of the repair.
  • Reoperate only for voiding symptoms. If repair is needed, an interposition inlay graft, as a staged step or in a single stage, avoids glans tension.

Meatal Stenosis

  • Definitions vary. Risk: urethral closure carried too far distally, or a tight glans closure. Longer stenting may help (weak evidence).
  • The postoperative meatus should calibrate ≥8 Fr, increasing with age. Clinically relevant stenosis means stranguria, deflected stream, poor flow, retention and a “laser-beam” stream.
  • Escalation:
    • Sound dilatation ± topical betamethasone in some cases.
    • Routine postoperative dilatation is unnecessary, but early dilatation may reveal subclinical stenosis.
    • Refractory to dilatation → dorsal midline meatotomy (rarely), otherwise redo urethroplasty.
  • Meatal retrusion: excessive distal tension, or after glans dehiscence. It results from poor planning: an immobile or poorly vascularised urethra retracts. Revise if the deflected stream prevents standing voiding.

Urethral Stricture

  • Rates depend on technique and urethroplasty length. Sites: native–neourethral junction, along the neourethra (especially at the coronal margin with a narrow plate), or within the glans. It presents with a weak stream, stranguria, retention or UTI.
  • Causes: technique, ischaemia, trauma, infection. Characterise by cystoscopy (length, calibre, site).
  • Short, mildly symptomatic strictures → dilatation or endoscopic incision, rarely durable. Internal urethrotomy succeeds poorly and can worsen scarring. Even strictures <1 cm with intermittent catheterisation seldom keep normal flow. Results may be better for short anastomotic strictures treated at 3 months, but the authors rarely see success.
  • Refractory or extensive strictures → revision urethroplasty. Expect tissue loss, so be ready to harvest local flap or buccal mucosa; this is often multistage.
  • Hair-bearing skin in the urethra after multistage or complex repairs, combined with narrowing, becomes a nidus for stones and UTI. Options: laser epilation; endoscopic stone removal. When stones, hair and UTI coexist → resect and replace with buccal graft or local skin.
  • Recurrent UTI → look for diverticulum or stricture. An enlarged prostatic utricle causing stasis → excise or fulgurate.

Urethral Diverticulum

  • It presents with a weak stream, post-void dribbling, UTI, rarely haematuria, ballooning of the shaft, or the need to “milk” the urethra.
  • Commoner after preputial flap, two-stage and proximal repairs: there is no spongiosum, and a rigid glanular neourethra raises penile urethral pressure.
  • Small saccular diverticulum → excise and reduce to a uniform calibre. Extensive diverticulum (more common) → excise redundant tissue, close the urethra and add multilayer reinforcement.
  • The redundant wall is well vascularised and can be reused as a flap to repair coexisting fistula or distal stricture. Extraurethral plication avoids entering the urethra.

Balanitis Xerotica Obliterans

  • Balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO), or lichen sclerosus, is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis. It occurs with phimosis in uncircumcised boys, with meatal stenosis, or with progressive urethral narrowing after repair, appearing pale and white.
  • Meatal disease only → topical betamethasone or clobetasol, or systemic tacrolimus. These usually fail.
  • Definitive: resect all involved tissue, send it for histology, and replace with inner prepuce or buccal mucosa in a staged repair. BXO can invade buccal mucosa.
  • Recurrence up to 40% on medium-term follow-up, so monitor closely.

BXO sequence: urethral “cutback” to healthy tissue → topical steroids → excise persistent disease → place a buccal graft and confirm take without recurrence → urethral closure.

Recurrent Penile Curvature

  • It damages urinary and sexual function and may worsen at puberty through differential dorsal–ventral corporal growth.
  • Causes: curvature underestimated or incompletely corrected, graft contraction after corporotomy, ventral skin traction, or a preserved tethering plate.
  • Assess by complete degloving with artificial erection. A tourniquet can hide basal curvature, so compress the corpora against the pubis instead.

Staged correction (counsel the family that several procedures are likely): stage 1 identifies the cause (watch for corporal disproportion after a previous dorsal plication). If a corporal graft is used, cover it with healthy dartos as a bed for buccal graft → stage 2: ventral buccal graft unless skin allows tension-free cover → stage 3: Cecil modification if shaft skin is deficient → stage 4: tension-free skin closure.

Figure legend, Fig. 56.38 — A 14-year-old after infant single-stage repair with dorsal plication presented with marked ventral curvature. Correcting it left a long urethral gap, which needed a buccal inlay and then final closure.

Skin Complications

  • Buried penis with shortening from concealment and poor skin fixation is prevented by degloving plus anchoring sutures from Buck's fascia to the dermis at the penopubic and penoscrotal junction.
  • Suture sinus tracts come from thin ventral skin, full-thickness sutures, or sutures tied too tight (ischaemia).
  • Penoscrotal transposition or webbing after proximal repair → scrotoplasty to prevent concealment.

Concept — Almost every urethroplasty complication traces back to distal obstruction, ischaemia or tension. That is why fistula and diverticulum repair start by excluding distal narrowing, and why every repair adds vascularised barrier layers.

HYPOSPADIAS OUTCOMES

  • The literature is weak: retrospective, small, technically varied, with poorly defined outcomes, no independent assessment and much loss to follow-up.
  • Largest meta-analysis of TIP repair (Pfistermuller et al., 2015; 49 studies, 4,675 boys, mean follow-up 16 months): complications 10.6%, reoperation 4.5%; fistula 5.7% > meatal stenosis 3.6% > stricture 1.3%. An extra dartos layer improved outcomes; proximal and redo cases did significantly worse.
  • TIP vs Mathieu (meta-analysis): similar overall complications and fistula rates, but meatal stenosis much commoner after TIP.
  • Recurrent UTI after repair is uncommon (about 2%). Imaging finds distal narrowing, VUR, diverticulum or an enlarged utricle. TIP → raised post-void residual; OIF → diverticulum or fistula. Image anatomically any child with recurrent UTI.
  • Distal hypospadias: success 83–95%. Complications in large single-centre series are higher with longer follow-up and a more proximal meatus. After TD repair, fistula and glans dehiscence occur early (first year) but stricture late.
  • Proximal hypospadias: complications 23–68%; fistula is again commonest (3–45%), then glans dehiscence and meatal stenosis. Diverticula are commoner after Byars flap or onlay than after two-stage graft repair (STAG or Bracka).
  • Recurrent curvature after two-stage repair is low (0–10%). Two-stage repair with corporoplasty gives greater penile length and better cosmesis. Penile length is the chief concern of adults repaired in infancy.
  • STAG: complications <25%, mainly glans dehiscence or fistula; recurrent curvature low. Extra layers and better blood supply reduce complications.
  • In a four-way comparison (TIP, preputial island onlay, island flap tube, two-stage Bracka), the approach did not change complications. Dorsal plication plus proximal urethral mobilisation corrects most curvature and avoids laying a skin graft over a corporal graft.
  • Byars flap two-stage repair: high complications (30–70%), mainly fistula then glans dehiscence. Diverticulum results from no spongiosum plus no fixation of the flap to the ventral shaft.
  • In one centre, single-stage repairs had more complications than planned two-stage repairs (not significant). Two-stage approaches that close the distal urethra and correct curvature at stage one report favourable results.
  • Penile perception scores were better with staged repair, which gave a longer penis, with similar complications and urinary complaints across four techniques.
  • No direct comparison identifies the ideal approach to proximal hypospadias with severe curvature, and long-term data are lacking.

Outcome Assessment

  • Fistulae are obvious; early stricture is subtle. Watch the stream in clinic or on family video. Office calibration is difficult, so examine under anaesthesia if concerned.
  • Uroflow helps, but normal values after repair are poorly defined, it cannot be done before toilet training, and flow improves with age.
  • Only about half of complications appear in the first year; about three-quarters are found by 3 years. Fistulae appear early and strictures late.
  • Authors' standard: follow through puberty, especially proximal cases. Review after toilet training (stream), and at puberty (velocity, curvature, sexual function).

Patient-Reported Outcomes

  • Surgeon-judged success may differ from patient and parent perception. Parental decisional regret follows complications, hesitancy about surgery and a wish for circumcision. Better education reduces conflict.
  • Decision aids (an online “hypospadias hub” of video and text): families engaged repeatedly, felt confident, and had less decisional regret.

  • Hypospadias-specific health-related quality of life (HRQoL) framework (scoping review) defines five domains: penile appearance, sexual health, psychological and behavioural function, social function, voiding function.   • Mild, undiagnosed distal hypospadias (no curvature) in adults: minimal sexual, fertility or voiding problems. Many men are unaware of it, adapt to spraying, are sexually active and father children. More proximal defects with curvature report worse sexual and functional outcomes.
  • After surgery:
    • Older studies reported more behavioural and school problems (worse with severity) and later, less frequent sexual activity.
    • A contemporary controlled series found equal fertility, sexual satisfaction and age at first intercourse overall, but worse satisfaction, fertility and sensation in proximal cases.
    • Adolescents after proximal repair worry about penile growth more than meatal position and are more hesitant about sexual contact.
  • Overall: no major psychopathology, but less satisfaction with appearance, sexual inhibition, more negative genital appraisal, anxiety and lower self-esteem. Earlier surgery may help. Lay observers see distal repairs as normal and proximal repairs as only slightly different.

KEY POINTS

  • About 60% of urethroplasty complications occur within 1 year; stricture and curvature can appear late, so follow proximal cases into puberty.
  • Risk factors: proximal meatus, glans width <14 mm, no barrier flap, previous hypospadias surgery.
  • Fistula is commonest: primary closure if tissue is supple; redo urethroplasty and glans closure if glans tissue is thin.
  • Meatotomy or skin flap for stenosis usually fails with BXO; excise all BXO and stage with buccal graft. Failure is up to 40%, so monitor.
  • Long term: more ejaculatory problems, less sexual satisfaction, more dissatisfaction with appearance. PROs are under-used.

Concept — Hypospadias outcomes are time-dependent and observer-dependent. Complications keep appearing for years, and surgeons, patients and parents judge success differently. Only follow-up through puberty with patient-reported measures captures the true result.

Hypospadias Reoperations

  • Simple problems (fistula, skin redundancy) can be fixed in one stage. Each unplanned operation degrades tissue, scarring the penis, disrupting the blood supply and shrinking options, so complication rates rise with each redo.
  • Devine (1973): after failure, surgeons tend to attempt “small operations”, which make matters worse. What is needed is “extensive resection of scarring, major shifts of tissues and meticulous reconstruction.”
  • Assessment: history of voiding, skin and erectile problems; previous operative notes (tunica vaginalis flaps, grafts, urethroplasty type, curvature correction). Have a low threshold for cystoscopy, and always perform artificial erection, because missed curvature has severe long-term consequences.
  • Blood supply of previously operated flaps is unpredictable, so add layers (tunica vaginalis).
  • Tubularisation redo (Mathieu, TD, redo TIP) has reasonable success. An inlay graft after incising the plate improves redo outcomes, but take care with tissue that has already failed.
  • Focal problem (fistula, diverticulum) without curvature → local or remote skin flaps, as onlay or multilayer cover, especially from redundant non-hair-bearing shaft skin.
  • Split-onlay skin (SOS) flap: a pedicled skin flap of about 1 cm, from anywhere redundant (shaft or circumcision line), split into an onlay for the urethra plus a second piece for barrier or skin cover, or de-epithelialised as an extra layer.

  • Staged buccal repair beats single-stage tubularisation (better graft take). A dorsal inlay is better than an onlay or complete buccal tube in redo cases with poor blood supply (Fig. 56.31).

Buccal graft technique: harvest 10% larger than the bed → leave the donor site to heal by secondary intention (closure adds pain, little benefit) → combine sources for long defects → fenestrate with an 18- or 21-gauge needle (less haematoma) and keep in saline → incise the recipient bed ventrally from the urethra through the glans to the planned meatus, preserving dartos (the blood supply) → suture along its axis without narrowing it → quilting sutures against haematoma → wait ≥6 months (up to 12) before urethroplasty, checking take and contraction → topical steroid for contraction (from 1 month) → family “graft massage” from about 2 weeks (vitamin E oil, silicone gel or petroleum jelly).

  • Results after completion: 30–75% success; meatal stenosis is the commonest failure, then stricture and breakdown.
  • Cecil–Culp (Cecil 1946; Culp 1951): urethroplasty → midline scrotal incision → recess the repaired penis into the scrotal dartos bed, where it parasitises the scrotal blood supply → release at a second stage (Cecil: 6–8 weeks).
  • It fell from favour with single-stage repair and has returned for salvage. The concerns are hair-bearing scrotal skin and multiple procedures.
  • Modification: delay release to 9–12 months. This transfers more vascularised layers, usually avoids using scrotal skin, and stretches deficient shaft skin for tension-free closure.

KEY POINTS

  • Lip mucosa within the glans: thinner, fewer complications.
  • Buccal mucosa is the graft of choice in complex reconstruction (properties as tabulated).
  • Cecil modification vascularises skin after multiple repairs.

Concept — Reoperation is a problem of blood supply, not technique. Each step (staging, vascular layers, buccal grafts on dartos, scrotal parasitisation, tissue expansion, nitroglycerin, hyperbaric oxygen) brings vascularised, non-hair-bearing tissue to a bed that previous surgery has impoverished.

FUTURE

  • Progress depends on honest reporting, standardised pre-, intra- and postoperative assessment and definitions, and multi-institutional collaboration to power studies of complication sources.
  • Coaching and teamwork, as in athletics, can refine technique for rare and difficult cases, particularly proximal hypospadias.
  • Priorities: lower complication rates in severe hypospadias, follow-up into adulthood, and long-term data to set expectations.
  • Open questions: the ideal meatal position; what curvature is acceptable; how much surgery patients will accept. One patient left with a coronal meatus but a straight penis and normal voiding is satisfied (Fig. 56.45). Others are long-term psychological effects and how best to educate families.
  • Technology: peri-operative videography and crowd-sourced assessment; AI and machine learning on recorded procedures; genomic, proteomic and microbiome risk profiling; a central registry linking anatomy, technique, outcomes and PROs; tissue banking; and perhaps printed replacement tissue.
