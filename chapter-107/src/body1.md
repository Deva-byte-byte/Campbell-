- **Lower urinary tract (LUT) dysfunction** produces lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) that impair quality of life, but it can also be **"silent"** — asymptomatic yet placing the upper tracts at risk.
- **Urodynamics (UDS)** is the dynamic study of the transport, storage and evacuation of urine: a set of tests, used singly or together, to **diagnose suspected dysfunction and assess the effect of known dysfunction**.
- The LUT has **two essential functions**: **low-pressure storage** (protects the kidneys, ensures continence) and **voluntary evacuation** (socially acceptable voiding without leakage or overdistension).
- Disruption produces either **bothersome LUTS** (frequency, urgency, urgency urinary incontinence (UUI), slow or interrupted stream) or **harmful sequelae** — urinary tract infection (UTI) and renal failure.
- International Continence Society (ICS) usage distinguishes **UDS studies** (the tests performed) from **UDS observations** (what is seen during testing).
- **What has changed**: not the principles or technique, but the **abandonment of absolute reliance on UDS**. Studies are now tailored to specific populations within a disease state, and used when available data are insufficient to choose treatment — to inform, reassure or uncover other pathology.
- The American Urological Association (AUA) now favours **disease-specific guidelines** — overactive bladder (OAB), neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD), stress urinary incontinence (SUI) — over the older **UDS-specific guideline** designed to help select tests after symptom characterisation.
- **Children**: principles and equipment are identical, but in children UDS **establishes a baseline** (anatomical and neurological abnormalities make function variable and unpredictable) so that change with treatment and growth can be tracked, and informs caregivers and patient about current and future function. Testing must still be **relevant, reliable and reproducible**. Adult conditions only are covered here.

## ROLE OF URODYNAMICS IN CLINICAL PRACTICE

- **Level 1 evidence for specific "indications" is limited**, for structural reasons:
    - randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are hard to justify where lower-level evidence strongly supports utility and **empirical treatment is potentially harmful or life-threatening** (e.g. NLUTD);
    - symptoms have many causes, so **homogeneous populations are hard to study**;
    - high-level data on UDS in counselling and shared decision-making are absent;
    - cost-effectiveness questions (e.g. who benefits from early surgery for bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) from benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)) need complex modelling or expensive long-term studies.
- UDS is **most useful when history, examination and simple tests are insufficient** — two general scenarios:
    - **Diagnosis** of the condition causing symptoms (LUTS, incontinence).
    - **Determining the impact of a disease capable of serious, irreversible upper- and lower-tract damage** — spinal cord injury (SCI), multiple sclerosis (MS), radiation cystitis. **Profound abnormalities may exist with few symptoms.**
- Rather than a list of indications, think of the **broader clinical roles** of UDS:
    - demonstrate the **pathophysiology** behind presenting signs or symptoms;
    - identify **contributing factors** and their relative importance;
    - obtain information on **other aspects of LUT function**, symptomatic or not;
    - **predict upper-tract consequences** (Box 107.1);
    - **predict treatment outcome and side effects**, aiding pre-operative counselling;
    - **confirm the effect or mode of action** of an intervention, especially a new one;
    - **explain failure** of previous treatment.
- **Governing principles of interpretation**:
    - the goal of a symptom-driven study is to **reproduce the symptom and identify its cause**;
    - **customise** the study — decide the questions first, then design the study to answer them;
    - UDS is an **unnatural setting**: an abnormality recorded without reproduction of symptoms is **not necessarily diagnostic**;
    - **failure to record an abnormality does not exclude it** (e.g. no detrusor overactivity (DO) seen in a patient with UUI);
    - **not every observation is clinically significant** — interpret with history, comorbidity, **postvoid residual (PVR)** and **frequency–volume charts**.
- **Technical care in data collection remains the cornerstone** of a good study, despite advances in recording and processing.

<p class="tcap">BOX 107.1 URODYNAMIC RISK FACTORS</p>

Findings that are potentially dangerous and **usually require intervention** to prevent upper- and lower-tract decompensation:

1. **Impaired compliance**
2. **Detrusor–external sphincter dyssynergia (DESD)**
3. **Detrusor–internal sphincter dyssynergia (DISD)**
4. **High-pressure DO present throughout filling**
5. **Elevated detrusor leak point pressure (> 40 cm H₂O)**
6. **Poor emptying with high storage pressures**

- **What the box is for**: every item is a route to **sustained high intravesical pressure**. The trap is to treat these as symptom findings — they mandate treatment **even when the patient is asymptomatic**.

## CONDUCTING A URODYNAMIC STUDY: PATIENT AND TECHNICAL FACTORS

### Functional Classification of Voiding Dysfunction

- **The single most important concept** for setting up, conducting and interpreting UDS — the **Wein functional classification (1981)**.
- **Functional division**:
    - **storage dysfunction** — failure to store;
    - **emptying dysfunction** — failure to empty;
    - **combined** — failure of both.
- **Anatomical subclassification** — the examinable pairing of site against direction:

| Site | Overactive | Underactive |
|---|---|---|
| **Bladder** | Failure to **store** | Failure to **empty** |
| **Bladder outlet** | Failure to **empty** | Failure to **store** |

- Plus **combined bladder and outlet dysfunction**.
- **Why it matters**: it generates the questions before the study, anticipates findings, and **organises treatment options**. It prevents the clinician becoming lost in neurophysiology instead of thinking practically: *is the problem bladder, outlet or both — and storage, emptying or both?*

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> <em>The classification is a <b>two-by-two of site against direction</b>: overactivity of the bladder and underactivity of the outlet both cause storage failure, and the reverse pair causes emptying failure. Each study is designed to locate the patient in that grid.</em></p>

### Preparing for Urodynamic Studies: Clinician, Patient, and Facility

#### Defining the Clinical Question

- **Three rules before any invasive study**: **decide the questions** → **design the study** to answer them → **customise** as necessary.
- **Symptoms alone are not an indication** — the information required must be defined in advance, informed by expert opinion, history, medication and examination.
- **Medication status must be decided and documented in advance** (antimuscarinics, β₃-adrenergic agonists):
    - **to assess a drug's therapeutic effect** → test **on** a regular dosing schedule;
    - **to uncover the aetiology** of symptoms treated empirically → consider **stopping** the drug first (highest diagnostic yield).
- **Pre-invasive work-up** recommended by the ICS: **validated symptom and bother score**, **3-day frequency–volume chart**, and **non-invasive uroflowmetry and PVR**. The International Urogynecological Association (IUGA) proforma begins with an exhaustive pre-study checklist.
- **Why**: a normal free flow and low PVR beforehand allow an abnormal flow during invasive testing to be recognised as **probably artefactual**.

#### Patient Preparation

- Explain **why the test is done, how it may change treatment and what to expect**.
- UDS is **generally well tolerated** — anxiety, discomfort and embarrassment are minimal to moderate, and most patients find it the same as or better than expected; most would repeat it. **Commonest complaint afterwards: pain on micturition.**
- **Younger patients report more pain and anxiety** → need more reassurance.
- **Simple environmental measures** — dimmed lighting and music (trend only), and **lavender aromatherapy (reduced anxiety in an RCT)** — may help.
- **Explanatory pamphlets have been disappointing**; well-trained, attentive, supportive staff matter most.
- **Urinalysis before every invasive study** (level 4 evidence).
- **Symptomatic UTI → postpone** until treatment is complete.
- **"Index" patients** (no UTI risk factors): the AUA Best Practice Policy (BPP) Statement on antimicrobial prophylaxis — **routine prophylaxis not indicated**.
- **Non-index patients**: the Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction (SUFU) BPP panel recommends a **single dose within 1 hour of UDS**:
    - **first choice: oral trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole**;
    - **alternatives**: first- or second-generation cephalosporin, amoxicillin–clavulanate, intravenous aminoglycoside ± ampicillin, fluoroquinolones (level 3).

- **What the table is for**: separating factors that genuinely raise the risk of **post-instrumentation infection or bacteraemia with consequences** (stasis, bacteriuria, foreign material, immune compromise, prostheses, age > 70) from factors that merely sound high-risk.
- **Pattern**: "yes" factors involve **stasis, colonisation, catheters, immune compromise or implants**; "no" factors are general medical states.
- **Level of evidence**: almost all level 4; only the **normal-anatomy "no"** is level 1, and **age > 70** and **menopausal status** level 2.
- **Traps**: **recurrent UTI, diabetes and cardiac valve disease are "no"**; **asymptomatic bacteriuria is "yes"** (unlike most urological contexts, where it is left untreated).

#### Facility Setup

- **Dedicated, quiet, private room** without interruptions — recreating a natural environment is already difficult.
- **Size**: enough to lie, stand and sit on a commode; room for **wheelchairs, walkers, scooters and hoists** (many patients have neurological mobility limits). **Videourodynamics (VUDS) needs more space** for fluoroscopy, and local radiation regulations apply.
- **Equipment ready beforehand**: **pessary kits, Scopette swabs and speculums** for pelvic organ prolapse (POP) reduction; a **step stool** (male urethral voiding images, or standing female stress views when the C-arm has limited minimum height); **large catheters** to drain a bladder when pre-invasive PVR is high.

#### Additional Factors

- An unanswered question → **repeat in the same session or later**; a **second filling and/or voiding cycle** is widely advocated.
- Even with best practice UDS **does not answer every question** — defining the information needed beforehand keeps unanswered questions to a minimum.
- **Proper performance with careful attention to technical detail** is the prerequisite for accurate interpretation (ICS Good Urodynamic Practices).
- **ICS terminology** should be used by all performing and interpreting UDS, for consistent reporting. **"Urodynamic observations"** = observations made and measured during the test itself. A list of common terms is given in **Box 107.2**; the ICS maintains an online glossary drawn from **19 publications**, including condition-specific terminology for **female pelvic floor dysfunction** and **adult neurogenic** patients.

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> <em>Preparation exists so that the invasive study answers a question decided in advance: the medication status, the pre-invasive flow and PVR, the antibiotic decision and the room itself are all chosen to make the result interpretable.</em></p>

## COMPONENTS OF THE URODYNAMIC STUDY

- Components can be used **individually or combined**; all are parts of multichannel UDS or VUDS.

*Postvoid Residual.*

- **Excellent assessment of emptying**; by **ultrasound (bladder scan) or catheterisation** — method must be specified.
- **Elevated PVR shows that emptying is impaired, not why.**
- **Confirm a raised PVR with a second measurement**, especially if the patient had no true desire to void.

*Uroflowmetry.*

- **Rate of urine flow over time**; also an assessment of emptying. Reportable parameters:
    - **voided volume (VV)**, mL; **flow rate (Q)**, mL/s;
    - **maximum flow rate (Qmax)** and **average flow rate (Qave)**, mL/s;
    - **voiding time** (total micturition time) vs **flow time** (time during which flow occurred);
    - **time to maximum flow** (onset to Qmax);
    - **voiding efficiency** = **VV / (VV + PVR) × 100**.
- **Curve shape** matters: **normal = bell-shaped** (Fig. 107.1). Interpretation is partly subjective.
- **Pattern → suggestion, not diagnosis**:
    - **interrupted or straining** → detrusor underactivity (or obstruction, or abdominal voiding);
    - **flattened (plateau)** → **fixed obstruction**.
- **Without detrusor pressure, the cause cannot be identified.**
- Standalone test: **preferred position** (sitting or standing); ask whether the flow was **representative**; compare VV with the frequency–volume chart; **record voiding position**.

*Cystometry (Filling Cystometrogram).*

- **Pressure–volume relationship of the bladder during filling**. Filling runs from the start of infusion to **"permission to void"**, which marks **maximum cystometric capacity (MCC)**.
- A single bladder line records **vesical pressure (Pves)** only, and cannot distinguish a detrusor rise from an **abdominal pressure (Pabd)** rise.
- **Recommended**: measure **both Pves and Pabd** (rectal or vaginal catheter) and derive the **detrusor pressure (Pdet)**:
    - **Pdet = Pves − Pabd** (Fig. 107.2).

*Pelvic Muscle Electromyography.*

- **Electromyography (EMG)** records potentials from depolarising muscle membranes — here the **striated perineal sphincter muscles** — during **filling and emptying**.
- **ICS standard: two skin-surface perineal electrodes with an appropriate reference**. Alternatives: vaginal probe, anal probe, perineal needle.

*Urethral Pressure Profilometry.*

- **Urethral pressure profilometry (UPP)**: intraluminal pressure along the urethra, recorded by **withdrawing a pressure sensor**.
- **Urethral pressure = fluid pressure needed just to open a closed urethra.**

*Pressure–Flow Studies.*

- **Pressure–flow studies (PFS)**: relationship between **Pdet and flow rate during voiding**, flow measured simultaneously by uroflowmeter.
- **Voiding phase**: from **permission to void** (or onset of uncontrollable voiding) until the **patient considers voiding finished**.

#### Figure Legends

- **Fig. 107.1 — Uroflow curves**: (A) **normal bell-shaped**; (B) **flattened — usually obstruction**; (C) **interrupted/straining** — impaired contractility, obstruction or abdominal straining. (Wein, English and Whitmore, 1988; Boone and Kim, 1998.)
- **Fig. 107.2 — Single-channel vs multichannel CMG**: (A) Pves alone shows multiple spikes whose origin cannot be determined; (B) adding Pabd shows the spikes were **abdominal (movement, coughing)** — the **subtracted Pdet is flat**. **Standard adult fill rates: 30–100 mL/min.** (Nitti, 1998.)

<p class="concept"><b>Concept —</b> <em>Non-invasive tests (PVR, uroflow) tell you <b>that</b> emptying is abnormal; only subtracted detrusor pressure tells you <b>why</b>. The whole architecture of multichannel UDS exists to separate the detrusor's contribution from the abdomen's.</em></p>
