Chapter 105 — Evaluation and Management of Women With Urinary Incontinence and Pelvic Organ Prolapse

Definition and Impact of Urinary Incontinence and Pelvic Organ Prolapse

Pelvic floor disorders.

  • Female pelvic floor disorders (PFDs) comprise urinary incontinence (UI), faecal incontinence (FI), pelvic organ prolapse (POP), sensory and emptying abnormalities of the lower urinary tract, and defecatory dysfunction.
  • About one woman in four has POP, UI or FI — UI the commonest (about one in six), then FI, then symptomatic POP.
  • Prevalence is predicted to rise because both drivers — ageing and obesity — are increasing in the population.

Urinary incontinence.

  • International Continence Society (ICS) definition: the complaint of involuntary loss of urine. Principal types: stress (SUI), urgency (UUI) and mixed (MUI); others are listed in Table 105.1.
  • Reported prevalence varies widely — about one-third of women over 60 worldwide on meta-analysis; up to four-fifths of frail elderly women; US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) cycles show a rising trend, to over half. Variation reflects lack of age standardisation, culture, willingness to report and inconsistent definitions.
  • Risk factors for all types: age, obesity, smoking, comorbidity and postmenopausal hormone therapy. Vaginal delivery → more SUI. Lower UUI with higher household income, physical activity and caesarean (not vaginal) delivery.
  • Pregnancy: UI rises from about one in ten in the first trimester to about one in three in the third.
  • Race: Asian American women have the lowest UI prevalence; White and Mexican-American women have more SUI than Black women; Black women have more UUI and detrusor overactivity (DO). Disparities in access to care probably distort these estimates.
  • Subtype order (current US data): isolated SUI > UUI > MUI. Earlier data ranked MUI second. SUI and MUI prevalence stable 2005–2018; UUI rising in women aged 60 and over.
  • Burden: direct costs in billions of dollars annually, rising as more women are treated; cumulative risk of SUI surgery 13.6% by age 80.
  • Functional impact: physical, social, psychological and sexual. Many women stop exercising because of UI → risk of diabetes and cardiovascular disease; UI impairs intimacy and leads to avoidance of intercourse.

Pelvic organ prolapse.

  • ICS definition: descent of one or more of the anterior vaginal wall, posterior vaginal wall, uterus and cervix, or vaginal vault (cuff after hysterectomy).
  • Commonest site: anterior wall > posterior wall > apex.
  • Anatomical vs clinical POP: about half of parous women have some degree of POP on examination, but the diagnosis requires symptoms attributable to it.
  • Symptoms: vaginal bulge, pelvic pressure, difficulty voiding or defecating, and splinting (hand or fingers in the vagina) for comfort or evacuation.
  • Vaginal bulge is the symptom most specific to POP and the one most likely to resolve after repair.
  • Symptomatic POP (asked as "Do you see or feel a bulge?") affects about 3% of US women, stable 2005–2010; highest in White women, lower in Hispanic, Black and Asian American women.
  • Cumulative risk of POP surgery 12.6% by age 80; direct surgical costs run to millions of dollars annually.

Knowledge and care-seeking.

  • Knowledge of UI and POP is poor among community-dwelling women, more so among non-White women → many suffer in silence.
  • Only 18–50% of women with PFDs seek care. Barriers are multifactorial; misbeliefs call for culturally competent education.
  • Costs, lost productivity, reduced quality of life (QoL) and inactivity-related illness → need for trained providers.

Diagnostic Evaluation

General Considerations

  • Patient-reported and QoL outcomes are now central; PFDs affect social, psychological, economic and sexual life, so treatment success is hard to define.
  • PFDs coexist and interact — POP may disturb voiding or defecation.
  • Women delay seeking care for 1 to 5 years or more after symptoms become bothersome and rarely volunteer them → ask proactively; a short validated screening questionnaire opens the discussion.
  • POP and UI are distinct diagnoses: patients may expect prolapse repair to cure leakage. Relief of bulge drives satisfaction after POP surgery → educate on anatomy and set expectations.
  • Inequities in care for minority women operate at patient, provider–patient and system levels; social determinants of health and systemic racism need evaluation.
  • Identify and quantify the UI type to target treatment; consider reversible causes — DIAPPERS (Table 105.2).
  • POP compartments: anterior (cystocele — bladder descent); posterior (rectocele — posterior wall protrudes); apical (uterine prolapse, vault, and enterocele — bowel at the vaginal apex).
  • Rectocele vs rectal prolapse: rectocele is posterior vaginal wall into the vagina; rectal prolapse is rectal mucosa out through the anus.
  • Evaluation → differential → shared decision-making; degree of bother balances risk against benefit, alongside the patient's goals.
  • Objective improvement does not always predict subjective improvement. Good communication improves satisfaction, adherence, emotional health, symptom resolution and pain control.

History

  • History is the foundation; women seeking help should be believed and their symptoms validated.
  • SUI is a urethral problem, UUI a bladder problem, MUI both; MUI may be stress- or urgency-predominant.
  • Accuracy of history: systematic reviews show a positive SUI history predicts urodynamic SUI in about three-quarters, but a negative history does not exclude it → examination, voiding diary or urodynamic studies (UDS) if the type is unclear.

History of Present Illness

  • Parameters: type, duration, severity, frequency of episodes, volume, bother, pad use, trajectory (worse, better, stable); alleviating and aggravating factors; prior treatments and results; treatment goals.
  • Typing questions: leakage with laughing, coughing or activity → SUI; leakage preceded by urgency or on the way to the toilet → UUI; both → MUI.
  • Bother is assessed per type — in MUI it decides which component is treated first.
  • Quantity: pads per day, pad size, containment, clothing changes, volume (drops → tablespoons → whole bladder). Pad weight testing is used mainly in trials and academic settings.
  • Voiding habits: hesitancy, incomplete emptying, stream strength, intermittency, straining, positional voiding, daytime and night-time frequency; urgency and frequency are the key lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) to characterise.
  • Onset: traumatic delivery; lifelong since childhood; peri- or postmenopause; after surgery; neurological disease. Urinary symptoms may precede a neurological diagnosis → screen for numbness, tingling, tremor, balance or coordination loss, and blurred or double vision.
  • QoL impact: patients modify lifestyle to avoid leakage; use questionnaires (see Questionnaires).
  • POP — bother is the key. Stage 1 (within the vagina) may be asymptomatic; symptoms usually begin when POP reaches or passes the hymen, where it can be seen and felt: stage 2 (−1 to +1 cm), stage 3 (>1 cm beyond), stage 4 (complete eversion).
  • POP is dynamic — worse later in the day and with activity.
  • POP symptoms: bulge, pressure, heaviness, "falling out". Splinting to void or defecate suggests high-grade POP; high-grade anterior POP can raise urethral resistance and impede voiding.
  • POP is associated with DO and overactive bladder (OAB); repair may improve OAB, but not in every patient → staged OAB treatment may be needed.
  • SUI coexists in about 40% of women with POP.
  • Posterior POP (rectocele): obstructed defecation, bulge, splinting for bowel movements, stool trapping, dyspareunia, poor self-image, reduced vaginal sensation, a sense of a wide hiatus or weakness, a ball interfering with activity.

Compassionate Care

  • Compassion improves outcomes and lowers costs — medical outcomes, parasympathetic responses, well-being, self-care, sense of purpose.
  • A strong doctor–patient relationship increases medication adherence.
  • Compassion need not take time: expressions lasting under 1 minute are meaningful — scripted reassurance by an oncologist reduced anxiety in women with breast cancer.
  • Validate and acknowledge concerns → patient-centred care.

Past Medical and Surgical History

  • Urological: infections, stones, haematuria, trauma, procedures (childhood and adult).
  • Neurological: neurological trauma, stroke, spinal cord injury or surgery, back pain or surgery, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson disease, multiple system atrophy, Huntington disease, myelodysplasia.
  • Medical: diabetes, thyroid disease, dementia; prior pelvic radiation.
  • Surgical: pelvic trauma or surgery (including prior UI or POP surgery, hysterectomy) may damage sphincter or bladder function; rectal resection (low anterior, abdominoperineal) and sacral lesion resection can injure pelvic nerves → urinary, bowel, pelvic and sexual dysfunction.
  • Obstetric and gynaecological: gravidity, number and mode of delivery, hormonal status (pre-, peri-, postmenopause; systemic or local hormone therapy; oral contraceptives; oophorectomy).
  • Obstetric risk factors for UI and POP: prolonged second stage, large fetal head circumference, high birth weight, forceps or vacuum delivery.

  • Pattern: every agent acts through one of four routes — outlet, detrusor, urine volume, or the patient's cognition and mobility.

Other Contributing Factors

  • Family history of POP → higher risk of POP and of recurrence; connective-tissue genetics (collagen, laminins, elastic fibres) implicated. Genetics also contribute to OAB, SUI and UUI.
  • Social history: smoking, substance use, sexual activity, partner, social support, physical activity, occupation.
  • Screen for pelvic trauma and abuse — trauma is common; asking creates a safe, healing environment (trauma-informed care).

Social Determinants of Health

  • Social determinants of health (SDOH) = conditions where people live and work that affect health and QoL; assess them in history-taking. They drive health disparities, which are a barrier to equity and an economic burden.

Key points

  • PFDs are common and rising; history and examination are central; bother guides therapy.
  • Adjuncts: UA, PVR, UDS, cystoscopy, imaging, questionnaires. Surgical and non-surgical options exist.
  • POP becomes symptomatic at or beyond the vaginal opening; screen for pelvic and sexual trauma.

Concept — the history sorts leakage into urethral (stress) versus bladder (urgency) failure and prolapse into anatomy versus bother; it is the patient's bother, not the finding, that decides what is treated and in what order.

Physical Examination

  • General: age, mobility and frailty inform performance status. Abdomen: habitus, incisions, hernias, organomegaly, bladder distension — relevant if abdominal surgery is considered.
  • Frailty predicts poor postoperative outcomes and complications in urological surgery → preoperative frailty assessment recommended at age ≥65.
  • Frailty is distinct from comorbidity: lack of resilience to external stressors, or decline in physiological reserve. No single standard definition or tool.

Physical Examination for Pelvic Organ Prolapse

  • Examine in lithotomy and standing (one foot on a step) to reproduce the greatest degree of prolapse the patient experiences.
  • Assess each compartment — anterior, posterior, apical (cervix or cuff) — and the perineal body, for position relative to the introitus and for laxity.

Split Graves speculum technique: one posterior blade retracts the posterior wall → examine anterior compartment → reposition blade anteriorly → examine posterior compartment → insert both blades (anterior and posterior) → isolate apex (cuff or cervix) → withdraw posterior blade slowly → examine posterior wall → with posterior blade in place, patient strains.

Prior mesh.

  • Prior polypropylene mesh or implant with pain or voiding symptoms → thorough history (device type, timing of symptom onset) and examination; some patients are asymptomatic.
  • Examine for presence, location and size of extrusion or exposure; document tenderness even without visible exposure.
  • Adjuncts: cystoscopy, digital rectal examination (DRE), translabial ultrasound (US; mesh relative to urethra and bladder), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).
  • Classify with the 2011 International Urogynecological Association (IUGA)/ICS joint terminology (Table 105.6).

  • Contrasts tested: prominence (no epithelial breach) vs separation (epithelial breach); extrusion (out through vaginal epithelium) vs perforation (into a viscus such as bladder or urethra); sinus tract = fistulous track without visible mesh.

Neurological examination.

  • Perform in any patient with known or suspected neurological disease: speech, gait, cognition, facial symmetry, lower-limb and perineal/perianal sensation, lower-limb power, vaginal and pelvic floor strength. Mobility and cognition govern timely toilet access.
  • Bulbocavernosus reflex (BCR) tests S2–S4; present in 70% of normal women vs 100% of normal men → an absent BCR in a woman is not by itself pathological.

Eliciting the BCR: squeeze the clitoris (or glans) → anal and pelvic floor contraction, seen or felt on DRE; alternatively, pull an indwelling catheter balloon against the bladder neck.

  • Anal sphincter tone (also S2–S4): ask the patient to tighten as if stopping the stream. Laxity = neurological deficit or poor voluntary muscle awareness.
  • Urethra: meatal stenosis, mass, tenderness, fullness, foreign body; expect postsurgical changes after anti-incontinence surgery or a mesh mid-urethral sling (MUS).

Trauma-Informed Care

  • Trauma-informed care = awareness of the patient's whole life situation; improves engagement and adherence. Recognise trauma in patients, families and staff; avoid retraumatisation; embed in policy.
  • Sexual abuse is common: about 1 in 4 women and 1 in 13 men in the USA experience child sexual abuse; rape-related post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) affects millions of American women.
  • Genital examination triggers flashbacks or anxiety in about half of trauma survivors.
  • Principles — safety, control and choice: patient chooses timing (now or later) and a support person; may self-insert or decline the speculum; clinician leaves the room while the patient undresses; privacy prioritised; expose only what is needed for the shortest time; discuss while the patient is dressed.

Chaperones During Physical Examinations

  • Role: comfort, safety, privacy and dignity for patient and provider; positioned to observe and assist; prevents misunderstandings. Supported by gowns, private undressing, sensitive draping, explanation.
  • American Medical Association (AMA) guidance — physicians should:
    • Adopt and communicate a policy that patients may request a chaperone; always honour the request.
    • Use an authorised member of the health-care team, bound by privacy and confidentiality.
    • Use a chaperone even when a trusted companion is present.
    • Offer private conversation without the chaperone; minimise sensitive history-taking during the chaperoned examination.
  • Patients and physicians differ on the role and choice of chaperone; offering one is respectful of privacy and autonomy.

Key points

  • Systematic genitourinary examination; POP supine and standing; SUI supine or standing.
  • Baden-Walker and POP-Q most used; neurological and rectal examination when indicated.
  • Trauma-informed principles; offer a chaperone.

Concept — the examination is designed to reproduce the patient's worst state — full bladder, standing, straining, prolapse reduced — because each omission (empty bladder, supine position, unreduced kinking prolapse) hides the pathology the patient actually lives with.

Supplemental Evaluation

  • Urinalysis (UA) and PVR should be performed in most patients evaluated for UI. No agreed standard exists for cystoscopy, UDS, imaging, diaries, pad tests or questionnaires → AUA/SUFU guidelines.
  • Weigh the value and accuracy (sensitivity, specificity) of any extra test against its cost and morbidity.

Symptom Quantification Instruments

Bladder Diary

  • Bladder diaries (voiding or micturition diaries, frequency–volume charts) quantify UI and are both diagnostic and therapeutic — patients gain insight into behaviours driving symptoms. Full discussion lies in the online chapter and Chapter 114.

Questionnaires

  • Severity alone does not show QoL impact; POP and UI are not life-threatening but can severely impair QoL → assess self-reported symptoms and their impact.
  • International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire (ICIQ): modular, validated, international standard for clinical and research use, in over 30 languages. Modules: UI short form (UI SF), bladder diary, male/female LUTS (MLUTS/FLUTS) and their sexual modules, nocturia (N), OAB, vaginal symptoms (VS); in development: cognitively impaired adults (Cog), underactive bladder (UAB), children (CLUTS).
  • ICIQ-UI SF correlates with 1-hour and 24-hour pad tests for SUI severity, and its change correlates with the Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I) after sling surgery.
  • Validated questionnaires are encouraged to standardise patient-reported outcomes; choose by clinical question. No single universal instrument, making comparison hard — but validated tools correlate well.

Nomograms and Risk Calculators

  • Risk models exist, though their use in practice is uncertain: postpartum UI (primiparous and multiparous); postpartum pelvic floor US predicting SUI; de novo SUI after POP surgery; long-term risk of POP, UI and FI (UR-CHOICE). Cleveland Clinic calculators (riskcalc.org) aid counselling.

Pad Tests

  • Non-invasive objective quantification of UI, used chiefly in trials. Protocols: 20-minute, standardised 1-hour, 24-hour, 48-hour — shorter tests use set activities but give a limited picture; longer tests are more reproducible but less complied with.
  • ICS: 3-day bladder diary and pad weight test may both be used in incontinence research. 24-hour testing suffices clinically; longer for research; 1-hour tests typically at initial evaluation.

ICS standardised 1-hour pad test: preweighed pad, no voiding → drink 500 mL sodium-free fluid in <15 min, then rest → walk 30 min including one flight of stairs up and down → stand from sitting ×10, cough vigorously ×10, run on the spot 1 min, bend to pick up an object ×5, wash hands in running water 1 min (adjusted to fitness) → weigh pad. If the patient must void, restart the test.

Urinalysis

  • UA is part of the diagnostic evaluation of LUTS and UI: haematuria, pyuria, glucosuria or proteinuria may indicate a secondary cause. Abnormal dipstick → microscopy or culture → further testing or treatment.

Postvoid Residual

  • PVR = urine remaining shortly after voiding; measures emptying. US preferred over catheterisation; portable bladder scanners are reliable, though devices vary.
  • Recommended by the European Association of Urology (EAU), ICS and AUA/SUFU — yet evidence linking PVR to dysfunction is weak:
    • Slow stream, straining and incomplete-emptying symptoms do not correlate with measured PVR.
    • Voiding dysfunction on UDS or uroflow may coexist with normal PVR; patients with and without LUTS have similar PVRs.
  • A single PVR is unreliable — repeat it. A single low PVR does not exclude incomplete emptying; a single high PVR does not predict emptying dysfunction.
  • No agreed cut-off — thresholds range 30–200 mL. In asymptomatic women almost all PVRs are <100 mL and few exceed 50 mL; defining normal in asymptomatic women may set cut-offs.
  • Elevated PVR does not identify the cause but warrants further evaluation and alters counselling before SUI procedures or drugs that reduce detrusor contractility for UUI; it may contribute to UTI, renal dysfunction and hydronephrosis.

Cystoscopy

  • Not routine in the index SUI patient; consider when an anatomical abnormality is suspected — urgency, haematuria, irritative LUTS.
  • After anti-incontinence surgery, mesh MUS or mesh reconstruction: look for foreign body (mesh, suture) in urethra or bladder. After pelvic radiation: radiation change.
  • Tumours, stones, cystitis and foreign bodies cause irritative LUTS, recurrent UTI and incontinence.
  • Survey: ureteric orifices (morphology, position, number, efflux); mucosa — trabeculation (outlet obstruction and/or DO) and oestrogen status; urethra — foreign body, stricture, diverticulum, fistula, position.
  • Yield in low-risk preoperative women is low (occasional early urothelial carcinoma, calculi, absent ureteric efflux) → cystoscopy not required before UI or POP surgery without another indication.

Key points

  • History alone may mislead; UA and PVR in most; diaries (diagnostic and therapeutic), pad tests and validated questionnaires quantify symptoms and QoL.

Concept — only UA and PVR are universal; every other test is earned by a departure from the index patient, and each must repay its cost and morbidity with information that changes management.

Radiographic Imaging

Voiding Cystourethrogram

  • Imaging is not needed in uncomplicated UI; image if renal damage or pelvic pathology is suspected.
  • Voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG): suspected vesicoureteric reflux (VUR) or urethral diverticulum; shows bladder contour and diverticula, bladder-neck configuration on voiding, bladder-base position on straining (cystocele grade), and fluoroscopically subtle leakage on cough or Valsalva.

Ultrasonography (Including Translabial Ultrasound to Visualise Mesh)

  • Renal US — non-invasive assessment of obstruction and hydronephrosis. Upper-tract imaging indications: chronic retention, severe POP (ureteric kinking → hydroureteronephrosis), suspected fistula, neurogenic detrusor dysfunction.
  • Bladder and urethral US: bladder wall thickness associates with OAB; in SUI, bladder-neck and urethral swing, bladder-neck funnelling, urethral position relative to the symphysis and hiatal area differ — role in management still unclear.
  • Translabial US for POP identifies involved structures (rectocele vs enterocele); three-dimensional translabial US detects levator ani defects with moderate-to-good agreement with MRI — defects associate with POP and recurrence. Translabial US ≠ pelvic US (uterus and adnexa).
  • Translabial US for mesh: the only modality that reliably images mesh and confirms the presence and location of a mesh MUS — proximity to urethra and bladder, proximal or distal position, path, contour, twisting, continuity after revision → plans revision or removal; helpful when mesh is impalpable or not seen cystoscopically. Office or theatre; distinct protocol.
  • Computed tomography (CT) and MRI are poor for mesh — seen only if infected, associated with other pathology, or inferred from surrounding structures.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

  • MRI gives soft-tissue detail of bladder neck, urethra, pelvic floor relaxation and prolapse.
  • MRI is the preferred, standard-of-care modality for urethral diverticulum. (Full discussion online and Chapter 122.)
  • Classic triad — the three Ds: dysuria, postvoid dribbling, dyspareunia — but often absent. Non-specific presentations: frequency, urgency, recurrent UTI, haematuria, tender mass, stones, purulent discharge, retention.
  • HMO system (H-line, M-line, organ prolapse) on a midsagittal image at maximal strain:
    • H-line = levator hiatus width: inferior pubic symphysis → posterior levator plate.
    • M-line = muscular floor relaxation: descent of the levator plate from the pubococcygeal line (inferior symphysis → first/second coccygeal junction).
    • O = organ prolapse beyond the H-line; POP on MRI = any organ beyond the H-line, in centimetres.
  • Dynamic MRI indications: complex patients, symptoms out of proportion to examination, prior repairs; with a POP protocol, confirms enterocele and rectocele and plans surgery.

Management

Urinary Incontinence Treatment Overview

  • Complete evaluation = history and examination, plus exclusion of UTI, outlet obstruction, stones, foreign body or tumour where suspected.
  • UI arises from the bladder, the outlet or both; treatment targets the cause, individualised to patient goals, risk–benefit and cost–benefit, with counselling to align them.
  • Full treatment discussion lies in the online chapter and Chapters 113–118.

Conclusions

  • Goal of reconstruction: restore normal anatomy and function of vagina, bladder and surrounding structures to improve QoL while minimising complications.
  • Pelvic floor care is refocusing on patient experience, goals and QoL and patient-centred outcomes research; the patient–surgeon relationship — open communication, trust, mutual respect — is pivotal.
