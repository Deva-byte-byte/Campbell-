Chapter 65 — Adolescent and Transitional Urology

What Constitutes Adolescent Urology?

  • Origin: the recognition that patients born with complex urogenital conditions need long-term adult care.
  • Evolution: from a stepwise paediatric → adolescent → adult sequence to seamless lifelong care. Adolescent urology is the bridge between paediatric and adult urology.
  • Adult reconstructive urologists can perform some of the operations but are underequipped to care for the whole patient, who brings:
    • multiple previous operations;
    • an anatomical, psychological and physiological landscape altered by the condition and prior surgery;
    • lifelong contact with health care, so patient and parents are often expert in the condition and unforgiving of a provider who is not;
    • expectations formed in a paediatric hospital, which must be managed for the adult relationship to succeed.
  • Many are underserved in adulthood. Clinical and inter-centre collaboration is needed to publish meaningful outcome data.
  • Role of the adolescent urologist: inform and recruit adult urologists into the field; publish and discuss outcomes with paediatric colleagues.

Concept — The gap is in whole-patient knowledge, not operative skill. Childhood reconstruction creates an adult whose anatomy, history and expectations differ from those of any other adult patient.

Definition of Transition

  • Got Transition definition: the move from a child/family-centred to an adult/patient-centred model of care, with or without a change of clinician. It has three parts: planning → transfer → integration.
  • Goals: (1) improve young people's ability to manage their own health care and use services effectively; (2) an organised process in both paediatric and adult practices covering preparation, transfer and integration.
  • In practice: independence in the care environment, education about the condition and its treatment, and navigation of adult care.
  • Transition vs transfer: transition is the whole process; transfer is one step within it and need not involve a new clinician.

Why Is Transition Needed?

  • Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) report: on first reading, transition care in its present form does not appear to make a significant difference.
  • But robust, reproducible metrics are lacking. Renal function and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) are expected to worsen with age, so stability is success, not improvement. Better metrics are needed.
  • Consistent finding: fewer patients are lost to follow-up when actively transitioned to a capable adult service.
  • Avoid the word "normal": the patient is their own baseline. Comparing an adult born with exstrophy to "normal" is meaningless and may stigmatise. Acknowledge the patient's wish for normality with sensitivity in order to gain trust.

Concept — The evidence for transition looks weak because the wrong endpoint is being measured. In progressive congenital disease, stability and retention in follow-up are the outcome.

Who Needs Transition?

  • Congenital and childhood-acquired conditions both carry risk from the condition or its prior treatment, so most patients need adult care.

What Happens When Transition Fails?

  • Begin discussions in early adolescence, individualised to the patient's developmental and physical needs.
  • Risks of non-transition:
    • a young adult who does not understand their condition or its treatment;
    • overbearing family control of an adult's health care;
    • loss to follow-up;
    • unrecognised complications and poor outcomes;
    • reliance on emergency care;
    • lack of social support (education, employment, housing, finance);
    • failure to recognise the patient's ability to achieve in society;
    • no access to peer and patient support groups.
  • Net result: non-adherence, more emergency department (ED) attendances and hospitalisations, higher morbidity and mortality, and family dissatisfaction.

Concept — Failed transition does its harm through loss of preventive surveillance. The patient reappears only through the emergency department, after complications that could have been prevented.

Stakeholders and Their Roles

Patient

  • The primary stakeholder. The patient must be medically and physically stable for handover, so avoid transition during a crisis such as deteriorating renal function or a need for urgent surgery.
  • Exception, pregnancy: obstetric care lies outside a paediatric hospital, so transfer is expedited, with co-management by paediatric and adult teams. This keeps the familiar team involved and shows the teams working together.

Parents

  • Handing decision-making to their child can overwhelm parents who have managed care since infancy, sometimes since before birth. They feel pride, a need to show trust, and fear of losing protection or of bad decisions. Separating from a trusted provider is itself a significant barrier.
  • Create private time for the patient alone, especially for sex, sexual function and sexual debut. Then ask whether the patient wants the conversation shared with their parents. These conversations are often omitted; in some cohorts most patients had never discussed sex with a provider.

Health Care Institutions

  • Access to adult care is difficult for young adults with complex chronic conditions. The conditions are rare and heterogeneous, so their financial benefit is hard to quantify. Show need through practice growth, and engage "allies": like-minded specialists, patient advocacy groups and the patients themselves.

When to Begin and Complete the Process

  • Begin: the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends about age 12. Content widens through the teens to cover diagnosis, past decisions, treatments and future considerations.
  • Practical skills: booking appointments themselves, navigating insurance, knowing whom to call, and what to do in an emergency. The most important element is teaching how and where to find valid information. Written questions and a portable health record help.
  • Complete: a paediatric setting becomes inappropriate for most patients by the late teens to early 20s. Age 26 is the commonly cited latest age for completion, being the upper age for cover on a parent's insurance plan in the United States.
  • Where independent living is not achievable (cognitive or physical limitation), the setting is a matter of judgement and needs ongoing reassessment. Plan medical custodianship (for example, a sibling as parents age or die) and involve future carers in discussions.

Multidisciplinary Care Models: Which Diagnoses Need Multidisciplinary Care?

  • The need for multidisciplinary care does not define the need for lifelong care. Single-system conditions (bladder exstrophy, severe hypospadias) may be managed by a urologist, but care depends on understanding their subtleties.
  • Adult urologists are more subspecialised (stones vs reconstruction vs fertility), so subspecialist support is common. Multidisciplinary surgery may be needed (gynaecology, colorectal, plastic surgery, urology).

Key Points
  • Preparation for transition should begin in early adolescence.
  • These are complex patients; many need multispecialty care.
  • Collaboration between paediatric and adult providers is vital.
  • Failed transition leads to negative health outcomes.
  • Transition websites and tools help a unit develop a good service.

Developmental Considerations

  • Factors in the decision to start treatment: provider advice, underlying risk (e.g. general anaesthesia), culture and religion, ease of access, and family resources (financial, intellectual, emotional). The urologist is often decisive.
  • Developmental paediatricians are usually excluded from timing decisions, although the emotional and psychological effects persist into adulthood. Urologists have therefore developed crude criteria. For example, augmentation cystoplasty is withheld until the child can help with catheterisation and says they want to be dry, a judgement left to surgeon and parents alone.
  • Relevance to the adult urologist: could surgery have been deferred until the patient could consent, and what is the risk of medical post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) from childhood treatment?

Development

  • Before the age of assent, two considerations apply: surgery can affect development, physiologically and/or psychologically; and its impact differs with life stage, so earlier intervention may cause less emotional or physical morbidity.
  • Weak evidence base: accepted beliefs about the timing of genital surgery (hypospadias repair, circumcision) rest on mid-20th-century psychoanalytic research, mainly observational narrative case histories.

Timing of Surgical Intervention

  • Near adolescence, major surgery becomes difficult when it depends on the adolescent's compliance.
  • Self-management has four components: medical adherence, medical responsibility, self-care skills, condition knowledge. Mastery commonly varies into the third decade, which raises the risk of a failed therapeutic relationship.
  • Emerging adults (18–25 years) are legal adults and make their own medical decisions unless specifically found to lack capacity.
  • Informed consent: the right to decide based on risks, benefits and alternatives. Emerging adults may legally give or withhold consent.
  • Capacity: may be lacking because of cognitive or developmental disability or incapacitating illness, in which case a parent or guardian is involved. More often, capacity is present but the processing skills for complex decisions are incomplete.
  • Spina bifida: usually normal intelligence and literacy, but more likely to have deficits in working memory and executive function, the domains critical to medical decision-making. A trusted urologist may be the only reliable bridge to safe decisions. Recruit extra resources, such as a nurse practitioner, to engage and consent these patients.

Medical PTSD and the Psychoemotional Impact of Chronic Care in Childhood

  • Definition: medical (iatrogenic) PTSD follows negative or traumatic experiences of medical care. Chronic childhood illness also has psychoemotional effects on the child and family.
  • Effects on young-adult decision-making:
    • distrust of providers and delay in seeking care;
    • challenging recommendations or seeking alternative treatments;
    • avoiding responsibility for their own health, having always had it managed by others;
    • anxiety, panic attacks and reliving past events, especially around invasive procedures.
  • Evidence: no English-language studies report the incidence or impact of medical PTSD in adults with congenital urological conditions. Congenital heart disease data suggest that risk relates to childhood surgical exposure, and that cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) with psychological support alongside medical treatment may reduce the long-term impact.

Concept — Childhood surgical timing has been governed by weak psychoanalytic theory and practical logistics rather than evidence. The adult urologist inherits the psychological consequences, so the recurring question is whether the decision could have waited for the patient's own consent.

The Practicalities: Setting Up a Service

Training

  • There is no defined training pathway. Paediatric urologists believe they are best suited to this care; adult reconstructive urologists believe the same of themselves.
  • Most providers train in both paediatric and reconstructive urology. Transitional sessions at urology conferences are increasing.

Institutional and Political Support

  • A dedicated clinic is impossible without institutional commitment. These patients need extra clinic time, resources and multispecialty visits, and are underserved under fee-for-service insurance.
  • Build the case with a business model (patient numbers and types, projected resource needs). Patient and family feedback to the institution is compelling.
