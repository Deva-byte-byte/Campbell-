## Q1. Describe in brief the anatomy of the retroperitoneum. Explain the principles of laparoscopic retroperitoneal surgery while mentioning the salient steps of retroperitoneal laparoscopic nephrectomy.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(related: 2019-Summer Paper 1, Q2; 2020-Summer Paper 1, Q3b)

### Anatomy: definition and boundaries
- The space behind the parietal peritoneum. **Anterior:** posterior peritoneal reflection; **posterior:** posterior abdominal wall; **cranial:** diaphragm; **caudal:** extraperitoneal pelvis.

| Group | Contents |
|---|---|
| Primarily retroperitoneal (formed there) | Kidneys, ureters, adrenals, aorta, IVC (inferior vena cava) |
| Secondarily retroperitoneal (mesentery fused to the wall) | Ascending and descending colon, duodenum D2–D3, pancreas (not the tail) |
| Vessels, lymphatics, nerves | Lumbar nodes, cisterna chyli, sympathetic trunks, autonomic plexuses, lumbosacral plexus |

### Posterior abdominal wall
- **Flank layers:** external oblique, internal oblique, transversus abdominis, transversalis fascia. **Posterior:** psoas, quadratus lumborum, erector spinae.
- **Lumbodorsal (thoracolumbar) fascia:** three layers that fuse into one at the **tip of the 12th rib**, the standard entry point for retroperitoneoscopy.
- **Ribs:** access above the 12th rib risks the pleura; the intercostal bundle runs under the rib above.

### Fasciae and spaces (three embryological strata)
| Space | Boundaries | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Anterior pararenal | Posterior peritoneum and anterior renal fascia | Colon, duodenum, pancreas |
| Perirenal | Anterior (Toldt) and posterior (Zuckerkandl) laminae of Gerota's fascia | Kidney, adrenal, perirenal fat, hilum, upper ureter, gonadal vessels |
| Posterior pararenal | Posterior lamina and transversalis fascia | Fat only |

- The laminae fuse laterally as the **lateroconal fascia**. The perirenal cone is **open inferiorly**, so urine, blood or pus track into the pelvis.
- Perirenal fat is fine and pale; pararenal fat is coarse and yellow-orange.

### Vessels, lymphatics and nerves
- **Aorta** enters at T12. Unpaired anterior branches supply the gut (coeliac, SMA (superior mesenteric artery), IMA (inferior mesenteric artery)); paired lateral branches supply adrenals, kidneys (L1–L2) and gonads; lumbar arteries go to the wall.
- At the hilum the **renal vein lies anterior to the artery**. The **left renal vein is long** (gonadal, adrenal and lumbar tributaries); the right is short.
- Lymph flows right to left; **cisterna chyli** at L1–L2.
- **Nerves at risk:** sympathetic trunk (medial to psoas), genitofemoral (on psoas), subcostal, iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal (on quadratus); hypogastric plexus (ejaculation).

### Principles of laparoscopic retroperitoneal surgery
1. **No natural cavity:** the working space must be created (finger and balloon dissection) before insufflation.
2. **Psoas is the key landmark:** it forms the floor. Keep it horizontal on screen; the kidney in Gerota's fascia lies anterior, and the peritoneum is pushed medially.
3. **Direct posterior route to the hilum:** the renal artery, posterior to the vein, is met first, allowing early arterial control.
4. **Peritoneum stays intact:** no bowel mobilisation or adhesiolysis; no spill of tumour, urine or pus into the peritoneum.
5. **Small space:** closely spaced ports, limited triangulation, lens smudging.
6. **Gas behaviour:** CO₂ (carbon dioxide) absorbs through fat, so hypercarbia and subcutaneous emphysema are commoner; haemodynamic swings are smaller than transperitoneal.

| Preferred for | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Adhesions, peritoneal dialysis, morbid obesity, pregnancy; posterior tumours, pyeloplasty, cyst surgery, renal biopsy | Small space, unfamiliar landmarks, harder suturing, learning curve |

- **Evidence:** randomised trials and meta-analysis: blood loss, complications and oncology similar to transperitoneal; the consistent advantage is **shorter operative time**.

### Salient steps: retroperitoneal laparoscopic nephrectomy
1. **Position:** full flank (90°), table flexed, axillary roll; surgeon behind the patient.
2. **Access (open Hasson):** 1.5–2.5-cm incision just below the 12th rib tip; split muscle, open the lumbodorsal fascia; finger confirms psoas behind and Gerota-covered lower pole in front.
3. **Space creation:** balloon dilator (commercial, or Gaur glove balloon with 600–800 mL saline) anterior to psoas, posterior to Gerota's fascia; blunt-tip balloon-cuff trocar seals the port.
4. **Ports:** two more trocars under vision, one off the 12th rib tip, one posterior and superior to the camera.
5. **Dissection:** along psoas, find the ureter and gonadal vein medially; lift ureter and lower pole to put the hilum on stretch; see arterial pulsation.
6. **Hilum:** clip or staple the **artery first**, then the vein; on the left a **lumbar vein** usually needs division.
7. **Mobilise:** upper pole (adrenal preserved or taken), then lateral and anterior attachments; radical = outside Gerota's fascia.
8. **Extract:** clip ureter, bag the specimen, remove via an extended port; close.
- **Problems:** peritoneal tear (space collapses), emphysema, pneumothorax, vascular injury, flank hernia (intramuscular balloon), rhabdomyolysis (about 1%). **Bail-out:** open the peritoneum and convert.

**Diagram to draw:** transverse section showing the three spaces, Gerota's laminae and the balloon anterior to psoas.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Three spaces: anterior pararenal, perirenal, posterior pararenal; perirenal cone open below.
> - Entry at the 12th rib tip, where the lumbodorsal fascia fuses.
> - Psoas is the landmark; artery met first.
> - Main advantage: shorter operative time, peritoneum untouched.

## Q2. Write a note on the embryological descent of the gonads, explaining the various phases of descent. Outline the evaluation and management of bilateral undescended testis in a 3 year old child.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in part in 2018-Summer Paper 3, Q3a, the treatment algorithm)

### Gonadal descent: starting point
- At **7–8 weeks** the gonad lies near the kidney (upper lumbar), held by the **cranial suspensory ligament (CSL)** above and the **gubernaculum** below. The gubernaculum appears at 7 weeks, attaches to the **epididymis** and runs to the future inguinal canal.
- **Female:** CSL persists; the gubernaculum becomes the ovarian and round ligaments; the ovary descends only into the pelvis.

### Phases of testicular descent (classic two-phase model)
| | Transabdominal | Inguinoscrotal |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Weeks 10–15 | From the 7th month; inguinal passage rarely before 22 weeks, most testes scrotal after 27 weeks |
| Events | **CSL regresses**; **gubernaculum swells** (outgrowth); its cord shortens, holding the testis at the internal ring while the kidney and abdomen grow away | Gubernaculum migrates **3–5 cm** to the scrotum; **processus vaginalis** forms within it; bulb then regresses; processus closes |
| Control | CSL regression by **androgen**; swelling by **INSL3 (insulin-like peptide 3)** from Leydig cells, via its receptor RXFP2 | **Androgen**, mainly indirectly via the **genitofemoral nerve (GFN)** releasing **CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide)**; intra-abdominal pressure |
| Failure gives | Intra-abdominal testis | Canalicular or ectopic testis |

- **Modern view:** the "transabdominal descent" is largely relative, not true migration. Barteczko and Jacob describe **five phases** (5 weeks to 28 weeks). The gubernaculum **swells to make room** (outgrowth), then **shrinks to let the testis pass** (regression). Testosterone (peak 14–16 weeks) and INSL3 (15–17 weeks) act together on gubernacular and cremaster growth.
- **Also know:** AMH (anti-Müllerian hormone) defects (persistent Müllerian duct syndrome) cause cryptorchidism; Insl3-knockout mice are cryptorchid; scrotum forms by 10–12 weeks under DHT (dihydrotestosterone).

| Failed step | Result |
|---|---|
| CSL retained | Intra-abdominal testis |
| Abnormal or ectopic gubernacular attachment | Canalicular testis; ectopic (superficial inguinal pouch, perineal, femoral, prepenile) |
| Processus not closed | Hernia, hydrocele |
| Fibrous remnant of processus | Acquired (ascending) testis |

### Bilateral undescended testis at 3 years: evaluation
- This is **late presentation**; surgery is advised from 6 months, preferably by 12 months and by 18 months at the latest.
- **History:** prematurity, testes once scrotal (acquired), hernia, family history, salt-wasting illness.
- **Examination:** warm room; supine, then cross-legged or squatting. Sweep from the anterior superior iliac spine down the canal to find the lowest position; check the superficial inguinal pouch. **Retractile** testis stays in the scrotum after gentle traction. Note penis size, meatus (hypospadias), scrotal development, syndromic features.
- **Bilateral palpable:** no investigations needed; plan orchidopexy.
- **Bilateral non-palpable (or with hypospadias or micropenis): treat as DSD (difference of sex development) until proven otherwise.**
  - **Karyotype**; electrolytes and **17-OHP (17-hydroxyprogesterone)** to exclude **CAH (congenital adrenal hyperplasia)** in a virilised 46,XX child.
  - **Hormones:** LH (luteinising hormone), FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone), testosterone, **AMH and inhibin B**. Measurable AMH or inhibin B confirms testicular tissue; high gonadotrophins with absent AMH suggests **anorchia**. The **hCG (human chorionic gonadotrophin) stimulation test** (testosterone rise) is now less used.
  - Pelvic ultrasound for Müllerian structures.
- **Imaging to localise the testis is not recommended:** ultrasound sensitivity about 45%; it never removes the need for surgery.

### Management
- **No hormonal therapy to induce descent:** LHRH (luteinising hormone-releasing hormone) works in about 20%; hCG may increase germ-cell apoptosis. Adjuvant LHRH (buserelin) in bilateral cases is experimental.
- **Palpable:** inguinal orchidopexy (free the cord, ligate the patent processus, retroperitoneal mobilisation, Prentiss manoeuvre if needed, **subdartos pouch**) or scrotal (Bianchi) orchidopexy for low testes. Both sides usually in one sitting.
- **Non-palpable:** examination under anaesthesia, then **diagnostic laparoscopy**:
```
Laparoscopy
 ├─ Testis seen → laparoscopic orchidopexy
 │     vessels short (testis not within 2–4 cm of ring) → Fowler–Stephens
 │     (clip testicular vessels; testis lives on vasal collaterals;
 │      one-stage, or two-stage with second stage at 6 months) or Shehata traction
 ├─ Vessels and vas enter ring → inguinal exploration, excise nubbin
 └─ Blind-ending vessels → stop (vanished testis)
```
- **Both testes very high or of doubtful viability:** stage the sides, the second at 6 months, to avoid bilateral atrophy. Primary orchidopexy (>90% success) beats Fowler–Stephens (60–97%).
- **Biopsy** only if DSD or dysgenesis is suspected. **Bilateral anorchia:** testosterone at puberty, prostheses later.

### Follow-up and prognosis
- Check position and size at 6 months.
- **Fertility:** worse when bilateral (oligospermia 42%, azoospermia 31% in one large review).
- **Cancer:** about **3-fold** risk; teach self-examination after puberty.

**Diagram to draw:** sagittal fetus showing CSL, gubernaculum, processus vaginalis and both phases.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Transabdominal: CSL regression plus INSL3-driven gubernacular swelling.
> - Inguinoscrotal: androgen via genitofemoral nerve and CGRP.
> - Bilateral non-palpable = DSD work-up first (karyotype, 17-OHP, AMH).
> - Laparoscopy decides; no imaging, no hormones.

## Q3a. Recist Criteria.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and evolution
- **RECIST (Response Evaluation Criteria In Solid Tumours):** standard imaging rules for tumour response and progression.
- **WHO (World Health Organization) 1981** (bidimensional) → **RECIST 2000** (unidimensional) → **RECIST 1.1 (2009)**, now standard. Used in trials for metastatic RCC (renal cell carcinoma), urothelial and prostate cancer.

### Lesions at baseline (RECIST 1.1)
| Category | Definition |
|---|---|
| Measurable lesion | Longest diameter **≥10 mm on CT (computed tomography)**, ≥20 mm on chest X-ray, ≥10 mm with callipers |
| Measurable node | **≥15 mm short axis**; 10–15 mm pathological but non-measurable; <10 mm normal |
| Non-measurable | Small lesions, blastic bone metastases, effusions, ascites |
| Target lesions | Up to **5 (maximum 2 per organ)**; largest, reproducible; **sum of diameters** recorded |
| Non-target | All other lesions, recorded as present or absent |

### Response categories
| Response | Target lesions |
|---|---|
| **CR** (complete response) | All disappear; nodes <10 mm |
| **PR** (partial response) | **≥30% fall** in sum versus **baseline** |
| **PD** (progressive disease) | **≥20% rise** versus **nadir** and **≥5 mm absolute**, or any **new lesion** |
| **SD** (stable disease) | Neither PR nor PD |

- **Overall response** combines target, non-target and new lesions; confirm response at ≥4 weeks in single-arm trials.
- **End points:** ORR (objective response rate = CR + PR), PFS (progression-free survival), duration of response.

### Limitations
- Size only, ignoring necrosis (TKIs (tyrosine kinase inhibitors)); bone poorly assessed; observer variability.

### Modifications
| Criteria | Use |
|---|---|
| **iRECIST (2017)** | Immunotherapy: first progression is "unconfirmed", confirmed only on the next scan, allowing for **pseudoprogression** |
| **Choi** | Density fall counts as response |
| **PCWG3 (Prostate Cancer Working Group 3)** | Bone: 12-week scan is the new baseline (flare), then **≥2 new lesions** = progression; PSA (prostate-specific antigen) progression ≥25% and ≥2 ng/mL above nadir; soft tissue by RECIST |
| PERCIST | PET (positron emission tomography) metabolic response |

**Diagram to draw:** number line of change in sum: −30% (PR), +20% (PD).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - RECIST 1.1: ≥10 mm lesion, node ≥15 mm short axis; 5 targets, 2 per organ.
> - PR ≥30% fall from baseline; PD ≥20% and ≥5 mm rise from nadir, or new lesion.
> - iRECIST for immunotherapy; PCWG3 for prostate.

## Q3b. Outline algorithm for difficult catheter placement.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- **Normal calibre:** meatus 24 Fr (narrowest), bladder neck 28 Fr, prostatic urethra 32 Fr.
- **Causes:** BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia, commonest), urethral stricture, false passage, meatal stenosis, bladder neck contracture, urethral trauma, abnormal anatomy.
- **Golden rule:** escalate **blind → wire → vision → suprapubic**; never repeat forceful blind passage.

### Before starting
- History (surgery, radiotherapy, AUS (artificial urinary sphincter), reconstruction), DRE (digital rectal examination).
- **Absolute contraindication:** suspected urethral injury (pelvic fracture, blood at meatus, high-riding prostate) → **RUG (retrograde urethrogram)**; pelvic fracture urethral injury → suprapubic tube.
- **Technique:** 10–15 mL lubricant (lidocaine gel), penis stretched, steady pressure; inflate only after urine flows at the hub.

### Algorithm (Campbell Fig. 15.6)
```
First attempt fails
 ├─ Prostatic obstruction suspected
 │     → 18-Fr coudé, tip (marker) pointing up
 │     → fails → flexible cystoscopy
 └─ Not suspected (stricture likely)
       → smaller catheter
       → fails → hydrophilic glidewire
             → fails → flexible cystoscopy
At cystoscopy / with wire in bladder:
 ├─ Stricture → wire across → 5-Fr catheter, aspirate urine to confirm
 │      → super-stiff wire → serial dilatation (1 Fr above catheter size)
 │      → Councill (open-tip) catheter over wire
 └─ False passage → pass under vision (scope anterior)
        → wire → Councill over wire
Wire cannot be passed / dense stricture / urethral disruption
 → suprapubic catheter (ultrasound-guided percutaneous, or open)
```
- **Why 18-Fr coudé:** stiffer, larger tip area, curved tip follows the bulbar-prostatic angle; 14–16 Fr usually buckle.
- **Blood on the tip** suggests false passage: stop blind attempts.

### Special situations
| Situation | Approach |
|---|---|
| AUS | Deactivate; smallest catheter 12–14 Fr; remove early |
| Obese or female | Exposure: Trendelenburg, retract pannus, two fingers in vagina to guide |
| Buried penis | Infrapubic pressure; flexible cystoscopy |
| Neobladder, continent pouch | Never force; scope + wire; suprapubic only with imaging guidance |
| PUV (posterior urethral valves) neonate | 6–8 Fr, or 5-Fr open-ended over glidewire; else suprapubic or vesicostomy |

**Also know:** filiforms, balloon dilators; complications: false passage, haematuria, stricture.

**Diagram to draw:** coudé catheter following the bulbar-prostatic curve with tip up.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Blind → wire → vision → suprapubic.
> - BPH: 18-Fr coudé, tip up.
> - Councill catheter over wire after dilatation.
> - Suspected urethral injury: RUG first, no blind catheter.

## Q3c. Chronic Prostatitis : diagnosis, classification and management.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2018-Summer Paper 4, Q3d)

### Classification: NIH (National Institutes of Health), 1999
| Category | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Acute bacterial prostatitis |
| II | **Chronic bacterial prostatitis (CBP)**: recurrent UTI (urinary tract infection), **same organism**; well between episodes |
| IIIA | **CP/CPPS (chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome)**, inflammatory: leucocytes in EPS (expressed prostatic secretion), VB3 (post-massage urine) or semen |
| IIIB | CP/CPPS, non-inflammatory (split has no treatment value) |
| IV | Asymptomatic inflammatory (incidental) |

- **CP/CPPS:** pelvic pain **≥3 of the last 6 months**, no uropathogens; 90–95% of cases. Infection, neurogenic inflammation, **pelvic floor dysfunction**, stress and **central sensitisation** interact.

### Diagnosis
- **Symptoms:** perineal (commonest), testicular, suprapubic, penile or ejaculatory pain; LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms); sexual dysfunction.
- **NIH-CPSI (Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index):** pain, urinary and quality-of-life domains.
- **DRE (digital rectal examination):** prostate tender in under half; palpate pelvic floor for **trigger points**.
- **Localisation tests:**
  - **Meares–Stamey 4-glass:** VB1 (urethra), VB2 (bladder), EPS, VB3 (prostate).
  - **2-glass** (pre- and post-massage urine): matches 4-glass in >95%.
- Urinalysis, culture, PVR (post-void residual), flow; cytology if irritative symptoms; cystoscopy if low flow or bladder pain.
- **UPOINT phenotyping:** Urinary, Psychosocial, Organ-specific, Infection, Neurological/systemic, Tenderness of muscles.

### Management
**Category II (CBP):** fluoroquinolone (lipid-soluble zwitterion, enters prostate) for **4–6 weeks** (EAU (European Association of Urology)); Chlamydia: azithromycin; resistant: TMP-SMX (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) 3 months; refractory: suppression or TURP (transurethral resection of prostate).

**Category III: multimodal, UPOINT-directed** (monotherapy fails)
| Domain | Therapy |
|---|---|
| Urinary | α-blocker if recent onset and α-blocker naive |
| Psychosocial | CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy), stress reduction |
| Organ | 5-ARI (5α-reductase inhibitor) if BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia); quercetin |
| Infection | Quinolone ≥6 weeks only if treatment-naive, <1 year; no repeat courses |
| Neurological | Pregabalin, amitriptyline, duloxetine |
| Tenderness | **Pelvic floor physiotherapy, myofascial release**, biofeedback |

- **Others:** acupuncture (61.5% vs 38.3% response with sham), aerobic exercise, PTNS (percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation). **Avoid** TURP or prostatectomy for pain.

**Diagram to draw:** UPOINT snowflake.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - NIH I–IV; III = pain ≥3 of 6 months, no infection.
> - CBP: same organism, fluoroquinolone 4–6 weeks.
> - CP/CPPS: NIH-CPSI to score, UPOINT to treat.

## Q3d. Multiparametric MRI : its components and interpretation for prostate cancer.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- **mpMRI (multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging):** anatomical plus functional sequences, usually at 3 T without endorectal coil. Reported by **PI-RADS v2.1 (Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System, 2019)**.

### Components
| Sequence | Principle | Cancer appearance |
|---|---|---|
| **T2W (T2-weighted)**, triplanar | Anatomy; normal PZ (peripheral zone) bright | Low-signal lesion; "erased charcoal" in TZ (transition zone); shows EPE (extraprostatic extension) and SVI (seminal vesicle invasion) |
| **DWI (diffusion-weighted imaging)** with **ADC (apparent diffusion coefficient)** map; high b-value (≥1400) | Cellular tumour restricts water movement | Bright on high-b DWI, **dark on ADC**; ADC falls as Gleason rises |
| **DCE (dynamic contrast-enhanced)** T1 with gadolinium | Angiogenesis: early enhancement and washout | Early focal enhancement (type 3 curve) |
- **Biparametric MRI (T2W + DWI):** no contrast, cheaper, similar detection; poorer staging.
- **PI-QUAL (Prostate Imaging Quality):** quality score (1–5) before reporting.

### Interpretation: PI-RADS v2.1
| Score | Clinically significant cancer |
|---|---|
| 1 | Highly unlikely |
| 2 | Unlikely |
| 3 | Equivocal |
| 4 | Likely; lesion <1.5 cm |
| 5 | Highly likely; ≥1.5 cm or definite EPE |

| Zone | Dominant sequence | Tie-breaker |
|---|---|---|
| Peripheral | **DWI** | DWI 3 + DCE positive → 4 |
| Transition | **T2W** | T2W 3 + DWI 5 → 4; T2W 2 + DWI ≥4 → 3 |
- **DCE only upgrades PZ 3 to 4**; it never changes 4–5 or TZ scores.
- Cancer in about one-third of PI-RADS 3, over half of 4, 90% of 5.
- **PRECISE** score (1–5) tracks change during active surveillance.

### Clinical use and evidence
- **PROMIS (2017):** sensitivity 93% vs 48% for TRUS (transrectal ultrasound) biopsy; 27% could avoid biopsy.
- **PRECISION (2018):** MRI pathway with targeted biopsy of PI-RADS ≥3 detected more significant cancer (38% vs 26%), 28% avoided biopsy.
- EAU (European Association of Urology) and AUA (American Urological Association): MRI **before biopsy**; also staging, active surveillance, focal therapy planning.
- Limitations: low specificity (Cochrane 0.37); reader-dependent.

**Diagram to draw:** prostate zones with a PZ lesion dark on ADC.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - T2W anatomy, DWI/ADC cellularity, DCE vascularity.
> - PZ ruled by DWI, TZ by T2W; DCE only lifts PZ 3 to 4.
> - PRECISION and PROMIS: MRI before biopsy.

## Q3e. Indications for outpatient cystourethroscopy.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- **Flexible cystourethroscopy** under local anaesthesia is the **commonest office procedure** in urology; no lithotomy needed.
- **Prerequisites:** consent; treat any UTI (urinary tract infection) first; lidocaine gel with **≥5 minutes dwell** (ideally 10–20); no antibiotic prophylaxis unless risk factors (age, diabetes, immunosuppression, catheters, prior UTI).

### Diagnostic indications
| Indication | Comment |
|---|---|
| **Haematuria** | All **gross haematuria** (with CT (computed tomography) urography, cytology). Microhaematuria by AUA/SUFU (American Urological Association/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction) risk: intermediate and high risk; shared decision in low risk |
| **Bladder cancer surveillance** | NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer): first check at 3 months; low risk then yearly; high risk 3–4-monthly for 2 years; also upper-tract follow-up of UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma) |
| Recurrent UTI | Look for stone, diverticulum, stricture, fistula, foreign body |
| LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) | Not routine; before invasive BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) treatment (median lobe before urethral lift, bladder-neck contracture, stricture) |
| Pain syndromes | IC/BPS (interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome): **Hunner lesion**; chronic pelvic pain with bladder pain or low flow |
| Women | Suspected urethral diverticulum, vesicovaginal fistula, mesh or suture erosion after sling, recurrent UTI after surgery |
| Others | Urethral stricture assessment and follow-up; abnormal urine cytology; radiation cystitis; foreign body |

### Therapeutic indications
- **Removal or exchange of ureteric stents** (exchange with fluoroscopy).
- **Biopsy and fulguration** of small (≤1 cm) low-grade recurrences; office laser ablation.
- **Intradetrusor botulinum toxin** injection (OAB (overactive bladder), neurogenic detrusor overactivity).
- Urethral bulking agents; stricture dilatation; small stones or foreign bodies; blue-light cystoscopy where available.

### When not indicated or done in theatre
- Not needed for the index stress incontinence patient, initial OAB or uncomplicated LUTS.
- **No surveillance cystoscopy** in chronic indwelling catheters (AUA/SUFU 2021).
- Theatre if TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour) needed, poor tolerance, children, or complex diversions.

**Diagram to draw:** haematuria risk-group flowchart.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Haematuria and NMIBC surveillance are the two commonest indications.
> - Flexible scope, lidocaine dwell ≥5 minutes, no routine prophylaxis.
> - Therapeutic: stent removal, botulinum toxin, fulguration of small recurrences.

## Q3f. Prone vs Supine PCNL: Indications, positioning, technique, specific complications, advantages and disadvantages of each.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(mini-PCNL: see 2022-Summer Paper 1, Q1)

### Indications for PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy)
- Renal stones **>2 cm**, staghorn, lower pole >1 cm, calyceal diverticulum, failed retrograde route or urinary diversion, UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) obstruction.

### Comparison
| Feature | Prone (Goodwin, 1955) | Supine (Valdivia-Uría, 1987) |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning | Lithotomy for ureteric catheter, then turned prone; chest and pelvic bolsters (horizontal bring kidney down more); arms ≤90°, face and knees padded | Ipsilateral flank at table edge on a bolster, arm across chest; **Galdakao-modified (Scoffone)**: legs in lithotomy for simultaneous retrograde access |
| Best suited | Standard anatomy; **upper pole and posterior calyx** access; multiple tracts | Obese, cardiopulmonary disease, kyphosis; ECIRS (endoscopic combined intrarenal surgery); ileal conduit or continent channel |
| Technique | Posterior calyx puncture under fluoroscopy or ultrasound; tract more vertical; large working area | Puncture in posterior axillary line; tract more horizontal and longer; kidney more mobile; air outlines anterior calyces |
| Drainage | Against gravity; higher pelvic pressure | **Gravity drains fragments**, low intrarenal pressure |
| Anaesthesia | Airway access difficult; turning needed | **Airway retained**; no repositioning |

### Specific complications
- **Prone:** ocular injury, neuropraxia (brachial plexus), pressure injury, airway tube dislodgement while turning.
- **Supine:** **colon injury** (if puncture too anterior), access failure to upper pole, kidney displacement during dilatation.
- **Both:** bleeding (transfusion now about 1%), pleural injury above the 12th rib, sepsis, bowel, spleen or liver injury.

### Advantages and disadvantages
| | Prone | Supine |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | Wide working area, easy upper pole access, familiar | Single position, shorter time, ECIRS, gravity clearance, safer anaesthesia |
| Disadvantages | Repositioning, positioning injuries, needs split-leg table for ECIRS | Smaller working area, collapsed system, longer tract, harder upper pole |

- **Evidence:** stone-free rates and complications similar; supine often shortens operating time; choice is mainly surgeon preference.

**Diagram to draw:** cross-section showing prone (vertical) and supine (horizontal) tracts.

> **Key points to remember:**

## Rapid Revision Sheet – 2024-Winter Paper 1

*All the key points from this paper in one place. Read this in the last 30 minutes before the exam.*


#### Q1. Describe in brief the anatomy of the retroperitoneum. Explain the principles of laparoscopic retroperitoneal surgery while mentioning the salient steps of retroperitoneal laparoscopic nephrectomy. (LAQ)

- Three spaces: anterior pararenal, perirenal, posterior pararenal; perirenal cone open below.
- Entry at the 12th rib tip, where the lumbodorsal fascia fuses.
- Psoas is the landmark; artery met first.
- Main advantage: shorter operative time, peritoneum untouched.

#### Q2. Write a note on the embryological descent of the gonads, explaining the various phases of descent. Outline the evaluation and management of bilateral undescended testis in a 3 year old child. (LAQ)

- Transabdominal: CSL regression plus INSL3-driven gubernacular swelling.
- Inguinoscrotal: androgen via genitofemoral nerve and CGRP.
- Bilateral non-palpable = DSD work-up first (karyotype, 17-OHP, AMH).
- Laparoscopy decides; no imaging, no hormones.

#### Q3a. Recist Criteria. (SAQ)

- RECIST 1.1: ≥10 mm lesion, node ≥15 mm short axis; 5 targets, 2 per organ.
- PR ≥30% fall from baseline; PD ≥20% and ≥5 mm rise from nadir, or new lesion.
- iRECIST for immunotherapy; PCWG3 for prostate.

#### Q3b. Outline algorithm for difficult catheter placement. (SAQ)

- Blind → wire → vision → suprapubic.
- BPH: 18-Fr coudé, tip up.
- Councill catheter over wire after dilatation.
- Suspected urethral injury: RUG first, no blind catheter.

#### Q3c. Chronic Prostatitis : diagnosis, classification and management. (SAQ)

- NIH I–IV; III = pain ≥3 of 6 months, no infection.
- CBP: same organism, fluoroquinolone 4–6 weeks.
- CP/CPPS: NIH-CPSI to score, UPOINT to treat.

#### Q3d. Multiparametric MRI : its components and interpretation for prostate cancer. (SAQ)

- T2W anatomy, DWI/ADC cellularity, DCE vascularity.
- PZ ruled by DWI, TZ by T2W; DCE only lifts PZ 3 to 4.
- PRECISION and PROMIS: MRI before biopsy.

#### Q3e. Indications for outpatient cystourethroscopy. (SAQ)

- Haematuria and NMIBC surveillance are the two commonest indications.
- Flexible scope, lidocaine dwell ≥5 minutes, no routine prophylaxis.
- Therapeutic: stent removal, botulinum toxin, fulguration of small recurrences.

#### Q3f. Prone vs Supine PCNL: Indications, positioning, technique, specific complications, advantages and disadvantages of each. (SAQ)

- Prone: posterior and upper pole access, big working space.
- Supine: airway, ECIRS, gravity drainage, no turning.
- Similar outcomes; choose by anatomy and experience.
