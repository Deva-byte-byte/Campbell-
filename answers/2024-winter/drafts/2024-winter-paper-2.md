## Q1. Pathophysiology, classification and management of LUTS.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(medical and surgical management of BPH also answered in 2015-Summer Paper 2, Q1)

### Definition
- **LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms):** symptoms of the storage, voiding and post-micturition phases; not disease-specific; up to half of older men.
- **BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia)** is histology; BPE is enlargement; BPO is prostatic obstruction; **BOO (bladder outlet obstruction)** has any cause. Not every man with LUTS has BPO.

### Classification
**1. By phase (ICS, International Continence Society)**

| Storage | Voiding | Post-micturition |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency, urgency, **nocturia** (waking ≥1 time to void), urgency incontinence | Weak or split stream, hesitancy, intermittency, straining, terminal dribble | Feeling of incomplete emptying, post-micturition dribble |

- Storage symptoms form **OAB (overactive bladder)**: urgency ± urgency incontinence, usually with frequency and nocturia; most bothersome.

**2. Functional (Wein)**

| | Because of the bladder | Because of the outlet |
|---|---|---|
| Failure to store | DO (detrusor overactivity), low compliance, hypersensitivity | Sphincter weakness (SUI, stress urinary incontinence) |
| Failure to empty | DUA (detrusor underactivity) | BPO, stricture, bladder neck contracture, sphincter dyssynergia |

**3. Severity:** IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score), 7 questions scored 0–5 plus a quality-of-life question: mild 0–7, moderate 8–19, severe 20–35.

**4. By cause:** prostate, bladder, urethra, neurological, polyuria, infection, stone, tumour.

### Pathophysiology
- **Prostate:** BPH is an increase in cell number of stroma and glands in the **transition zone** and periurethral region. **Type 2 5α-reductase** in stromal fibroblasts makes **DHT (dihydrotestosterone)**; androgens are permissive, not mitogenic. Ageing, inflammation and metabolic syndrome contribute.
- **Static component:** gland bulk; the **capsule** transmits expansion to the urethra (TUIP, transurethral incision of the prostate, relieves obstruction without removing tissue).
- **Dynamic component:** **α1A-adrenergic** smooth-muscle tone; the basis of α-blockers.
- Size does not correlate with symptoms or obstruction.
- **Bladder response:** hypertrophy → compensation → decompensation. Collagen causes trabeculation and low compliance; DO causes storage symptoms; late detrusor failure causes retention. One-third keep storage symptoms after surgery.
- **Also know:** nocturnal polyuria (night output >33% of 24 h over age 65); BPO complications: AUR (acute urinary retention), UTI (urinary tract infection), stones, haematuria, renal failure.

### Evaluation (EAU, European Association of Urology, men ≥40)
- **Basic:** history (comorbidity, drugs, sexual function), IPSS, DRE (digital rectal examination) and neurological check, **urinalysis (mandatory)**, PVR (post-void residual), PSA (prostate-specific antigen) if life expectancy >10 years.
- **Frequency–volume chart, 3 days:** for storage symptoms or nocturia.
- **Before treatment:** uroflowmetry (Qmax <10 mL/s suggests BOO); ultrasound for volume and median lobe.
- **Selected:** creatinine and renal ultrasound if large PVR; cystoscopy for haematuria, stricture or before PUL (prostatic urethral lift).
- **Pressure–flow study** (separates BOO from DUA) before surgery if voided volume <150 mL, PVR >300 mL, age <50 or >80, or failed surgery.

### Management: EAU algorithm
```
Bothersome LUTS?  No  -> watchful waiting, lifestyle advice
Absolute surgical indication? Yes -> surgery
Otherwise:
  Nocturnal polyuria      -> lifestyle +/- desmopressin
  Storage predominant     -> antimuscarinic or beta3-agonist
  Prostate >40 mL         -> 5ARI +/- alpha1-blocker
  Others                  -> alpha1-blocker or PDE5I
  Residual storage        -> add antimuscarinic or beta3-agonist
Failure or complication   -> surgery
```
- **Watchful waiting:** education, fluid 1.5–2 L/day (none 2 h before bed), less caffeine and alcohol, double voiding, bladder training; yearly review.

| Drug | Example | Mechanism and notes |
|---|---|---|
| α1-blocker | Tamsulosin 0.4 mg, silodosin 8 mg, alfuzosin 10 mg | Relax prostatic smooth muscle; acts in days; dizziness, ejaculatory dysfunction, IFIS (intraoperative floppy iris syndrome) |
| 5ARI (5α-reductase inhibitor) | Finasteride 5 mg, dutasteride 0.5 mg | Lower DHT; volume falls about 20% over 6–12 months; **PSA halves**; reduces AUR and surgery |
| Antimuscarinic | Solifenacin, tolterodine | Block detrusor M3 receptors; for storage symptoms if PVR <200 mL |
| β3-agonist | Mirabegron 50 mg, vibegron | Relax detrusor during storage |
| PDE5I (phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor) | Tadalafil 5 mg daily | Smooth-muscle relaxation; useful with ED (erectile dysfunction) |

- **MTOPS:** progression cut by doxazosin 39%, finasteride 34%, combination 66%; **CombAT** confirmed. Combine when volume >30–40 mL and PSA >1.5 ng/mL.

### Surgery
- **Indications:** refractory retention, recurrent UTI, bladder stones, recurrent haematuria, BPO-related renal insufficiency; failed medical therapy.

| Prostate volume | Options |
|---|---|
| <30 mL | TUIP, TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate) |
| 30–80 mL | **TURP (reference standard)**, bipolar TURP, laser vaporisation (PVP), enucleation, PUL, Rezum (water vapour), Aquablation |
| >80 mL | **HoLEP (holmium laser enucleation)** or ThuLEP, open or robotic simple prostatectomy |

- Ejaculation preserved: PUL, Rezum. Anticoagulated: PVP, HoLEP. PAE (prostatic artery embolisation) in selected men.
- **TURP complications:** bleeding, **TUR syndrome** (hyponatraemia with glycine; avoided by bipolar saline), retrograde ejaculation, stricture, incontinence.
- **Also know:** iTind; female LUTS follow OAB and SUI guidelines.

**Diagram to draw:** BPH → BPE → BPO → BOO → bladder changes → LUTS.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - ICS: storage, voiding, post-micturition; IPSS 0–7, 8–19, 20–35.
> - Static (bulk, capsule) plus dynamic (α1A) obstruction; size ≠ symptoms.
> - 5ARI for >40 mL; MTOPS combination cut progression 66%.
> - TURP 30–80 mL; HoLEP or simple prostatectomy for >80 mL.

## Q2. Etiology, pathogenesis and management of acute renal failure.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(acute tubular necrosis also answered in 2022-Summer Paper 2, Q3c)

*Acute renal failure is now called AKI (acute kidney injury).*

### Definition and staging: KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes)
- AKI = rise in serum creatinine **or** fall in urine output; either alone is enough.

| Stage | Serum creatinine | Urine output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rise ≥0.3 mg/dL in 48 h, or ×1.5 baseline in 7 days | <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 h |
| 2 | ×2 baseline | <0.5 mL/kg/h for 12 h |
| 3 | ×3, or ≥4 mg/dL, or RRT (renal replacement therapy) | <0.3 mL/kg/h for 24 h, or anuria 12 h |

- One-fifth to one-third of inpatients; mortality about 25%, about 50% if RRT is needed.

### Etiology
| Type | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Prerenal** | Hypovolaemia (bleeding, vomiting, burns), heart failure, sepsis, hepatorenal syndrome; worsened by NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) and ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors |
| **Intrinsic** | **ATN (acute tubular necrosis):** ischaemia, contrast, aminoglycosides, cisplatin, myoglobin. **AIN (acute interstitial nephritis):** β-lactams, NSAIDs, PPIs (proton pump inhibitors), checkpoint inhibitors. Glomerulonephritis; vascular (renal artery or vein thrombosis, atheroemboli) |
| **Postrenal** | BOO (bladder outlet obstruction), bilateral ureteric obstruction or obstructed single kidney: stones, tumours, retroperitoneal fibrosis, ureteric injury at surgery |

- Prerenal AKI and ATN cause about three-quarters of hospital AKI.

### Pathogenesis
- **Autoregulation** holds GFR (glomerular filtration rate) by afferent dilatation (prostaglandins, myogenic reflex) and efferent constriction (angiotensin II, tubuloglomerular feedback).
- **Prerenal:** low perfusion lowers glomerular pressure. Tubules are intact, so they **conserve sodium and water**. NSAIDs block afferent dilatation; ACE inhibitors block efferent constriction. Prolonged prerenal state becomes ATN.
- **Ischaemic ATN:**
  - The medulla works near hypoxia (oxygen tension 10–20 mmHg); **S3 proximal tubule** and **medullary thick ascending limb** are most vulnerable.
  - Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase loses its basolateral position → sodium reabsorption fails → high sodium at macula densa → afferent constriction → GFR falls (protective).
  - Cells die (mostly apoptosis) and slough → **muddy-brown casts** block tubules; filtrate leaks back; endothelial injury and inflammation worsen hypoxia.
  - Phases: initiation → maintenance (oliguria) → recovery (polyuria).
- **Toxic ATN:** vasoconstriction plus direct tubular injury. **Contrast:** risk with age, diabetes, CKD (chronic kidney disease). **Rhabdomyolysis** (long lateral positioning): myoglobin causes toxicity, cast obstruction and hypoperfusion.
- **Postrenal:** raised tubular pressure, then afferent vasoconstriction; recovery depends on duration (complete obstruction: near-full recovery by 1–2 weeks, little by 6 weeks in animals).

### Evaluation
- History (fluid loss, hypotension, drugs, contrast), volume status, bladder scan.
- **Ultrasound in every AKI** to exclude obstruction.

| Index | Prerenal | ATN |
|---|---|---|
| Urine sodium | <20 mmol/L | >20 mmol/L |
| FeNa (fractional excretion of sodium) | <1% | >1% |
| FeUrea (on diuretics) | <35% | >35% |
| Specific gravity | >1.015 | <1.010 |
| Sediment | Bland | Granular casts, tubular cells |
| Fluid challenge | Quick recovery | Lag of days to weeks |

- Creatine kinase; white cells (AIN); red cell casts (glomerulonephritis); biopsy only if recovery lags.

### Management
**General**
- Treat the cause; **avoid hypotension** (MAP, mean arterial pressure, >65 mmHg or near baseline) and nephrotoxins; stop NSAIDs and ACE inhibitors or ARBs (angiotensin receptor blockers); adjust drug doses.
- Isotonic crystalloid for hypovolaemia; aggressive fluids in rhabdomyolysis; IV fluid before contrast.
- **Postrenal:** urethral or suprapubic catheter; **DJ stent or PCN (percutaneous nephrostomy)**; infected obstructed kidney is an emergency.
- AIN: stop drug ± steroids; glomerulonephritis: nephrology and biopsy.

**Complications**
- **Hyperkalaemia:** calcium gluconate 10 mL of 10% (protects heart) → insulin 10 U with 50 mL 50% dextrose, salbutamol nebuliser (shift) → binders, diuretics or dialysis (remove).
- **Volume overload:** loop diuretics in escalating doses. **Diuretics do not treat ATN.**
- Acidosis: bicarbonate. Uraemic bleeding: desmopressin 0.3 µg/kg.

**Dialysis indications (AEIOU), refractory to medical therapy**

| | Indication |
|---|---|
| A | Acidosis |
| E | Electrolytes, mainly hyperkalaemia |
| I | Intoxications (lithium, salicylate, alcohols) |
| O | Overload with respiratory distress |
| U | Uraemia: encephalopathy, pericarditis, bleeding |

- Intermittent haemodialysis via 10–12 Fr jugular or femoral catheter; **CRRT (continuous RRT)** if haemodynamically unstable. **STARRT-AKI (2020):** early start gave no survival benefit.

**Post-obstructive diuresis**
- Causes: retained urea (osmotic diuresis), volume expansion with ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide), and down-regulated aquaporin-2 and sodium transport.
- Up to 300 mL/h for about 2 days. Free oral fluids; if unable to drink, replace about 75% of output with 0.45% saline; electrolytes every 4–6 hours. Gradual bladder decompression has no role.

### Outcome
- Full or partial recovery, or dialysis dependence; persistence ≥90 days = CKD.
- **Also know:** biomarkers NGAL (neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin), KIM-1 (kidney injury molecule-1); AKD (acute kidney disease, 7–90 days); gadolinium (groups I and III) avoided in AKI.

**Diagram to draw:** tubular cell losing polarity → sloughing → casts and back-leak.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - KDIGO: creatinine ≥0.3 mg/dL or ×1.5; urine <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 h.
> - FeNa <1% prerenal, >1% ATN; ultrasound in every AKI.
> - Diuretics only for overload; dialysis for refractory AEIOU.
> - Post-obstructive diuresis: oral fluids; replace 75% with 0.45% saline if needed.

## Q3a. ALPP
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 1, Q3e and 2019-Summer Paper 1, Q3b)

### Definition
- **ALPP (abdominal leak point pressure):** the **intravesical pressure** at which urine leaks because of raised abdominal pressure, **without a detrusor contraction**.
- It measures sphincter strength: **lower ALPP = weaker sphincter**. Seen only in SUI (stress urinary incontinence); there is **no normal value**.
- Provoked by Valsalva (**VLPP**, Valsalva leak point pressure) or cough (**CLPP**, cough leak point pressure). VLPP is lower than CLPP, so state the method.

### Technique
1. Urethral and rectal catheters; standing; fill to **150 mL**.
2. Graded Valsalva or cough.
3. Record **total Pves (vesical pressure) at visible leak**, including baseline.
4. No leak: repeat every 50 mL to capacity; then remove the urethral catheter and read pressure from the rectal line.
5. Report volume, catheter size and provocation.

### Interpretation (McGuire 1993)
| ALPP (cmH₂O) | Inference |
|---|---|
| **<60** | **ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency)** |
| 60–90 | Equivocal |
| **≥90** | **Urethral hypermobility**, little ISD |

### Clinical uses
- Separates ISD from hypermobility; low ALPP traditionally favours a sling or bulking agent over colposuspension.
- Post-prostatectomy incontinence: many men leak only once the urethral catheter is removed.

### Limitations
- Falls with larger catheters and volumes; invalid with DO (detrusor overactivity); prolapse masks leak.
- ISD and hypermobility coexist; ALPP alone predicts surgical success poorly.
- **VALUE trial (2012):** office evaluation was non-inferior to urodynamics before surgery for uncomplicated SUI.

### ALPP versus DLPP
| ALPP | DLPP (detrusor leak point pressure) |
|---|---|
| Sphincter against abdominal pressure | Bladder pressure against outlet resistance |
| **Low = bad** | **High = bad**: >40 cmH₂O → upper-tract damage in 85% (McGuire 1981) |
| SUI | Neurogenic, low-compliance bladder |

- **Also know:** newer term "urodynamic stress test" (ICS, International Continence Society); EAU (European Association of Urology): do not use leak point pressure to grade severity.

**Diagram to draw:** Pves, Pabd and Pdet traces with leak on Valsalva, flat Pdet.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Pves at leak with strain, no detrusor contraction; start at 150 mL.
> - <60 ISD; 60–90 equivocal; ≥90 hypermobility.
> - VLPP < CLPP; catheter size and volume alter it.

## Q3b. Tivozanib
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- Oral **VEGFR TKI (vascular endothelial growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor)** used in advanced RCC (renal cell carcinoma).
- Targets **VEGFR-1, -2 and -3** (also c-KIT and PDGFR-β, platelet-derived growth factor receptor-β); highly potent and selective.

### Mechanism and rationale
- In clear cell RCC, **VHL (von Hippel–Lindau) loss** lets HIF (hypoxia-inducible factor) accumulate, driving VEGF and angiogenesis.
- Tivozanib blocks VEGFR signalling, starving the tumour of new vessels.
- High selectivity means fewer off-target effects (less hand-foot skin reaction and diarrhoea than sorafenib).

### Dose and pharmacology
- **1.34 mg orally once daily, 21 days of a 28-day cycle.**
- Long half-life (about 4–5 days); dose reduction for toxicity or hepatic impairment.

### Key trials
| Trial | Setting | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **TIVO-1** (phase III) | 0–1 prior therapy, versus sorafenib | PFS (progression-free survival) 11.9 vs 9.1 months; response 33% vs 23%; OS (overall survival) 28.8 vs 29.3 months |
| **TIVO-3** (phase III) | After 2–3 prior systemic therapies including a VEGFR TKI, versus sorafenib | PFS 5.6 vs 3.9 months (HR, hazard ratio, 0.73); response 18% vs 8%; OS not different (HR 0.97); benefit also after prior checkpoint inhibitor |
| **TiNivo-2** (phase III) | After checkpoint inhibitor; tivozanib ± nivolumab | **Negative:** adding nivolumab did not improve PFS (no immunotherapy re-challenge) |

### Approval and place
- FDA (Food and Drug Administration) declined in 2013 because of the OS concern in TIVO-1; EMA (European Medicines Agency) approved first-line use in 2017.
- **FDA March 2021:** relapsed or refractory advanced RCC **after ≥2 prior systemic therapies**.
- NCCN (National Comprehensive Cancer Network): later-line option, mainly after immunotherapy combinations.

### Adverse effects
- **Hypertension** (commonest; control before starting), dysphonia, fatigue, diarrhoea, proteinuria, hypothyroidism.
- Bleeding, arterial thrombosis, cardiac failure, **impaired wound healing** (stop before surgery), RPLS (reversible posterior leukoencephalopathy syndrome).
- **Monitoring:** blood pressure, thyroid tests, urine protein, liver tests.

**Diagram to draw:** VHL loss → HIF → VEGF → VEGFR, blocked by tivozanib.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Selective VEGFR-1–3 TKI; 1.34 mg daily, 3 weeks on, 1 off.
> - TIVO-3: PFS 5.6 vs 3.9 months; OS not different.
> - FDA 2021: after ≥2 prior systemic therapies.
> - TiNivo-2 negative; main toxicity hypertension.

## Q3c. 46 XX Male
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2022-Summer Paper 1, Q3e)

*Assumed meaning: 46,XX testicular DSD (difference of sex development), "XX male" or de la Chapelle syndrome.*

### Definition
- **Testes and male genitalia in a 46,XX individual.** Old term "XX sex reversal"; renamed **46,XX testicular DSD** (Chicago consensus 2006). About **1 in 20,000** males.
- In the DSD classification it is a 46,XX DSD of gonadal development, with ovotesticular DSD; **CAH (congenital adrenal hyperplasia)** is the androgen-excess type.

### Genetics
| Type | Mechanism | Phenotype |
|---|---|---|
| **SRY-positive** (about 80–90%) | **SRY** (sex-determining region Y) crosses from Yp to X during paternal meiosis | Usually normal male genitalia |
| SRY-negative | Testis genes switched on without SRY: **SOX9** duplication, SOX3, loss of RSPO1 or WNT4 | More hypospadias, undescended testes, ovotestis |

- SRY switches on SOX9 → Sertoli cells → **AMH (anti-Müllerian hormone)**: no uterus. Leydig testosterone virilises.
- The **AZF (azoospermia factor) region of Yq is absent**, so spermatogenesis always fails.

### Clinical features
- Male identity; found at **infertility work-up**, or prenatally (cell-free DNA 46,XX, male ultrasound).
- Small firm testes, **azoospermia**, hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, gynaecomastia; progressive testicular regression.

| Feature | 46,XX male | Klinefelter (47,XXY) |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 1 in 20,000 | 1 in 500–1000 |
| Height | Normal or short | Often tall |
| Genital anomalies | More common | Rare |
| Sperm retrieval | **Never (no AZF)** | Possible by micro-TESE (microdissection testicular sperm extraction) |

### Evaluation
- Semen analysis; FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) and LH (luteinising hormone) high, testosterone low or low-normal.
- **Karyotype** 46,XX; **FISH (fluorescence in situ hybridisation) for SRY**; gene panel if SRY-negative; scrotal ultrasound.
- In an infant with atypical genitalia, exclude CAH (17-hydroxyprogesterone) and ovotesticular DSD.

### Management
- **Testosterone replacement** when hypogonadal (libido, bone, muscle).
- **Infertility:** sperm retrieval is futile; counsel **donor sperm or adoption**.
- Repair hypospadias or undescended testis; reduction mammoplasty for persistent gynaecomastia.
- Genetic counselling, psychological support, bone density follow-up.

**Diagram to draw:** X and Y chromosomes showing Xp–Yp exchange carrying SRY onto X.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 46,XX testicular DSD: SRY on X in about 80–90%; 1 in 20,000.
> - Azoospermia always (AZF absent): no sperm retrieval.
> - Shorter than Klinefelter; FISH for SRY confirms.

## Q3d. Brain stem Death: Biology and clinical implications.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2022-Summer Paper 4, Q3b and 2015-Summer Paper 1, Q3d)

### Definition
- **Irreversible loss of capacity for consciousness and breathing**; legal death in India (THOA, Transplantation of Human Organs Act, 1994; Rules 2014).

### Biology
```
Brain injury -> oedema -> raised ICP (intracranial pressure)
 -> falling cerebral perfusion -> herniation ("coning")
 -> Cushing reflex (hypertension, bradycardia)
 -> catecholamine storm (hypertension, tachyarrhythmia)
 -> loss of sympathetic tone -> vasodilatation, hypotension
```

| System | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Heart and vessels | Storm causes myocardial injury and arrhythmia, then **hypotension** |
| Lungs | Neurogenic pulmonary oedema |
| Pituitary | **Diabetes insipidus** (no ADH, antidiuretic hormone): polyuria, hypernatraemia; low thyroid hormone and cortisol |
| Hypothalamus | **Hypothermia** (poikilothermia) |
| Blood | DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation) from brain thromboplastin |
| Whole body | Cytokine injury to organs; hyperglycaemia |

### Diagnosis
- **Preconditions:** apnoeic coma of known irreversible cause; exclude drugs, hypothermia, metabolic causes, shock.

| Brainstem reflex (all absent) | Method | Cranial nerves |
|---|---|---|
| Pupillary | Bright light: pupils fixed | II, III |
| Corneal | Touch cornea: no blink | V, VII |
| Oculocephalic | Turn head: eyes move with head | VIII, III, VI |
| Oculovestibular | 50 mL ice-cold water in each ear: no eye movement | VIII, III, VI |
| Motor in cranial nerve area | Supraorbital pressure: no grimace | V, VII |
| Gag and cough | Pharynx touch, tracheal suction: no response | IX, X |

- **Apnoea test (last):** pre-oxygenate, disconnect ventilator with oxygen insufflation; **no breathing at PaCO₂ (arterial carbon dioxide tension) ≥60 mmHg**.
- **India:** 4-member Board (no transplant-team member); **two tests ≥6 hours apart**; Form 10. Ancillary tests (angiography, perfusion scan) if examination impossible.

### Clinical implications: donor management
- **Rule of 100s:** systolic pressure >100 mmHg, urine >100 mL/h, PaO₂ (arterial oxygen tension) >100 mmHg, haemoglobin >100 g/L.
- **Vasopressin** (pressor, treats DI); thyroid hormone, methylprednisolone, insulin; warming; protective ventilation.
- **DBD (donation after brain death)** keeps circulation, so warm ischaemia is minimal; mandatory request for donation.

**Diagram to draw:** herniation sequence leading to storm, DI, hypothermia, DIC.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Raised ICP → Cushing reflex → storm → hypotension, DI, hypothermia, DIC.
> - Six reflexes absent; apnoea at PaCO₂ ≥60 mmHg.
> - Two tests ≥6 h apart; Rule of 100s.

## Q3e. Immunotherapy therapy for metastatic RCC
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(overall management of metastatic RCC: see 2024-Winter Paper 3, Q3b)

### Rationale
- RCC (renal cell carcinoma) is immunogenic (spontaneous regressions, T-cell infiltration) and chemoresistant.
- **Checkpoints:** PD-1 (programmed death-1) on CD8 T cells binds tumour PD-L1 and switches the T cell off; CTLA-4 (cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen-4) on regulatory T cells damps priming. Blocking them **releases the brake** on existing anti-tumour immunity.
- VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) is immunosuppressive, so TKIs (tyrosine kinase inhibitors) synergise.

### Cytokine era
- Interferon-α: response 10–15%. **High-dose IL-2 (interleukin-2):** durable complete response 7–9%, but capillary leak needs ICU care.

### Checkpoint inhibitors: trials (all clear cell)
| Trial | Regimen | Result |
|---|---|---|
| CheckMate 025 | Nivolumab vs everolimus, after TKI | OS (overall survival) 25.0 vs 19.6 months |
| **CheckMate 214** | Nivolumab + ipilimumab vs sunitinib | Intermediate/poor risk: OS HR (hazard ratio) 0.63; complete response 9% |
| KEYNOTE-426 | Pembrolizumab + axitinib vs sunitinib | Better PFS (progression-free survival) and OS |
| CheckMate 9ER | Nivolumab + cabozantinib vs sunitinib | PFS 16.6 vs 8.3 months |
| **CLEAR** | Pembrolizumab + lenvatinib vs sunitinib | PFS 23.9 vs 9.2 months; response 71% |

- **First line:** an IO/TKI (immuno-oncology plus TKI) combination for all IMDC (International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium) risk groups; nivolumab + ipilimumab for intermediate/poor risk only.
- **After IO:** switch to a VEGF TKI; IO re-challenge failed (CONTACT-03, TiNivo-2).

### Adjuvant
- **KEYNOTE-564:** pembrolizumab for 1 year after nephrectomy in high-risk clear cell RCC, including resected metastases (M1 NED, no evidence of disease); DFS (disease-free survival) HR 0.68, later OS benefit (HR 0.62). Atezolizumab and nivolumab-based adjuvant trials were negative.

### Toxicity: irAEs (immune-related adverse events)
- Colitis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, thyroiditis, hypophysitis, nephritis, myocarditis, rash.

| Grade | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Continue, monitor |
| 2 | Hold; prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg |
| 3–4 | Stop; methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg; infliximab or mycophenolate if refractory |

- Endocrinopathies: hormone replacement.

### Biomarkers
- **PD-L1 does not select patients.** Sarcomatoid tumours respond well. Gene signatures investigational; antibiotics may reduce benefit.

**Diagram to draw:** T cell–tumour synapse with PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4/B7 blocked.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - IO/TKI for all risks; nivolumab + ipilimumab for intermediate/poor only.
> - KEYNOTE-564: adjuvant pembrolizumab, DFS and OS benefit.
> - irAE grade ≥2: hold drug and give steroids.

## Q3f. Sperm extraction in Obstructive azoospermia.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 4, Q2)

### Introduction
- **OA (obstructive azoospermia):** no sperm in the centrifuged ejaculate despite **normal spermatogenesis**, from blockage of the ducts.
- Causes: **CBAVD (congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens)**, vasectomy, epididymal or ejaculatory duct obstruction, hernia-repair injury.
- **Clues:** normal testes, **FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) ≤7.6 IU/L and testis long axis >4.6 cm**; CBAVD → CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) testing of both partners.

### Indications for retrieval
- CBAVD; unreconstructable obstruction; failed or declined vasectomy reversal; backup at vasoepididymostomy, frozen for IVF (in vitro fertilisation).
- Retrieved sperm need **ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection)**, because sperm from obstructed systems have poor motility.

### Techniques
| Technique | Method | Merits and limits |
|---|---|---|
| **MESA** (microsurgical epididymal sperm aspiration) | Tubule opened under microscope; aspirated | **Best in OA**: many motile sperm, multiple vials; needs microsurgery |
| **PESA** (percutaneous epididymal sperm aspiration) | Fine needle into caput | Simple; fewer sperm, may obstruct epididymis |
| **TESA** (testicular sperm aspiration) | 23 G needle with suction | Least invasive; immature, immotile sperm |
| Percutaneous core biopsy | 14 G biopsy gun | Fresh retrieval in OA; blind |
| **TESE** (testicular sperm extraction) | Open biopsy | When epididymis is absent or empty; gold standard for tissue |
| Vasal aspiration | Vasotomy, testicular end flushed | At reconstruction |

### MESA technique
1. Testis delivered; epididymis inspected at 16–25×.
2. Dilated tubule opened with a microknife; fluid checked at once.
3. No motile sperm: close and **move proximally**, up to the efferent ductules (proximal sperm are more motile).
4. Collect by **capillary action** into micropipettes, never suction.
5. **Cryopreserve** in several aliquots; close tubule with 10-0 suture.

### Choice and outcome
- Epididymal sperm first; testicular if that fails. Retrieval succeeds in nearly all OA; frozen sperm match fresh (except spinal cord injury).
- **Complications:** haematoma, infection, pain, epididymal obstruction.
- **Also know:** micro-TESE is for NOA (non-obstructive azoospermia).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - OA: normal FSH and testes; ICSI essential.
> - MESA is best: many motile sperm, multiple vials.
> - Move proximally; capillary action, never suction.
> - TESA/TESE when epididymal sperm absent.
