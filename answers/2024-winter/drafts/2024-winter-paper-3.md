## Q1. Discuss the diagnosis, staging and management of renal upper tract Urothelial tumor.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2023-Winter Paper 2, Q1, with emphasis on aetiology)

### Introduction
- UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma) is 5–10% of urothelial cancers. Pelvicalyceal tumours are about twice as common as ureteric ones. Peak age 70–90.
- About 60% are invasive at diagnosis, because the upper tract has a thin muscle wall. Concurrent bladder cancer in 17%.

### Clinical features
- **Haematuria** (56–98%), **flank pain** (20–32%, dull from hydronephrosis or colic from clots); lumbar mass about 10%; incidental about 15%.
- Weight loss, bone pain or a mass suggest advanced disease.

### Diagnosis
**1. Cystoscopy:** always, to exclude coexisting bladder cancer. Positive cytology with a normal bladder and urethra means the upper tracts must be examined.

**2. Imaging**
| Test | Role and key points |
|---|---|
| **CTU (computed tomography urography)** | First line; highest accuracy (sensitivity ~92%, specificity ~95%). Radiolucent **filling defect**, hydronephrosis, non-visualisation. Tumour ~46 HU (Hounsfield units) versus uric acid stone ~100 HU. Misses flat lesions (CIS, carcinoma in situ) |
| MRU (magnetic resonance urography) | If contrast or radiation is contraindicated; sensitivity ~75% for tumours <2 cm |
| Retrograde pyelography | Poorly filled calyces; accuracy ~75%; done with ureteroscopy |
| FDG-PET/CT (fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography) | Nodes and metastases when contrast is contraindicated |
| Renogram | Split function; contralateral kidney must be assessed |
- **Hydronephrosis** predicts invasive disease (up to 80% in ureteric tumours).

**3. Urine cytology**
- Sensitivity ~65%, grade-dependent (grade 1 ~20%, grade 3 ~75%). **Selective** ureteric washings are more accurate than voided urine. Take it before contrast. FISH (fluorescence in situ hybridisation) is optional for surveillance.

**4. Flexible URS (ureteroscopy) with biopsy:** the key test
- Adds accuracy (75–85% → ~90%). Biopsy gives **grade in ~90%**, but **stage cannot be assessed** (shallow samples; a Ta biopsy is often T3 at surgery).
- Indications: diagnostic doubt, planned kidney-sparing treatment, solitary kidney.
- Diagnostic URS before RNU (radical nephroureterectomy) raises bladder recurrence, without changing survival.

### Staging (TNM 8th edition, 2017)
| T | Definition |
|---|---|
| Ta / Tis | Non-invasive papillary / carcinoma in situ |
| T1 | Subepithelial connective tissue |
| T2 | Muscularis |
| T3 | Pelvis: **peripelvic fat or renal parenchyma**; ureter: periureteric fat |
| T4 | Adjacent organs, or **through the kidney into perinephric fat** |
- **N1** single node ≤2 cm; **N2** node >2 cm or multiple. **M1** distant metastasis.
- **AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer) grouping:** stage I = T1, II = T2, III = T3; **any N+, T4 or M1 = stage IV**.
- **Grade:** WHO (World Health Organization) 2004/2016: PUNLMP (papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential), low grade, high grade.
- Regional nodes: pelvis and upper ureter → hilar and retroperitoneal; lower ureter → pelvic.

### Risk stratification (EAU, European Association of Urology)
| Low risk: all needed | High risk: any one |
|---|---|
| Unifocal, <2 cm | Multifocal or >2 cm |
| Low-grade cytology and biopsy | High-grade cytology or biopsy |
| No invasion on CTU | Hydronephrosis; invasion on imaging |
| — | Previous cystectomy; variant histology |
- AUA/SUO (American Urological Association/Society of Urologic Oncology) 2023 splits each group into favourable and unfavourable.
- **Prognostic factors:** stage (strongest), grade, LVI (lymphovascular invasion), nodes, sessile architecture, necrosis >10%, ureteric site, surgical delay >12 weeks.

### Management
**A. Kidney-sparing (low risk; imperative in solitary kidney, bilateral disease, CKD [chronic kidney disease])**
- **Ureteroscopic laser ablation** (holmium or thulium) after biopsy; percutaneous resection for large pelvic tumours (tract seeding risk).
- **Segmental (distal) ureterectomy** with reimplantation or psoas hitch.
- Adjuvant upper-tract BCG (bacillus Calmette–Guérin) or MMC (mitomycin C); UGN-101 mitomycin gel for low grade. Lifelong endoscopic surveillance.

**B. RNU with bladder cuff: standard for high risk**
- Kidney outside Gerota's fascia; avoid opening the tract; remove the **intramural ureter and cuff**. Open, laparoscopic and robotic routes are equivalent.
- **Template lymphadenectomy** for high risk (hilar plus paracaval or para-aortic).
- **Single postoperative intravesical chemotherapy:** ODMIT-C (MMC 40 mg) cut bladder recurrence 28% → 16% at 1 year.

**C. Perioperative chemotherapy**
- **POUT (2020):** adjuvant platinum for pT2–T4 or N+ improved disease-free survival.
- Neoadjuvant platinum is preferred when post-RNU renal function would exclude cisplatin.

**D. Metastatic:** enfortumab vedotin + pembrolizumab (EV-302) first line; gemcitabine–platinum with avelumab maintenance; erdafitinib for FGFR3 alterations.

### Follow-up
- After RNU: cystoscopy and cytology from 3 months; CTU yearly. After kidney-sparing: URS and CTU at closer intervals.

**Diagram to draw:** TNM levels of a renal pelvic tumour (T3 parenchyma versus T4 perinephric fat), beside the diagnostic flowchart CTU → cystoscopy → URS biopsy → risk group.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - CTU is the best test; URS biopsy gives grade, not stage.
> - Pelvic tumour into parenchyma is T3; through kidney to perinephric fat is T4; any N+ is stage IV.
> - EAU low risk needs all favourable features; one adverse feature means high risk.
> - RNU + cuff + single MMC (ODMIT-C); adjuvant platinum (POUT).

## Q2. Discuss the Aetiopathogenesis, Clinical feature and management of Chyluria.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2018-Summer Paper 1, Q1, with lymphatic anatomy)

### Definition
- **Chyluria** is the passage of **chyle** (intestinal lymph rich in chylomicrons and protein) in the urine, making it milky.
- It needs an abnormal **lymphourinary fistula**, usually at a renal calyceal fornix (pyelolymphatic fistula).

### Relevant lymphatic anatomy
- Intestinal lacteals → intestinal trunk → **cisterna chyli** (L1–L2) → **thoracic duct** → left subclavian–jugular junction.
- Lumbar (para-aortic) trunks from the kidneys join the cisterna. Lymph flows cephalad and right to left.
- Valves normally stop backflow from the cisterna into renal hilar lymphatics.

### Aetiology
| Parasitic (commonest; India, China) | Non-parasitic |
|---|---|
| **Wuchereria bancrofti** (lymphatic filariasis; Culex mosquito in urban India) | Congenital: lymphangiectasia, renal lymphangioma |
| Rarely other parasites | Trauma or surgery (partial nephrectomy, RPLND [retroperitoneal lymph node dissection]) |
|  | Retroperitoneal tumour, fibrosis, tuberculosis, pregnancy |
- Genital and urinary filariasis is almost always *W. bancrofti*; Brugia species spare the genitalia.

### Pathogenesis
- **Life cycle:** a mosquito deposits infective larvae, which mature in central lymphatics over 6–9 months. Adults live 5–7 years in retroperitoneal and genital lymphatics. Females release microfilariae with **nocturnal periodicity**.
- **Obstructive theory:** adult worms and the host reaction cause **lymphangitis and fibrosis** of lymphatics between the intestinal trunks and thoracic duct.
- Lymphatics below the block **dilate**, their **valves fail**, and chyle **refluxes** into renal hilar and peripelvic lymphatics.
- Rising pressure ruptures the thin-walled lymphatics into a calyceal fornix, creating a **pyelolymphatic fistula**. Rarely the fistula is in the ureter or bladder.
- Chyluria appears **earlier than elephantiasis**, is usually **intermittent** and may remit spontaneously.

### Clinical features
- **Milky urine**, worse after fatty meals and on standing; may clear after fasting.
- **Chylous clots:** colic, retention.
- **Haematochyluria** (blood-stained chylous urine).
- **Systemic effects** of fat and protein loss: weight loss, **hypoproteinaemia**, oedema, anaemia, fatigue, lowered immunity.
- Endemic residence, filarial fever, hydrocele or lymphoedema.
- **Severity:** mild (intermittent, no systemic effect); moderate (persistent, clots); severe (haematochyluria, clot retention, weight loss, hypoalbuminaemia).

### Evaluation
**1. Confirm chyle**
- **Ether (chloroform) test:** milky urine shaken with ether clears, because the fat dissolves.
- **Urine triglycerides** positive; Sudan III staining shows fat globules; urine protein raised.
- Differentiate from **phosphaturia** (clears with acetic acid) and **pyuria** (leucocytes, no clearing).

**2. Filarial tests**
- Night (midnight) blood smear: often negative by this stage.
- **Circulating filarial antigen** (card test), eosinophilia, PCR (polymerase chain reaction); ultrasound "filarial dance sign" in scrotal lymphatics.

**3. Lateralise and localise the fistula**
- **Cystoscopy after a fatty meal:** milky efflux from one ureteric orifice; bilateral in some.
- **Retrograde pyelography:** pyelolymphatic backflow.
- **Lymphoscintigraphy:** non-invasive; tracer seen in the kidney and bladder. MR lymphangiography or CT; pedal lymphangiography is the historical gold standard.

**4. Baseline:** albumin, haemoglobin, renal function.

### Management
```
Mild             -> diet + antifilarial drugs
Moderate/persist -> add endoscopic sclerotherapy (repeatable)
Severe or failed -> renal hilar lymphatic disconnection
```
**1. Conservative**
- **Low-fat, high-protein diet with MCT (medium-chain triglyceride) oil**, plenty of fluids. MCTs are absorbed directly into portal blood, not lacteals, so less chyle forms.
- **DEC (diethylcarbamazine)** 2 mg/kg three times daily, traditionally for 12 days (a 1-day course is as effective). It kills microfilariae but only modestly kills adults.
- **Doxycycline** (anti-*Wolbachia*, 200 mg daily for 4–8 weeks); albendazole; ivermectin.

**2. Endoscopic sclerotherapy (renal pelvic instillation)**
- Ureteric catheter on the affected side, head-down position. Instil **silver nitrate (0.5–1%)** or **povidone-iodine**, repeated over several sessions.
- **Mechanism:** chemical lymphangitis and fibrosis seal the fistula.
- Good initial response; recurrence common.
- **Complications:** flank pain, haematuria, papillary necrosis, ureteric stricture, anuria; rarely renal failure or death (silver nitrate).

**3. Surgery: renal hilar lymphatic disconnection (chylolymphatic disconnection)**
- **Indications:** severe disease, failed sclerotherapy, haematochyluria with clot retention, marked weight loss.
- **Steps:**
  1. Full renal mobilisation (nephrolysis).
  2. **Skeletonise the renal artery and vein**, ligating, clipping or sealing every hilar lymphatic.
  3. Strip the upper ureter; remove perinephric fat.
  4. Nephropexy; optional **omental wrap** around the hilum.
  5. On-table cystoscopy confirms clear efflux.
- Reflux occurs only through **hilar lymphatics**, so upper-pole dissection can be limited.
- **Approach:** open, laparoscopic (transperitoneal or retroperitoneal) or robotic. **Retroperitoneoscopic** ligation beats open (shorter operation and stay, less blood loss, fewer complications).
- **Also know:** lymphovenous anastomosis; autotransplantation (rare); nephrectomy obsolete.

**4. Prevention**
- Mass drug administration (albendazole with DEC or ivermectin), bednets; clip lymphatics during renal and nodal surgery.

**Diagram to draw:** retroperitoneal lymphatics with obstructed thoracic duct, dilated hilar lymphatics and pyelolymphatic fistula at a calyceal fornix.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Chyluria = pyelolymphatic fistula, usually from *W. bancrofti* obstruction.
> - Ether test and urine triglycerides confirm; cystoscopy after a fatty meal lateralises.
> - Diet with MCT and DEC → silver nitrate sclerotherapy → laparoscopic hilar lymphatic disconnection.
> - Chyluria precedes elephantiasis and is often intermittent.

## Q3a. Vascular Surgery in Urology.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(vascular grafts answered in 2021-Summer Paper 3, Q2)

*Assumed meaning: vascular procedures performed or met by the urologist (control and repair of vessels, caval surgery, transplant and renovascular surgery, penile vascular surgery and access), not vascular surgery as a specialty.*

### Principles
- **Control first:** proximal and distal control with vessel loops, Rummel tourniquets and atraumatic clamps (bulldog, Satinsky, DeBakey). Artery before vein.
- Systemic heparin before clamping; flush air and debris before tying; tension-free, everting anastomosis.
- **Sutures:** polypropylene (Prolene), double-armed. IVC (inferior vena cava) or aorta 3-0/4-0; renal vessels 4-0/5-0; transplant anastomoses 5-0/6-0.
- **Grafts:** autologous vein or artery for small or contaminated fields; PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) or Dacron for large vessels.

### Applications
| Setting | Vascular procedure |
|---|---|
| **RCC (renal cell carcinoma) with IVC thrombus** | Mayo levels 0–IV: level I milking and partial caval clamp; II full caval control; III liver mobilisation ± venovenous bypass; IV cardiopulmonary bypass ± deep hypothermic arrest. Patch cavoplasty if lumen <50%; caval replacement if wall invaded |
| **Renal transplantation** | Renal vein and artery end-to-side to external iliac vessels; Carrel patch; multiple arteries reconstructed on the bench |
| **Renovascular disease** | Ex vivo bench repair and **autotransplantation** (branch aneurysm, stenosis, ureteric loss). Angioplasty or stent first for fibromuscular dysplasia |
| **Trauma** | Main renal artery: endovascular stent; open repair only for solitary kidney or bilateral injury |
| **PC-RPLND (post-chemotherapy RPLND) and sarcoma** | IVC may be ligated or patched; aorta must be repaired or grafted |
| **Penis** | **Arterial revascularisation** (inferior epigastric artery to dorsal artery) for young men with post-traumatic pudendal stenosis; venous ligation investigational; priapism shunts (Grayhack saphenous–cavernosal) |
| **Dialysis access** | Arteriovenous fistula; PTFE graft if veins are poor |

- **Endovascular:** angioembolisation for trauma, AML (angiomyolipoma) bleeding, pseudoaneurysm and AVF (arteriovenous fistula) after partial nephrectomy.

### Complications
- Bleeding, thrombosis, stenosis, graft infection, pseudoaneurysm.

**Diagram to draw:** Mayo IVC thrombus levels with the matching vascular control.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Proximal and distal control, heparin, flush, everting Prolene anastomosis.
> - Mayo levels decide caval control and bypass.
> - Bench surgery plus autotransplantation for complex renovascular lesions.

## Q3b. Discuss the management of Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 2, Q1; immunotherapy in detail in 2024-Winter Paper 2, Q3e)

### Assessment
- CT chest, abdomen and pelvis; bone scan or brain MRI if symptomatic. **Biopsy** for histology.
- **IMDC (International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium) risk factors:** Karnofsky <80%; haemoglobin < normal; calcium > ULN (upper limit of normal); neutrophils > ULN; platelets > ULN; <1 year from diagnosis to systemic therapy.
- Favourable 0, intermediate 1–2, poor ≥3; median survival 43.2, 22.5 and 7.8 months.

### First-line systemic therapy (clear cell RCC; NCCN [National Comprehensive Cancer Network] and EAU [European Association of Urology])
| IMDC risk | Preferred | If IO (immuno-oncology) not possible |
|---|---|---|
| Favourable | Pembrolizumab + axitinib; nivolumab + cabozantinib; pembrolizumab + lenvatinib | Sunitinib, pazopanib; active surveillance for slow, low-volume disease |
| Intermediate/poor | Same three, **or nivolumab + ipilimumab** | Cabozantinib, sunitinib, pazopanib |
- **Rationale:** VHL (von Hippel–Lindau) loss → HIF (hypoxia-inducible factor) → VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor), the target of TKIs (tyrosine kinase inhibitors). Checkpoint inhibitors release T-cell brakes.
- **Immunotherapy** trials (CheckMate 214, KEYNOTE-426, CheckMate 9ER, CLEAR) and toxicity: see 2024-Winter Paper 2, Q3e.

### Later lines: switch mechanism
- After IO: a VEGF TKI not used before (cabozantinib, axitinib, tivozanib) or **belzutifan** (HIF-2α inhibitor; LITESPARK-005).
- After TKI alone: nivolumab (CheckMate 025) or cabozantinib (METEOR).

### Cytoreductive nephrectomy (CN)
- **CARMENA (2018):** sunitinib alone non-inferior to CN + sunitinib in intermediate/poor risk. **SURTIME:** deferred CN safe.
- Now: immediate CN only for fit, low-burden patients; **deferred CN** in responders; avoid in poor risk. Palliative nephrectomy or embolisation for bleeding.

### Metastasectomy and radiotherapy
- Complete resection of solitary or few metastases: 5-year survival 35–50%.
- **SBRT (stereotactic body radiotherapy)** for oligometastases; radiosurgery for brain; palliative radiotherapy for bone pain.

### Supportive and special groups
- Zoledronic acid or denosumab for bone metastases. Non-clear cell: trials, cabozantinib or sunitinib. Adjuvant pembrolizumab after complete metastasectomy (KEYNOTE-564).

**Diagram to draw:** flowchart: biopsy + IMDC → IO combination → deferred CN in responders → switch class on progression.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - IMDC 6 factors; poor = ≥3.
> - IO/TKI for all risks; nivolumab + ipilimumab for intermediate/poor.
> - CARMENA and SURTIME: no routine upfront CN.
> - Belzutifan after IO and TKI.

## Q3c. Prognostic indicators in emphysematous pyelonephritis.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- EPN (emphysematous pyelonephritis) is an acute **necrotising** infection of the renal parenchyma and perirenal tissue by **gas-forming organisms**. It is a urological emergency.
- Most patients are **diabetic**; obstruction in about a quarter.
- Organisms: *E. coli* (commonest), *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*. Proposed mechanism: high tissue glucose fermented to CO₂ in poorly perfused tissue.

### Imaging-based prognostic classifications
**1. Huang–Tseng CT classification (2000)**
| Class | Gas location |
|---|---|
| 1 | Collecting system only (emphysematous pyelitis) |
| 2 | Renal parenchyma, no extrarenal extension |
| 3A | Extension into **perinephric** space |
| 3B | Extension into **pararenal** space |
| 4 | **Bilateral** EPN or EPN in a **solitary kidney** |

**2. Wan classification (CT pattern)**
| Type | CT | Mortality |
|---|---|---|
| I | Streaky or mottled gas, **no fluid**; rapid parenchymal destruction | 50–60% |
| II | Renal or perirenal **fluid**, bubbly or loculated gas, or gas in collecting system | <20% |
- **Why:** fluid means a walled-off collection that can be drained. Streaky gas without fluid means necrosis faster than the host can contain it.

### Clinical and laboratory prognostic factors
- **Huang–Tseng risk factors:** **thrombocytopenia**, **AKI (acute kidney injury)/raised creatinine**, **altered sensorium**, **shock**.
- **Other adverse factors:** hypoalbuminaemia, bacteraemia, need for haemodialysis, polymicrobial infection.

### How prognosis guides management
```
All: resuscitation, glucose control, IV antibiotics, relieve obstruction (stent/PCN)
Class 1–2               -> antibiotics + PCD
Class 3–4, <2 risk factors -> antibiotics + PCD (usually succeeds)
Class 3–4, >=2 risk factors or PCD failure -> nephrectomy
```
- PCD (percutaneous catheter drainage); PCN (percutaneous nephrostomy).
- Reported mortality 19–43%; routine emergency nephrectomy still carried 40–50%, so drainage-first care is now standard.
- **Also know:** emphysematous pyelitis (gas in the collecting system only) is often non-diabetic and responds to antibiotics.

**Diagram to draw:** CT sketches of Huang–Tseng classes 1, 2, 3A/3B and 4.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Huang–Tseng: 1 pelvis, 2 parenchyma, 3A perinephric, 3B pararenal, 4 bilateral or solitary.
> - Four risk factors: thrombocytopenia, AKI, altered sensorium, shock.
> - Wan type I (no fluid) 50–60% mortality; type II <20%.
> - Drainage first; nephrectomy for extensive disease with ≥2 risk factors.

## Q3d. Mirabegron in over active bladder.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- **Mirabegron** is the first **β3-adrenoceptor agonist** for OAB (overactive bladder: urgency, with or without urgency incontinence, usually with frequency and nocturia). FDA (Food and Drug Administration) approval 2012.

### Mechanism of action
- **β3 receptors** are the most highly expressed adrenoceptor in human detrusor.
- Activation → adenylyl cyclase → **↑ cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate)** → detrusor relaxation during **storage**.
- Reduces non-voiding contractions and afferent firing. The voiding contraction (muscarinic M3) is spared, so PVR (post-void residual) barely rises.

### Pharmacology and dose
- Oral, extended release, once daily: start **25 mg**, increase to **50 mg** after 4–8 weeks.
- **25 mg maximum** in severe renal impairment (eGFR [estimated glomerular filtration rate] 15–29) or moderate hepatic impairment; avoid in end-stage renal disease and severe hepatic impairment.
- **Moderate CYP2D6 inhibitor:** raises levels of metoprolol, desipramine, flecainide; monitor digoxin.

### Efficacy
- Phase III trials (SCORPIO, ARIES, CAPRICORN): fewer incontinence episodes and micturitions than placebo. **TAURUS** (12 months): efficacy similar to tolterodine, with less dry mouth.
- **Combination with solifenacin** (SYNERGY, SYNERGY II, BESIDE): better than either alone, without extra cardiovascular effects.

### Adverse effects and contraindications
- Hypertension, tachycardia, headache, nasopharyngitis, UTI (urinary tract infection).
- **Dry mouth and constipation near placebo rates**, far below antimuscarinics.
- **Contraindicated:** severe uncontrolled hypertension (≥180/110 mmHg). Caution with arrhythmia and BOO (bladder outlet obstruction) with antimuscarinics (retention).

### Place in therapy
- After behavioural therapy, as first-line pharmacotherapy alongside antimuscarinics (AUA/SUFU [American Urological Association/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction]).
- **Preferred in the elderly and cognitive impairment:** no anticholinergic burden. Antimuscarinic use >3 months is linked to 10–65% more new-onset dementia (SUFU white paper).
- Add-on to α-blocker in men with BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia); neurogenic detrusor overactivity.
- **Also know:** vibegron 75 mg (no CYP2D6 interaction).

**Diagram to draw:** detrusor cell: noradrenaline → β3 → cAMP → relaxation, versus acetylcholine → M3 → contraction.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - β3 → cAMP → storage-phase relaxation; voiding spared.
> - 25 mg → 50 mg; avoid if blood pressure ≥180/110; CYP2D6 interaction (metoprolol).
> - Preferred over antimuscarinics in the elderly (dementia risk).
> - Add-on to solifenacin (SYNERGY) works.

## Q3e. Management of stress urinary incontinence.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and mechanism
- **SUI (stress urinary incontinence):** involuntary leakage on effort, coughing or sneezing (ICS, International Continence Society).
- Two mechanisms: **urethral hypermobility** (loss of the suburethral "hammock") and **ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency)**.

### Evaluation (brief)
- Bladder diary, pad test, **cough stress test**, PVR (post-void residual), urinalysis; Q-tip >30° = hypermobility.
- **Urodynamics not needed** in the uncomplicated (index) patient (VALUE trial); use if mixed, recurrent, neurogenic or with prolapse.

### Female SUI
**1. Conservative (first line)**
- Weight loss, fluid and caffeine adjustment.
- **Supervised PFMT (pelvic floor muscle training)** for at least 3 months (Cochrane: more cure than no treatment).
- **Pessary**; vaginal oestrogen for atrophy.
- **Duloxetine** (serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor): increases Onuf's nucleus drive to the sphincter; approved in Europe, not USA.

**2. Surgery**
| Operation | Principle | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **MUS (mid-urethral sling)**: retropubic (TVT, tension-free vaginal tape) or transobturator (TOT) | Tension-free tape acts as a new pubourethral ligament | First line for index SUI. **TOMUS:** 80.8% vs 77.7% cure (equivalent). Retropubic: more bladder perforation; transobturator: more groin pain |
| Single-incision sling | Short tape, vaginal only | Option; non-inferior to TOT in most studies |
| **Burch colposuspension** (open or laparoscopic) | Paravaginal fascia to Cooper's ligament; restores bladder neck position | 85–90% at 1 year; for women declining mesh |
| **Autologous pubovaginal sling** (rectus fascia) | Bladder-neck support and compression | ISD, recurrent SUI, after mesh removal. **SISTEr:** sling 47% vs Burch 38%, more voiding dysfunction |
| **Urethral bulking agents** | Submucosal coaptation | Frail patients; less durable |
| **AUS (artificial urinary sphincter)** | Circumferential cuff | Severe refractory ISD |

- **MUS complications:** perforation, retention, de novo urgency, mesh exposure (~2%), pain.

### Male SUI (brief)
- Usually after radical prostatectomy. PFMT first; no surgery for 6–12 months, as continence keeps improving.
- **AUS** for moderate–severe or irradiated men (gold standard, ~80% continent); **male sling** for mild–moderate leakage without radiotherapy. Bulking agents not advised.

**Diagram to draw:** sagittal pelvis showing retropubic and transobturator tape paths and Burch sutures.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - PFMT first; MUS first-line surgery.
> - TOMUS: retropubic = transobturator; SISTEr: fascial sling > Burch.
> - VALUE: no urodynamics in index SUI.
> - Men: AUS gold standard; sling for mild, non-irradiated.

## Q3f. Sting procedure.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q3c)

*Taken as STING (subureteric Teflon injection), the endoscopic treatment of VUR (vesicoureteral reflux).*

### Definition and principle
- Endoscopic injection of a **bulking agent** beneath the ureteric orifice. The mound supports the intramural ureter and restores the **flap-valve**.
- Popularised by **O'Donnell and Puri, 1986**.

### Bulking agents
| Agent | Particle | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Teflon (PTFE, polytetrafluoroethylene paste) | 90% <40 µm, permanent | **Migration**; never FDA (Food and Drug Administration) approved |
| **Dx/HA (dextranomer/hyaluronic acid, Deflux)** | **80–250 µm**; gel resorbed | Volume loss about 23%; FDA approved 2001; standard agent |
| Polyacrylate (Vantris) | 300 µm, permanent | Late ureteric obstruction |

### Indications
- Persistent primary VUR (grades II–IV) with breakthrough febrile UTI (urinary tract infection), new scars or parental preference; residual VUR after reimplantation.
- **Relative contraindications:** grade V, **UVJ (ureterovesical junction) obstruction**, untreated BBD (bladder and bowel dysfunction).

### Technique
1. Antibiotic at induction; offset-lens cystoscope; 3.7–5 Fr needle; bladder partly filled.
2. **STING:** needle enters **2–3 mm below the orifice at 6 o'clock**, bevel up, advanced **4–5 mm** submucosally.
3. Inject slowly; a mound should appear within **0.1–0.2 mL**, else reposition.
4. Aim for a **"volcano" mound** with a slit-like orifice. Do not cauterise bleeding (implant extrudes).

| Technique | Needle entry | Result |
|---|---|---|
| STING | Below orifice | Baseline |
| **HIT** (hydrodistension implantation technique) | Inside hydrodistended orifice | Better than STING, all grades |
| **Double HIT** | Two intraureteric sites | About 90% resolution |

### Results
- Low-grade VUR about 90% after one injection. Elder 2006 meta-analysis: grade I–II about 79%, III 72%, IV 63%, V 51%.
- **Swedish Reflux Trial:** 20% recurrence at 2 years.

### Complications
- Failure (repeat 1–2 injections, then reimplant); contralateral VUR; **early or late obstruction**; calcified mound mimics a stone.

### Follow-up
- Antibiotics 3 months; ultrasound at 2–6 weeks; ultrasound ± VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) at 3 months.

**Diagram to draw:** needle sites: STING below the orifice, HIT and double HIT inside the tunnel, with volcano mound.

> - O'Donnell and Puri 1986; needle 2–3 mm below orifice at 6 o'clock.
