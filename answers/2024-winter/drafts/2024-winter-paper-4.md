## Q1. Discuss the recent trends in the evaluation. Diagnosis and management of interstitial cystitis.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2017-Winter Paper 2, Q3b and 2021-Summer Paper 4, Q3d)

*Read as: recent trends in the evaluation, diagnosis and management of IC/BPS (interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome).*

### Definition: how it has changed
- **NIDDK (National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases, 1987):** research criteria; missed about 60% of patients, so the definition was broadened.
- **AUA (American Urological Association), current:** an unpleasant sensation (pain, pressure, discomfort) perceived to be related to the bladder, with LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) for **more than 6 weeks**, without infection or other identifiable cause.
- **ESSIC (European Society for the Study of Interstitial Cystitis, 2008):** chronic (>6 months) bladder pain with at least one other urinary symptom, after confusable diseases are excluded.
- **ICD-11 (International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision):** "chronic primary bladder pain syndrome".
- **Trend: splitting the syndrome.** "IC" is reserved for **Hunner lesion (HL) disease**, a true inflammatory disease; BPS without HL is a symptom complex. Both sit within UCPPS (urological chronic pelvic pain syndrome).

### Pathophysiology (current view)
- Multifactorial: leaky GAG (glycosaminoglycan) layer; mast-cell activation; neurogenic inflammation; **central sensitisation**; stress-axis dysfunction; small-fibre neuropathy. No infection proven.
- HL disease shows dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate; non-HL bladders are often histologically normal.
- **Associated:** IBS (irritable bowel syndrome, >30%), fibromyalgia, vulvodynia, endometriosis; tender pelvic floor in about 80%.

### Recent trends in evaluation (AUA 2022)
- **Diagnosis of exclusion plus localisation:** tests aim to find the pain generator.
- **History:** pain worse on filling, relieved by voiding; "painful urgency" without urge incontinence, unlike OAB (overactive bladder). **Ask about ketamine.**
- **Examination:** **six-site pelvic-floor palpation** (anterior and posterior levator ani, obturator internus, each side); vulval swab test; DRE (digital rectal examination) in men.
- **Basic tests:** PVR (post-void residual), urinalysis, culture; cytology if risk factors; frequency–volume chart.
- **Scores:** O'Leary–Sant ICSI/ICPI (Interstitial Cystitis Symptom/Problem Index), GUPI (Genitourinary Pain Index), pain VAS (visual analogue scale); **score pain and urinary symptoms separately.**
- **Body map:** widespread pain = 3 or more of 12 regions besides the pelvis.
- **Office cystoscopy if over 50 or HL suspected** (AUA 2022 change): HL is a stellate red area with radiating vessels, found only by looking.
- **Local anaesthetic challenge** (lidocaine ± bupivacaine) localises pain to the bladder and selects intravesical therapy.
- **Dropped:** potassium sensitivity test (neither sensitive nor specific); routine hydrodistension (glomerulations non-specific); routine urodynamics.
- **Biopsy** only to exclude CIS (carcinoma in situ).

**ESSIC classification** (cystoscopy with hydrodistension + biopsy, e.g. "3C"):

| Cystoscopy | Biopsy |
|---|---|
| X not done; 1 normal; 2 glomerulations; 3 Hunner lesion | X not done; A normal; B inconclusive; C positive (inflammation, mastocytosis, fibrosis) |

**Exclude:** UTI (urinary tract infection) including tuberculosis, CIS, stones, radiation or cyclophosphamide cystitis, ketamine cystitis, OAB, diverticulum, endometriosis.

### Phenotype-based diagnosis (key trend)
| Phenotype | Identified by | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Hunner lesion | Cystoscopy | Fulguration and/or triamcinolone; ciclosporin A if refractory |
| Anaesthetic-responsive bladder pain | Local anaesthetic challenge | Intravesical therapy |
| Pelvic-floor tenderness | Pelvic-floor examination | Myofascial physiotherapy |
| Widespread (centralised) pain | Body map, associated pain conditions | Systemic: amitriptyline, gabapentinoids, CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy), pain team |

- **MAPP (Multi-Disciplinary Approach to the Study of Chronic Pelvic Pain) reanalysis, 2023:** hydroxyzine/PPS (pentosan polysulfate) helped localised pain; amitriptyline helped widespread pain.

### Recent trends in management (AUA 2022)
- **Tiers abandoned:** the six-tier ladder is replaced by grouped options and shared decisions; multimodal treatment allowed early; major surgery last. Stop treatments that fail.

| Group | Options |
|---|---|
| Behavioural | Education, avoid trigger foods, stress management, flare plan |
| Physical | **Myofascial pelvic-floor therapy** (Standard); avoid Kegel exercises |
| Oral | **Amitriptyline** 10 mg at night, up to about 50 mg; hydroxyzine; cimetidine; **PPS 100 mg three times daily** (pigmentary maculopathy: baseline and periodic eye checks) |
| Intravesical | **DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide) 50%**; heparin; alkalinised lidocaine; also hyaluronic acid, chondroitin |
| Procedures | **HL: fulguration and/or triamcinolone** (75–90% improve); short low-pressure hydrodistension; **onabotulinumtoxinA 100 U into the trigone** (must accept self-catheterisation); SNM (sacral neuromodulation) |
| Refractory HL | **Oral ciclosporin A** (watch kidney function and blood pressure) |
| Last resort (<10%) | Cystoplasty or diversion ± cystectomy |

- **Not offered:** long-term antibiotics, intravesical BCG (bacille Calmette–Guérin), long-term oral steroids.

### Recent advances
- Urinary biomarkers and functional MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) for phenotyping (research); phenotype-stratified trials.
- **Also know:** tanezumab (anti-NGF [nerve growth factor]) not adopted; liposomal intravesical botulinum toxin no better than placebo; pudendal neuromodulation.

**Diagram to draw:** cystoscopic Hunner lesion with radiating vessels, and the four-phenotype treatment chart.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - AUA definition: bladder pain with LUTS >6 weeks; ESSIC >6 months.
> - AUA 2022: no tiers; cystoscopy if over 50 or HL suspected; anaesthetic challenge, not potassium test.
> - HL: fulguration or triamcinolone; ciclosporin if refractory.
> - PPS causes maculopathy; antibiotics and BCG not offered.

## Q2. Rationale of using cancer vaccines in Urology. Explain their mechanism of action. Enumerate various vaccines for renal, prostate and bladder malignancies . Write a note on Sipuleucel-T.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- A **cancer vaccine** is a therapeutic agent that trains the patient's own T cells to recognise **tumour antigens**. It treats existing cancer; it does not prevent it.
- **BCG (bacille Calmette–Guérin)**, a tuberculosis vaccine, was among the first immune treatments for any cancer. It is non-specific. Cancer vaccines aim for a **specific** anticancer response.

### Rationale in urology
- **Immune editing (three Es):** Elimination → Equilibrium → **Escape**. A clinical cancer has escaped by losing antigens, losing MHC (major histocompatibility complex), or recruiting Tregs (regulatory T cells). Vaccines try to restart the response.
- **Kidney:** rare spontaneous regression of metastases after nephrectomy; responses to IL-2 (interleukin-2) and interferon.
- **Prostate:** slow growth gives time to build a response; **organ-specific antigens** (PSA [prostate-specific antigen], PAP [prostatic acid phosphatase]).
- **Bladder:** BCG success; high mutation load gives many neoantigens.
- Low toxicity; possible lasting memory.

### Mechanism of action
- Two components: **antigen** (overexpressed in tumour) + **adjuvant** (a danger signal, e.g. GM-CSF [granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor], which activates DCs [dendritic cells]).

```
Intradermal antigen + adjuvant
  → immature DC takes up antigen; adjuvant matures it
  → DC migrates to lymph node (CCR7)
  → peptide on MHC I/II = signal 1; B7 costimulation = signal 2
  → CD4 Th1 help (IL-2, IFN-γ) + CD8 cytotoxic T cells
  → T cells traffic to tumour → apoptosis of antigen-bearing cells
  → memory T cells
```

- Without signal 2 the T cell becomes tolerant, hence the adjuvant.
- **Prime–boost:** first dose primes, later doses boost.
- **Delayed effect:** survival may improve without tumour shrinkage or PSA fall.
- **Resistance:** PD-L1 (programmed death ligand-1) on tumour, Tregs, MDSCs (myeloid-derived suppressor cells), MHC loss. This is the basis for combining vaccines with checkpoint inhibitors.

### Types (platforms)
| Platform | Example |
|---|---|
| Whole-cell (autologous or allogeneic) | Reniale (RCC [renal cell carcinoma]); GVAX (prostate) |
| Peptide/protein | IMA901 |
| Dendritic-cell (ex vivo loaded) | Sipuleucel-T; AGS-003 (rocapuldencel-T) |
| Viral vector (poxvirus) | PROSTVAC-VF; TroVax (MVA-5T4) |
| Heat-shock protein–peptide | Vitespen (HSPPC-96) |
| DNA/mRNA, personalised neoantigen | Phase I–II trials |

### Vaccines by organ
**Renal cell carcinoma**
- **IMA901:** 10 tumour-associated peptides + GM-CSF, low-dose cyclophosphamide to deplete Tregs; HLA-A*02+ (human leucocyte antigen) metastatic RCC with sunitinib. **No OS (overall survival) benefit.**
- **AGS-003 (rocapuldencel-T):** autologous DCs loaded with tumour RNA, with sunitinib. **Terminated for lack of efficacy.**
- **TroVax** with standard first-line therapy: no OS benefit. **Vitespen** (adjuvant): no overall benefit.
- **Reniale** (adjuvant autologous tumour-cell vaccine): claimed benefit, not adopted.
- Checkpoint inhibitors, not vaccines, are today's RCC immunotherapy.

**Prostate cancer**
- **Sipuleucel-T:** the **only approved** vaccine (below).
- **PROSTVAC-VF:** vaccinia prime, fowlpox boost, encoding PSA + TRICOM (three costimulatory molecules). Phase III **closed for futility**.
- **GVAX:** allogeneic whole-cell lines secreting GM-CSF. Phase III trials **failed**.
- Also: DCVAC/PCa (phase III negative); DNA vaccines against PAP (trials).

**Bladder cancer**
- **BCG:** intravesical live attenuated mycobacteria; standard for high-risk NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer).
- **Gene and viral therapy:** **nadofaragene firadenovec** (adenovirus carrying IFN-α2b gene; FDA [Food and Drug Administration] approved December 2022 for BCG-unresponsive CIS); **cretostimogene** (oncolytic adenovirus with GM-CSF gene).
- **N-803:** IL-15 superagonist given with BCG.
- Peptide and neoantigen vaccines: experimental only.

### Why most vaccines failed
- Tolerance to self-antigens; immunosuppressive tumour environment; dysfunctional patient DCs; bulky late disease; progression endpoints miss delayed effects.
- **Pattern: only sipuleucel-T succeeded in phase III.**

### Note on Sipuleucel-T
- **Nature:** autologous cellular immunotherapy: the patient's **CD54+ APCs (antigen-presenting cells)** activated with **PA2024**, a fusion protein of **PAP + GM-CSF**.
- **Manufacture and dosing:**
  1. Leukapheresis collects PBMCs (peripheral blood mononuclear cells).
  2. Cells incubated about 36 h with PA2024 at a central facility.
  3. Infused intravenously about 3 days after collection.
  4. **Three doses at 2-week intervals** (one month course).
- **Mechanism:** primes PAP-specific CD4 and CD8 T cells; later infusions boost.
- **IMPACT trial (Kantoff, NEJM 2010):** 512 men, 2:1 versus placebo, asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic mCRPC (metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer).
  - **OS 25.8 vs 21.7 months (HR [hazard ratio] 0.78)**, gain 4.1 months, despite placebo crossover.
  - **No change in time to progression or PSA.**
- **FDA approval April 2010:** first therapeutic vaccine for a solid tumour.
- **Indication:** asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic mCRPC, good performance status, **no visceral metastases, no opioid need**; usually before chemotherapy.
- **Toxicity:** mild infusion reactions (fever, chills, fatigue); premedicate with paracetamol and antihistamine.
- **Limitations:** cost, logistics, no response marker; not available in India.

**Diagram to draw:** sipuleucel-T flow: leukapheresis → PA2024 loading → reinfusion → T-cell priming.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Vaccine = antigen + adjuvant; DC gives signal 1 (MHC–peptide) and signal 2 (costimulation).
> - IMA901, AGS-003, PROSTVAC, GVAX all failed in phase III.
> - Sipuleucel-T: PAP–GM-CSF; IMPACT OS 25.8 vs 21.7 months; no PSA or progression effect.
> - BCG is the original urological immunotherapy.

## Q3a. Newer methods of stone analysis and their significance in management of calculus disease.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Why analyse stones
- Analyse a stone at least once in every former (AUA [American Urological Association], EAU [European Association of Urology]); repeat if stones recur on treatment.
- Composition points to the cause: **uric acid** suggests metabolic disease, **cystine** a genetic one, **struvite** infection.

### Why wet chemical analysis is obsolete
- Detects ions, not **crystal phases**, so it cannot separate COM (calcium oxalate monohydrate, whewellite) from COD (dihydrate, weddellite), or apatite from brushite.
- High error rates; misses rare stones (2,8-DHA [2,8-dihydroxyadenine], drugs); needs a large sample.

### Newer methods
| Method | Principle | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **FTIR (Fourier-transform infrared) spectroscopy** | Each compound absorbs a characteristic infrared spectrum | Commonest reference method; small sample; identifies proteins and drugs |
| **XRD (X-ray diffraction)** | Crystal lattice diffracts X-rays in a unique pattern | Gold standard for crystals; misses amorphous material |
| **Polarising microscopy** | Crystal optics (birefringence) | Cheap; experienced observer |
| **Morpho-constitutional analysis (Daudon)** | Stereomicroscopy of surface and section + FTIR | Subtypes (e.g. type Ia COM low urine volume, Ic primary hyperoxaluria) |
| **SEM (scanning electron microscopy) ± EDX (energy-dispersive X-ray)** | Surface structure, elements | Research |
| **Micro-CT (micro-computed tomography)** | 3-D non-destructive structure | Research |
| **Raman spectroscopy** | Scattered light spectrum | Research |

### In vivo prediction
- **DECT (dual-energy CT):** two energies (e.g. 80 and 140 kVp) separate **uric acid from non–uric acid stones** with near-perfect accuracy.
- **Hounsfield units:** >1000 HU predicts SWL (shock wave lithotripsy) failure.
- Endoscopic recognition (lost when stones are dusted).

### Significance in management
- **Uric acid:** oral dissolution (potassium citrate, urine pH 6.0–6.5).
- **Struvite:** complete clearance, culture, antibiotics.
- **Cystine:** hydration, alkalinisation, tiopronin.
- **COM, brushite, cystine:** hard, SWL-resistant; prefer endoscopy.
- **Brushite:** often converts from calcium oxalate; avoid over-alkalinisation.
- **2,8-DHA:** APRT (adenine phosphoribosyltransferase) deficiency, treat with allopurinol. **Drug stones** (indinavir, triamterene): stop the drug.

**Diagram to draw:** FTIR spectrum outline and COM versus COD crystal shapes.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - FTIR and XRD are reference methods; wet chemistry is obsolete.
> - DECT separates uric acid stones in vivo.
> - Composition guides dissolution, SWL choice and prevention.

## Q3b. Enlist the steps in female to male gender reassignment surgery.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Preconditions
- Gender dysphoria diagnosed per **WPATH (World Professional Association for Transgender Health)** by an MDT (multidisciplinary team).
- **Testosterone:** chapter cites **at least 12 months** before genital surgery (clitoral growth plateaus at about 2 years). WPATH SOC8 (Standards of Care 8, 2022) relaxed this to about 6 months.
- **Urological screening:** uroflowmetry, PVR (post-void residual), renal ultrasound; treat LUTD (lower urinary tract dysfunction) first.

### Stage 1: removal of female structures
1. **Subcutaneous mastectomy** (usually first).
2. **Hysterectomy + bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy**, preferably laparoscopic; remove whole cervix ± upper third of vagina.

### Stage 2: genitoperineal transformation
3. **Vaginectomy** (needed if the urethra is lengthened):
   - Hydrodissection; circumferential introital incision.
   - Rectovaginal plane bluntly; urethrovaginal plane sharply, **no diathermy near the sphincter**.
   - Paravaginal haemostasis; levators closed in midline.
4. **Urethral lengthening, fixed (perineal) part:** vestibular mucosa and labia minora tubularised over 18 Fr; covered with bulbospongiosus.
5. **Phallus construction**, either:
   - **Metoidioplasty:** release clitoral chordee and urethral plate; lengthen urethra to the glans (preputial or labial flap ± buccal graft). Gives a 3–8 cm phallus with erection and sensation.
   - **Phalloplasty:** **RFFF (radial forearm free flap)** or ALT (anterolateral thigh) flap with **tube-in-tube urethra**; microvascular anastomosis; **clitoral nerve coapted** to a flap nerve.
6. **Urethral anastomosis** of phallic to fixed part; neomeatus at the tip.
7. **Scrotoplasty:** labia majora flaps rotated 180° in front (Ghent type). Monsplasty and perineal closure.
8. **Drainage:** **SPC (suprapubic catheter)** + urethral catheter; urethral catheter out at 14–21 days.

### Stage 3: prostheses
9. **Testicular prostheses** (17 mL) at 6 months or more.
10. **Erectile prosthesis** after 1 year, once protective sensation returns; single cylinder fixed to pubic bone (dedicated devices, e.g. ZSI 475 FTM [female-to-male]).

### Complications
- **Fistula** (phallic base), **stricture** (fixed–phallic anastomosis), hair growth, dribbling, prosthesis infection or erosion, vaginectomy bleeding or rectal/bladder injury.

**Diagram to draw:** three parts of the neourethra (native, fixed, phallic).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Mastectomy → hysterectomy/oophorectomy → vaginectomy + urethroplasty + phallus + scrotum → prostheses.
> - No urethral lengthening without vaginectomy (fistula risk).
> - Commonest stricture: fixed–phallic anastomosis.

## Q3c. Newer generation laser technologies.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Background
- **Ho:YAG (holmium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet), 2120 nm:** standard for stones and enucleation; water chromophore; 0.4 mm penetration; **photothermal**.
- Newer lasers aim at **less retropulsion, finer dust, faster work**.

### 1. High-power holmium (60–120 W)
- High frequency allows **dusting** (0.2–0.3 J × 40–80 Hz) and "popcorning"; long-pulse modes reduce retropulsion.

### 2. Pulse modulation (Moses technology and similar)
- **Moses effect:** the start of a pulse boils a **vapour channel**; vapour absorbs less light, so the rest reaches the stone.
- **Moses technology** splits each pulse in two: the first opens the channel, the second hits the target.
- Less retropulsion; may shorten **HoLEP (holmium laser enucleation of prostate)**.

### 3. TFL (thulium fibre laser), 1940 nm
- **Diode-pumped silica fibre**, at the **water absorption peak** (about 4× holmium absorption); penetration about 0.2 mm.
- **Low peak power, long pulses, very high frequency (above 2000 Hz).**
- **Thin fibres** (down to about 150 µm): more irrigation and scope deflection.
- **Stones:** finer dust, minimal retropulsion. RCTs (randomised controlled trials): better than **low-power holmium**; **equal** to 120 W pulse-modulated holmium.
- **Prostate:** ThuFLEP (thulium fibre laser enucleation).
- Compact and quiet.

### 4. Thulium:YAG (2013 nm)
- Continuous wave: ThuVARP (vaporesection), ThuVEP, **ThuLEP**; carbonisation a drawback.
- **Pulsed Tm:YAG:** newer pulsed solid-state version for lithotripsy and enucleation.

### Comparison
| | Ho:YAG | TFL |
|---|---|---|
| Wavelength | 2120 nm | 1940 nm |
| Water absorption | High | About 4× higher |
| Peak power | High | Low |
| Retropulsion | More | Minimal |
| Fibre | ≥200 µm | ≥150 µm |

### Safety (applies to all)
- **Heat follows power, not laser type:** ≤10 W in the ureter, ≤25 W in the kidney; cool irrigation and access sheath (damage above 43 °C). Eyewear for all.

### Others
- **Also know:** KTP/LBO (potassium titanyl phosphate/lithium triborate) 532 nm vaporisation; diode lasers; suction sheaths.

**Diagram to draw:** water absorption curve marking 1940 and 2120 nm.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - TFL 1940 nm: water peak, low peak power, minimal retropulsion.
> - Moses: two-part pulse through a vapour channel.
> - TFL beats low-power, equals high-power pulse-modulated holmium.
> - Heat depends on power: ≤10 W in the ureter.

## Q3d. Urolift.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Read as PUL (prostatic urethral lift, UroLift) for BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia).*

### Introduction
- A **non-ablative MIST (minimally invasive surgical therapy)**. Permanent implants pull the lateral lobes apart and open the prostatic urethra.
- **FDA (Food and Drug Administration) approval 2013**; extended to ≤100 mL in 2020.
- **Main advantage:** preserves erectile and **ejaculatory** function.

### Implant and mechanism
- Spring-loaded implant with T-shaped ends:
  - **Capsular tab:** nitinol.
  - **Urethral end:** stainless steel (resists encrustation).
  - **Connector:** polyethylene terephthalate suture under tension.
- Delivered through a **19-gauge needle** from a cystoscopic device.
- The gland is more compliant than the capsule, so tension compresses the lobe outward; later local atrophy and fibrosis. MRI (magnetic resonance imaging)-conditional.

### Indications
- **AUA (American Urological Association): prostate 30–80 mL, no obstructing median lobe** (confirm by cystoscopy or ultrasound). EAU (European Association of Urology): <70 mL, no middle lobe, men wanting ejaculation preserved.
- Men wishing to stop medication.
- **Avoid:** obstructing median lobe, large gland, active infection.

### Technique
1. Local anaesthesia; rigid cystoscope.
2. Compress lateral lobe, fire needle, deploy implant; repeat on the other side.
3. **Usually 4–6 implants**, anterolateral, from bladder neck to apex, avoiding the neurovascular bundles.
4. **Keep implants >1.5 cm distal to the bladder neck** (encrustation).
5. Most go home without a catheter.

### Evidence
| | **L.I.F.T. (2013)** | **BPH6 (2015)** |
|---|---|---|
| Design | PUL vs sham, 2:1, 19 centres | PUL vs TURP (transurethral resection of prostate), 10 European centres |
| Result | IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score) about halved, twice the sham effect; Qmax (maximum flow) +4 mL/s; no new sexual dysfunction | Composite end point met 52.3% vs 20.0%; antegrade ejaculation 100% vs 60.6% |
| Durability | 13.6% surgical retreatment at 5 years | TURP better IPSS, Qmax, PVR |

### Complications
- Transient dysuria, haematuria, urgency (<2 weeks); encrustation; rare pelvic haematoma; device malfunction.
- **Higher retreatment than TURP.**

**Diagram to draw:** axial prostate with implants pulling lateral lobes to the capsule.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 30–80 mL, no median lobe; local anaesthesia.
> - Ejaculation preserved; L.I.F.T. retreatment 13.6% at 5 years.
> - TURP wins on IPSS and flow.

## Q3e. Define Marginal donor. Mention components of Remuzzi scoring system for preemptive renal biopsy of marginal donor kidneys.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2016-Summer Paper 3, Q3f)

*"Preemptive" is read as pre-implantation (procurement) biopsy.*

### Definition
- A **marginal (extended) donor** is one whose kidney carries a **higher risk of DGF (delayed graft function) and shorter graft survival** than a standard donor. Such kidneys are used because of organ shortage.
- Still better survival than dialysis (Ojo, 2001).

### Categories
- **ECD (expanded criteria donor), UNOS (United Network for Organ Sharing):**
  - Age **60 or over**, or
  - Age **50–59 with 2 or more of:** hypertension, **creatinine >1.5 mg/dL**, death from **CVA (cerebrovascular accident)**.
  - Graft-loss risk about 1.7 times that of a standard donor.
- **KDPI (Kidney Donor Profile Index) >85%** replaced ECD in the US (2014); 10 donor factors (age, height, weight, ethnicity, hypertension, diabetes, cause of death, creatinine, HCV [hepatitis C virus], DCD).
- **Others:** DCD (donation after circulatory death); donor AKI (acute kidney injury); HCV-positive donors; older or hypertensive living donors.

### Remuzzi score (Karpinski/Remuzzi)
- Pre-implantation biopsy (ideally at least 25 glomeruli), read on rapid sections.
- **Four components, each 0–3: total 0–12.**

| Component | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Glomerular global sclerosis** | None | <20% | 20–50% | >50% |
| **Tubular atrophy** | None | <20% | 20–50% | >50% |
| **Interstitial fibrosis** | None | <20% | 20–50% | >50% |
| **Arterial and arteriolar narrowing** | None | Wall thickness < lumen diameter | Wall = or slightly > lumen | Wall far > lumen, severe narrowing or occlusion |

### Allocation by score
| Total | Grade | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **0–3** | Mild | **Single** kidney transplant |
| **4–6** | Moderate | **Dual** kidney transplant |
| **7–12** | Severe | **Discard** |

- Remuzzi (NEJM 2006): biopsy-guided older-donor kidneys, single or dual, had graft survival similar to young-donor kidneys.
- **Limitations:** sampling error; wedge biopsy overestimates sclerosis; interobserver variation.

### Optimising marginal kidneys
- Short cold ischaemia; **HMP (hypothermic machine perfusion)** reduces DGF; old-for-old matching; informed consent.

**Diagram to draw:** Remuzzi grid with four compartments scored 0–3.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - ECD: ≥60, or 50–59 with 2 of hypertension, creatinine >1.5, CVA death.
> - Remuzzi: glomeruli, tubules, interstitium, vessels; 0–3 each.
> - 0–3 single, 4–6 dual, ≥7 discard.

## Q3f. PARP inhibitors in Prostate cancer.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- **PARP (poly-ADP-ribose polymerase)** repairs single-strand DNA breaks. **PARPIs (PARP inhibitors):** olaparib, rucaparib, niraparib, talazoparib.

### Mechanism: synthetic lethality
- **Catalytic inhibition** and **PARP trapping** on DNA leave single-strand breaks unrepaired.
- At replication these become **double-strand breaks**, normally repaired by **HRR (homologous recombination repair)**.
- Tumours lacking HRR (e.g. **BRCA2** loss) die; normal cells survive.
- **AR (androgen receptor)–PARP synergy:** AR maintains HRR genes, so AR blockade adds repair deficiency: basis for PARPI + ARPI (androgen receptor pathway inhibitor).

### Who benefits: testing
- **HRR alterations in mCRPC (metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer): up to 20–25%.** Genes: **BRCA2 (largest benefit)**, BRCA1, ATM (weak), PALB2, CHEK2, CDK12.
- **Germline testing** in all metastatic disease, strong family history or Ashkenazi ancestry; **somatic testing** on tissue or ctDNA (circulating tumour DNA), where clonal haematopoiesis can mislead.

### Key trials
| Trial | Setting | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **PROfound** | Olaparib vs enzalutamide/abiraterone after ARPI | BRCA/ATM cohort: imaging PFS (progression-free survival) **7.4 vs 3.6 months (HR [hazard ratio] 0.34)**; OS (overall survival) 18.5 vs 15.1 months |
| **TRITON3** | Rucaparib vs physician's choice | BRCA: PFS 11.2 vs 6.4 months (HR 0.50); no ATM benefit |
| **PROpel** | Olaparib + abiraterone, first-line mCRPC | OS 42.1 vs 34.7 months (P = 0.054, not significant); greatest in BRCA |

- **Also know:** TOPARP-A/B, TRITON2, GALAHAD (phase II).

### Approvals (FDA [Food and Drug Administration])
- **Olaparib monotherapy:** HRR-mutated mCRPC after ARPI (2020).
- **Rucaparib:** BRCA-mutated mCRPC.
- **Olaparib + abiraterone; niraparib + abiraterone:** BRCA-mutated mCRPC (2023).
- **Talazoparib + enzalutamide:** HRR-mutated mCRPC (2023).

### Toxicity
- **Anaemia** (commonest grade 3–4; monitor counts), fatigue, nausea; rare **MDS/AML (myelodysplastic syndrome/acute myeloid leukaemia)**, pulmonary embolism.

### Recent advances
- **mCSPC (metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer)** trials: AMPLITUDE, TALAPRO-3; ATR inhibitors for ATM loss.
