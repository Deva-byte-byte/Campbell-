## Q1. Discuss the pathology, etiology, Clinical features diagnostic evaluation and treatment of retroperitoneal fibrosis.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(medical management also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 2, Q3e)

### Introduction
- RPF (retroperitoneal fibrosis) is a rare inflammatory fibrosis encasing the aorta, IVC (inferior vena cava) and ureters.
- Incidence about 1 per 200,000–500,000 a year; age 40–60; male:female 2–3:1.

### Etiology

| Idiopathic (about 70%) | Secondary (about 30%) |
|---|---|
| Part of **chronic periaortitis**, a large-vessel vasculitis | **Drugs** (commonest identifiable cause): methysergide and other ergots, β-blockers, phenacetin |
| Proposed antigen: **ceroid** (oxidised lipid in plaque) | **Malignancy:** lymphoma (commonest), myeloma, carcinoid, pancreas, prostate, sarcoma |
| Probably autoimmune: membranous glomerulonephritis, ankylosing spondylitis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis; ANA (antinuclear antibody) positivity predicts relapse | Radiotherapy; asbestos |
| Part of multifocal fibrosclerosis: sclerosing mediastinitis and cholangitis, orbital pseudotumour, Riedel thyroiditis | Infection: tuberculosis, actinomyces, gonorrhoea, schistosomiasis |

- Many idiopathic cases are now classed as **IgG4 (immunoglobulin G4)-related disease**.

### Pathology
- **Gross:** flat, tan, dense plaque centred on the **distal aorta at L4–L5**, wrapping the ureters; may invade ureter or psoas.
- **Histology changes with stage:**
  - Early (active): collagen with capillary proliferation and lymphocytes, plasma cells and fibroblasts.
  - Late (burnt-out): acellular, avascular sheets of collagen.
- **Trap:** malignant RPF looks identical except for small islands of tumour cells, so take **deep, multiple biopsies**.
- **Mechanism of obstruction:** extrinsic compression and loss of ureteric peristalsis; the lumen stays patent.

### Clinical features
- **Dull lower back or flank pain** (commonest); weight loss, anorexia, nausea, malaise, fever, hypertension.
- About half present with **uraemia** or oliguria/anuria; symptoms last 4–6 months before diagnosis.
- **Extension:** IVC (leg oedema, DVT [deep venous thrombosis]); renal vein (gross haematuria); spermatic cord and scrotum. Examination is usually unrevealing.

### Diagnostic evaluation
**Laboratory**
- Raised ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) and CRP (C-reactive protein) in half to two-thirds; leucocytosis; anaemia; raised creatinine; ANA; serum IgG4.

**Imaging**

| Test | Findings and role |
|---|---|
| CT (computed tomography) / CT urography | Homogeneous soft-tissue mass enveloping aorta, IVC and ureters; **medial deviation** of proximal and mid-ureters with smooth tapering |
| Retrograde pyelography | When renal function is poor |
| MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) | Low T1; **high T2 signal means active disease**; enhancement falls with treatment |
| **PET-CT (positron emission tomography)** | **Most sensitive test of activity**; guides biopsy and follow-up |
| Renography | Split function (repair or nephrectomy) |

- **Biopsy** (CT-guided or at ureterolysis) if malignancy is suspected; may be omitted with no malignancy history, no lymphadenopathy and typical imaging.

### Treatment

```
RPF with hydronephrosis
  -> Relieve obstruction (stents; nephrostomy if anuric or septic)
  -> Treat post-obstructive diuresis
  -> Stop culprit drug; exclude malignancy
  -> Active disease: steroids (+/- second-line drugs)
  -> Failure or burnt-out fibrosis: ureterolysis
```

**1. Initial management**
- Emergent **stents or PCN (percutaneous nephrostomy)**; nephrostomy if critically ill with little urine. Replace fluid during **post-obstructive diuresis**.

**2. Medical therapy**

| Agent | Role and regimen |
|---|---|
| **Prednisone** (first line) | 60 mg daily (about 1 mg/kg), tapered to 5 mg, up to 2 years. About 80% respond; relapse on tapering 25–50% |
| Azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclophosphamide, ciclosporin, colchicine | Second line or steroid-sparing |
| Tamoxifen; medroxyprogesterone | Thought to inhibit fibroblasts |
| **Rituximab** | Refractory or IgG4-related disease |

- Steroids work on the cellular, active phase: pain settles in days and the mass shrinks.
- **Vaglio RCT (randomised controlled trial; Lancet 2011):** relapse 17% with prednisone vs 50% with tamoxifen.
- Drainage plus medical therapy clears obstruction in about two-thirds; only about **15% need ureterolysis**.

**3. Surgical ureterolysis**
- For failed medical therapy, burnt-out fibrosis or tissue diagnosis. **Bilateral**, even if hydronephrosis is unilateral; open, laparoscopic or robotic.
- Steps:
  1. Pre-place stents to identify the ureters.
  2. Mobilise the colon; take deep biopsies for frozen section.
  3. Start at the distal, non-dilated ureter.
  4. "Split and roll": right-angle clamp between ureter and plaque; incise the fibrosis over it.
  5. Close any ureterotomy with absorbable suture.
  6. **Separate the ureter from the fibrotic bed so it cannot be re-encased.**

| Protection method | How |
|---|---|
| Intraperitonealisation | Ureter lateralised; peritoneum closed behind it |
| Lateral retroperitoneal placement | Ureter displaced laterally (equivalent results) |
| **Omental wrap** | Omentum split on the gastroepiploic arteries, wrapped round each ureter; adds blood supply; best for extensive disease |

- Postoperative steroids may reduce recurrence.
- **Outcomes:** open about 94% stent-free at 1 year; laparoscopic about 90%; robotic with omental wrap about 86%.

**4. Other options:** long-term metallic stents (unfit); autotransplantation if ureterolysis is impossible; nephrectomy for an unrecoverable kidney.

### Follow-up
- Symptoms, ESR/CRP, creatinine, ultrasound; MRI or PET-CT for activity; remove stents once ureters drain; watch steroid side effects.

**Diagram to draw:** periaortic plaque at L4–L5 drawing both ureters medially, with an omental wrap.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Idiopathic 70% (periaortitis); methysergide and lymphoma classic secondary causes.
> - Medially deviated ureters; PET-CT best shows activity; biopsy deep.
> - Drain first, then prednisone (about 80% respond; Vaglio 2011 beat tamoxifen).
> - About 15% need bilateral ureterolysis with intraperitonealisation or omental wrap.

## Q2. Discuss Etiology, Diagnosis and Management of Upper ureteric injury.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

*Assumed meaning: injury to the upper (proximal) ureter, from the PUJ (pelviureteric junction) to the pelvic brim, from external trauma or surgery.*

### Introduction
- Ureteric trauma is uncommon: 1–2.5% of genitourinary trauma. **Iatrogenic injury is commoner overall** than external trauma.
- **The proximal ureter is the commonest site of external injury.** The distal ureter is the commonest iatrogenic site in non-urological surgery.
- Injury is often **missed** and declares itself later as urinoma, sepsis, stricture or fistula.

### Etiology
**1. External trauma**
- **Penetrating** (most): gunshot wounds up to about 90%. Blast damages the intramural blood supply up to 2 cm from the wound.
- **Blunt** (up to about 40%): rapid deceleration avulses the ureter at the **PUJ**; linked to lumbar transverse process fractures.
- **Over 90% have associated injuries** (small bowel commonest, colon, vessels, kidney 10–28%). Mortality 6–9% reflects these.

**2. Iatrogenic**

| Source | Upper-ureter relevance |
|---|---|
| **Ureteroscopy** | Commonest iatrogenic cause in some series: abrasion, perforation (0.2–2%), avulsion (<0.3%); stricture 0.3–3%. Risk: proximal stone, impacted stone, long operation, large sheath, inexperience |
| Partial nephrectomy, thermal ablation | Proximal stricture months later (lower-pole and hilar tumours) |
| Vascular (aortic) surgery | Devascularisation; up to 85% missed initially |
| Colorectal, spinal, retroperitoneal surgery | Ligation, transection, cautery |

- **Mechanisms:** ligation, transection, avulsion, devascularisation, thermal injury.

### Classification: AAST (American Association for the Surgery of Trauma) ureter scale

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Contusion or haematoma without devascularisation |
| II | Laceration <50% of circumference |
| III | Laceration ≥50% |
| IV | Complete transection with <2 cm devascularisation |
| V | Avulsion with >2 cm devascularisation |

- Advance one grade for bilateral injury, up to grade III.

### Diagnosis
**Suspicion**
- Penetrating flank or abdominal wound; high-energy deceleration.
- **Haematuria is absent in 25–45%**, so its absence does not exclude injury.
- Late or postoperative signs: fever, ileus, flank pain, leucocytosis, rising creatinine, drain or wound urine leak, urinoma.

**Intraoperative**
- Inspect the ureter at laparotomy; IV (intravenous) methylene blue or indigo carmine; on-table retrograde pyelogram. About 11% are missed at trauma laparotomy.

**Imaging**

| Test | Role |
|---|---|
| **CT urography with 10-minute delayed images** | Best test in stable patients. Signs: extravasation, non-opacified distal ureter, delayed nephrogram, urinoma |
| **Retrograde pyelography** | Most sensitive; shows site; allows stenting at once |
| Antegrade nephrostogram | When retrograde fails or gap is wide |
| One-shot IVU (intravenous urography) | Non-diagnostic in 33–100%; never rely on it |

- **Drain-fluid creatinine** far above serum creatinine confirms urine.

### Management
**Principles**
- **Stability decides timing; level and length decide the operation.**
- Repair: mobilise sparing adventitia; debride to bleeding edges; **spatulated, tension-free, watertight, stented** anastomosis with fine absorbable suture; drain; retroperitonealise; omental wrap for blast or vascular injury.
- **No bowel interposition or autotransplantation acutely.**

```
Ureteric injury suspected
 |-- Unstable -> damage control: ligate + later PCN,
 |               or exteriorised stent / cutaneous ureterostomy
 |-- Stable, found at surgery -> immediate repair over stent
 |-- Found late / postoperatively
       -> Attempt retrograde stent (+/- PCN, drain)
       -> Fails -> PCN; delayed definitive repair
```

**Repair by grade**
- **Grade I–II (contusion, partial):** double-J stent for about 6 weeks; or close a laceration transversely over a stent. Excise non-viable contused ureter.
- **Grade III–V:** formal reconstruction.

**Options for the upper ureter**

| Procedure | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Ureteroureterostomy** | Commonest acute repair; short defects (2–3 cm); spatulate 5–6 mm, 180° apart; 5-0 absorbable over double-J; up to 90% success; leak 10–24%, stricture up to 12% |
| **Ureteropyelostomy** (reimplant into pelvis) | PUJ avulsion; spatulated |
| **Ureterocalicostomy** | Pelvis or PUJ destroyed; lower-pole parenchyma resected |
| **Renal descensus** | Mobilise kidney downwards; gains up to 8 cm |
| **TUU (transureteroureterostomy)** | Delayed; long loss when the bladder cannot reach; 90–97% success. Avoid with stones, RPF (retroperitoneal fibrosis), TB (tuberculosis), urothelial cancer or radiation |
| **Ileal ureter / Yang–Monti** | Long or panureteric loss; 81–100% success. Avoid if creatinine >2 mg/dL, bladder dysfunction, inflammatory bowel disease, radiation enteritis. Acidosis about 20% |
| Appendiceal interposition | Right side; about 92% short-term success |
| **Buccal mucosa graft ureteroplasty** | Delayed long strictures; onlay with omental wrap, often robotic; about 87% success |
| **Autotransplantation** | Last resort before nephrectomy; 5–10% renal units lost |
| Nephrectomy | Rarely; unstable with unreconstructable kidney, or poor function |

**Iatrogenic endoscopic injury**
- Stop the procedure and place a **stent** (± nephrostomy); later reconstruction if stricture develops.

### Complications and follow-up
- Urinoma, abscess, leak, fistula, stricture, loss of renal function.
- Persistent leak: check stent position, take drain off suction, catheterise the bladder, then nephrostomy.
- **Follow-up:** ultrasound or diuretic renogram at 4–12 weeks and at 1 year; late stenosis can be silent.

**Diagram to draw:** spatulated ureteroureterostomy over a double-J stent.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Proximal ureter commonest site of external injury; haematuria absent in 25–45%.
> - AAST: 50% circumference (II vs III) and 2 cm devascularisation (IV vs V).
> - CT with 10-minute delays; retrograde pyelogram most sensitive.
> - Stable: immediate stented ureteroureterostomy; unstable: damage control; bowel and autotransplant only delayed.

## Q3a. Premature ejaculation.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **ISSM (International Society for Sexual Medicine) unified definition (2014):** all three needed:
  1. Ejaculation always or nearly always within **about 1 minute** of vaginal penetration (lifelong), or a clinically significant, bothersome fall in latency, often to **≤3 minutes** (acquired).
  2. Inability to delay ejaculation on all or nearly all penetrations.
  3. Negative personal consequences: distress, frustration, avoidance of intimacy.
- **AUA/SMSNA (American Urological Association/Sexual Medicine Society of North America) 2020:** lifelong PE (premature ejaculation) within about **2 minutes**.
- **Types:** lifelong, acquired, variable and subjective (normal latency).

### Aetiology
- **Lifelong:** neurobiological and genetic; 5-HT2C hyposensitivity and/or 5-HT1A hypersensitivity.
- **Acquired:** performance anxiety, relationship problems, **ED (erectile dysfunction)**, chronic prostatitis, hyperthyroidism, drug withdrawal.

### Evaluation
- Diagnosis is by **history**: latency, control, bother; lifelong or acquired.
- **IELT (intravaginal ejaculatory latency time):** self-estimate in clinic; stopwatch in trials. Population median 5.4 minutes.
- **PEDT (Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool):** 5 items; score ≥11 suggests PE, 9–10 probable PE. Also PEP (Premature Ejaculation Profile) and IPE (Index of Premature Ejaculation).
- IIEF-5 (International Index of Erectile Function) for ED; examination; TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) only if suggested.

### Management
- **Psychosexual education** for all; behavioural therapy (stop–start, squeeze).
- Treat the cause in acquired PE (ED, prostatitis, thyroid).

| Drug | Dose and timing |
|---|---|
| **Dapoxetine** (short-acting SSRI [selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor]; only licensed oral drug) | 30–60 mg, 1–3 h before intercourse |
| Paroxetine (strongest delay), sertraline, fluoxetine, citalopram | Daily, off-label; full effect 2–3 weeks; do not stop abruptly |
| Clomipramine | 12.5–50 mg daily or 3–4 h before |
| **Topical lidocaine–prilocaine** (spray licensed in Europe 2014) | 5–30 min before; condom avoids partner numbness |
| PDE5Is (phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors) | With ED, alone or with SSRI |
| Tramadol | Low evidence; addiction risk |

- SSRIs raise synaptic serotonin, which delays ejaculation. Avoid in bipolar disorder.

**Diagram to draw:** office algorithm (confirm PE, then treat cause or classify lifelong/acquired).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - ISSM: about 1 min (lifelong), about 3 min (acquired) plus poor control plus distress.
> - Lifelong is neurobiological; acquired is usually secondary.
> - Dapoxetine 30–60 mg on demand; paroxetine gives the greatest delay.

## Q3b. Desmopressin.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Pharmacology
- DDAVP (**1-deamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin**), a synthetic analogue of AVP (arginine vasopressin/antidiuretic hormone).
- Deamination and D-arginine substitution prolong action and make it a **selective V2 agonist without pressor (V1) effect**.
- **Mechanism:** V2 receptor on collecting-duct cells → cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate) → **aquaporin-2** insertion → water reabsorption → less, concentrated urine.
- Endothelial V2 action releases von Willebrand factor and factor VIII (haemostatic use).

### Formulations

| Form | Dose |
|---|---|
| Tablet | 0.2–0.4 mg at bedtime (enuresis) |
| Oral lyophilisate (melt) | 120–240 µg, about 1 h before bed |
| Low-dose nasal spray (nocturia) | 0.83 or 1.66 µg, 30 min before bed |
| Sublingual ODT (orally disintegrating tablet) | 27.7 µg women, 55.3 µg men |
| IV / subcutaneous | 0.3 µg/kg (bleeding) |

### Uses in urology
1. **Monosymptomatic nocturnal enuresis:** first line with the alarm. Rule of thirds: one-third full, one-third partial, one-third no response. Acts at once but relapses on stopping. Restrict fluids in the evening.
2. **Nocturia from nocturnal polyuria** (NPi [nocturnal polyuria index] >20–33%, age-dependent). EAU (European Association of Urology): offer below 65; low dose above 65 with ≥2 voids. Add to α-blocker for refractory nocturia in BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia).
3. **Neurogenic nocturia** (multiple sclerosis, MSA [multiple system atrophy]).
4. **Bleeding:** uraemic platelet dysfunction, von Willebrand disease (including acquired in Wilms tumour). Tachyphylaxis after about 3 doses.
5. **Diagnosis:** central versus nephrogenic DI (diabetes insipidus).

### Adverse effects
- **Hyponatraemia** (most important): headache, nausea, confusion, seizures.
- Risk factors: **age ≥65**, baseline sodium <135 mmol/L, CrCl (creatinine clearance) <60 mL/min, higher dose; women are more sensitive.
- Most events occur in the **first 2–3 weeks**.

### Monitoring and contraindications
- Sodium at baseline, then within 1 week and at 1 month (EAU adds day 3), and after dose change.
- **Avoid:** hyponatraemia, polydipsia, heart failure, SIADH (syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone), significant renal impairment, **loop diuretics**.

**Diagram to draw:** V2 receptor–cAMP–aquaporin-2 pathway in the collecting duct.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Selective V2 agonist; no pressor effect.
> - Enuresis: 0.2–0.4 mg tablet or 120–240 µg melt; rule of thirds.
> - Hyponatraemia: age ≥65, low sodium; check sodium early.

## Q3c. Snodgrass repair for Hypospadias.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- **TIP (tubularised incised plate) urethroplasty**, described by Snodgrass in 1994 (based on Rich's hinging incision, 1989).
- Used for **over 90% of distal and about 80% of midshaft repairs**.

### Principle
- A **deep midline relaxing incision** of the urethral plate widens a narrow plate, so it can be tubularised without tension.
- The incised raw area **heals by re-epithelialisation** without a graft, giving a **vertical slit-like meatus**.

### Indications and prerequisites
- Distal and midshaft hypospadias, and redo cases with a healthy plate, when curvature is mild (<30°) and corrected by degloving or plication.
- Adequate plate and glans. **Glans width <14 mm** raises complications; give preoperative testosterone.
- **POST (plate objective scoring tool):** a low ratio of neomeatus length to glans fusion length means a poor plate, which favours an inlay graft.
- Not for severe curvature needing plate division; proximal TIP is abandoned.

### Operative steps
1. Glans stay suture; tourniquet.
2. U-incision around plate and meatus; deglove; artificial erection.
3. Separate glans wings deeply from the plate.
4. **Midline incision of the plate** from the meatus to the plate end, down to corpora.
5. Tubularise over a **6–8 Fr stent** with subepithelial 6-0/7-0 absorbable sutures, from distal to proximal, stopping 2–3 mm short to leave an oval meatus.
6. **Dartos flap** (dorsal preputial) over the neourethra as a barrier.
7. Two-layer glansplasty; mucosal collar; skin closure (Byars flaps) or preputioplasty.
8. Stent 5–7 days; compressive dressing; oxybutynin for spasm.

### Complications and outcomes
- Pfistermuller meta-analysis (2015, 4,675 boys): complications 10.6%, reoperation 4.5%; **fistula 5.7%**, meatal stenosis 3.6%, stricture 1.3%.
- **Meatal stenosis** is commoner than after Mathieu. Glans dehiscence occurs.
- Proximal and redo cases do worse; a dartos layer improves results.

### Modifications
- **Dorsal inlay graft** (inner preputial skin) for a shallow plate; lateral incisions to 12 mm width.

**Diagram to draw:** cross-sections showing plate incision, tubularisation and dartos cover.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Snodgrass 1994; incise and tubularise; heals by epithelialisation.
> - Slit-like meatus; dartos barrier reduces fistula.
> - Fistula 5.7%, meatal stenosis 3.6% (Pfistermuller 2015).

## Q3d. Urological Complications post Kidney Transplantation.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- Urological complications are the **commonest surgical complications** (8–9.5%). Most arise from the **ureter and ureterovesical anastomosis**.
- The distal donor ureter depends on a single renal-artery blood supply, so ischaemia is the root cause. **Denervation** masks pain.
- A **routine stent** reduces leak and stricture; remove at **14–21 days** (earlier increases leak; later increases UTI [urinary tract infection]).

### Complications

| Complication | Key facts | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Urine leak** (1.2–8.9%; commonest early) | Distal ureteric necrosis; lost lower-pole artery; technical | Fluid creatinine far above serum. Foley + PCN (percutaneous nephrostomy) + antegrade stent; early reimplant or native ureteroureterostomy if large |
| **Ureteric stricture** (0.5–9%; commonest beyond 3 months) | Ischaemia; long cold ischaemia, rejection, BK (BK polyomavirus) and CMV (cytomegalovirus) | Nephrostomy first; balloon or laser endoureterotomy; **open reimplant, native ureteroureterostomy or Boari flap best long-term** |
| **Lymphocele** (symptomatic 0.03–26%; peak 6 weeks) | Divided lymphatics; mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin) inhibitors | Treat only if symptomatic; **laparoscopic peritoneal fenestration** best |
| VUR (vesicoureteric reflux) | 50–86% radiological; <1% significant | Treat cause; endoscopic injection low grade; reimplant |
| Haematuria | Anastomotic; persistent after biopsy = AV fistula | Self-limiting; selective embolisation |
| Stones (about 1%) | Hyperparathyroidism; calcineurin inhibitors | As native kidney; watch silent obstruction |
| UTI (commonest infection) | Stent, reflux, steroids | Treat 14–21 days if pyelonephritis |

- **Obstruction:** early is technical, oedema or compression (haematoma, lymphocele); late is stricture, stone or lymphocele.
- **Encrusted stent:** CT, nephrostomy, then PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy), RIRS (retrograde intrarenal surgery) or ESWL (shock-wave lithotripsy).
- **Lower tract:** bladder capacity recovers; BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) treated by TURP (transurethral resection of prostate) after 3 weeks, never with a stent in place.
- **Also know:** ED (erectile dysfunction) persists in 20–50%; bladder cancer risk about threefold; RCC (renal cell carcinoma) mostly in native kidneys; renal artery stenosis and thrombosis.

**Diagram to draw:** transplant kidney in iliac fossa showing leak, stricture and lymphocele sites.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Ureter is the weak point; ischaemia is the cause.
> - Leak early, stricture late; open repair best.
> - Stent out at 2–3 weeks; laparoscopic fenestration for lymphocele.

## Q3e. Pathogenesis, Clinical importance and Management of Carcinoma in situ urinary bladder.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **CIS (carcinoma in situ, Tis):** flat, non-invasive, **high-grade** urothelial carcinoma. It is not "premalignant".
- Histology: severe atypia, loss of polarity, non-cohesive pleomorphic cells, **loss of umbrella cells** (dysplasia keeps them).
- **Types:** primary (isolated, about 10%), secondary or concurrent (with papillary tumour, about 90%).

### Pathogenesis
- **Flat pathway:** dysplasia → CIS → high-grade invasive cancer.
- Driven by **TP53, RB and PTEN** loss; **no FGFR3** mutation (unlike low-grade papillary tumours).
- **Field change:** multifocal; spreads to prostatic urethra, distal ureters and upper tract.

### Clinical importance
- Untreated, **40–83% progress** to muscle invasion. Primary CIS progresses in >50%.
- "CIS alone" hides invasion at cystectomy in up to 20%.
- **Second strongest prognostic factor after grade**; any CIS makes NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer) **high risk**.
- About 80% have **irritative symptoms**; it mimics cystitis.

### Diagnosis
- **Urine cytology:** most useful in CIS, because the cells are poorly cohesive. Positive cytology with normal cystoscopy needs mapping.
- Cystoscopy: red velvety patch or normal-looking mucosa; **blue-light cystoscopy** (hexaminolevulinate) detects about one-third more CIS; NBI (narrow-band imaging).
- **Biopsies:** cold-cup from suspicious and random sites; prostatic urethra; upper tract imaging.

### Management
- **BCG (bacillus Calmette–Guérin) is first line:**
  - Induction: 6 weekly instillations.
  - **Maintenance** (SWOG [Southwest Oncology Group] 2000: 3 weekly doses at 3 and 6 months, then 6-monthly for 3 years): median recurrence-free survival 76.8 vs 35.7 months.
  - Complete response up to 84%; durable in about half.
- **Persistent CIS at 3 months:** second BCG course; 30–50% more respond.
- **BCG-unresponsive** (persistent or recurrent CIS within 12 months of adequate BCG): **radical cystectomy**.
- Unfit or unwilling: **pembrolizumab** (KEYNOTE-057: complete response 41%), nadofaragene firadenovec, gemcitabine–docetaxel.
- Prostatic urethral CIS: TURP plus BCG; ductal involvement needs cystoprostatectomy.

### Follow-up
- Cystoscopy and cytology 3-monthly for 2 years, then 6-monthly; upper-tract imaging.

**Diagram to draw:** flat versus papillary pathways of bladder cancer.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Flat, high-grade; TP53/RB; no FGFR3.
> - Untreated 40–83% progress; always high risk.
> - BCG with maintenance; cystectomy if BCG-unresponsive.

## Q3f. Laboratory Diagnosis of Genitourinary Tuberculosis.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2022-Summer Paper 3, Q2, as part of renal TB)

### Principle
- GUTB (genitourinary tuberculosis) is paucibacillary with intermittent shedding, so **multiple samples** are needed.
- **Hierarchy of proof:** positive culture or DNA → caseating granulomas on histology → clinical picture with response to treatment.

### Specimen
- **Early-morning first-void urine, 3–5 samples on consecutive days**, sent at once (acidic urine kills bacilli). Avoid prior fluoroquinolones.
- Also tissue, pus, FNAC (fine-needle aspiration cytology) or semen.

### Tests

| Test | Method | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Urine routine** | Microscopy | **Sterile pyuria** ± haematuria in >90% |
| **ZN (Ziehl–Neelsen) smear** | Carbol fuchsin, acid-alcohol, methylene blue: red bacilli. Auramine fluorescence is more sensitive | Sensitivity <50%; saprophytic mycobacteria give false positives |
| **Culture: gold standard** | Decontaminate and concentrate. **LJ (Löwenstein–Jensen)** solid: 4–6 weeks. **MGIT (mycobacteria growth indicator tube)** liquid: about 10 days | 80–90% sensitivity with ideal technique; gives DST (drug-susceptibility testing); monitors treatment |
| **NAAT (nucleic acid amplification test): GeneXpert MTB/RIF** | Automated cartridge PCR (polymerase chain reaction); about 2 hours | Detects MTB and **rifampicin resistance** (MDR [multidrug-resistant] TB surrogate). **Xpert Ultra** is more sensitive. Cannot monitor treatment |
| TrueNat | Indian chip-based NAAT | NTEP (National TB Elimination Programme) |
| **LPA (line probe assay)** | DNA strip hybridisation | Rifampicin, isoniazid or second-line drug resistance |
| **LF-LAM (lateral-flow lipoarabinomannan)** | Urine antigen strip, <30 min | HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) with CD4 <200 or advanced disease (WHO 2019) |
| **Histopathology** | Biopsy (bladder, epididymis) | Caseating granulomas; NAAT on tissue raises yield |
| **TST (tuberculin skin test)** | 5 TU (tuberculin units) PPD intradermal; read induration at 48–72 h | Shows infection, not disease; BCG false positive |
| **IGRA (interferon-γ release assay)** | ESAT-6/CFP-10 blood test (QuantiFERON, T-SPOT) | Not affected by BCG; cannot separate latent and active |

### Supportive tests
- ESR, blood count, renal function, HIV, blood sugar; sputum and chest radiograph (lung TB in up to one-fifth).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 3–5 early-morning urine cultures; culture is the gold standard.
> - GeneXpert detects rifampicin resistance in 2 hours; cannot monitor treatment.

#### Q1. Discuss the pathology, etiology, Clinical features diagnostic evaluation and treatment of retroperitoneal fibrosis. (LAQ)

- Idiopathic 70% (periaortitis); methysergide and lymphoma classic secondary causes.
- Medially deviated ureters; PET-CT best shows activity; biopsy deep.
- Drain first, then prednisone (about 80% respond; Vaglio 2011 beat tamoxifen).
- About 15% need bilateral ureterolysis with intraperitonealisation or omental wrap.

#### Q2. Discuss Etiology, Diagnosis and Management of Upper ureteric injury. (LAQ)

- Proximal ureter commonest site of external injury; haematuria absent in 25–45%.
- AAST: 50% circumference (II vs III) and 2 cm devascularisation (IV vs V).
- CT with 10-minute delays; retrograde pyelogram most sensitive.
- Stable: immediate stented ureteroureterostomy; unstable: damage control; bowel and autotransplant only delayed.

#### Q3a. Premature ejaculation. (SAQ)

- ISSM: about 1 min (lifelong), about 3 min (acquired) plus poor control plus distress.
- Lifelong is neurobiological; acquired is usually secondary.
- Dapoxetine 30–60 mg on demand; paroxetine gives the greatest delay.

#### Q3b. Desmopressin. (SAQ)

- Selective V2 agonist; no pressor effect.
- Enuresis: 0.2–0.4 mg tablet or 120–240 µg melt; rule of thirds.
- Hyponatraemia: age ≥65, low sodium; check sodium early.

#### Q3c. Snodgrass repair for Hypospadias. (SAQ)

- Snodgrass 1994; incise and tubularise; heals by epithelialisation.
- Slit-like meatus; dartos barrier reduces fistula.
- Fistula 5.7%, meatal stenosis 3.6% (Pfistermuller 2015).

#### Q3d. Urological Complications post Kidney Transplantation. (SAQ)

- Ureter is the weak point; ischaemia is the cause.
- Leak early, stricture late; open repair best.
- Stent out at 2–3 weeks; laparoscopic fenestration for lymphocele.

#### Q3e. Pathogenesis, Clinical importance and Management of Carcinoma in situ urinary bladder. (SAQ)

- Flat, high-grade; TP53/RB; no FGFR3.
- Untreated 40–83% progress; always high risk.
- BCG with maintenance; cystectomy if BCG-unresponsive.

#### Q3f. Laboratory Diagnosis of Genitourinary Tuberculosis. (SAQ)

- 3–5 early-morning urine cultures; culture is the gold standard.
- GeneXpert detects rifampicin resistance in 2 hours; cannot monitor treatment.
- TST and IGRA show infection, not active disease.
