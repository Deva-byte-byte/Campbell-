## Q1. Etiopathogenesis and management of Upper tract TCC
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(management also answered in 2016-Summer Paper 3, Q1)

*TCC (transitional cell carcinoma) is taken as UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma).*

### Introduction
- UTUC is 5–10% of urothelial cancers. Pelvicalyceal tumours are about twice as common as ureteric ones. Peak age 70–90; male:female 2:1.
- About 60% are invasive at diagnosis, because the upper tract has a thin muscle wall. Bladder recurrence follows in 22–47%.

### Etiopathogenesis
| Factor | Key facts and mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Tobacco** | Most important modifiable factor; risk 2.5–7-fold. Aromatic amines are activated to mutagenic N-hydroxylamines |
| **Aristolochic acid** | Balkan endemic nephropathy (UTUC 40% of renal tumours there) and Chinese herb nephropathy. Aristolactam–DNA adducts cause A:T → T:A transversions in TP53. Familial but **not inherited**; often bilateral |
| **Lynch syndrome** (HNPCC, hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer) | Germline MMR (mismatch repair) mutation: MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2. Risk ~14-fold, highest with MSH2; found in 5–9% of screened UTUC |
| **Occupation** | "Amino tumours": benzidine, β-naphthylamine (dyes, rubber, petrochemicals); latency up to 20 years |
| **Analgesic abuse** | Phenacetin: papillary necrosis; capillarosclerosis is pathognomonic |
| **Arsenic** | Well water in south-west Taiwan (blackfoot disease) |

- **Also know:** cyclophosphamide (acrolein); chronic infection and stones (squamous carcinoma); alcohol.
- **Lynch screening:** suspect if age <60, or a personal or family history of Lynch-spectrum cancer (colon, endometrium). Test the tumour for MSI (microsatellite instability) or MMR immunohistochemistry, then germline testing.
- **Pathogenesis:** two theories both operate. **Intraluminal seeding** (one clone spreading downstream) explains frequent bladder recurrence. **Field effect** (carcinogen in urine, independent clones) explains multifocality.
- Sequence: hyperplasia → dysplasia → CIS (carcinoma in situ). Variant histology in ~25%. Molecular subtypes include FGFR3-mutant (low grade), TP53/MDM2-mutant and hypermutated (Lynch).

### Diagnosis
- Haematuria (56–98%), flank pain (20–32%).
- **CTU (computed tomography urography):** best test (sensitivity ~92%). **Cystoscopy** always (concurrent bladder cancer 17%). Selective cytology.
- **Flexible URS (ureteroscopy) with biopsy:** grade is accurate in ~90%, but **stage cannot be assessed**. Staging is TNM (renal parenchymal invasion by a pelvic tumour = T3).

### Risk stratification (EAU, European Association of Urology)
| Low risk: all present | High risk: any one |
|---|---|
| Unifocal | Multifocal |
| Tumour <2 cm | Tumour >2 cm |
| Low-grade cytology and biopsy | High-grade cytology or biopsy |
| No invasion on CTU | Hydronephrosis |
| — | Previous radical cystectomy; variant histology |

- AUA/SUO (American Urological Association/Society of Urologic Oncology) 2023 splits each group into favourable and unfavourable.

### Management
**A. Kidney-sparing surgery**
- **Elective:** low-risk disease, whatever the other kidney. **Imperative:** solitary kidney, bilateral tumours, CKD (chronic kidney disease), unfit patient.
- **Ureteroscopic ablation:** biopsy, then holmium or thulium laser. Closed system; avoid circumferential ablation (stricture).
- **Percutaneous resection:** large pelvic or lower-pole tumours; risk of tract seeding.
- **Distal ureterectomy** with reimplantation (psoas hitch, Boari flap): gives a staging specimen.
- Adjuvant upper-tract BCG (bacillus Calmette–Guérin) or MMC (mitomycin C); **UGN-101** mitomycin gel for low grade.
- Lifelong endoscopic surveillance: upper-tract recurrence in about half.

**B. RNU (radical nephroureterectomy) with bladder cuff: standard for high risk**
- Clip the ureter early; kidney outside Gerota's fascia; never open the urinary tract; remove the **whole intramural ureter and cuff** (stump recurrence 30–75% if left); bag extraction.
- Open, laparoscopic and robotic routes are oncologically equivalent. Abandoned: endoscopic "pluck", laparoscopic stapling.
- **Template lymphadenectomy** for high risk: hilar plus paracaval (right) or para-aortic (left); pelvic nodes for lower ureter.
- **Single postoperative intravesical instillation:** ODMIT-C (MMC 40 mg) cut bladder recurrence 28% → 16% at 1 year.

**C. Perioperative chemotherapy**
- **POUT (2020):** adjuvant platinum after RNU for pT2–T4 or N+: 3-year DFS (disease-free survival) **71% vs 46%**. Gemcitabine–cisplatin; carboplatin if creatinine clearance 30–49.
- **Neoadjuvant** platinum when post-RNU renal function would rule out cisplatin.
- **Also know:** adjuvant nivolumab (CheckMate 274) if platinum-ineligible, but no clear benefit in the UTUC subgroup.

**D. Metastatic disease (brief)**
- First line: **enfortumab vedotin + pembrolizumab** (EV-302, 2023): overall survival ~31 vs ~16 months versus platinum chemotherapy. Alternative: gemcitabine–platinum, then avelumab maintenance.
- Later: pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-045), erdafitinib for FGFR3 alterations. Node-positive: chemotherapy first, then consolidative surgery.

### Follow-up
- After RNU: cystoscopy and cytology from 3 months; CTU yearly. After kidney-sparing: URS and CTU at closer intervals for life.

**Diagram to draw:** RNU specimen with bladder cuff, beside lymph node templates.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Tobacco, aristolochic acid (A:T → T:A in TP53) and Lynch (MSH2) are the key causes.
> - EAU low risk needs every favourable feature; one adverse feature means high risk.
> - RNU with complete bladder cuff; single postoperative MMC (ODMIT-C 28% → 16%).
> - POUT: adjuvant platinum, 3-year DFS 71% vs 46%; EV-302 for metastatic disease.

## Q2. Use of intestinal segments in urology
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered as an SAQ in 2022-Summer Paper 1, Q3a)

### Introduction
- Bowel is used to divert urine (conduit, continent pouch, neobladder), enlarge the bladder (augmentation), replace the ureter and make catheterisable channels.
- Choice depends on renal function, previous surgery or radiation, bowel disease, mesenteric length and the reconstruction needed.

### Choice of segment
| Segment | Electrolyte disturbance | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stomach** | Hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis | Secretes H⁺ and Cl⁻; less mucus; for renal failure, short gut, dense adhesions | **Haematuria–dysuria syndrome**, ulcers |
| **Jejunum** | Hyponatraemic, hypochloraemic, **hyperkalaemic acidosis** | Outside radiation field | Severe salt loss; avoid |
| **Ileum** | **Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis** | Mobile, constant blood supply; default conduit, ureteric replacement | B12 and bile-salt loss; obstruction ~10% |
| **Colon** | Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis | Easy antireflux tunnel; transverse colon outside pelvic radiation field | Diarrhoea if ileocaecal valve used; obstruction ~5% |
| Appendix | — | Mitrofanoff catheterisable channel | — |

### Technical principles
- **Preparation:** ERAS (enhanced recovery after surgery); routine mechanical bowel preparation not needed. Assess renal function.
- **Mesentery:** transilluminate, keep a major arcade, wide base; avoid watershed areas (Griffiths point at splenic flexure, Sudeck point at rectosigmoid).
- **Bowel anastomosis:** good blood supply, no tension, no spillage, close mesenteric window.
- **Detubularisation** (Kock): opening the bowel on its antimesenteric border and refolding it gives a spherical reservoir. This **abolishes coordinated contractions** and increases volume for the same surface area, so pressure stays low (Laplace's law).

### Ureterointestinal anastomoses
| Technique | Type | Stricture |
|---|---|---|
| **Bricker** | Refluxing, end to side | 4–22% (mean 6%) |
| **Wallace** | Refluxing, ureters joined end to end | ~3% (lowest) |
| Leadbetter–Clarke, Goodwin | Nonrefluxing tunnel in colon | 8–14% |

- Principles: spatulate, preserve periureteric tissue, tension-free, mucosa to mucosa, soft stents (leak 3–5% without). Bring the bowel to the ureter, not the ureter to the bowel.
- Fix and retroperitonealise the anastomosis. The left ureter passes behind the sigmoid mesentery; avoid angulation under the IMA (inferior mesenteric artery), a common stricture site.
- Reflux with normal unobstructed ureters has not been shown to harm the adult kidney; antireflux tunnels stricture more.

### Applications
- **Incontinent diversion:** ileal conduit (10–15 cm ileum, 10–15 cm from ileocaecal valve); colon conduit after pelvic radiation.
- **Continent cutaneous pouch** (Indiana, Mainz, Kock) with appendix or tapered ileum channel.
- **Orthotopic neobladder** (Studer, Hautmann): about 50–70 cm detubularised ileum joined to the urethra.
- **Ureterosigmoidostomy:** now rare (acidosis, cancer).
- **Augmentation cystoplasty** ("clam" ileocystoplasty, ~25 cm ileum) for refractory neurogenic or small contracted bladder; needs CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation).
- **Ileal ureter:** isoperistaltic ileum for long defects. Contraindicated if creatinine >2 mg/dL, bladder outlet obstruction, inflammatory bowel disease or radiation enteritis.
- **Catheterisable channels:** Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy, Yang–Monti ileal tube.

### Stomas
- **Protruding (nipple, "rosebud") stoma** for appliance wearers: less stenosis, better appliance fit. **Flush stoma** for continent pouches that are catheterised.
- **Loop-end (Turnbull) stoma** for obese patients with a short mesentery. Site is marked before surgery, through the rectus muscle.

### Renal function needed for continent diversion
- Creatinine <2 mg/dL; GFR (glomerular filtration rate) >35 mL/min; urine pH ≤5.8 after ammonium chloride load; urine osmolality ≥600 mOsm/kg after water deprivation; minimal proteinuria.

### Complications
**Metabolic** (worse with longer segment, longer dwell time, poorer renal function)
- **Hyperchloraemic acidosis (ileum, colon):** NH₄⁺ replaces Na⁺ on the Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger, coupled with Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchange, so ammonium chloride is absorbed. Treat with sodium bicarbonate or citrate; chlorpromazine or nicotinic acid limit chloride transport.
- **Hypokalaemia:** worse with colon; replace potassium before correcting acidosis (paralysis risk).
- **Jejunal syndrome:** treat with sodium chloride and thiazide. **Gastric alkalosis:** H2 blockers or PPI (proton-pump inhibitor).
- **Altered sensorium:** hyperammonaemia (drain the pouch, lactulose), hypomagnesaemia.
- **Drug reabsorption:** phenytoin, methotrexate; drain pouches during chemotherapy.
- **Osteomalacia:** acidosis, vitamin D resistance, calcium loss; growth retardation in children.
- **Nutrition:** B12 deficiency (give parenteral B12 after 5–7 years of ileal diversion), bile-salt diarrhoea.

**Other**
- Infection (bacteriuria in about three-quarters of conduits; treat pure Proteus or Pseudomonas).
- **Stones:** colon conduit 3–4%, ileal conduit 10–12%, continent reservoir ~20% (mucus, staples).
- **Augment perforation:** surgical emergency; regular CIC prevents it.
- Ureterointestinal stricture (lifelong risk), renal deterioration, bowel obstruction, stomal stenosis and parastomal hernia.
- **Cancer:** ureterosigmoidostomy 6–29% after 10–20 years (endoscopy from 5 years); augments (endoscopy from 10 years).

### Follow-up
- Lifelong: electrolytes, bicarbonate, renal function, B12, upper-tract imaging (ultrasound or CT) for stricture and stones; endoscopy of colonic or augmented segments as above.

### Recent advances
- Robotic intracorporeal diversion (complications similar to open in experienced hands); tissue-engineered bladder (experimental).

**Diagram to draw:** ileal conduit with mesenteric arcade and Wallace anastomosis.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Ileum and colon: hyperchloraemic acidosis (NH₄Cl absorption).
> - Jejunum: hyperkalaemia; stomach: hypochloraemic alkalosis.
> - Continent diversion: GFR >35, pH ≤5.8, osmolality ≥600.
> - Wallace has the lowest stricture rate; detubularisation gives low pressure.

## Q3a. GFR clinical assessment
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(donor setting answered in 2019-Summer Paper 1, Q3a)

### Definition
- GFR (glomerular filtration rate) is the plasma volume filtered by both kidneys per minute; normal about 120 mL/min/1.73 m². It is the standard index of kidney function. The ideal marker is freely filtered and neither secreted nor reabsorbed.

### Methods
**1. Serum creatinine and eGFR (estimated GFR)**
- Creatinine comes from muscle; freely filtered but also secreted, so it **overestimates GFR** as function falls.
- Varies with **muscle mass, diet and age**; trimethoprim blocks secretion.
- **CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) 2021 equation:** age and sex, **no race term**; currently recommended. MDRD (Modification of Diet in Renal Disease) and Cockcroft–Gault are older.

**2. Cystatin C**
- 13-kDa protein from all nucleated cells; freely filtered, fully reabsorbed and broken down. Independent of muscle mass; affected by thyroid state, steroids, inflammation.
- **Combined creatinine–cystatin C equation** is the most accurate estimate.

**3. 24-hour creatinine clearance**
- (Urine creatinine × urine volume) ÷ serum creatinine. Collection errors; secretion causes overestimation.

**4. mGFR (measured GFR): reference**
| Marker | Method |
|---|---|
| **Inulin** | Urinary clearance; gold standard, research only |
| **Iohexol, iothalamate** | Plasma or urinary clearance |
| **⁵¹Cr-EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid)** | Plasma clearance |
| **⁹⁹ᵐTc-DTPA (diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid)** | Plasma sampling or gamma camera (Gates method); also gives **split function** |

### Clinical use
- **CKD (chronic kidney disease) staging (KDIGO, Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes):** G1 ≥90, G2 60–89, G3a 45–59, G3b 30–44, G4 15–29, G5 <15 mL/min/1.73 m², always with albuminuria (ACR, albumin-to-creatinine ratio).
- **Living donor (KDIGO 2017):** ≥90 accept, 60–89 individualise, <60 decline; confirm with mGFR.
- **Urology:** drug and contrast dosing, cisplatin eligibility, continent diversion (>35 mL/min), partial versus radical nephrectomy.
- **Acute change:** eGFR is invalid when function is unstable; AKI (acute kidney injury) uses creatinine change and urine output. NGAL (neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin) is emerging.

**Diagram to draw:** plasma tracer clearance curve after a single injection.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Creatinine overestimates GFR (secretion); CKD-EPI 2021 is race-free.
> - Creatinine–cystatin C combined equation is most accurate.
> - Inulin is gold standard; DTPA gives GFR and split function.

## Q3b. Neuromodulation
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 4, Q1)

*Taken as neuromodulation for lower urinary tract dysfunction.*

### Definition and mechanism
- Electrical stimulation of a peripheral nerve that changes central reflex pathways controlling the bladder. It acts on **afferents**, not directly on muscle.
- **OAB (overactive bladder):** afferent input damps PMC (pontine micturition centre) firing, so fewer involuntary contractions.
- **Non-obstructive retention:** restores awareness of filling and relaxes the external sphincter. Hence one therapy treats opposite disorders.
- Exclude anatomical causes first (obstruction, stones, cancer).

### Modalities
| Modality | Method | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| **SNM (sacral neuromodulation)** | Tined lead in **S3** foramen + implanted generator | Refractory OAB, NOUR (non-obstructive urinary retention), faecal incontinence |
| **PTNS (percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation)** | 34-gauge needle above medial malleolus | Refractory OAB |
| Implantable tibial devices | Small device over tibial nerve | OAB without clinic visits |
| Pudendal stimulation | Lead at Alcock's canal | SNM failure (off-label) |
| **SARS (sacral anterior root stimulator)** | Anterior S2–S4 roots + dorsal rhizotomy | Complete spinal cord injury |

### Sacral neuromodulation
- **Third-line** for OAB (AUA, American Urological Association; EAU, European Association of Urology).
- **Test phase:** PNE (percutaneous nerve evaluation, 5–7 days) or staged tined lead (up to 4 weeks). **Implant if ≥50% improvement** in diary symptoms.
- **S3 response:** perineal "bellows" plus great-toe flexion.
- **Evidence:** InSite: success 61% vs 42% with medical therapy. ROSETTA: onabotulinumtoxinA 200 U slightly better for UUI (urgency urinary incontinence) but more UTI (urinary tract infection) and self-catheterisation.
- **Problems:** pain, lead migration, loss of effect; revision >30% at 5 years; cost. Newer rechargeable, MRI-conditional devices.

### Tibial nerve stimulation
- Tibial nerve carries **S2–S3** fibres. Needle 3–4 cm above medial malleolus; toe flexion confirms placement; **30 min weekly × 12**, then maintenance.
- **SUmiT:** response 55% vs 21% with sham.

- **Also know:** IVES (intravesical electrical stimulation) in children; dorsal genital nerve stimulation.

**Diagram to draw:** S3 lead with buttock generator, and PTNS needle above the medial malleolus.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Afferent modulation explains benefit in both OAB and retention.
> - S3: bellows + great-toe flexion; implant if ≥50% improvement.
> - PTNS: 12 weekly sessions; SUmiT 55% vs 21%.

## Q3c. Post transplant malignancy
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- Cancer risk after kidney transplantation is about 2–3 times the general population; a leading cause of late death with a functioning graft.

### Causes
- **Lost immune surveillance** from immunosuppression (dose and duration matter; depleting antibodies add risk).
- **Oncogenic viruses:** EBV (Epstein–Barr virus) → PTLD (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder); HHV-8 (human herpesvirus-8) → Kaposi sarcoma; HPV (human papillomavirus) → skin and anogenital cancer; hepatitis B/C → liver cancer.
- **Direct drug effects:** azathioprine sensitises skin to UV (ultraviolet) light; calcineurin inhibitors promote tumour growth. mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin) inhibitors are **antitumour**.
- **Pre-existing risk:** ESRD (end-stage renal disease), acquired cystic disease, analgesic nephropathy; rarely donor-transmitted.

### Common cancers
| Cancer | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Non-melanoma skin** | Commonest; squamous exceeds basal cell (reverse of general population); multiple, aggressive |
| **PTLD** | Mostly B-cell, EBV-driven; highest in first year, children and **EBV-negative recipient of EBV-positive donor**; may present as graft mass |
| **Kaposi sarcoma** | HHV-8; regresses with less immunosuppression and switch to sirolimus |
| **RCC (renal cell carcinoma)** | 4.6% of post-transplant cancers; **90% in native kidneys**; graft tumours mostly papillary, low grade |
| **Bladder cancer** | About **3-fold** risk; more aggressive |
| Prostate | Same incidence as general population |

### Management
- **Reduce immunosuppression** and switch to an mTOR inhibitor; sirolimus conversion reduced new skin squamous cancers (Euvrard, 2012).
- **PTLD:** reduce immunosuppression, rituximab, then chemotherapy (R-CHOP).
- RCC, prostate and bladder: treat as in the general population; protect graft vessels and ureter at surgery. BCG (bacillus Calmette–Guérin) is usable in selected bladder cancer.

### Prevention and screening
- Pre-transplant cancer screening and waiting periods (none after curative treatment of small RCC <3 cm, Gleason 6 prostate cancer, superficial bladder cancer).
- Sun protection, yearly skin examination, age-appropriate screening.
- KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) does **not** endorse routine native-kidney screening; screen only high-risk recipients.

**Diagram to draw:** pie chart of post-transplant cancers (skin largest).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Skin cancer commonest; squamous more than basal cell.
> - PTLD: EBV-negative recipient of EBV-positive donor, first year.
> - RCC mostly in native kidneys; bladder cancer 3-fold and aggressive.
> - Reduce immunosuppression and switch to mTOR inhibitor.

## Q3d. Biomarkers for patients with advanced RCC
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- A **prognostic** biomarker predicts outcome regardless of treatment; a **predictive** biomarker predicts benefit from a specific drug.
- In advanced RCC (renal cell carcinoma), only clinical risk models are used routinely. No molecular biomarker is yet validated to select treatment (EAU, European Association of Urology; NCCN, National Comprehensive Cancer Network).

### Clinical and laboratory: risk models
| Factor | MSKCC (Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer Center) | IMDC (International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium) |
|---|---|---|
| Karnofsky performance status <80% | Yes | Yes |
| Haemoglobin < lower limit of normal | Yes | Yes |
| Corrected calcium high | >10 mg/dL | > upper limit of normal |
| LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) >1.5 × normal | Yes | No |
| Time factor | No prior nephrectomy | <1 year from diagnosis to treatment |
| Neutrophils, platelets > normal | No | Yes |

- IMDC: favourable 0 factors (median survival 43 months), intermediate 1–2 (22.5 months), poor ≥3 (7.8 months).
- IMDC group guides first-line choice (nivolumab–ipilimumab not for favourable risk).
- **Also know:** neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, CRP (C-reactive protein), liver or bone metastases (poor prognosis).

### Tissue biomarkers
- **PD-L1 (programmed death-ligand 1):** benefit of checkpoint combinations is **independent of PD-L1**, so it is not used to select patients.
- **Sarcomatoid features:** respond well to checkpoint inhibitors.
- **Gene expression signatures:** IMmotion150 showed high T-effector signature tumours do better with atezolizumab–bevacizumab; high angiogenic signature tumours do well with sunitinib. Investigational.
- **Genomic:** BAP1 loss and CDKN2A loss indicate poor prognosis; PBRM1 data conflicting. MET alterations in papillary RCC (cabozantinib); FH (fumarate hydratase) deficiency. TMB (tumour mutational burden) is low and not predictive.

### Circulating and other biomarkers
- **KIM-1 (kidney injury molecule-1):** high serum levels predict recurrence and benefit from adjuvant immunotherapy (investigational).
- **ctDNA (circulating tumour DNA):** RCC sheds little DNA; methylation assays are promising.
- **Also know:** antibiotics during checkpoint therapy reduced survival (microbiome); sunitinib-induced hypertension is linked to better outcome.

**Diagram to draw:** IMDC risk groups with survival curves.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - IMDC: 6 factors; poor risk ≥3; LDH only in MSKCC.
> - PD-L1 does not select patients in RCC.
> - Sarcomatoid tumours respond well to immunotherapy.

## Q3e. Pre transplant Immunosupression treatment options
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(maintenance drugs answered in 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q1)

*Taken as induction immunosuppression and desensitisation given before or at transplantation.*

### Principle
- Rejection risk is highest in the first weeks. **Induction** gives intense immunosuppression around transplant; **desensitisation** removes preformed antibody in HLA (human leucocyte antigen)- or ABO-incompatible recipients.
- Immunological risk is assessed first: cPRA (calculated panel-reactive antibody), DSA (donor-specific antibody) on Luminex beads, crossmatch (CDC, complement-dependent cytotoxicity, and flow).

### Induction agents
| Agent | Mechanism | Use and drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Basiliximab** | Anti-CD25 (IL-2, interleukin-2, receptor); non-depleting | Low-risk recipients; 20 mg on days 0 and 4; few side effects |
| **rATG (rabbit antithymocyte globulin)** | Polyclonal T-cell depletion | High-risk (sensitised, retransplant, delayed graft function); less rejection than basiliximab; cytokine release, leucopenia, infection, PTLD (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder) |
| **Alemtuzumab** | Anti-CD52; profound depletion | Allows steroid avoidance; off-label |
| IV methylprednisolone | Cytokine suppression | Given intraoperatively with all regimens |

- Over 90% receive induction, then **tacrolimus + mycophenolate** ± steroids. In living-donor transplants, tacrolimus and mycophenolate often start days before surgery.

### Desensitisation: HLA-incompatible
- **Plasmapheresis** (removes antibody) plus **low-dose IVIG (intravenous immunoglobulin)** after each session; or **high-dose IVIG** (immunomodulation).
- **Rituximab** (anti-CD20) reduces B cells that renew antibody. Bortezomib (plasma cells) and eculizumab (anti-C5) in selected cases.
- **Imlifidase:** bacterial enzyme that **cleaves all IgG** within hours, turning a positive crossmatch negative; EMA (European Medicines Agency) conditional approval 2020 for highly sensitised patients.
- Desensitised live-donor transplant gives better survival than staying on dialysis (Montgomery, 2011).

### Desensitisation: ABO-incompatible
- **Rituximab** 2–4 weeks before; **plasmapheresis or immunoadsorption** to lower anti-A/B titres to a centre target (commonly ≤1:8); IVIG; tacrolimus–mycophenolate started before surgery. Splenectomy is now obsolete.
- Post-transplant titre monitoring; A2 donors carry lower risk.

### Alternatives
- **Kidney paired exchange** avoids desensitisation; acceptable-mismatch programmes.

**Diagram to draw:** antibody titre falling with plasmapheresis sessions to target before transplant.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Basiliximab for low risk; rATG for high immunological risk.
> - HLA-incompatible: plasmapheresis + IVIG ± rituximab; imlifidase cleaves IgG.
> - ABO-incompatible: rituximab + plasmapheresis to target titre.

## Q3f. Physiology of UPJ in relation to urine transport
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*UPJ = ureteropelvic (pelviureteric) junction.*

### Anatomy relevant to function
- The UPJ is the apex of a funnel-shaped pelvis; its spiral smooth muscle is continuous with calyces and ureter.

### Pacemaker and electrical activity
- Peristalsis is **myogenic**: the excised or denervated ureter keeps contracting. Nerves carry pain and only modulate.
- **Primary pacemaker: atypical smooth muscle cells** at the pelvicalyceal border; they fire spontaneously (HCN, hyperpolarisation-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated, channels).
- **ICC-like (interstitial cells of Cajal-like) cells** (c-Kit positive) in the pelvis and UPJ relay the signal to typical smooth muscle and act as back-up pacemakers.
- Signal spreads through **gap junctions**. Calcium entry gives a plateau action potential; calcium–calmodulin activates myosin light-chain kinase, causing contraction.

### Bolus formation and transport
1. Urine fills the pelvis at low pressure; the UPJ is closed.
2. Pelvic contraction pushes urine through the opening UPJ into the upper ureter.
3. The UPJ closes behind it, forming a **bolus**.
4. Ureteric walls **coapt** behind the bolus; the wave (2–6 per minute, 20–80 cm H₂O) carries it to the bladder.
- At **low flow**, the UPJ acts as a filter: several pelvic contractions occur for each ureteric contraction.
- As **flow rises**, ureteric frequency reaches 1:1, then bolus volume rises.
- At **very high flow**, boluses merge into a continuous column and the ureter dilates **without obstruction**.

### Effect of obstruction
- **Acute:** stronger, more frequent contractions.
- **Chronic:** dilatation, walls fail to coapt, peristalsis becomes ineffective; raised pelvic pressure lowers GFR (glomerular filtration rate).
- **Intrinsic UPJO (UPJ obstruction):** abnormal muscle, excess collagen, fewer ICC-like cells, so an **aperistaltic segment** cannot transmit the wave.
- **Also know:** endotoxin and progesterone reduce peristalsis. Diuretic renogram and Whitaker test (chapter: pressure difference >12 cm H₂O suggests obstruction; classic teaching: <15 unobstructed, >22 obstructed) assess transport.

**Diagram to draw:** pelvis, UPJ and ureter showing bolus formation and coaptation.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Myogenic peristalsis from atypical smooth muscle pacemakers; ICC-like cells back up.
> - Low flow: several pelvic beats per ureteric beat; very high flow: dilatation without obstruction.
> - Intrinsic UPJO: aperistaltic segment with fewer ICC-like cells.
