## Q1. Management of chyluria
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2024-Winter Paper 3, Q2 and 2018-Summer Paper 1, Q1)

### Definition and mechanism
- **Lymphatic anatomy:** intestinal lacteals → intestinal trunk → **cisterna chyli** (L1–L2) → **thoracic duct** → left subclavian–jugular junction. Renal lumbar lymphatics join the cisterna; valves normally prevent backflow.
- **Chyluria** is the passage of **chyle** (intestinal lymph rich in chylomicrons and protein) in urine, which looks milky.
- It needs a **lymphourinary fistula**, usually a **pyelolymphatic fistula** at a calyceal fornix.
- **Pathogenesis (filarial):** adult *Wuchereria bancrofti* worms and the host reaction cause **lymphangitis and fibrosis** between the intestinal trunks, cisterna chyli and thoracic duct. Lymphatics below the block dilate and their valves fail. Chyle refluxes into renal hilar lymphatics, which rupture into a calyx.
- It appears **earlier than elephantiasis**, is usually **intermittent** and may remit spontaneously. So every treatment is judged against natural remission.

### Causes
| Parasitic (commonest; India, China) | Non-parasitic |
|---|---|
| *W. bancrofti* (lymphatic filariasis, Culex mosquito) | Congenital lymphangiectasia, renal lymphangioma |
| | Trauma or surgery (partial nephrectomy, RPLND [retroperitoneal lymph node dissection]) |
| | Retroperitoneal tumour, fibrosis, tuberculosis, pregnancy |

### Clinical features and severity
- Milky urine, worse after fatty meals; **chylous clots** (colic, retention); **haematochyluria**.
- Loss of fat and protein: weight loss, **hypoproteinaemia**, oedema, anaemia, fatigue, lowered immunity.

| Grade | Features |
|---|---|
| Mild | Intermittent; no systemic effect |
| Moderate | Persistent; clots |
| Severe | Haematochyluria, clot retention, weight loss, hypoalbuminaemia |

### Evaluation (before treatment)
**1. Confirm chyle**
- **Ether (chloroform) test:** urine shaken with ether clears, because the fat dissolves.
- Urine **triglycerides** and protein raised; Sudan III stains fat globules.
- Exclude **phosphaturia** (clears with acetic acid) and **pyuria** (leucocytes, no clearing).

**2. Filarial status**
- Night blood smear (often negative late); **circulating filarial antigen** card test; eosinophilia; PCR (polymerase chain reaction).

**3. Lateralise and localise the fistula**
- **Cystoscopy after a fatty meal:** milky efflux from one ureteric orifice (sometimes both).
- **Retrograde pyelography:** pyelolymphatic backflow.
- **Lymphoscintigraphy** (non-invasive); MR (magnetic resonance) lymphangiography; pedal lymphangiography is historical.

**4. Baseline:** albumin, haemoglobin, renal function; urine culture before instillation.

### Management algorithm
```
Confirm chyle -> grade severity
   Mild             -> diet + antifilarial drugs; observe
   Moderate/persist -> add endoscopic sclerotherapy (repeatable)
   Severe or failed -> renal hilar lymphatic disconnection
```

**1. Conservative (all patients)**
- **Low-fat, high-protein diet with MCT (medium-chain triglyceride) oil** and plenty of fluids.
- **Why MCT:** medium-chain fats are absorbed directly into portal blood, not into lacteals, so less chyle is formed.
- **DEC (diethylcarbamazine)** 2 mg/kg orally three times daily, traditionally 12 days; a 1-day course is as effective. It kills microfilariae but has only modest effect on adults.
- **Doxycycline** 200 mg daily for 4–8 weeks kills *Wolbachia* (endosymbiont) and so sterilises or shortens the life of adult worms. Albendazole and ivermectin are alternatives.
- Correct anaemia and protein deficit. Most mild cases settle on this, and many remit spontaneously.

**2. Endoscopic sclerotherapy (renal pelvic instillation sclerotherapy)**
- Ureteric catheter on the leaking side, head-down position. Instil **silver nitrate 0.5–1%** or **povidone-iodine**; repeat over several sessions.
- **Mechanism:** chemical lymphangitis and fibrosis seal the fistula.
- **Contraindications:** active UTI (urinary tract infection), bilateral simultaneous instillation (risk of anuria).
- Good initial response; recurrence is common, but it is repeatable and avoids surgery.
- **Complications:** flank pain, haematuria, papillary necrosis, ureteric stricture, anuria; rarely renal failure or death with silver nitrate.

**3. Surgery: renal hilar lymphatic disconnection (chylolymphatic disconnection)**
- **Indications:** severe disease, failed sclerotherapy, haematochyluria with clot retention, marked weight loss.
- **Steps:**
  1. Full renal mobilisation (nephrolysis).
  2. **Skeletonise the renal artery and vein**, ligating, clipping or sealing every hilar lymphatic.
  3. Strip the upper ureter; remove perinephric fat.
  4. Nephropexy; optional **omental wrap** around the hilum as a barrier.
  5. **On-table cystoscopy** confirms clear efflux.
- Reflux occurs only through **hilar lymphatics**, so upper-pole dissection can be limited.
- **Approach:** open, laparoscopic (transperitoneal or retroperitoneal) or robotic. **Retroperitoneoscopic** ligation beats open in meta-analysis: shorter operation and stay, less blood loss, faster bowel recovery, fewer complications.
- **Complications:** lymphocele, renal vein injury, recurrence.
- **Also know:** lymphovenous anastomosis; autotransplantation (rare); nephrectomy now obsolete.

**4. Follow-up and prevention**
- Urine triglycerides and weight; recurrence needs repeat sclerotherapy or surgery.
- Mass drug administration (albendazole with DEC or ivermectin), bednets; clip lymphatics during renal and nodal surgery.

**Diagram to draw:** retroperitoneal lymphatics with obstructed thoracic duct, dilated hilar lymphatics and pyelolymphatic fistula at a calyceal fornix.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Pyelolymphatic fistula from *W. bancrofti* lymphatic obstruction; often intermittent.
> - Ether test and urine triglycerides confirm; cystoscopy after a fatty meal lateralises.
> - Diet with MCT plus DEC → silver nitrate sclerotherapy → hilar lymphatic disconnection.
> - Retroperitoneoscopic disconnection beats open.

## Q2. Physiological and hemodynamic effects of laparoscopy. Write in detail about lap donor nephrectomy
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in Practice Set 3 Paper 1, Q1 and Practice Set 2 Paper 4, Q3a)

### Part A: Physiological and haemodynamic effects of laparoscopy
- **Pneumoperitoneum** is CO₂ (carbon dioxide) insufflation at 10–15 mmHg. CO₂ is used because it is non-combustible and highly blood-soluble, so embolism is less dangerous. Its effects have **two drivers**: **pressure** (compression of cava, renal veins and diaphragm) and **absorbed CO₂** (hypercarbia, acidosis, sympathetic drive).

| Effect (supine) | 10 mmHg | 20 mmHg | 40 mmHg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | ↑ | ↑ | ↓ |
| MAP (mean arterial pressure), SVR (systemic vascular resistance) | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ |
| Cardiac output | ↔/↑ | ↔/↓ | ↓ |
| GFR (glomerular filtration rate), urine output | ↓ | ↓↓ | ↓↓ |
| Arterial PCO₂ | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ |

- **Cardiovascular:** afterload rises (aortic compression, catecholamines, vasopressin). Preload depends on volume: in hypovolaemia IVC (inferior vena cava) compression lowers venous return. **CVP (central venous pressure) reads falsely high.** Hypercarbia gives tachycardia and ectopics; peritoneal stretch gives **vagal bradycardia**.
- **Respiratory:** splinted diaphragm lowers FRC (functional residual capacity) and compliance, raises airway pressure; atelectasis. Barotrauma (pneumothorax) at high pressure.
- **Acid–base:** absorbed CO₂ causes respiratory acidosis; raise ventilation. In COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) check arterial gases; **hypercarbia peaks after extubation.**
- **Renal:** **oliguria** from **renal vein and parenchymal compression**; reversible on desufflation.
- **Others:** splanchnic flow falls; ICP (intracranial pressure) and intraocular pressure rise; smaller stress and immune response than open surgery; hypothermia; shoulder-tip pain.
- **Position:** head-down raises MAP and output but worsens lungs; head-up lowers output. Retroperitoneal insufflation causes smaller haemodynamic swings.
- **Safe practice:** preoperative cardiopulmonary assessment; optimise volume; **work at 10–12 mmHg**, 20 mmHg only briefly; monitor end-tidal CO₂, airway pressure and urine output.
- **Also know:** helium or argon for lung disease (poorly soluble, so embolism risk); low-pressure surgery with valveless insufflators.

### Part B: Laparoscopic donor nephrectomy
**Introduction**
- First described by **Ratner (1995)**; now the preferred approach (open under 2% in the United States). **Donor safety comes first** at every stage.

**Donor evaluation**
- Questionnaire → physical and psychosocial review, blood group, HLA (human leukocyte antigen) typing → **GFR by at least two methods, one measured** → metabolic, serology, cancer screening → **CT (computed tomography) angiography** for vessels → cardiac review if needed.
- Exclusions: kidney disease, malignancy, active infection, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, significant obesity; bilateral stones generally exclude.

**Side selection**
- **Left preferred:** longer renal vein, simpler dissection. **Right** if the left has multiple arteries or complex veins, or the right kidney is smaller: **the donor keeps the better kidney.**

- **Anatomy matters:** arterial variation in 25–40%; early (prehilar) branching and accessory arteries must be seen on CT, as they may contraindicate the laparoscopic route.

**Techniques**
| Technique | Features |
|---|---|
| Pure transperitoneal | 3–4 ports; colon reflected; Pfannenstiel extraction |
| **Hand-assisted** (about two-thirds) | Hand port doubles as extraction site; tactile feedback, quick haemostasis |
| Retroperitoneoscopic | No bowel mobilisation; direct hilum; less room |
| Robotic; LESS (laparoendoscopic single-site surgery) | Wristed instruments; single incision, less pain |

**Operative steps (left)**
1. Reflect colon; divide **gonadal, adrenal and lumbar veins**.
2. Preserve periureteric tissue and the **gonadal–ureter triangle** (ureteric blood supply).
3. Keep pressure ≤10 mmHg; hydration and mannitol or furosemide to keep urine >100 mL/hour (counters pneumoperitoneum oliguria).
4. Divide ureter, then **artery first, then vein**. Vein stapled lateral to the adrenal vein; Endo-TA (non-cutting) stapler gives extra vein length.
5. Extract quickly; cold flush. Warm ischaemia a few minutes.
- **Hem-o-lok:** manufacturer contraindicates it on the donor renal artery after stump bleeds.

- **Open donor nephrectomy** is kept for complex anatomy or previous surgery, more often on the right.

**Advantages versus open**
- Less pain, shorter stay, faster return to work, better cosmesis; **more people volunteer to donate**; equal graft function.

**Disadvantages and complications**
- Longer operation, learning curve, shorter vessels (right vein), slightly longer warm ischaemia.
- Complications about **16%** (mostly gastrointestinal); Clavien ≥IV 2.5%; **mortality 3 per 10,000**; conversion 0.26–3.34%, mainly for vascular injury.

**Long-term donor counselling**
- **Also know:** kidney paired donation for ABO-incompatible or crossmatch-positive pairs; living-donor grafts survive longer than deceased-donor grafts.
- Function 60–80% of pre-donation; lifetime ESRD (end-stage renal disease) **90 per 10,000** versus 14 in matched non-donors (Muzaale, 2014); more gestational hypertension.

**Diagram to draw:** left renal hilum with gonadal, adrenal and lumbar veins, stapled artery and vein, and the gonadal–ureter triangle.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Pneumoperitoneum: pressure plus CO₂; afterload rises, oliguria from renal vein compression.
> - Ratner 1995; left kidney preferred; hand-assisted in about two-thirds.
> - Mortality 3 per 10,000; conversion 0.26–3.34%.
> - Donor ESRD 90 versus 14 per 10,000 (matched non-donors).

## Q3a. Nocturnal enuresis
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in Practice Set 1 Paper 3, Q3e)

### Definition (ICCS [International Children's Continence Society])
- Intermittent incontinence **during sleep** in a child **aged 5 years or more**.
- **Primary:** never dry. **Secondary:** wetting after at least **6 months** dry (look for stressors, diabetes, UTI [urinary tract infection], neurological disease).
- **MNE (monosymptomatic):** no daytime LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms). **NMNE (non-monosymptomatic):** with daytime LUTS.

### Epidemiology
- About **5–10% at 7 years**; falls with age; about 2% of adults; familial.

### Pathophysiology: three-way mismatch
1. **Nocturnal polyuria** (poor night-time ADH [antidiuretic hormone] rise).
2. **Small nocturnal bladder capacity** or DO (detrusor overactivity).
3. **Failure to arouse** from sleep.
- **Why sleep apnoea matters:** obstructed breathing makes arousal difficult and releases atrial natriuretic peptide, which raises urine output.
- Comorbidities: constipation, sleep-disordered breathing, ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder).

### Evaluation
- History, examination (spine, genitalia, abdomen), urinalysis.
- **Bladder diary:** voided volumes against EBC (expected bladder capacity) = (age + 2) × 30 mL; night output by weighing nappies.
- Uroflowmetry, PVR (post-void residual), ultrasound only for NMNE or before anticholinergics.

### Management
- **Treat daytime LUTS and constipation first.** Education; waking, fluid restriction and punishment do not work.

| | **Alarm** | **Desmopressin** |
|---|---|---|
| Target | Arousal (conditioning) | Night urine output |
| Use | Nightly; until 14 dry nights | 0.2–0.4 mg tablet or 120–240 µg melt, 1 hour before bed |
| Response | 50–70%; durable | Rule of thirds; relapses off drug |
| Caution | Motivated family | Limit evening fluid (hyponatraemia) |
- **Failure:** switch or combine. **Second line:** add oxybutynin after checking PVR and bowels. **Resistant:** imipramine (cardiotoxic; ECG [electrocardiogram]), off-label β3-agonists, adenotonsillectomy for sleep apnoea.
- **Adults:** new enuresis may signal high-pressure chronic retention: catheterise and treat obstruction.

**Diagram to draw:** triad: nocturnal polyuria, small capacity, arousal failure.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - ≥5 years; secondary after 6 months dry.
> - Alarm (durable) and desmopressin (rule of thirds) are first line.
> - Treat daytime symptoms and constipation first.

## Q3b. BXO
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 1, Q3c)

*BXO (balanitis xerotica obliterans) is taken as genital lichen sclerosus (LS) in the male.*

### Definition
- Chronic, progressive, **scarring inflammatory disease of genital skin**; possibly premalignant. BXO is its late stage in men.

### Epidemiology and aetiology
- 6–10 times commoner in females; in men, older men and boys with phimosis. 15–20% have extragenital disease.
- Cause unknown. Autoimmune links (thyroid disease, vitiligo), familial clustering; **chronic occlusion by urine** under the foreskin (dribbling, obesity) definitely increases risk. Infection and trauma are proposed but unproven.

### Clinical features
- White, atrophic sclerotic plaques with loss of architecture, purpura, fissures; itch, painful erection, poor stream, spraying.
- Sequence: prepuce → **phimosis** → glans → **meatal stenosis** → fossa navicularis and penile urethral stricture.
- **Spares the bulbar and posterior urethra.**
- **Stricture mechanism:** voiding against meatal stenosis forces urine into the glands of Littre → inflammation → spongiofibrosis.

### Evaluation
- **Biopsy** if doubtful: hyperkeratosis, epidermal atrophy, basal vacuolation, homogenised dermal collagen, band-like lymphocytes.
- Uroflowmetry; **RUG (retrograde urethrogram)** before meatal surgery, because a proximal stricture may coexist.
- **Differential:** lichen planus, Zoon balanitis, penile intraepithelial neoplasia.

### Management
| Situation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Skin disease | **Clobetasol propionate 0.05%** for about 3 months, then twice-weekly maintenance; calcineurin inhibitors second line |
| Phimosis | **Circumcision**, usually curative |
| Meatal stenosis | Meatotomy; BMG (buccal mucosal graft) meatoplasty |
| Anterior stricture | **Oral mucosa, never genital skin** (LS recurs in skin) |
| Panurethral or unfit | **Perineal urethrostomy** (easy because the proximal urethra is spared); self-dilatation |
- Recurrence is common after medical treatment, and restricture rates exceed other aetiologies; treatment follows the patient's goals.

### Malignancy
- Penile SCC (squamous cell carcinoma) in **2.3–9%**, HPV (human papillomavirus)-independent type. **Lifelong follow-up**; biopsy any ulcer or nodule.

**Diagram to draw:** penis showing prepuce, glans, meatus and fossa involved, proximal urethra spared.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Meatus is the sentinel site; proximal urethra spared.
> - Clobetasol first; oral mucosa, never skin.
> - SCC in 2.3–9%: lifelong follow-up.

## Q3c. Autotransplantation
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 3, Q3c and 2025-Winter Paper 3, Q3c)

*Taken as renal autotransplantation.*

### Definition and principle
- Removing a kidney and re-implanting it, usually in the **ipsilateral iliac fossa** on the iliac vessels, with **bench (ex vivo) surgery** if needed. First done in 1963 for proximal ureteric injury.
- Bringing the kidney to the bladder replaces the ureter without bowel.

### Indications
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Ureteric loss (commonest)** | Long iatrogenic or traumatic loss; failed repairs (**delayed setting, never acute**) |
| **RPF (retroperitoneal fibrosis)** | Ureterolysis impossible |
| **Tumour** | UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma) in a solitary kidney; bench partial nephrectomy |
| **Vascular** | Complex renal artery aneurysm or AVM (arteriovenous malformation) unsuitable for endovascular repair; nutcracker |
| **Pain** | Selected loin pain–haematuria syndrome |
- **Preferred over ileal ureter** when the other kidney is poor or bowel unsuitable (creatinine >2 mg/dL, bowel disease, radiation).
- **Contraindications:** poor renal function, severe iliac atherosclerosis, unfit patient.

### Procedure
```
CT (computed tomography) angiography, renogram
→ harvest as for live donation: maximal vessel length,
  preserve ureteric blood supply
→ cold perfusion ± bench repair
→ Gibson incision, extraperitoneal iliac fossa
→ vein to external iliac vein (end-to-side);
  artery to external iliac (end-to-side) or internal iliac (end-to-end)
→ pyelovesicostomy (± Boari flap), ureteroneocystostomy,
  or native ureter left in continuity
→ stent, catheter, drain
```
- Laparoscopic harvest speeds recovery; robotic autotransplantation emerging.

### Complications and outcome
- Arterial or venous thrombosis, bleeding, urine leak, anastomotic stricture, lymphocele, infection.
- **Follow-up:** Doppler ultrasound early; renogram and blood pressure later.
- **Renal unit loss 5–10%**; major (Clavien–Dindo ≥III) complications about 15–20%; **success about 90%**.

**Also know:** alternatives: Boari flap, TUU (transureteroureterostomy), ileal ureter, Yang–Monti.

**Diagram to draw:** kidney in the iliac fossa with vascular anastomoses and pyelovesicostomy.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - For long ureteric loss when the other kidney is poor or bowel unsuitable.
> - Delayed setting only; bench surgery for vascular or tumour cases.
> - Unit loss 5–10%; success about 90%.

## Q3d. Congenital Penile curvature..
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and principle
- Curvature of the erect penis present from birth, with an **orthotopic meatus**.
- Rigidity needs **symmetric compliance** of the tunica albuginea; curvature means one aspect is less compliant.

### Embryology
- DHT (dihydrotestosterone) drives fusion of the urethral folds and maturation of spongiosum, Buck's and dartos fascia. The urethra may form normally while the mesenchyme stays deficient → dysgenetic, inelastic ventral tissue.
- Normal fetal ventral curvature resolves by 20 weeks.

### Devine and Horton classification (1973)
| Type | Abnormal tissue |
|---|---|
| I | Corpus spongiosum deficient (chordee without hypospadias) |
| II | Dysgenetic Buck's and dartos fascia; normal spongiosum |
| III | Dartos only |
| IV | Normal layers; **asymmetric corporal compliance** (true congenital curvature) |
| V | Congenitally short urethra (very rare; doubted) |

### Two clinical groups
| Feature | Chordee without hypospadias (I–III) | Congenital curvature (IV) |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Ventral ± torsion | Ventral, lateral (often left), rarely dorsal |
| Erection size | In proportion to flaccid size | **Noticeably large** |
| Presentation | Childhood | 18–30 years, noticed at puberty |
- **Versus acquired** (Peyronie disease, fracture): congenital is painless, no plaque, no shortening.

### Evaluation
- Home photographs of erection; **artificial erection** (Gittes–McLaughlin saline test or prostaglandin) in theatre.
- Grade: mild <30°, moderate 30–45°, severe ≥45°.

### Management
- Surgery if curvature hinders intercourse or distresses.
1. Deglove; excise ventral dysgenetic tissue; mobilise spongiosum from glans to perineum.
2. Residual curve: **midline ventral septotomy** under artificial erection.
3. Still curved: **dorsal plication** contralateral to the curve: Nesbit (excise ellipses, close transversely), Yachia (Heineke–Mikulicz), 16-dot or TAP (tunica albuginea plication). **12 o'clock is nerve-free**, so midline plication is safe.
4. Severe: ventral **corporotomy with graft** (dermis, tunica vaginalis).
- **Rule:** if the meatus reaches the glans tip, do not divide the urethra; the corpora usually limit straightening.
- **Results:** single operation in almost all; recurrence 2–15%, mostly after plication.

**Diagram to draw:** penile cross-section: nerves at 11 and 1 o'clock, plication at 12.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Devine–Horton I–III = chordee without hypospadias; IV = corporal asymmetry.
> - Congenital curvature: young man, large erection, orthotopic meatus.
> - Dorsal 12 o'clock plication; never divide a urethra that reaches the glans tip.

## Q3e. Maturation Arrest.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Taken as spermatogenic maturation arrest on testicular histology.*

### Definition
- A histological pattern of **spermatogenic failure** in which germ cells develop normally up to one stage and **the sequence halts** at a premeiotic, meiotic or postmeiotic step. No mature sperm, or very few, are made.
- One of three patterns in NOA (non-obstructive azoospermia):

| Pattern | Histology |
|---|---|
| Hypospermatogenesis | All stages present, fewer cells |
| **Maturation arrest** | Halts at one stage |
| SCO (Sertoli cell–only) | No germ cells |

### Classification
- **Early:** arrest at spermatogonia or **primary spermatocyte** (meiotic failure).
- **Late:** arrest at the **spermatid** stage.
- **Complete** (all tubules) versus **incomplete** (foci progress to sperm): incomplete gives better retrieval.

### Causes
- **Genetic:** complete **AZFb (azoospermia factor b) deletion** → arrest; meiotic recombination defects in about half of men with maturation arrest; karyotype anomalies.
- **Acquired:** heat (varicocele, cryptorchidism), chemotherapy, radiation, drugs, orchitis; idiopathic often.
- Intratesticular testosterone is essential for postmeiotic maturation.

### Clinical features and diagnosis
- Azoospermia or severe oligospermia, normal semen volume, palpable vasa.
- **Testis size and FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) are often normal**, so it can mimic obstruction (FSH ≤7.6 IU/L, long axis >4.6 cm).
- **Diagnosis needs testicular biopsy** (Bouin's fixative, never formalin), usually done as sperm extraction.
- **Karyotype and Y microdeletion** testing in all azoospermic men (AUA/ASRM [American Urological Association/American Society for Reproductive Medicine]).

### Management
- Optimise: treat varicocele, stop exogenous testosterone; off-label hormonal stimulation (clomiphene, hCG [human chorionic gonadotropin]) unproven.
- **Micro-TESE (microdissection testicular sperm extraction)** with ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection): retrieval **about 40%** (versus 30% in SCO).
- **Complete AZFb deletion: retrieval highly unlikely; do not operate.**
- No sperm: donor sperm or adoption. Round spermatid injection is experimental.
- Genetic counselling: sons inherit Y deletions.

**Diagram to draw:** seminiferous tubule showing germ cells up to spermatocytes with no spermatids or sperm.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Arrest at one stage; early (spermatocyte) or late (spermatid).
> - FSH and testis size may be normal: biopsy diagnoses.
> - Micro-TESE retrieval about 40%; none with complete AZFb deletion.

## Q3f. Detrusor sphincter dyssynergia
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2016-Summer Paper 1, Q3a)

### Definition
- **DSD (detrusor–external sphincter dyssynergia):** involuntary contraction of the striated sphincter during detrusor contraction, causing functional obstruction, high pressure and poor emptying.
- **Lesion between the pons and the sacral cord:** suprasacral SCI (spinal cord injury), MS (multiple sclerosis), transverse myelitis, myelomeningocele. **Never with brain lesions** (stroke, Parkinson's).
- Lesions at or above T6 may add **smooth sphincter (bladder-neck) dyssynergia** and autonomic dysreflexia.

### Blaivas classification
| Type | EMG (electromyography) pattern | Lesion |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Activity rises with detrusor pressure, relaxes suddenly at peak | Incomplete |
| 2 | Sporadic contractions throughout voiding | Complete |
| 3 | Crescendo–decrescendo; obstruction throughout | Complete |
- Also intermittent versus continuous. Type is unrelated to level.

### Diagnosis
- **Pressure–flow study with EMG:** EMG rises with the detrusor contraction; high pressure, interrupted flow. Needle EMG is more reliable.
- **VUDS (videourodynamics):** narrow membranous urethra, dilated posterior urethra; VUR (vesicoureteral reflux).
- **Exclude:** guarding reflex (normal); **pseudodyssynergia** (straining or suppressing a contraction); dysfunctional voiding (learned, no neurological lesion).

### Significance
- Worse when complete, continuous and **in men**: hydronephrosis, VUR, stones, urosepsis. Other risk factors: high filling pressure, indwelling catheter.
- **Management logic:** DSD is both a storage and an emptying failure. Lower bladder pressure, then empty it.
- DLPP (detrusor leak point pressure) ≥40 cmH₂O endangers the kidneys.

### Management
- **First line:** CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) plus antimuscarinic or β3-agonist; then intradetrusor botulinum toxin.
- **CIC impossible (men):** sphincterotomy or sphincteric botulinum toxin with condom drainage; DLPP ≥40 afterwards means failure.
- Others: suprapubic catheter, Brindley stimulator with deafferentation, diversion. Urethral stents abandoned. Credé and Valsalva voiding contraindicated.
- **Also know:** α-blockers for bladder-neck dyssynergia; lifelong upper-tract surveillance.

**Diagram to draw:** urodynamic trace with rising detrusor pressure, EMG bursts and interrupted flow.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Lesion between pons and sacral cord; never brain lesions.
> - Blaivas 1 incomplete; 2 and 3 complete.
> - Worst in men; DLPP ≥40 cmH₂O hostile.
