## Q1. Briefly outline flexible endourological instrument and its role in management of urological disease
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(flexible ureteroscopy in detail: see Practice Set 1 Paper 2, Q3b; digital scopes also in 2019-Summer Paper 4, Q3c)

### Introduction
- A flexible endoscope has a **deflectable tip** and carries its image by a fibre bundle or a tip-mounted chip. It follows the natural curves of the urethra, ureter and collecting system.
- Physical basis: light travels along a curved glass fibre by **total internal reflection** (Tyndall).
- Marshall reported the first flexible URS (ureteroscopy) in 1964. It became therapeutic only when a **working channel** and **active tip deflection** were added in the early 1980s.

### Components
| Component | Key features |
|---|---|
| Optics | **Fibreoptic:** a *coherent* image bundle carries the picture; a *random* light bundle carries illumination; cladding improves reflection. **Digital:** CCD (charge-coupled device) or CMOS (complementary metal oxide semiconductor) chip at the tip with LED (light-emitting diode) light; no camera head or light cable |
| Deflection | Lever → control wires through movable metal rings → wire fixed at the tip. "Intuitive" = tip moves with the lever. The reticle (notch) marks the plane of deflection |
| Working channel | Single channel shared by irrigation and instruments, so instruments reduce flow and deflection |
| Shaft | Tapered tip; passive (secondary) deflection segment behind the tip helps lower-pole access |

### Types
| Instrument | Specifications | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| Flexible cystoscope | 14–17 Fr; 37–40 cm; deflection 120–220°; channel 6.4–7.5 Fr; field about 120° | Office diagnosis and minor therapy |
| Flexible ureteroscope | Tip 4.5–7.5 Fr; deflection ≥270° both ways; channel **3.6 Fr**; dual-channel scope (2 × 3.3 Fr) exists | Kidney and upper ureter |
| Flexible nephroscope (or flexible cystoscope used through the tract) | Larger calibre; too wide for the UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) | Clearing calyces during PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) |

### Fibreoptic vs digital
| | Fibreoptic | Digital |
|---|---|---|
| Image | Honeycomb pattern; black dots as fibres break | Sharper, larger image; no focusing or white balance |
| Size | Smallest tips | Larger; more access-sheath use |
| Outcome | Reference | Shorter operating time, equal stone-free rate |
| Durability | Fragile; over-rotation damages fibres | Not clearly better in trials |

- Surprise finding: in vitro, illumination was better with the fibreoptic cystoscope.

### Single-use scopes
- **LithoVue (2016):** first single-use digital ureteroscope; CMOS, tip 7.7 Fr, shaft 9.5 Fr, 3.6 Fr channel, 270° each way. Single-use cystoscopes followed (2020).
- Rationale: reusable ureteroscopes may need repair after only **3–5 cases**; reprocessing and repair add cost.
- Cost advantage depends on case volume; environmental impact is similar to reusables.

### Accessories
- **Guidewires:** 0.035/0.038 inch PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene)-coated safety wire; hydrophilic wires to pass impacted stones (exchange, as too slippery for safety); nitinol-core and hybrid wires.
- **Access sheath** (11–16 Fr): repeated passes, lower intrarenal pressure; small risk of ureteric injury.
- **Laser fibres** 200–365 µm; **nitinol tipless baskets** ≤3 Fr; three-prong graspers; 3 Fr cup biopsy forceps; 3 Fr balloon dilating to 12 Fr; fulguration electrodes; pressure irrigation pumps.
- Flexible EHL (electrohydraulic lithotripsy) probes work; flexible pneumatic probes restrict deflection.

### Role in urological disease
| Area | Role |
|---|---|
| **Lower tract** | Haematuria work-up; bladder cancer surveillance, office biopsy and fulguration; NBI (narrow-band imaging) and blue light; stent removal or exchange; bladder stones, foreign bodies, strictures; botulinum toxin injection; diversions through a catheterisable channel; difficult ureteric access via a suprapubic tract |
| **Stones** | RIRS (retrograde intrarenal surgery): renal stones <20 mm, lower pole <10 mm; >2 cm when PCNL is unsafe |
| **UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma)** | Diagnostic URS and biopsy (predicts grade in about 9 of 10); laser ablation of low-grade tumours; prefer to percutaneous route (seeding) |
| **Obstruction** | Endopyelotomy without crossing vessels; endoureterotomy for strictures <1.5 cm |
| **Diagnosis** | Positive cytology, filling defects, lateralising haematuria (haemangioma fulgurated), recurrent one-sided infection; foreign body retrieval |
| **PCNL** | Antegrade or retrograde flexible endoscopy raises stone-free rate; ECIRS (endoscopic combined intrarenal surgery) |

### Care and complications
- **Scope damage:** deflection wear; laser fibre scraping and perforating the channel. Pass fibres with the scope straight; fire only with the fibre tip seen on the stone; leave the bladder empty to avoid buckling.
- **Sterilisation:** ethylene oxide, glutaraldehyde or peracetic acid.
- **Patient:** ureteric injury, stricture, sepsis from high pressure. Use saline irrigation and antibiotic prophylaxis for all upper tract procedures.

### Recent advances
- Pressure (and temperature) sensors in the scope; AI (artificial intelligence); robotic flexible URS; suction access sheaths; miniaturisation.

**Diagram to draw:** cross-section of a flexible ureteroscope: chip, LED, working channel and deflection wires.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Working channel plus active deflection made flexible scopes therapeutic.
> - fURS: 3.6 Fr channel, ≥270° deflection; cystoscope 14–17 Fr.
> - Digital: better image, larger tip, durability not better.
> - Golden rule: never fire unless the fibre tip is seen on the stone.

## Q2. Describe energy sources for breaking stones in Urology
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also covered in 2025-Winter Paper 4, Q2 and Q3b)

### Introduction
- Stones are **brittle**: they crack under tension and shear. Tissue is **elastic** and survives the same stress. Every lithotripter exploits this difference.
- Energy reaches the stone **extracorporeally** (SWL, shock wave lithotripsy) or **intracorporeally** through an endoscope.

### Classification
| Group | Sources |
|---|---|
| Extracorporeal | SWL: electrohydraulic, electromagnetic, piezoelectric; BWL (burst wave lithotripsy, investigational) |
| Intracorporeal mechanical | Ballistic (pneumatic), ultrasonic, combined ultrasonic–pneumatic |
| Intracorporeal electrical | EHL (electrohydraulic lithotripsy) |
| Intracorporeal light | Lasers: Ho:YAG (holmium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet), TFL (thulium fibre laser), pulsed Tm:YAG (thulium:YAG); historic pulsed dye and FREDDY |

### 1. Shock wave lithotripsy
- **Shock wave:** peak positive pressure 30–100 MPa, then a tensile tail of about −10 MPa. It is generated at F1, focused, and delivered to the stone at F2.

| Generator | Mechanism | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electrohydraulic (original HM3, 1980) | Underwater spark at F1 of an ellipsoid reflector | Very effective | Electrode life <40,000 shocks; focus drifts |
| Electromagnetic (modern standard) | Coil pushes a metal membrane; lens or reflector focuses | Consistent; high energy at F2; long life | — |
| Piezoelectric | Many ceramic elements on a spherical dish | Long life; little pain | Small focal zone, low energy |

- **Fracture mechanisms:** shear and tear forces; **spallation** at the distal face; quasistatic squeezing; **cavitation** (bubble collapse erodes the surface); dynamic squeezing (most powerful). A focal zone wider than the stone works best.
- **Technique:** 60–90 shocks/min beats 120/min, because bubble clouds shield the stone and injure the kidney; voltage ramping; bubble-free gel coupling.
- Poor results: calcium oxalate monohydrate, brushite, cystine, density >1000 HU (Hounsfield units), skin-to-stone distance >10 cm.

### 2. Ballistic (pneumatic) lithotripsy
- Compressed air drives a projectile against a metal probe, a **jackhammer** (about 12 Hz).
- Breaks all stones cheaply with little ureteric injury. Drawbacks: **retropulsion**; rigid probes only, as flexible probes block deflection.

### 3. Ultrasonic lithotripsy
- A piezoceramic transducer vibrates a hollow probe at ultrasonic frequency; contact drills the stone.
- **Suction** through the hollow probe removes fragments at once. Best for soft stones in PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy); weak on hard stones; needs irrigation for cooling.

### 4. Combined devices
- Ultrasonic plus ballistic in one probe (for example Trilogy, ShockPulse-SE): ballistic cracks hard stones while ultrasound and suction clear fragments. Faster PCNL.

### 5. Electrohydraulic lithotripsy
- A spark at the electrode tip forms a **vapour bubble**; expansion and collapse send pressure waves in all directions.
- Cheap; 3–5 Fr flexible probes. Poorly focused, so it injures the ureter and the scope; weak on cystine. AUA (American Urological Association) advises against it as first-line ureteric lithotripsy; still used for bladder stones.

### 6. Lasers
- **Pulse duration decides mechanism.** Short pulses (<10 µs: pulsed dye, FREDDY) are photomechanical, with cavitation shocks. Long pulses (>250 µs: holmium, thulium) are **photothermal**: the stone is heated until it melts, decomposes or flashes its water to steam.

| | Ho:YAG | TFL |
|---|---|---|
| Wavelength | **2120 nm** (often quoted 2100) | 1940 nm (water absorption peak) |
| Frequency | Up to about 120 Hz | Up to about 2000 Hz |
| Peak power | High | Low → less retropulsion |
| Fibre | ≥200 µm | 50–150 µm (more flow and deflection) |
| Product | Fragments and dust | Finer dust |

- **Holmium:** standard since the 1990s; breaks **every** composition; flexible quartz fibres. Fragmenting 0.6–1.0 J × 6–10 Hz; dusting 0.2–0.3 J × 40–80 Hz; "popcorning" in a calyx.
- **Moses effect:** the start of the pulse boils a vapour channel through which the rest reaches the stone; pulse modulation reduces retropulsion.
- **TFL RCTs (randomised controlled trials):** superior to low-power (30 W) holmium; equal stone-free rate to 120 W pulse-modulated holmium.
- **Retropulsion** rises with pulse energy, not frequency, and falls with longer pulses.

### Safety
- Laser heat follows total power: ≤10 W in the ureter, pauses, high flow, access sheath. Eyewear for all.
- Never fire without seeing the fibre tip on the stone.
- SWL: haematoma, arrhythmia, steinstrasse. Contraindications: pregnancy, coagulopathy or anticoagulants, untreated infection, aneurysm, distal obstruction.

### Choice by site
| Site | Usual energy |
|---|---|
| Ureter | Laser (semirigid or flexible); pneumatic distally; SWL |
| Kidney | SWL; laser via flexible URS (ureteroscopy); PCNL with ultrasonic, combined or laser |
| Bladder | Laser, pneumatic, EHL, mechanical |

### Recent advances
- **BWL:** handheld ultrasound bursts (5–100 cycles) break stones without anaesthesia. **Ultrasonic propulsion** moves fragments.
- Suction sheaths; AI (artificial intelligence)-adjusted laser settings.

**Diagram to draw:** SWL electrohydraulic ellipsoid with F1 and F2, stone at F2.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - SWL: electromagnetic is the modern standard; slow rate (60–90/min) is better.
> - Holmium 2120 nm, photothermal, breaks every composition.
> - TFL 1940 nm: low peak power, finer dust, less retropulsion.
> - EHL is poorly focused, so it is not first-line in the ureter.

## Q3a. Antioxidants in urological malignancy
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(prevention trials also in 2017-Summer Paper 4, Q3a)

### Rationale
- ROS (reactive oxygen species) cause **oxidative DNA damage**; polyunsaturated fat and excess iron increase it. Antioxidants scavenge ROS, so diet studies suggested protection.
- Randomised trials of single high-dose supplements have been **null or harmful**.

### Prostate cancer
| Agent | Evidence | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **SELECT** (Selenium and Vitamin E Cancer Prevention Trial) | 35,533 men; selenium 200 µg, vitamin E 400 IU, both, or placebo | Stopped early (median 5.46 years) for futility. No benefit. Vitamin E alone trended to more cancer (P = 0.06); extended follow-up showed a significant **increase (about 17%)** |
| ATBC (Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta-Carotene) | Low-dose vitamin E in heavy smokers | Secondary analysis: prostate cancer ↓32%, deaths ↓41%; beta-carotene ↑ lung cancer |
| Selenised yeast | Skin-cancer trial | Secondary fall in prostate cancer prompted SELECT |
| Lycopene (cooked tomato) | Meta-analysis: modestly lower risk, mainly advanced cancer | WCRF (World Cancer Research Fund) downgraded "probable" (2007) to "no conclusion" (2018); supplement trials mostly null |
| Green tea catechins | HGPIN (high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia)/ASAP trials | Large fall in progression to cancer (meta-analysis); dose impractical |
| Vitamin C, high-dose antioxidants | High doses act as pro-oxidants | Supplements above median → more advanced cancer |

- **Also know:** vitamin D (VITAL, no effect); coffee (modestly lower advanced risk); pomegranate, soy and curcumin unproven.

### Bladder cancer
- Supplemental beta-carotene, vitamins C and E and folate do not lower risk; SELECT showed no effect.
- Fruit and vegetable benefit disappears after adjusting for smoking. Smoking cessation is the real prevention.

### Kidney cancer
- No antioxidant trial has shown benefit.

### Why the trials failed
- Single nutrient at high dose; wrong chemical form; non-smoking populations; PSA-era indolent cancers; U-shaped dose effects.

### Current position
- Antioxidant supplements are **not recommended** for cancer prevention. Advise a balanced diet, no smoking and normal weight.

**Diagram to draw:** ROS → DNA damage → cancer, with antioxidants blocking the first arrow.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - SELECT: no benefit; vitamin E alone increased prostate cancer.
> - ATBC benefit limited to smokers on low dose.
> - Lycopene downgraded to "no conclusion" (2018).

## Q3b. Bipolar energy
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also covered in 2025-Winter Paper 4, Q2)

### Principle
- Radiofrequency current (about 500 kHz) passes **only between two electrodes on the same instrument**. No dispersive pad; the patient is not in the circuit.
- Less power and less lateral thermal spread (1–6 mm vs 5–7 mm monopolar). In endoscopy, **saline** irrigation can be used.

### Monopolar vs bipolar
| | Monopolar | Bipolar |
|---|---|---|
| Circuit | Loop → patient → pad | Active and return poles on the instrument |
| Irrigant | Glycine 1.5%, sorbitol–mannitol | Normal saline |
| TUR (transurethral resection) syndrome | Yes | Theoretically eliminated |
| Capacitive coupling, pad burns | Yes | No |

### Endoscopic uses
- **Bipolar TURP (transurethral resection of prostate):**
  - *Plasma system:* energy vaporises saline around the loop into plasma (orange glow of sodium); cuts at lower temperature while sealing vessels; platinum–iridium loop.
  - *TURis (transurethral resection in saline):* the sheath acts as return electrode; chapter notes report strictures and fistulae.
  - *True bipolar:* dual loops.
  - Outcomes: equal IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score) and Qmax; less transfusion and clot retention; shorter catheter time and stay. NNT (number needed to treat) about 50 to prevent one TUR syndrome.
- **Bipolar TUVP (transurethral vaporisation):** PlasmaButton; AUA (American Urological Association) option for <30 and 30–80 cc.
- **Bipolar enucleation:** plasmakinetic energy can perform AEEP (anatomic endoscopic enucleation of prostate) satisfactorily; comparative data limited.
- **TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour):** fewer obturator jerks and less bleeding in reviews; one RCT (randomised controlled trial) showed only less thermal artefact.

### Laparoscopic and open uses
- Bipolar forceps for pinpoint haemostasis.
- **Advanced vessel sealers (LigaSure, EnSeal, Caiman):** compression plus bipolar current fuses collagen and elastin. Impedance feedback stops output. Seal vessels **≤7 mm**; spread 1–3 mm; one application only.
- Uses: prostatic pedicles, cystectomy.

### Safety
- Safer near pacemakers; touching tines complete the circuit without tissue.
- Obturator reflex reduced, not abolished.

**Diagram to draw:** monopolar loop-to-pad circuit beside bipolar loop with local return.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Bipolar: no pad, saline irrigation, no TUR syndrome.
> - Bipolar TURP equals monopolar efficacy with fewer complications.
> - Vessel sealers: vessels ≤7 mm, spread 1–3 mm.

## Q3c. Thalium laser in urology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also covered in 2024-Winter Paper 4, Q3c)

*Assumed meaning: thulium laser ("thalium" is a spelling error).*

### Types and physics
| | Tm:YAG (thulium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet) | TFL (thulium fibre laser) |
|---|---|---|
| Wavelength | 2013 nm | **1940 nm** (water absorption peak) |
| Source | Diode-pumped solid crystal | Diode-pumped thulium-doped silica fibre |
| Output | Continuous wave (now also pulsed) | Pulsed or continuous |
| Water absorption | High | About 4× holmium |

- Optical penetration is shallowest of urological lasers: about 0.2 mm (chapter values for TFL: 0.08–0.2 mm), against 0.31–0.4 mm for Ho:YAG (holmium:YAG, 2120 nm).
- Shallow absorption gives precise vaporisation and clean incision, but **carbonisation**.

### Stone lithotripsy (TFL)
- Frequency up to about 2000 Hz (holmium about 120 Hz); pulse energy as low as 0.025 J.
- **Low peak power, long pulse** → photothermal "dusting", minimal retropulsion.
- Fibres 50–150 µm → better irrigation and scope deflection. Small, quiet unit; no dedicated high-voltage outlet.
- Settings: start 0.6 J × 6 Hz; dusting 0.2 J × 40 Hz; avoid >40 Hz (pulses miss the stone). "Paint, do not drill."
- RCTs (randomised controlled trials): better than 30 W holmium; equal stone-free rate to 120 W pulse-modulated holmium with less retropulsion.

### Prostate
- **Techniques:** ThuVARP (vaporesection), ThuVAP (vaporisation), ThuVEP (vapoenucleation), **ThuLEP** (enucleation, 2010; mostly blunt dissection), ThuFLEP (fibre laser enucleation).
- Settings: 70–90 W enucleation, 30 W coagulation; morcellation as for HoLEP (holmium laser enucleation).
- Outcomes: ThuLEP equals HoLEP; size-independent; safe on anticoagulants (AUA [American Urological Association]). Retrograde ejaculation about four-fifths.

### Other uses
- **Also know:** en bloc bladder tumour resection; UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma) ablation; incision of strictures.

### Safety
- Heat follows power: ≤10 W in the ureter, high flow, access sheath. Eyewear for all.
- Concerns: efficacy on all stone types; thermal injury at high power.

**Diagram to draw:** water absorption curve marking 1940, 2013 and 2120 nm.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - TFL 1940 nm, Tm:YAG 2013 nm; water absorption about 4× holmium.
> - TFL: low peak power, fine dust, thin fibres.
> - ThuLEP is size-independent and equal to HoLEP.

## Q3d. Morcellation
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- Cutting a large specimen into small pieces so it leaves through a port, small incision or endoscope.
- **Manual:** ring forceps or Kelly clamp inside a bag. **Power (electromechanical):** rotating cylindrical blade (laparoscopy) or endoscopic tissue morcellator (prostate).

### 1. Endoscopic morcellation after enucleation
- Made **HoLEP (holmium laser enucleation of prostate)** practical; also ThuLEP (thulium laser enucleation).
- **Steps:**
  1. Achieve good haemostasis first.
  2. Pass a long nephroscope with a 5 mm channel.
  3. Run two inflow lines fully open to keep the bladder distended.
  4. Engage lobes with suction; cut only in mid-bladder, away from the wall.
  5. Collect fragments in a trap for histology.
- Devices: Piranha (rotating blades), VersaCut (oscillating).
- **Complications:** bladder mucosal injury (stop, release suction, disengage; mostly catheter only); intraperitoneal perforation needs laparotomy; **retained adenoma** (from malfunction or poor vision) needs repeat morcellation.

### 2. Laparoscopic morcellation (kidney, spleen)
- **LapSac** (nylon with polyurethane lining) is impermeable to tumour cells and bacteria.
- **Rules:** purpose-built bag; drapes; change gloves and instruments after; no deep forceps passes; keep pneumoperitoneum and watch the bag.

| | Intact extraction | Morcellation |
|---|---|---|
| Incision | Larger | Small, better cosmesis |
| Pain, time, stay | Similar | Similar |
| Pathology | Full staging | Type and grade kept; size, capsule, vein invasion, and depth in UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma) lost |
| Seeding | — | Rare port-site recurrence |

- Splenectomy morcellation risks **splenosis** if the bag ruptures.

### 3. FDA 2014 warning (gynaecology)
- In 2014 the FDA (Food and Drug Administration) warned against laparoscopic power morcellation in hysterectomy or myomectomy for fibroids, because unsuspected uterine sarcoma (about 1 in 350) was spread and upstaged.
- A boxed warning followed; contained (in-bag) morcellation is advised.
- Lesson for urology: avoid uncontained power morcellation of possible malignancy.

### Complications summary
- Bag rupture, seeding, lost staging, visceral or vascular injury, retained tissue.

**Diagram to draw:** enucleated lobes in a distended bladder with morcellator suction.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Morcellator made HoLEP practical; distend bladder, haemostasis first.
> - Morcellation loses stage information; histology still obtained.
> - FDA 2014: uncontained power morcellation spreads occult sarcoma.

## Q3e. KTP\*\*\* laser in urology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(lasers in BPH also in 2017-Winter Paper 2, Q3a)

*Assumed meaning: KTP (potassium titanyl phosphate) laser, the "GreenLight" laser; the asterisks are ignored.*

### Physics
- A 1064 nm Nd:YAG (neodymium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet) beam passes through a **KTP or LBO (lithium triborate) crystal**. This doubles frequency and halves wavelength to **532 nm** (green).
- Green light is absorbed by **haemoglobin**, not water. Optical penetration 0.8 mm; tissue effect 1–3 mm.
- Effect: **photoselective vaporisation** with a thin coagulation rim, so vaporisation is bloodless.

### Platforms
| Generation | Power |
|---|---|
| KTP | 80 W |
| LBO HPS (High Performance System) | 120 W |
| LBO XPS | **180 W**; 750 µm MoXy fibre (metal tip, fluid cooling) |

- Side-firing fibre emits at 70°; 23–24 Fr cystoscope; saline irrigation.

### PVP (photoselective vaporisation of prostate) technique
- Identify ureteric orifices; open the bladder neck first.
- Sweep 15–30° in 1–2 seconds at ≤2 mm working distance. Large bubbles mean efficient vaporisation.
- Pulsatile bleeding: coagulation mode (about 30 W). Energy >5 kJ/cc predicts complete vaporisation.

### Indications
- AUA (American Urological Association): prostates <30 cc and 30–80 cc; men at **higher bleeding risk** (anticoagulation continued). Not >80 cc (high retreatment).

### GOLIATH trial
- RCT (randomised controlled trial), 29 European centres, about 270 men, prostate ≤100 cc: **180 W PVP vs TURP** (transurethral resection of prostate).
- PVP **non-inferior** for IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score), Qmax and complications at 6 months, 1 and 2 years.
- PVP: shorter catheter time and stay, fewer early reinterventions. TURP: faster. Retrograde ejaculation (about two-thirds) and 2-year reoperation equal.

### Complications
- Dysuria and storage symptoms (highest of transurethral techniques); UTI (urinary tract infection) and epididymitis from coagulum; capsular perforation 0.2–1%; retrograde ejaculation.
- No TUR (transurethral resection) syndrome; transfusion very rare.
- **No tissue** for histology.

### Other uses
- **Also know:** laparoscopic non-contact cutting (largely supplanted); KTP enucleation possible.

**Diagram to draw:** side-firing fibre at 70° vaporising lateral lobe.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 532 nm: frequency-doubled Nd:YAG; haemoglobin chromophore.
> - GOLIATH: 180 W PVP non-inferior to TURP.
> - Best for anticoagulated men; not >80 cc.

## Q3f. Stem cell therapy
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(tissue engineering also in 2023-Winter Paper 1, Q1)

### Definition
- Stem cells **self-renew** and **differentiate** into specialised cells. Therapy uses them to repair or replace damaged tissue.

### Types
| Type | Source | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Embryonic | Blastocyst | Pluripotent; ethics, teratoma |
| Adult mesenchymal | Bone marrow, adipose (ADSC, adipose-derived stem cells), umbilical cord | Easy harvest; mainly paracrine |
| Muscle-derived (MDSC) | Skeletal muscle biopsy | Used for sphincter and detrusor |
| iPSCs (induced pluripotent stem cells) | Reprogrammed adult cells | Patient-specific; tumour risk |
| Urine-derived, spermatogonial | Urine; testis | Research |

### Mechanisms
- Differentiation into muscle, nerve or endothelium.
- **Paracrine action:** growth factors, anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic and neurotrophic signals.
- Homing to injured tissue; seeding of scaffolds.

### Applications
- **SUI (stress urinary incontinence):**
  - Autologous muscle-derived cells: thigh muscle biopsy → culture → transurethral or periurethral injection into the sphincter.
  - Aim: regenerate the striated sphincter, not just bulk it.
  - Early studies improved; placebo-controlled trials have not shown clear superiority, so it remains investigational.
  - After childbirth injury, stem cell homing chemokines (CXCL12, CCL7) rise.
- **ED (erectile dysfunction):** intracavernosal bone-marrow, adipose or cord cells; animal and small uncontrolled human series only. SMSNA (Sexual Medicine Society of North America): experimental, IRB (institutional review board) protocols only.
- **Underactive detrusor:** muscle-derived cells injected into the bladder wall; two open-label studies improved voiding; research.
- **Tissue engineering:** cell-seeded scaffolds for bladder (Atala, 2006) and urethra (Raya-Rivera, 2011); adipose stem cell-seeded grafts for Peyronie disease (experimental).
- **Fertility:** spermatogonial stem cell banking in prepubertal cancer patients.
- **Kidney:** iPSC kidney organoids (research).

### Challenges
- Poor cell survival and retention; tumour formation (embryonic, iPSC); standardisation, cost and regulation.
- Unregulated "stem cell clinics" offer unproven treatments outside trials, a growing ethical concern.
- **Also know:** cancer stem cells (a different concept) may drive tumour recurrence and resistance.

**Diagram to draw:** muscle biopsy → culture → periurethral injection into sphincter.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Adult stem cells act mainly by paracrine signals.
> - SUI muscle-derived cells and ED stem cells are investigational.
> - Embryonic and iPSCs carry tumour risk.
