## Q1. Discuss anatomy of kidney. Describe role of nephron sparing nephrectomy in small renal mass.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2017-Summer Paper 1, Q1; NSS also in 2022-Summer Paper 3, Q1 and 2025-Winter Paper 1, Q2)

*"Nephron sparing nephrectomy" is read as nephron-sparing surgery (NSS): partial nephrectomy and ablation.*

### Surgical anatomy of the kidney
**Position:** retroperitoneal, T12–L3; right kidney 1–2 cm lower (liver). About 11 cm, 125–170 g. It lies on the cone-shaped psoas, so the upper pole is medial and posterior and the kidney is angled 30–50° behind the coronal plane. It descends about 3 cm on inspiration.

**Relations**

| Aspect | Right | Left |
|---|---|---|
| Superior | Liver, adrenal, 12th rib | Spleen, stomach, adrenal, 11th–12th ribs |
| Medial/anterior | Duodenum (2nd part), hepatic flexure | Pancreatic tail with splenic vessels, splenic flexure, jejunum |
| Posterior (both) | Diaphragm, psoas, quadratus lumborum; subcostal, iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal nerves | Same |

**Coverings:** true capsule, perirenal fat, **Gerota fascia**, pararenal fat. Gerota fascia is closed above and laterally but open below, so it contains tumour and collections, yet fluid tracks into the pelvis. Transperitoneal access: reflect the colon along the white line of Toldt; Kocher manoeuvre for the right hilum.

**Internal structure:** cortex (about 1 cm) and 8–18 pyramids; columns of Bertin carry the interlobar arteries. 0.4–1.2 million nephrons per kidney. Collecting system: see Q3d.

**Vascular anatomy (the basis of partial nephrectomy)**
- Hilum, anterior to posterior: vein, artery, pelvis, posterior segmental artery.
- Renal artery from the aorta at L1–L2; the right passes behind the IVC (inferior vena cava).
- **Five segmental end arteries**: posterior (first branch, behind the pelvis) and four from the anterior division (apical, upper, middle, lower). With no anastomoses, occlusion causes segmental infarction, but each segment can be clamped or resected separately.
- **Brödel line:** avascular plane between anterior (two-thirds) and posterior (one-third) territories, just behind the lateral border.
- **Accessory arteries** in 25–28%, the sole supply of their territory (see Practice Set 1 Paper 2, Q3c).
- **Veins anastomose freely**, so a segmental vein can be ligated safely. Left renal vein 6–10 cm, receives adrenal, gonadal and lumbar veins; circumaortic in 2–16%.
- **Also know:** lymph to left para-aortic (left) or interaortocaval and paracaval nodes (right); sympathetic T8–L1.

**Diagram to draw:** transverse kidney section with segmental arteries and Brödel line.

### Small renal mass (SRM)
- An enhancing renal mass **≤4 cm (cT1a)**, usually incidental.
- 20–30% of masses ≤4 cm and about 40% ≤2 cm are benign (oncocytoma, fat-poor angiomyolipoma). Most cancers are indolent; under 2% metastasise.
- **Work-up:** multiphase CT (computed tomography) or MRI (magnetic resonance imaging), chest imaging, eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) and proteinuria. **RMB (renal mass biopsy)** when it may change management: seeding almost unreported, non-diagnostic about 14%.
- **R.E.N.A.L. nephrometry** (Radius, Exophytic, Nearness to collecting system, Anterior/posterior, Location): 4–6 low, 7–9 moderate, 10–12 high complexity.

### Why spare nephrons
- RN (radical nephrectomy) causes CKD (chronic kidney disease), linked to cardiovascular events and death. In one cohort CKD followed RN in 65% versus 20% after PN (partial nephrectomy).
- PN keeps about 90% of global function, RN 65–70%, with equal cancer control for cT1a.

### Indications

| Category | Situations |
|---|---|
| Imperative | Solitary kidney, bilateral tumours, RN would mean dialysis |
| Relative | Contralateral kidney threatened (stones, stenosis, diabetes, hypertension); hereditary RCC (renal cell carcinoma) |
| Elective | cT1a with a normal contralateral kidney: **standard of care** |

- **AUA (American Urological Association) 2021:** prioritise PN for cT1a. RN only if all three: high complexity; no CKD or proteinuria; normal contralateral kidney with eGFR likely >45 after RN.

### Partial nephrectomy
1. Open, laparoscopic or robotic (equal oncology); early hilar control.
2. Intraoperative ultrasound maps the tumour.
3. Clamp the artery; ice slush if ischaemia ≥25–30 minutes is expected.
4. Excise with a thin rim; a negative margin matters, its width does not.
5. Close vessels and collecting system; capsular renorrhaphy; drain.
- Function depends mainly on the **parenchyma preserved**; ischaemia is secondary. Off-clamp showed no lasting functional gain in randomised trials.
- **Enucleation** along the pseudocapsule for familial, multifocal disease or severe CKD.
- **Trifecta:** negative margin, no major complication, warm ischaemia ≤25 minutes.

### Other nephron-sparing options
- **Thermal ablation** (cryoablation, radiofrequency): alternative for cT1a **<3 cm**, percutaneous, biopsy first. Local recurrence is higher than PN but salvaged by repeat ablation.
- **Active surveillance:** <2 cm, elderly or comorbid. Intervene if >3 cm or growth >5 mm a year. DISSRM registry: almost no metastases.

### Complications of PN
- Urine leak (drain, stent); haemorrhage; **delayed haematuria from pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula** (angioembolisation); acute kidney injury; positive margin.
- **Hyperfiltration** when >50% of a solitary kidney is lost: proteinuria, FSGS (focal segmental glomerulosclerosis); ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitor.

### Outcomes and evidence
- Elective PN: local recurrence 1–2%; imperative 3–5%.
- **EORTC (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer) 30904 (2011):** PN versus RN, ≤5 cm: better function, similar oncology; overall survival slightly favoured RN, but the trial is flawed.
- **Recent advances:** 3D (three-dimensional) models, segmental clamping, robotic single-port PN.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Five segmental end arteries; veins anastomose; Brödel line avascular.
> - SRM ≤4 cm: 20–30% benign; PN is standard, ablation <3 cm, surveillance <2 cm.
> - AUA: RN only if complex + no CKD + eGFR likely >45.
> - Preserved parenchyma, not clamp time, governs function; EORTC 30904.

## Q2. Anatomy of prostate and its neurovascular bundles with its clinical details.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(nerve-sparing techniques also in 2023-Winter Paper 4, Q3a)

### Introduction
- A fibromuscular, glandular organ around the proximal urethra, from the bladder neck (**base**) to the urogenital diaphragm (**apex**), directly in front of the rectum. Normal adult weight about 20 g.
- Develops from the urogenital sinus at week 10; androgen acts through the **mesenchymal** AR (androgen receptor). 5α-reductase type 2 deficiency gives an incomplete prostate.

### Relations
- **Anterior:** retropubic space (of Retzius), puboprostatic ligaments, DVC (dorsal venous complex).
- **Posterior:** Denonvilliers fascia, then rectal ampulla (DRE [digital rectal examination], TRUS [transrectal ultrasound]).
- **Lateral:** levator ani with its fascia; NVB (neurovascular bundle) posterolaterally.
- **Base:** bladder neck; seminal vesicles and vasa behind.
- **Inside:** urethra bends about 35° at the **verumontanum**, where the ejaculatory ducts open (5 and 7 o'clock) beside the midline utricle.

### Zonal anatomy (McNeal 1981)

| Zone | Glandular share | Position | Clinical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peripheral (PZ) | About 70% | Posterior and lateral; longest ducts | **70% of cancers**; felt on DRE; biopsy target |
| Central (CZ) | About 25% | Cone around the ejaculatory ducts at the base | Few cancers; mimics HGPIN (high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia) |
| Transition (TZ) | About 5% | Two lobes beside the preprostatic sphincter; shortest ducts | **BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia)**; anterior cancers (about 15%) |
| Periurethral glands | Small | Within the preprostatic sphincter | Middle-lobe BPH |
| Anterior fibromuscular stroma | No glands | Anterior | — |

- **Capsule:** no true histological capsule, so cancer shows "extraprostatic extension". In BPH the compressed PZ forms the **surgical capsule**, the plane of enucleation; corpora amylacea mark it. The capsule transmits growth pressure to the urethra, which is why TUIP (transurethral incision) relieves obstruction.

### Fascial investments

| Layer | Position | Surgical rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Prostatic fascia** | On the gland; carries DVC tributaries anteriorly | Stays on the prostate in nerve sparing |
| **Levator fascia** | Covers levator; fuses with prostatic fascia as the lateral pelvic fascia | Incised to reach the NVB |
| **Denonvilliers fascia** | Between prostate and rectum; thickest at base and vesicles | Excised completely for the posterior margin |

- Endopelvic fascia and arcus tendineus anchor the gland; puboprostatic ligaments fix it to the pubis.

### Blood supply and lymphatics
- **Inferior vesical artery** ends as: **urethral group** (enters at the vesicoprostatic junction, 5 and 7 o'clock; supplies the adenoma) and **capsular group** (posterolateral, in the lateral pelvic fascia, marking the cavernous nerves).
- **Accessory pudendal arteries** (obturator or vesical) run over the anterolateral prostate; dividing them reduces penile inflow.
- **Veins:** deep dorsal vein passes under the pubis and divides into a superficial branch and lateral plexuses (**Santorini plexus**), draining via inferior vesical veins to the internal iliac vein. They anastomose freely, so tears bleed heavily.
- **Lymph:** obturator, internal and external iliac, presacral nodes.

### Neurovascular bundle
- **Pelvic plexus:** parasympathetic S2–S4 plus sympathetic via the hypogastric nerves (T10–L2); beside the rectum 5–11 cm from the anal verge, centred on the seminal vesicle tip.
- **NVB of Walsh (Walsh and Donker, 1982):** capsular vessels plus **cavernous nerves**. The nerves are invisible; the vessels mark them.
- It forms 20–30 mm below the prostatovesical junction, posterolaterally, **between prostatic and levator fascia**, 3–5 mm from the capsule, outside Denonvilliers fascia.
- At the apex it lies at **5 and 7 o'clock**, posterolateral to the sphincter. The nerves then swing to about **3 and 9 o'clock** at the mid-urethra and enter the corpora behind the dorsal penile artery. The apex is where they are most often injured.
- Distribution varies: main posterolateral bundle, a posterior "hammock" and anterolateral fibres (basis of high anterior release).
- **Striated sphincter:** a vertical tube over the apex (Oelrich), pudendal nerve; passive continence.

**Diagram to draw:** axial prostate showing the three fasciae and NVBs at 5 and 7 o'clock.

### Clinical details
- **BPH surgery:** TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate) stays proximal to the verumontanum (sphincter lies just distal); urethral-group vessels at 5 and 7 o'clock bleed.
- **Cancer:** PZ cancer exits posterolaterally by perineural invasion; seminal vesicle invasion is via the base. Anterior TZ tumours are missed by DRE and biopsy, so MRI helps.
- **Radical prostatectomy (RP):**
  1. Incise endopelvic fascia; spare the pubourethral ligament.
  2. Ligate the DVC over the urethra before division; do not enter the anterior apex.
  3. Preserve accessory pudendal arteries where possible.
  4. Excise Denonvilliers fascia completely; the apex is the commonest positive-margin site.

| Nerve-sparing plane | Course | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Interfascial** | Between prostatic and levator fascia | **Standard plane** |
| Intrafascial | Under the prostatic fascia | Most nerves; more positive margins |
| Extrafascial | Outside levator fascia | Widest margin; ED (erectile dysfunction) likely |

- **NVB injury:** incision, entrapment, **heat**, **traction**. So release antegrade, athermal and traction-free. Excise one NVB for palpable extracapsular disease or high-risk MRI; margins are equal with or without nerve sparing.
- **Continence:** Retzius-sparing (Bocciardi 2010) and hood techniques preserve anterior support: earlier continence, equal at 12 months.
- **Also know:** rectal injury at the ampulla (rectourethralis); ED after rectal surgery; PDE5 (phosphodiesterase type 5) inhibitor rehabilitation.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - PZ 70% of cancers; TZ gives BPH; CZ around ejaculatory ducts.
> - NVB between prostatic and levator fascia, 5 and 7 o'clock; apex most vulnerable.
> - Interfascial plane is standard; avoid heat and traction.
> - Ligate DVC; excise Denonvilliers fascia; spare accessory pudendals.

## Q3a. MR urography
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- MRU (magnetic resonance urography): MRI of kidneys, ureters and bladder giving anatomy and function **without ionising radiation**.

### Techniques

| | Static MRU | Excretory MRU |
|---|---|---|
| Sequence | Heavily T2-weighted; urine is bright | Gadolinium-enhanced T1 |
| Contrast | None | GBCA (gadolinium-based contrast agent) |
| Depends on function | No | Yes; low-dose furosemide improves it |
| Best for | Dilated or obstructed system | Non-dilated system, excretion |

- **Functional MRU (children):** hydration, bladder catheter, furosemide 1 mg/kg (maximum 20 mg), T2 images, then dynamic T1 after slow contrast injection. Patlak analysis gives differential function, GFR (glomerular filtration rate) and drainage.

### Appearances
- Stones and calcification give **signal voids**; tumour, clot, gas and sloughed papillae are low-signal defects in bright urine.
- Fast images (about 2 seconds) separate lesions from peristalsis.

### Indications
- CT urography contraindicated: contrast allergy, renal insufficiency, **pregnancy** (separates physiological from calculous dilatation; small stones missed).
- **Children:** complex anomalies, ectopic ureter in the incontinent girl, duplex systems, megaureter, crossing vessels in UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) obstruction, ectopic kidneys.
- Hydronephrosis: alternative to MAG3 (mercaptoacetyltriglycine) renography; equivalence not yet proven.
- Upper tract tumours and renal masses (added to mass protocols).

### Advantages and limitations

| Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|
| No radiation; best anatomical detail | Cost, availability, long study (up to 1 hour) |
| Anatomy, function and drainage in one test | Sedation often needed under 6 years |
| As accurate as CTU (CT urography) for obstruction | Poor for stones; complex post-processing |
| Safe in iodinated contrast allergy | Pacemakers, metal implants, claustrophobia |

### Gadolinium safety
- **NSF (nephrogenic systemic fibrosis)** was the concern at GFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m². Group II agents (gadobenate, gadoteridol, gadoterate, gadobutrol) carry little or no risk even in end-stage disease.
- **Pregnancy:** gadolinium only if it clearly helps.
- **EAU (European Association of Urology):** CTU is preferred to MRU for upper tract urothelial cancer.

**Diagram to draw:** coronal static T2 MRU of a hydronephrotic kidney with a dark stone in the ureter.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Static T2 (no contrast, function-independent) versus excretory gadolinium T1.
> - Stones are signal voids; CTU preferred for urothelial cancer.
> - Functional MRU: furosemide 1 mg/kg, Patlak analysis for split function.
> - NSF risk minimal with group II agents.

## Q3b. Urine cytology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and principle
- Microscopy of exfoliated urothelial cells (Papanicolaou, 1945).
- **Principle:** high-grade cells are poorly cohesive and shed readily, with obvious nuclear atypia. Low-grade cells look almost normal. So cytology detects **high-grade tumours and CIS (carcinoma in situ)** but misses low-grade tumours.

### Specimens
- Voided urine (avoid the first morning sample, as cells degenerate overnight); bladder washing (barbotage) gives more cells; selective upper tract urine or washings; ileal conduit urine.
- Paris System 2nd edition advises at least 30 mL of voided urine.

### Performance
- Sensitivity 31–62%, specificity 94–100%; strongly grade-dependent.
- **False positives:** instrumentation, stones, infection, radiotherapy, intravesical BCG (bacille Calmette–Guérin) or chemotherapy, polyomavirus ("decoy" cells), ileal conduit.
- **False negatives:** low-grade tumour, low cellularity, degenerated cells.

### The Paris System
- Introduced 2013 (International Congress of Cytology), published 2016; **2nd edition 2022**. Focus: detection of **HGUC (high-grade urothelial carcinoma)**. Key criterion: **N:C (nuclear-to-cytoplasmic) ratio**, plus hyperchromasia, coarse chromatin and irregular nuclear membrane.

| Category | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Non-diagnostic | Inadequate sample |
| Negative for HGUC | N:C <0.5, or atypia explained by a benign cause |
| AUC (atypical urothelial cells) | N:C ≥0.5 and one feature |
| Suspicious for HGUC | N:C ≥0.7, at least two features, few cells (<5–10) |
| HGUC | N:C ≥0.7, at least two features, many cells |
| LGUN (low-grade urothelial neoplasm) | Papillary fragments; low-grade cannot be confirmed on cytology alone |
| Other malignancy | Squamous, adenocarcinoma, secondary tumours |

- It reduces equivocal "atypia" reports and links each category to a rising risk of high-grade cancer.

### Clinical uses
- **Not** for screening or initial microhaematuria evaluation (AUA/SUFU [American Urological Association / Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction] 2020).
- Gross haematuria work-up; NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer) surveillance (mandatory in high risk); suspected CIS; upper tract tumours.
- **Positive cytology with normal cystoscopy:** check upper tracts (CT urography, selective washings, ureteroscopy) and take random bladder and prostatic urethral biopsies.
- **Also know:** adjuncts FISH (fluorescence in situ hybridisation; chromosomes 3, 7, 17, 9p21), NMP22 (nuclear matrix protein 22), BTA (bladder tumour antigen), uCyt, CxBladder: more sensitive, less specific; none replaces cystoscopy.

**Diagram to draw:** normal urothelial cell beside an HGUC cell with a high N:C ratio and coarse chromatin.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - High specificity, low sensitivity; good for high grade and CIS.
> - Paris System: N:C ≥0.7 for suspicious and HGUC; AUC N:C ≥0.5.
> - Positive cytology, normal cystoscopy: upper tracts plus random biopsies.
> - Not for screening or initial microhaematuria.

## Q3c. Varicocele – Pathophysiology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and epidemiology
- Abnormal dilatation and tortuosity of the **pampiniform plexus** of the internal spermatic veins.
- About 15% of men, 35% with primary and 75–81% with secondary infertility. Adolescents 8–16%; appears after age 10, peaks at Tanner 3.
- About 90% are left-sided; sensitive tests show many are bilateral.

### Venous anatomy
| Group | Drains to |
|---|---|
| Anterior (internal spermatic) | IVC (inferior vena cava) on the right, left renal vein on the left |
| Middle (deferential) | Pelvic veins |
| Posterior (cremasteric) | Epigastric and pudendal veins |
- The middle and posterior groups provide return after repair and explain recurrence.

### Why it develops
1. **Left-sided anatomy:** the left vein enters the left renal vein at a right angle, 8–10 cm higher than the right enters the IVC. The taller hydrostatic column overloads its valves. The right vein enters the IVC obliquely, which acts as a valve.
2. **Valve incompetence** or absence, with reflux; continuous reflux predicts progression.
3. **Nutcracker phenomenon:** left renal vein compressed between aorta and SMA (superior mesenteric artery).
4. **Collateral venous pathways** (commoner in adults).
5. **Genetic:** 4–8 times commoner in first-degree relatives. **Tall, thin habitus.**
- **Secondary:** a **right-sided or non-decompressing** varicocele suggests a retroperitoneal mass or renal tumour with venous obstruction, so image the abdomen.

### How it damages the testis

| Mechanism | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Heat (primary)** | Loss of countercurrent heat exchange; the testis normally runs 2–4 °C below core temperature |
| Oxidative stress | Raised reactive oxygen species → sperm DNA fragmentation |
| Hypoperfusion and stasis | Hypoxia and toxin (renal and adrenal metabolite) accumulation |
| Apoptosis | Germ cell loss |
| Leydig dysfunction | Lower testosterone than controls |
- The injury is **progressive and duration-dependent**, and may affect **both** testes.

### Consequences
- **Testicular hypotrophy:** asymmetry above about 20% is the usual adolescent indication.
- Impaired semen parameters, raised DNA fragmentation, possible non-obstructive azoospermia.
- Hypogonadism; dull ache on standing (pain in 2–11%).

### Grading
| Grade | Finding |
|---|---|
| Subclinical | Doppler only (do not treat) |
| I | Palpable only with Valsalva |
| II | Palpable at rest, not visible |
| III | Visible |

### Reversibility (proof of mechanism)
- Repair improves semen, DNA fragmentation, testosterone and pregnancy. **Madgar RCT (randomised controlled trial, 1995):** pregnancy 44% versus 10% at 1 year. Earlier repair recovers more.

**Diagram to draw:** left and right testicular veins draining into renal vein and IVC.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Left vein: right-angled entry, 8–10 cm higher column; 90% left.
> - Heat from lost countercurrent exchange is the main injury; oxidative DNA damage.
> - Right or non-decompressing varicocele: image for a renal mass.
> - Injury is progressive; subclinical varicocele is not treated.

## Q3d. Anatomy of renal collecting system and its relevance to PCNL
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2022-Summer Paper 1, Q1 and 2017-Winter Paper 1, Q1)

### Anatomy of the collecting system
- Papilla → minor calyx → infundibulum → major calyx → pelvis → UPJ (ureteropelvic junction).
- **Minor calyx:** a cup around a papilla; its side projections are the **fornices**. **Compound calyces** (2–3 papillae) cluster at the poles.
- 7–9 calyces (range 4–19): upper pole 3, interpolar 3–4, lower pole 2–3. Hilar calyces form **anterior and posterior rows**.
- **Pelvis:** 3–10 mL; intrarenal or extrarenal. A **duplex pelvis** has no link between upper and lower calyces.
- Kidney rotated 30–50° posteriorly, so **posterior calyces point posterolaterally** towards the prone surgeon.

### Sampaio classification (endocasts)
| Type | Frequency | Midzone drainage |
|---|---|---|
| A-I | 45% | Via superior or inferior group |
| A-II | 17.2% | **Crossed calyces** to both groups |
| B-I | 21.4% | Independent major calyx |
| B-II | 16.4% | Minor calyces straight into the pelvis |
- **Perpendicular minor calyx (11.4%):** hidden on a pyelogram; narrow neck (≤4 mm).

### Vascular relations
- Five segmental end arteries; interlobar arteries run in the **columns of Bertin**, beside the infundibula. The posterior segmental artery passes behind the pelvis.
- **Brödel line:** avascular plane just behind the lateral border; posterior calyces lie along it.

| Puncture site (Sampaio) | Vascular injury |
|---|---|
| Upper-pole infundibulum | 67% (arterial 26%) |
| Mid-pole infundibulum | Arterial 23% |
| Lower-pole infundibulum | Infundibular artery in 38% |
| **Calyceal fornix** | Venous <8%, **no arterial injury** |

### Relevance to PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy)
- **Target:** a posterior calyx with a short, wide infundibulum, punctured **through the papilla and fornix** along the calyx axis, never the infundibulum or pelvis.
- **Identify posterior calyces:** rotating the C-arm towards the surgeon shortens them; injected air fills them in the prone patient; ultrasound.
- **Upper-pole access:** straightest route to the pelvis, UPJ and staghorn branches. Above the 12th rib, chest complications rise; highest above the 11th. Puncture in expiration.
- **Lower-pole access:** chest-safe, but colon risk (retrorenal colon, left side) and poor reach to the UPJ.
- **Pitfalls:** perpendicular calyx, crossed calyces, duplex pelvis, compound polar calyces.
- **Also know:** horseshoe kidney (medial, upper-pole access); calyceal diverticulum (direct puncture); bleeding from AV (arteriovenous) fistula or pseudoaneurysm needs angioembolisation.

**Diagram to draw:** transverse section showing anterior and posterior calyces, Brödel line and the needle through a posterior fornix.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Sampaio A-I 45%, A-II 17.2%, B-I 21.4%, B-II 16.4%.
> - Fornix puncture: no arterial injury; upper-pole infundibulum 67%.
> - Posterior calyx, short wide infundibulum, along the Brödel line.
> - Supracostal risk rises above the 12th rib, highest above the 11th.

## Q3e. Compare and contrast various detrusor relaxants in management of OAB
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(mirabegron also in 2024-Winter Paper 3, Q3d)

### Introduction
- OAB (overactive bladder): urgency, with or without urgency incontinence, usually with frequency and nocturia.
- Drugs follow behavioural therapy (AUA/SUFU [American Urological Association / Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction]). Two classes: **antimuscarinics** and **β3-adrenoceptor agonists**.

### Mechanisms
- **Antimuscarinics:** acetylcholine acts on **M3** receptors (Gq, IP3 [inositol trisphosphate], calcium) to contract the detrusor; M2 is commoner and enhances it. Blockade reduces involuntary contractions and afferent (urothelial) signalling.
- **β3 agonists:** β3 is the commonest adrenoceptor in the detrusor. Activation raises **cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate)** and relaxes the detrusor during storage. Voiding contraction is spared.

### Antimuscarinic agents

| Drug | Distinguishing feature | Usual dose |
|---|---|---|
| Oxybutynin | Non-selective; direct relaxant; metabolite causes dry mouth | IR 5 mg two to three times daily; ER 5–15 mg; patch |
| Tolterodine | Bladder over salivary selective | ER 4 mg daily |
| Fesoterodine | Prodrug of tolterodine's active metabolite | 4–8 mg daily |
| Solifenacin | 10-fold M3 > M2; long half-life | 5–10 mg daily |
| Darifenacin | About 30-fold M3-selective | 7.5–15 mg daily |
| Trospium | Quaternary amine; does not cross the blood–brain barrier; renal excretion | 20 mg twice daily |
| β3: mirabegron | CYP2D6 (cytochrome P450 2D6) inhibitor | 25 mg, up to 50 mg |
| β3: vibegron | No CYP2D6 interaction | 75 mg daily |

### Comparison

| Feature | Antimuscarinics | β3 agonists |
|---|---|---|
| Efficacy | Similar; response is individual | Similar (TAURUS: mirabegron ≈ tolterodine) |
| Dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision | Common; main reason for stopping | Near placebo |
| Cognition | M1 blockade; **dementia risk 10–65% higher with use >3 months** (SUFU) | No anticholinergic burden |
| Retention | Up to 3% in men | Low |
| Cardiovascular | Tachycardia | Raised blood pressure; avoid if ≥180/110 mmHg |
| Contraindications | Urinary or gastric retention, uncontrolled narrow-angle glaucoma | Severe uncontrolled hypertension |
| Cost | Lower (generics) | Higher |

- **Choice:** in the elderly or cognitively impaired, prefer a β3 agonist or ER trospium, darifenacin or fesoterodine. ER forms cause less dry mouth.

### Combination
- **SYNERGY and BESIDE:** solifenacin plus mirabegron beats each alone; in solifenacin non-responders it matches solifenacin 10 mg with less dry mouth. Men with BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia): add to an α-blocker, watch PVR (post-void residual).

### Others
- **Also know:** intravesical oxybutynin (neurogenic bladder); imipramine; calcium antagonists and potassium-channel openers failed. Refractory OAB: onabotulinumtoxinA 100 U, tibial or sacral neuromodulation.

**Diagram to draw:** detrusor cell: acetylcholine → M3 → contraction, versus noradrenaline → β3 → cAMP → relaxation.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - M3 mediates contraction; β3 raises cAMP to relax.
> - Equal efficacy; β3 avoids dry mouth and cognitive burden.
> - Antimuscarinics >3 months: 10–65% more dementia; trospium avoids the brain.
> - Solifenacin + mirabegron (SYNERGY) works.

## Q3f. B/l congenital VUR - Pathophysiology and natural history
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(VUR management also in 2015-Summer Paper 3, Q2 and 2020-Summer Paper 1, Q2)

*"B/l congenital VUR" is read as bilateral primary (congenital) vesicoureteral reflux, often detected antenatally.*

### Definition
- VUR (vesicoureteral reflux): retrograde flow of urine from bladder to upper tract. **Primary** VUR is a congenital deficiency of the UVJ (ureterovesical junction); secondary VUR follows high bladder pressure.

### Pathophysiology
**1. Embryology**
- If the ureteric bud arises too early, it over-rotates: the orifice lies **high and lateral** with a short tunnel, causing reflux. Abnormal bud–metanephros contact also causes **renal dysplasia**. So high-grade VUR and dysplasia travel together.

**2. Failed antireflux mechanism**
- **Passive flap valve:** filling compresses the intramural ureter against detrusor backing. Tunnel length to ureteric diameter is about **5:1** normally versus 1.4:1 in refluxing units (Paquin).
- Ureteric peristalsis and bladder dynamics also matter. Infant boys void at high pressure; BBD (bladder and bowel dysfunction) adds a secondary component.

**3. Renal damage: two kinds**

| | Reflux-associated dysplasia | Acquired scar |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Abnormal bud development | Pyelonephritis: infected urine plus VUR |
| Typical patient | Male infant, grade IV–V, antenatal hydronephrosis | Girl, recurrent febrile UTI (urinary tract infection), BBD |
| Preventable | No | Yes |

- **Intrarenal reflux** occurs through compound polar papillae at only 2 mmHg in neonates, so scars are polar.
- **Sterile reflux rarely scars**, except with high pressure (valves).
- **Massive bilateral VUR:** voided urine returns to the bladder, giving a thin-walled megacystis and megaureters (**megacystis–megaureter association**).

### Grading (International Reflux Study)
| Grade | Appearance |
|---|---|
| I | Ureter only |
| II | Ureter, pelvis and calyces; no dilatation |
| III | Mild–moderate dilatation |
| IV | Moderate dilatation and tortuosity |
| V | Gross dilatation; papillary impressions lost |

### Natural history
- Resolution comes from **elongation of the intravesical ureter** with growth and stable bladder dynamics.
- Grade II resolves in 63–85%, grade III in about 50%, **grade IV–V and bilateral grade III rarely** (IRSC [International Reflux Study in Children]).
- **Bilaterality and older age delay resolution:** unilateral grade III at age 1–2 resolves most; bilateral at 6–10 least. Persistence is highest in bilateral grade IV.
- Infants with grade IV–V can still resolve in year 1. Observation traditionally lasts 5 years.
- **Consequences of bilateral damage:**
  - Hypertension in 34–38% of scarred children in adult life.
  - CKD (chronic kidney disease): reflux nephropathy is the second commonest diagnosis in paediatric transplant recipients (non-Black).
  - Poor somatic growth.
  - Pregnancy: more pyelonephritis, pre-eclampsia (bilateral scars more than unilateral).
- Correction does not reverse dysplasia or lower blood pressure.

**Diagram to draw:** normal oblique long tunnel versus lateral orifice with short tunnel.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Lateral orifice, short tunnel (5:1 lost) and dysplasia share one embryological cause.
> - Dysplasia (boys, congenital) versus scar (girls, infection).
> - Grade IV–V and bilateral grade III rarely resolve.
> - Scarred children: 34–38% hypertension in adult life.
