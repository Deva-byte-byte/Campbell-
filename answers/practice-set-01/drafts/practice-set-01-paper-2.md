## Q1. Treatment of metastatic CA prostate in 60 yr male with retention of urine and bone pain
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(mHSPC systemic therapy also answered in 2025-Winter Paper 3, Q3b)

### Introduction
- This is newly diagnosed (synchronous) **mHSPC (metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer)** with two urgent problems: an obstructed outlet and painful bone metastases.
- Treat the emergencies first, start castration safely (no flare), then intensify systemic therapy.

### Immediate assessment
- **Ask and examine for cord compression:** back pain, leg weakness, sensory level, bowel symptoms. DRE (digital rectal examination).
- **Bloods:** PSA (prostate-specific antigen), creatinine, haemoglobin, calcium, ALP (alkaline phosphatase), testosterone.
- **Imaging:** ultrasound for hydronephrosis and residual; **bone scan and CT (computed tomography)**; PSMA (prostate-specific membrane antigen) PET is more sensitive but trials used conventional imaging. **MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) of the whole spine** if any back pain or neurological sign.
- **Histology:** prostate biopsy (or TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate) chips). **Germline genetic testing** for all men with metastatic disease.

### Step 1: Relieve the retention
- **Urethral catheter** (suprapubic if urethral fails). Watch for post-obstructive diuresis.
- Persisting renal impairment with hydronephrosis means ureteric obstruction by tumour at the trigone: **nephrostomy or stent**.
- ADT (androgen deprivation therapy) shrinks the prostate, so try **TWOC (trial without catheter)** after a few months of ADT.
- If TWOC fails or symptoms persist: **channel (palliative) TURP**. ADT alone does not prevent local progression (older series: about a third needed TURP within 60 days of orchidectomy).

### Step 2: Start ADT at once, without flare
| Option | Point |
|---|---|
| **Bilateral orchidectomy** | Castrate within hours; best for impending cord compression |
| **GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) antagonist**: degarelix 240 mg subcutaneously, then 80 mg monthly; oral relugolix | **No flare**; castrate by day 3 in >95% (degarelix) |
| **LHRH (luteinising hormone-releasing hormone) agonist + antiandrogen** | Agonist raises LH (luteinising hormone) up to 10-fold for 10–20 days (**flare**). Give bicalutamide 50 mg daily with or before the agonist, for 21–28 days |

- **Why avoid flare:** the testosterone surge can worsen bone pain, retention and cord compression in spinal metastases.
- Target castrate testosterone **<50 ng/dL**. Antiandrogen alone is not recommended.

### Step 3: Bone pain
- **Analgesia** by the WHO (World Health Organization) ladder; neuropathic pain needs gabapentinoids or tricyclics.
- **Focal pain: EBRT (external beam radiotherapy) single 8 Gy fraction.** Pain relief equals multiple fractions; retreatment is commoner.
- X-ray or CT of painful weight-bearing sites to exclude lytic lesions and impending fracture; **orthopaedic fixation** then RT (radiotherapy).
- **Diffuse pain:** steroids, wide-field RT. Radium-223 is licensed only in castration-resistant disease.
- **Bone agents:** denosumab 60 mg 6-monthly or zoledronic acid yearly protect against ADT osteoporosis. SRE (skeletal-related event) doses (zoledronic acid 4 mg or denosumab 120 mg 4-weekly) are for CRPC (castration-resistant prostate cancer). Give calcium, vitamin D and a dental check (osteonecrosis of the jaw).

### Spinal cord compression: watch constantly
- Every focal back pain is cord compression until proven otherwise.
- **Dexamethasone** at once (load 10 mg intravenously), urgent whole-spine MRI, then **RT ± decompressive surgery**. Surgery plus RT beats RT alone (**Patchell, 2005**).
- Castrate with orchidectomy or antagonist, never an agonist alone.

### Step 4: Intensify systemic therapy (mHSPC)
- ADT alone is no longer standard. Classify volume: **CHAARTED high volume** = ≥4 bone metastases with ≥1 outside the axial skeleton, or visceral metastasis.

| Add to ADT | Trial (overall survival HR (hazard ratio)) |
|---|---|
| Abiraterone 1000 mg + prednisone 5 mg | LATITUDE (0.62); STAMPEDE arm G |
| Enzalutamide or apalutamide (ARPIs (androgen receptor pathway inhibitors)) | ENZAMET (0.67); TITAN (0.67) |
| Docetaxel 75 mg/m² × 6 + abiraterone or darolutamide (**triplet**) | PEACE-1; ARASENS (0.68) |

- Hormonal agents work regardless of volume; docetaxel helps only high volume, so it is now given within a triplet.
- **Low-volume synchronous disease:** add **RT to the prostate** (STAMPEDE arm H, 3-year survival 81% vs 73%); it also helps local control.

### Follow-up
- PSA, liver tests and blood count every 3–6 months; testosterone if PSA rises. Rising PSA with castrate testosterone = CRPC.
- PSA nadir ≤0.2 ng/mL at 7 months predicts long survival (SWOG 9346).
- Manage ADT effects: bone loss (DEXA (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry)), diabetes, cardiovascular risk, hot flushes.

```
Retention + bone pain + metastases
 ├─ Catheter ± nephrostomy;   cord signs? → dexamethasone → MRI → RT ± surgery
 ├─ Orchidectomy / antagonist / agonist + antiandrogen
 ├─ Analgesia + 8 Gy single-fraction RT; bone protection
 ├─ Add ARPI or triplet; prostate RT if low volume
 └─ TWOC after ADT → channel TURP if it fails
```

**Diagram to draw:** the flowchart above.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Never start an LHRH agonist alone with spinal metastases: cover the flare or use an antagonist.
> - Single 8 Gy fraction gives the same pain relief as longer courses.
> - Focal back pain = cord compression until MRI proves otherwise.
> - ADT plus abiraterone or an ARPI, or a triplet, for every fit man.

## Q2. Describe neuromuscular dysfunction and management of bladder dysfunction following cervical spine surgery in young adult male
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

*Assumed meaning: cervical spinal cord injury (traumatic, operated, or from the surgery itself), giving a suprasacral lesion above T6.*

### Introduction
- A cervical cord lesion lies **between the PMC (pontine micturition centre) and the sacral cord**. It causes tetraplegia and the classic suprasacral pattern.
- Complete transection is rare; incomplete lesions are commonest. Manage by **urodynamics, not neurology**.

### Pathophysiology
- The PMC normally coordinates detrusor contraction with sphincter relaxation. Cutting the descending pathway leaves a **spinal reflex**.
- **Why NDO (neurogenic detrusor overactivity) appears:** unmyelinated C-fibre afferents become sensitised (NGF (nerve growth factor)), sprout and drive a new sacral reflex. Bulbospinal inhibition is lost.

| Phase | Detrusor | Outlet | Clinical |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spinal shock** (usually 6–12 weeks; up to 1–2 years) | Areflexic, smooth bladder | Bladder neck closed; reduced closure pressure | **Retention**; overflow only |
| **Recovery (suprasacral pattern)** | **NDO**, no sensation below lesion | **Striated DSD (detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia)**; lesion above T7–T8 adds **smooth sphincter dyssynergia** | Reflex leakage, incomplete emptying, high pressure |

- **Blaivas DSD types:** type 1, sphincter relaxes at peak pressure (incomplete lesions); type 2, sporadic contractions; type 3, crescendo–decrescendo obstruction (types 2–3 with complete lesions).
- **Consequences:** high storage and voiding pressure, VUR (vesicoureteric reflux) in 17–25%, hydronephrosis, stones (about 7% in 10 years), UTI (urinary tract infection). Untreated, about half of men with DSD deteriorate (worse if complete, continuous DSD).
- Upper tract risk also rises with low compliance (<12.5 mL/cm H₂O) and NDO for over a third of filling.
- **VUR:** treat the bladder first (lower the pressure); reflux surgery in a thick bladder is difficult.
- **Also know:** cervical myelopathy may instead give an underactive detrusor.

### Autonomic dysreflexia
- Occurs with lesions **above T6** (cervical 60%), after spinal shock. Bladder or bowel distension triggers unopposed spinal sympathetic outflow, as medullary inhibition is lost.
- **Features:** pounding headache, severe hypertension (systolic rise ≥20 mmHg defines it at urodynamics), bradycardia, sweating and flushing above the lesion. It can cause stroke or seizures.
- **Treatment:** sit up, **drain the bladder** (check catheter), then **nitroglycerin 2% paste** above the lesion; intravenous nitroprusside or labetalol if persistent. Prophylaxis with terazosin or prazosin. Monitor blood pressure during cystoscopy and urodynamics; use spinal or general anaesthesia.

### Evaluation
- History, examination, **hand function** (decides self-catheterisation), transfers, carers.
- Urinalysis, creatinine, **renal ultrasound**. Men with good hand function can self-catheterise; others need carers or a suprapubic catheter.
- **VUDS (videourodynamics) after spinal shock**, with blood pressure monitoring: compliance, NDO, DSD, VUR, **DLPP (detrusor leak point pressure) ≥40 cm H₂O** = upper tract risk.
- AUA/SUFU (American Urological Association/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction) 2021: suprasacral lesions are "unknown risk" until tested; DSD or poor compliance = high risk.

### Management
**Goals:** protect kidneys, low-pressure storage, low-pressure emptying, no infection, continence, social acceptability. Start with the simplest reversible option.

- **Spinal shock:** **CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation)** preferred; indwelling catheter early is acceptable. Defer irreversible procedures.

| Aim | Options |
|---|---|
| **Emptying** | **CIC: gold standard**, volumes <500 mL; carer CIC in tetraplegics. **Suprapubic catheter** better than long-term urethral catheter. Credé/Valsalva not recommended |
| **Storage (lower pressure)** | **Antimuscarinics** ± mirabegron (β3 agonist); **intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA 200 U** if refractory (repeat when the effect wears off) |
| **Refractory small bladder** | **Augmentation ileocystoplasty** ± continent catheterisable channel (Mitrofanoff) |
| **Men unable to do CIC** | **External sphincterotomy** or intrasphincteric botulinum toxin → reflex voiding into condom catheter. High failure; DLPP ≥40 cm H₂O afterwards = failure. Urethral stents abandoned |
| **Neuromodulation** | **SARS (sacral anterior root stimulator, Brindley) + posterior sacral rhizotomy**: abolishes NDO and dysreflexia, allows voiding on demand |
| **Last resort** | Ileovesicostomy; bladder neck closure + suprapubic catheter; ileal conduit |

- **Also know:** sacral neuromodulation is used for neurogenic bladder without SCI (spinal cord injury); hydrophilic catheters delay first UTI.

### Complications and follow-up
- **UTI:** treat only symptomatic bacteriuria (5–14 days); no routine prophylaxis.
- Stones, VUR (treat the bladder first), bladder cancer (no screening cystoscopy, AUA/SUFU 2021).
- Lifelong: yearly review and renal function, upper tract imaging every 1–2 years, repeat urodynamics if anything changes.

```
Cervical SCI → spinal shock (retention): CIC
   → VUDS at 3 months: NDO + DSD
   → antimuscarinic + CIC → Botox → augmentation
   → cannot CIC: sphincterotomy + condom / SARS / ileovesicostomy
```

**Diagram to draw:** neural axis: PMC, lesion at cervical cord, sympathetic T10–L2, sacral S2–S4, with the resulting NDO + DSD.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Suprasacral lesion = NDO + striated DSD; above T7–T8 also smooth sphincter dyssynergia.
> - Autonomic dysreflexia above T6: drain bladder first, nitrates next.
> - CIC + bladder relaxation (antimuscarinic, Botox 200 U) is the mainstay.
> - DLPP ≥40 cm H₂O threatens the kidneys.

## Q3a. CAPD
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2018-Summer Paper 2, Q3e)

### Definition and principle
- CAPD (continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis): home dialysis using the **peritoneum** as the membrane.
- **Diffusion** removes urea, creatinine and potassium into the dialysate. **Ultrafiltration** removes water by osmosis: **glucose** (1.5%, 2.5%, 4.25%) or **icodextrin** for the long dwell.
- About **4 exchanges of 2 L daily**, 4–6-hour dwells. APD (automated peritoneal dialysis) uses a night cycler.

### Catheter
- **Tenckhoff catheter:** silicone, **two Dacron cuffs** (deep cuff in rectus; superficial cuff near the exit), downward exit, tip in the pelvis.
- Insertion: open, **laparoscopic** (rectus sheath tunnel, omentopexy, adhesiolysis) or percutaneous. Delay full volumes about 2 weeks to prevent leaks.

### Adequacy
- Weekly **Kt/V urea ≥1.7** (urea clearance × time ÷ urea distribution volume) plus clinical well-being. PET (peritoneal equilibration test) identifies high transporters, who suit short dwells.

### Advantages over haemodialysis
- Home-based, no vascular access, **haemodynamically gentle**, **preserves residual renal function**. Favoured in children, unstable heart disease and poor vascular access.

### Contraindications
- **Absolute:** peritoneal scarring or adhesions, membrane failure, uncorrectable hernia, nobody to do exchanges.
- **Relative:** recent abdominal surgery, stoma, polycystic kidneys, morbid obesity.

### Complications
- **Peritonitis** (2 of 3): pain or **cloudy effluent**; effluent **WCC (white cell count) >100/µL, >50% neutrophils**; positive culture. Commonest: coagulase-negative *Staphylococcus*. Give **intraperitoneal** Gram-positive and Gram-negative cover; remove catheter if fungal or refractory (5 days).
- **Exit-site and tunnel infection** (topical mupirocin).
- **Mechanical:** outflow failure (constipation, omental wrap), leak, **hernia**, hydrothorax.
- **Metabolic:** hyperglycaemia, weight gain, protein loss.
- **Long term:** ultrafiltration failure; **EPS (encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis)**.
- **Also know:** remove the PD catheter after a successful transplant; retroperitoneal laparoscopy protects the peritoneum for PD (peritoneal dialysis) patients.

**Diagram to draw:** Tenckhoff catheter with two cuffs, tunnel, downward exit, pelvic tip.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Peritoneum is the membrane; glucose or icodextrin drives ultrafiltration.
> - Two-cuff Tenckhoff catheter; Kt/V ≥1.7 weekly.
> - Peritonitis: cloudy fluid, WCC >100/µL with >50% neutrophils.

## Q3b. Flexible URS
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 2, Q3b; digital scopes in 2019-Summer Paper 4, Q3c)

### Introduction
- Flexible URS (ureteroscopy), or RIRS (retrograde intrarenal surgery), treats the whole upper tract by the natural route. Marshall first reported it; a working channel and active deflection made it therapeutic.

### Instrument
| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Deflection | **≥270° both ways** (reaches lower pole) |
| Working channel | **3.6 Fr** (laser fibre, basket) |
| **Fibreoptic** | Smallest tip; fibres break → black spots; repair about every 15 cases |
| **Digital** ("chip-on-tip", CMOS (complementary metal-oxide semiconductor)) | Better image; larger tip (about 8.5 Fr); not more durable |
| **Single-use** (LithoVue, 2016) | No repair or reprocessing; cheaper below about 99 cases/year |

### Indications
- **Stones:** renal <2 cm; lower pole <1 cm (or 1–2 cm as PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) alternative); **must continue anticoagulants**; failed SWL (shock wave lithotripsy); obesity.
- **Others:** diagnosis and laser ablation of low-grade UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma); strictures, endopyelotomy, calyceal diverticulum, haematuria.

### Technique
1. Sterile urine; single-dose antibiotic; α-blocker or **pre-stenting** if the ureter is tight.
2. Cystoscopy, **safety guidewire**, retrograde pyelogram.
3. **UAS (ureteral access sheath)**, 12/14 Fr: lowers intrarenal pressure and temperature, eases repeated passes, but injures the ureter in nearly half (severe in about 1 in 8).
4. Low-pressure irrigation. **Holmium:YAG (yttrium-aluminium-garnet) or thulium fibre laser:** fragment and basket, or dust. Keep power ≤10 W in ureter, ≤25 W in kidney (heat above 43 °C damages tissue).
5. Relocate lower pole stones to an upper calyx.
6. Inspect on withdrawal. **Omit stent** if normal anatomy, normal renal function, no injury and no second stage.

### Complications
- Stent symptoms (commonest), **sepsis about 5%**, stricture about 3%, perforation, bleeding <1%, **avulsion <1%** (nephrostomy, delayed repair), scope damage.

### Recent advances
- Pressure-sensing scopes, suction sheaths, thulium fibre laser, robotic URS, AI (artificial intelligence) navigation.

**Diagram to draw:** scope through access sheath, deflected into lower pole calyx.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 270° deflection, 3.6 Fr channel.
> - Access sheath: lower pressure, but ureteric injury.
> - Modality of choice when anticoagulants cannot stop.

## Q3c. Anomalies of renal artery.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Normal pattern
- One renal artery from the aorta at L1–L2; anterior and posterior divisions; **five segmental end arteries** (apical, upper, middle, lower, posterior). The right artery passes behind the IVC (inferior vena cava).

### Terminology
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Multiple renal arteries** (preferred) | More than one vessel from the aorta to one kidney |
| **Aberrant** | Arises from a vessel other than aorta or main renal artery (iliac, SMA (superior mesenteric artery), gonadal) |
| **Accessory** | Two or more branches supplying the same segment |

### Incidence
- Single artery in **71–85%**; variants in 25–40%. Accessory arteries 25–28% (aorta T11–L4), usually to the **lower pole**. True aberrant vessels are rare except in **ectopic and horseshoe kidneys**.
- **Also know:** early prehilar branching; right artery anterior to IVC in about 5%.

### Embryology
- The ascending kidney takes successive aortic branches; the lower ones normally degenerate. **Failure of degeneration** gives polar or multiple arteries.

### Clinical significance
- **PUJ (pelviureteric junction) obstruction** from a lower-pole crossing vessel; but crossing vessels occur in a fifth to a quarter of normal PUJs.
- Compression of an infundibulum: hydrocalyx, pain, stones, UTI (urinary tract infection).
- **Surgery:** bleeding at endopyelotomy (incise posterolaterally); polar arteries are end arteries, so ligation infarcts that segment; **donor nephrectomy** needs CT (computed tomography) angiography; aortic surgery.
- Not linked to hypertension.

### Renal artery aneurysm
- About **0.1%**. **Abeshouse types:** saccular (about 93%), fusiform, dissecting, arteriovenous.
- Mostly asymptomatic; hypertension, haematuria, pain; wreath-like calcification.
- **Treat** (endovascular or open) if rupture, **>2 cm**, expanding, **pregnancy or planned pregnancy**, symptoms or emboli.

### Renal arteriovenous fistula
| | Congenital (cirsoid AVM (arteriovenous malformation)) | Acquired |
|---|---|---|
| Share | <25% | Majority (biopsy, PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy), trauma) |
| Features | Bruit, haematuria, high-output failure | Often resolves |
| Treatment | Selective embolisation | Observe; embolise if bleeding |

**Diagram to draw:** kidney with main artery, lower-pole accessory artery crossing the PUJ.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Single artery in 71–85%; accessory arteries usually lower pole.
> - Crossing vessel can cause PUJ obstruction.
> - Treat aneurysm >2 cm or in pregnancy.

## Q3d. Pheochromocytoma
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(pre-operative management in detail: 2020-Summer Paper 2, Q3c)

### Definition and epidemiology
- Catecholamine-secreting tumour of adrenal medullary chromaffin cells; **paraganglioma** is the extra-adrenal form (organ of Zuckerkandl, bladder).
- 1–2 per 100,000 a year; about 5% of adrenal incidentalomas.
- **"Rule of 10" is outdated:** extra-adrenal up to 25%, **hereditary about one-third**, bilateral commoner in hereditary disease.

### Genetics
- **MEN2 (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2)**, RET: about 50% risk, adrenal, low malignancy. **VHL (von Hippel–Lindau)**: normetanephrine only. **NF1 (neurofibromatosis type 1)**. **SDHB (succinate dehydrogenase subunit B):** extra-adrenal, **30–50% metastatic**.
- Offer genetic testing to all.

### Clinical features
- Triad: **headache, sweating, palpitations**. Sustained hypertension is commoner than paroxysmal; pallor, hyperglycaemia; >20% asymptomatic. Epinephrine-dominant tumours can cause hypotensive episodes.

### Diagnosis
- **Biochemistry first:** plasma free or 24-hour urinary fractionated **metanephrines**. Tumours convert catecholamines continuously, so metanephrines are more sensitive than episodic catecholamines. Chromogranin A; clonidine suppression for borderline results.
- **Imaging:** CT (computed tomography): >10 HU (Hounsfield units) unenhanced, no rapid washout; MRI (magnetic resonance imaging). **Functional:** ⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE PET (positron emission tomography), FDG (fluorodeoxyglucose) PET (best for SDHB), ¹²³I-MIBG (metaiodobenzylguanidine) (best in MEN2).
- **Never biopsy** (hypertensive crisis). Iodinated contrast is safe.

### Pre-operative preparation (7–14 days)
- **α-blockade for all:** phenoxybenzamine 10 mg twice daily, titrated, or doxazosin; calcium channel blocker as alternative.
- **β-blocker only after α-blockade**, because unopposed α-stimulation causes crisis.
- **High salt and fluids**; target blood pressure 120–130/80 mmHg with mild postural drop. Echocardiography.

### Surgery
- **Laparoscopic adrenalectomy** (open for large or invasive); minimal handling; early adrenal vein ligation. **Cortical-sparing** partial adrenalectomy in MEN2/VHL.
- Post-op: **hypotension** and **rebound hypoglycaemia**; HDU (high dependency unit) monitoring.

### Metastatic disease and follow-up
- Malignancy is defined only by **metastases**. Treat with metastasectomy, ¹³¹I-MIBG, CVD (cyclophosphamide, vincristine, dacarbazine).
- **Lifelong yearly metanephrines** (recurrence up to 16%).

**Diagram to draw:** catecholamine pathway to metanephrines (COMT (catechol-O-methyltransferase)).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Metanephrines first; never biopsy.
> - α before β, plus volume expansion.
> - One-third hereditary; SDHB most often metastatic.

## Q3e. Microscopic Hematuria
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2016-Summer Paper 3, Q3d)

### Definition
- **MH (microscopic haematuria): ≥3 RBCs/HPF (red blood cells per high-power field)** on microscopy of one properly collected sample (AUA/SUFU (American Urological Association/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction) 2020).
- **Dipstick alone is insufficient**: myoglobin, semen and bacterial peroxidases give false positives.
- Prevalence about 6.5%; malignancy in about 3% (over 85% bladder cancer).

### Causes
- Malignant: bladder, UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma), RCC (renal cell carcinoma), prostate.
- Benign: UTI (urinary tract infection), stones, BPE (benign prostatic enlargement), stricture, TB (tuberculosis), schistosomiasis.
- **Medical renal:** IgA nephropathy (dysmorphic RBCs, casts, proteinuria).
- Anticoagulants do not explain haematuria: evaluate as usual.

### Initial evaluation
- History (smoking, occupation, cyclophosphamide, pelvic radiation, Lynch syndrome), examination, **blood pressure, creatinine**.
- Suspected UTI → culture, treat, **repeat urinalysis**; persistent MH → full evaluation.
- Renal features → nephrology **and** urological evaluation.

### AUA/SUFU 2020 risk stratification
| | Low (all) | Intermediate (any) | High (any) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Age | Women <50; men <40 | Women 50–59; men 40–59 | ≥60 |
| Smoking (pack-years) | Never or <10 | 10–30 | >30 |
| RBCs/HPF | 3–10 | 11–25 | >25 |
| Gross haematuria history | No | No | **Yes** |
| Cancer risk | 0–0.4% | 1–2.5% | About 6% |
| **Work-up** | Shared decision: repeat urinalysis in 6 months **or** cystoscopy + RUS (renal ultrasound) | Cystoscopy + RUS | Cystoscopy + **CT (computed tomography) urography** |

- Additional risk factors (irritative symptoms, radiation, cyclophosphamide, occupational exposure) move low to intermediate.
- CT contraindicated → MR (magnetic resonance) urography, or retrograde pyelograms.
- **No cytology or urine markers initially**; white-light cystoscopy.
- Persistent MH after a negative ultrasound → CT or retrograde imaging. Hereditary RCC risk (VHL (von Hippel–Lindau)) → upper tract imaging in any stratum.

### Follow-up
- Negative evaluation → consider repeat urinalysis within 12 months; release if negative.
- Re-evaluate for gross haematuria, rising MH or new symptoms.

**Diagram to draw:** AUA/SUFU flowchart from MH to risk-based work-up.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - ≥3 RBCs/HPF on microscopy; dipstick not enough.
> - Intermediate: cystoscopy + ultrasound; high: cystoscopy + CT urography.
> - Any gross haematuria = high risk.

## Q3f. PIN
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Assumed meaning: prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia.*

### Definition
- PIN (prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia): **architecturally benign** acini or ducts lined by **cytologically atypical** cells, with an intact or patchy **basal cell layer**.
- Graded **LGPIN (low-grade PIN)** and **HGPIN (high-grade PIN)**. LGPIN is not reported: it is irreproducible and carries no excess risk.

### Histology
- HGPIN: nuclear enlargement, hyperchromasia, **prominent nucleoli**.
- Patterns: tufting, micropapillary, flat. **Cribriform lesions are now classed as IDC-P (intraductal carcinoma of the prostate) or AIP (atypical intraductal proliferation).**
- Immunohistochemistry: basal cells stain with **HMWCK (high-molecular-weight cytokeratin) and p63**; AMACR (α-methylacyl-CoA racemase) positive. Mainly in the peripheral zone.

### Evidence that HGPIN is a precursor
- More and larger HGPIN foci in cancerous prostates; carcinoma seen budding from HGPIN.
- About 20% carry **TMPRSS2:ERG fusion** (about 50% of cancers). Extra genomic changes are needed for invasion.

### Incidence
- About **4–5%** of biopsies (range 0–25%, due to observer variation).

### Related lesions
| Lesion | Meaning | Action on isolated finding |
|---|---|---|
| HGPIN | Weak risk marker | **No repeat biopsy** |
| PINATYP (HGPIN with adjacent atypical glands) | As "atypical" | Manage as ASAP (atypical small acinar proliferation) |
| ASAP | Cancer on re-biopsy about 40% | Repeat biopsy ± MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) |
| AIP | May herald IDC-P | Consider re-biopsy |
| IDC-P | Usually high-grade cancer present | Definitive treatment; excludes active surveillance |

### Management
- Isolated HGPIN, unifocal or multifocal: **no immediate repeat biopsy**. Decide further testing on PSA (prostate-specific antigen), DRE (digital rectal examination) and **mpMRI (multiparametric MRI)**.
- HGPIN on TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate) chips: no further sampling on current data.
- **Chemoprevention:** none proven; green tea showed reduced progression in small trials.
- *Guideline note:* EAU (European Association of Urology) guidelines still list extensive HGPIN (≥3 biopsy sites) as a reason for repeat biopsy; the chapter does not.

**Diagram to draw:** benign gland, HGPIN gland (atypical cells, intact basal layer) and cancer (no basal layer).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Atypical cells, benign architecture, basal layer retained.
> - Isolated HGPIN needs no repeat biopsy; ASAP does.
> - Cribriform "PIN" is now IDC-P or AIP.
