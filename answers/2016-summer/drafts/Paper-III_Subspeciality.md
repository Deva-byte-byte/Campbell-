## III-1. Discuss minimally invasive and endoscopic management of upper urinary tract malignancy.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- **UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma)** is 5–10% of urothelial cancers. Bladder recurrence follows in 22–47%, mostly from downstream seeding.
- Standard is **RNU (radical nephroureterectomy) with bladder cuff**; kidney-sparing versus radical surgery is chosen by **risk**.
- URS (ureteroscopy) biopsy is small and shallow, so it **cannot stage**. Stage is inferred from grade, endoscopic appearance, imaging and cytology together.

### Risk stratification
**EAU (European Association of Urology)**
| Low risk (all present) | High risk (any one) |
|---|---|
| Unifocal | Multifocal |
| Under 2 cm | 2 cm or more |
| Low-grade cytology and biopsy | High-grade cytology or biopsy |
| No invasion on CTU (computed tomography urography) | Hydronephrosis; invasion on CT; prior radical cystectomy; variant histology |

- **AUA/SUO (American Urological Association/Society of Urologic Oncology) 2023** splits each into favourable and unfavourable (obstruction, multifocality, lower-tract involvement). Ablation is **preferred** in low-risk favourable, **offered** in low-risk unfavourable, **rare** in high-risk favourable, **palliative** in high-risk unfavourable. Endoscopic therapy suits tumours **under 1.5 cm**.

### Indications for kidney-sparing treatment
- **Elective**: low-risk disease, whatever the other kidney.
- **Imperative**: solitary kidney, bilateral tumours, CKD (chronic kidney disease), unfit for RNU, after shared decision.
- The patient must accept frequent recurrence and **lifelong endoscopic surveillance**.

### Ureteroscopic (retrograde) management
- Closed system, lowest morbidity; for small ureteric and renal tumours.
- **Steps**: cystoscopy → selective cytology and saline barbotage → no-touch semirigid then flexible URS → map all lesions → biopsy (3 Fr forceps or basket) into fixative → ablate the base with laser → stent optional.

| Laser | Penetration | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Ho:YAG (holmium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet)** | Under 0.5 mm | Safe in thin ureter |
| **Nd:YAG (neodymium:YAG)** | Up to 5–6 mm | Coagulative necrosis; transmural injury risk |
| **Thulium** | Under 0.2 mm | Strong haemostasis |

- Avoid circumferential fulguration (stricture); stage large tumours.
- Results: upper-tract recurrence 23–90%, bladder recurrence 15–45%, progression 0–20%. **Grade is the strongest predictor.**

### Percutaneous (antegrade) management
- For large pelvic tumours, lower-pole or inaccessible calyces and urinary diversions.
- **Steps**: retrograde catheter → puncture a calyx away from the tumour (upper or middle calyx for pelvic tumours) → dilate to 30 Fr → cold-cup, bipolar resection or laser; base sent separately → nephrostomy left → **second-look nephroscopy**.
- Removes more tumour and can **stage**; the tract allows topical therapy. But it is an **open system**: tract seeding about 0.3%, bleeding.

### Segmental ureterectomy
- **Distal ureterectomy with bladder cuff and reimplantation** (psoas hitch or Boari flap) for distal tumours, including high-grade ones when the kidney must be saved. It gives a staging specimen; add pelvic lymphadenectomy if high grade.
- Mid-ureteric resection (frozen-section margins) is rare. Laparoscopic or robotic routes are feasible; outcomes equal RNU in selected patients.

### Laparoscopic and robotic RNU
- **Principles**: clip the ureter early; never open the collecting system; remove the **whole intramural ureter and cuff**; bag extraction intact; watertight bladder closure; template lymphadenectomy for high risk.
- **Steps**: modified flank, 3–4 ports → reflect colon → clip ureter → artery then vein → kidney outside Gerota's fascia (adrenal spared) → ureter traced under the superior vesical artery → cuff excised (extravesical or open) → two-layer closure.
- Abandoned (higher recurrence): transurethral "pluck", stripping, laparoscopic staple.
- Oncologically **equivalent to open** (AUA/SUO), with less blood loss and shorter stay; less favoured in bulky T3/T4. Robotic yields more nodes.

### Adjuvant topical therapy
- **Single postoperative intravesical dose** (AUA/SUO strong): **ODMIT-C (2011)** MMC (mitomycin C) 40 mg, 28% → 16% bladder recurrence at 1 year; **Ito (2013)** pirarubicin, 42% → 17% at 2 years.
- **Upper-tract instillation** via nephrostomy (low pressure, 25 cm H2O), ureteric catheter or reflux up a double-J. **BCG (bacillus Calmette–Guérin)**: 6-week course for upper-tract CIS (carcinoma in situ), and after complete ablation of high-risk favourable Ta/T1. Risks: sepsis, stricture.
- **UGN-101 (Jelmyto)**: mitomycin reverse-thermal gel, **FDA (Food and Drug Administration) 2020** for low-grade UTUC not manageable endoscopically. **OLYMPUS**: complete response 59%, 56% disease-free at 12 months; ureteric stricture about 44%.

### Systemic therapy
- **POUT (2020)**: adjuvant platinum after RNU for pT2–T4 or N+: 3-year disease-free survival 71% vs 46%. Neoadjuvant platinum if renal function is borderline (RNU removes cisplatin eligibility).

### Surveillance (AUA/SUO)
- **Kidney-sparing**: cystoscopy and URS within 3 months, URS again at 6 and 12 months; imaging 3–6-monthly (high risk) or 6–9-monthly (low risk), then yearly to 5 years.
- **After RNU**: cystoscopy and cytology from 3 months; CT yearly (more often if pT2 or more).

### Recent advances
- Lynch syndrome screening; thulium fibre laser; single-use digital ureteroscopes.

**Diagram to draw:** ureteroscopic laser ablation of a pelvic tumour beside RNU with bladder cuff.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - EAU low risk needs all: unifocal, under 2 cm, low grade, no invasion; any single adverse feature makes it high risk.
> - Ureteroscopy is closed but cannot stage; percutaneous stages but can seed the tract.
> - Single postoperative intravesical MMC: bladder recurrence 28% to 16% (ODMIT-C).

## III-2. Laparoscopic pyeloplasty.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- **PUJO (pelviureteric junction obstruction)** is impaired flow from pelvis to ureter that, if untreated, causes progressive renal damage (Koff).
- Causes: **intrinsic** adynamic segment (abnormal circular muscle), **extrinsic** crossing lower-pole vessel, high insertion; secondary (failed repair, stone).
- **Anderson–Hynes (1949) dismembered pyeloplasty** is the gold standard by any route. First laparoscopic pyeloplasty **1993 (Schuessler)**; robotic **2002**. Success **over 90–95%**, equal to open, with less morbidity.

### Indications
- Symptoms (pain, Dietl's crisis), infection, stones, hypertension (rare).
- Impaired split function or **fall of 10% or more** on serial renography; rising APD (anteroposterior diameter); obstructed solitary kidney or bilateral disease.
- Children: most antenatal hydronephrosis resolves; about one-third need surgery.
- **Contraindications**: uncorrected coagulopathy, untreated UTI (urinary tract infection), cardiopulmonary disease barring pneumoperitoneum.
- **Nephrectomy instead** if split function under 15% in adults with a normal other kidney.

### Preoperative evaluation
- **USG (ultrasonography)**: large pelvis, small calyces, ureter not seen.
- **MAG3 (mercaptoacetyltriglycine) diuretic renogram**: split function and an obstructive curve that does not wash out after furosemide. Usual teaching: washout half-time under 10 minutes normal, 10–20 minutes equivocal, over 20 minutes obstructed (Ch 93 quotes over 40 minutes). Split function under 40% supports repair.
- **CT (computed tomography) angiography**: anatomy, **crossing vessels**, stones.
- **Retrograde pyelogram** at surgery: confirms the level, excludes ureteric stricture.
- **Whitaker test** if equivocal: perfusion 10 mL/min; pelvic pressure over 15–20 cm H2O means obstruction.
- Sterile urine. Drain beforehand (stent or nephrostomy) only for infection, azotaemia or severe pain.

### Approaches
| | Transperitoneal | Retroperitoneal |
|---|---|---|
| Space | Large, familiar landmarks | Small, created by balloon |
| Bowel | Colon reflected (left: **transmesocolic** window) | Not entered; leak stays extraperitoneal |
| Use | Commonest; easier suturing | Avoids bowel; harder orientation |
- One paediatric randomised trial favoured retroperitoneal (shorter stay); overall outcomes similar.

### Steps (transperitoneal Anderson–Hynes)
1. Cystoscopy, retrograde pyelogram, catheter.
2. 45–60° flank; pneumoperitoneum 12–15 mmHg in adults (under 12 mmHg in children); 3–4 ports, camera at umbilicus.
3. Reflect colon medially, or open the mesocolon over the pelvis.
4. Find the ureter; trace it to the PUJ with its periureteric tissue; minimal diathermy. Look for a **crossing vessel**.
5. Hitch stitch on the pelvis; transect the pelvis above the PUJ; **reduce** a redundant pelvis if needed.
6. **Spatulate the ureter laterally** into healthy ureter; keep the narrow segment as a handle (no-touch).
7. **Transpose** the PUJ in front of any crossing vessel.
8. Anastomose the spatulation apex to the most dependent pelvis with 4-0 absorbable (5-0/6-0 in infants): back wall, stent, front wall, then close the pelvis. Watertight, tension-free, funnel-shaped.
9. **Antegrade double-J** over a wire; clamp the catheter: urine from the stent side holes confirms the bladder coil.
10. Excise the narrow segment; drain near (not on) the suture line.

### Crossing vessel
- It lowers endopyelotomy success (42% vs 86%), so pyeloplasty is preferred. **Never divide** a lower-pole vessel (segmental infarction).
- **Vascular hitch (Hellström)**: vessel moved up onto the pelvis, only if the PUJ is intrinsically normal and peristalsing.

### Stenting and postoperative care
- Double-J for **4–6 weeks** (children 1–4 weeks: no difference). Externalised stent in infants avoids a second anaesthetic.
- Foley for 24–48 hours (bladder pressure refluxes up the stent); drain out when dry. If drain output rises after Foley removal, replace the Foley.
- **MAG3 renogram** after stent removal, then ultrasound; follow for years.

### Robotic pyeloplasty
- 3D vision, wristed suturing, tremor filtration, **shorter learning curve**. Now first-line at major centres.
- Success 94.7–100%, the same as laparoscopy; advantage is ergonomic, at higher cost.

### Outcomes
- Laparoscopic: operating time 2–4 hours; complications 2–16%; conversion 0–5.5%; stay about 2.7 vs 4.2 days (open); success 87–99%.
- **Up to 30% of failures appear after 2 years**, so follow-up must be long.

### Complications
- Urine leak and urinoma, stent symptoms or migration, UTI, haematuria, ileus, bowel or vessel injury, port-site hernia, recurrent obstruction.

### Management of failure
```
Early (clot, oedema)        -> nephrostomy, wait
Persistent obstruction      -> CT angiography (missed vessel?)
  short, no vessel          -> endopyelotomy (about 70%)
  otherwise                 -> redo laparoscopic/robotic pyeloplasty
                               (about 89% after failed open; buccal graft onlay)
  small intrarenal pelvis   -> ureterocalicostomy
  poor function             -> nephrectomy
```

**Also know:** Y-V plasty (high insertion); Culp spiral flap (long narrowing); stones removed through the pyelotomy with a flexible scope; safe in horseshoe and pelvic kidneys.

### Recent advances
- Single-port robot (2018); indocyanine green fluorescence for redo cases; buccal mucosa ureteroplasty.

**Diagram to draw:** Anderson–Hynes steps: transection, lateral spatulation, transposition over the crossing vessel, dependent anastomosis.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Anderson–Hynes dismembered pyeloplasty is gold standard by any route; laparoscopic success over 90%.
> - Transpose over a crossing vessel; never divide it.
> - Double-J 4–6 weeks; MAG3 follow-up; up to 30% of failures are late.
> - Robotic equals laparoscopic in outcome, with easier suturing.

## III-3a. Oligoanaesthospermia.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Assumed meaning: oligoasthenozoospermia (low sperm count with poor motility).*

### Definition (WHO (World Health Organization) 2021, 6th edition, 5th centiles)
| Parameter | Lower reference |
|---|---|
| Concentration | 16 million/mL (total 39 million) |
| Progressive motility | 30% |
| Total motility | 42% |
| Volume | 1.4 mL |
| Normal forms | 4% |
| Vitality | 54% |
- These are reference values from fertile men, not pass/fail cut-offs. Severe oligozoospermia: under 5 million/mL.

### Causes
- **Varicocele** (commonest correctable; heat and oxidative stress) and **idiopathic** (largest group).
- Hormonal: hypogonadotropism, exogenous testosterone or anabolic steroids, obesity (high oestradiol), hyperprolactinaemia.
- Testicular: cryptorchidism, mumps orchitis, torsion, chemotherapy or radiation; genetic (Klinefelter, AZF (azoospermia factor) c deletion).
- Motility: infection or **leucocytospermia** (over 1 million/mL), antisperm antibodies, partial ejaculatory duct obstruction, PCD (primary ciliary dyskinesia).
- Lifestyle: smoking, alcohol, cannabis, heat, gonadotoxins.

### Evaluation
- **Two semen analyses** (2–7 days abstinence); history and examination (testis size, varicocele, vasa).
- Morning **testosterone, LH (luteinising hormone), FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone)**; oestradiol and prolactin if testosterone is low.
- Under 5 million/mL (especially with raised FSH or small testes): **karyotype and Y-microdeletion** (AUA/ASRM (American Urological Association/American Society for Reproductive Medicine) 2020 guideline).
- Selected: vitality test (eosin or hypo-osmotic swelling), antisperm antibodies, semen culture, scrotal Doppler, TRUS (transrectal ultrasonography) if volume low, DNA fragmentation.

### Management
- **General**: stop smoking, weight loss, avoid heat; **stop exogenous testosterone**; treat infection.
- **Varicocelectomy** (microsurgical subinguinal) for palpable varicocele with abnormal semen; improves parameters and pregnancy. Not for subclinical ones.
- **Medical**: hypogonadotropic → hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) with or without FSH. Idiopathic with low testosterone → off-label clomiphene 25 mg daily, or anastrozole 1 mg daily if testosterone:oestradiol ratio under 10. Antioxidants: weak evidence.
- **ART (assisted reproductive technology)**: mild → IUI (intrauterine insemination); severe, or failed IUI → **IVF/ICSI (in vitro fertilisation/intracytoplasmic sperm injection)**. Spermatogenesis takes about 64 days, so judge response after 3–6 months.

**Diagram to draw:** hypothalamic–pituitary–testis axis with sites of treatment.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - WHO 2021: concentration 16 million/mL, progressive motility 30%.
> - Karyotype and Y-microdeletion if under 5 million/mL.
> - Repair palpable varicocele only; never give testosterone.

## III-3b. Sperm retrieval techniques
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Indications
- **OA (obstructive azoospermia)**: CBAVD (congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens), unreconstructable obstruction, failed or declined vasectomy reversal, or at the time of reconstruction (back-up).
- **NOA (non-obstructive azoospermia)**: testicular failure; only testicular retrieval works.
- Anejaculation (spinal cord injury): vibratory stimulation or electroejaculation first.
- All need **ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection)**; cryopreserve spare sperm.

### Techniques
| Technique | Method | Best for | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PESA (percutaneous epididymal sperm aspiration)** | Needle into caput under local | OA | Few sperm, may obstruct epididymis |
| **MESA (microsurgical epididymal sperm aspiration)** | Single tubule opened at 16–25×; micropipette by capillary action | OA (best) | Microsurgical skill |
| **TESA (testicular sperm aspiration)** | 23 G needle, suction, many passes | OA | Immotile sperm, small yield; poor in NOA |
| **TESE (testicular sperm extraction)** | Open biopsy, one or several sites | OA, NOA | Blind sampling |
| **Micro-TESE** | Testis bivalved; larger opaque tubules picked under the microscope | **NOA (best)** | Long operation |

- **MESA points**: move proximally until motile sperm appear (most motile in efferent ductules); 4–12 pipettes, frozen in aliquots.
- **Micro-TESE logic (Schlegel 1999)**: spermatogenesis in NOA is **focal**; tubules with sperm are larger and plumper than thin white Sertoli-cell-only tubules. Incision through an avascular plane, then the other testis if negative. It removes the least tissue for the most sperm.

### Results
- OA: retrieval almost always succeeds.
- NOA micro-TESE: sperm in **about half**; better than conventional TESE.

| Condition | Retrieval |
|---|---|
| Post-orchidopexy | 74% |
| AZF (azoospermia factor) c deletion | 70% |
| Klinefelter | 68% |
| Post-chemotherapy | 53% |
| Maturation arrest | 40% |
| Sertoli-cell-only | 30% |
| **AZFa or AZFb deletion** | **0% (do not operate)** |

- NOA pregnancies have more miscarriage (sperm aneuploidy and DNA damage).

### Complications
- Haematoma, infection, epididymal obstruction (PESA), testicular atrophy or transient fall in testosterone (TESE).

**Also know:** testicular sperm may beat ejaculated sperm when DNA fragmentation is high; postmortem retrieval only with prior consent; microfluidic sperm sorting.

**Diagram to draw:** micro-TESE with dilated tubules beside thin Sertoli-cell-only tubules.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - OA: epididymal (MESA); NOA: micro-TESE.
> - Micro-TESE finds sperm in about half of NOA.
> - AZFa/AZFb deletion: no sperm, do not operate.

## III-3c. Treatment strategies for bilateral Wilm's tumour
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- **Synchronous bilateral WT (Wilms tumour)** is **5–7%**: **COG (Children's Oncology Group) stage V**. Each kidney is staged separately.
- Linked to WAGR (Wilms, aniridia, genitourinary anomalies, retardation), Denys–Drash and BWS (Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome); **nephrogenic rests in over 90%**.
- Aim: **cure while preserving renal tissue**. Renal failure is about 15% at 15 years, mainly from bilateral nephrectomy.

### Evaluation
- Ultrasound, contrast CT (computed tomography) or MRI (magnetic resonance imaging), chest CT; IVC (inferior vena cava) and coagulation checks.

### COG approach (AREN0534)
```
VCR + AMD + DOX x 6 weeks (no upfront biopsy)
        |
   imaging at week 6
   /              \
NSS feasible     not feasible
 -> surgery       -> 6 more weeks chemotherapy
                   -> surgery by week 12
                   -> if poor response: open BILATERAL biopsy
```
- VCR = vincristine, AMD = actinomycin D (dactinomycin), DOX = doxorubicin; NSS = nephron-sparing surgery.
- **Why stop at 12 weeks**: in NWTS-4 (National Wilms Tumor Study 4), prolonged chemotherapy in poor responders led to anaplasia. Non-shrinkage usually means differentiation, cured by excision.
- **Why open, bilateral biopsy**: anaplasia is discordant between kidneys in about 4 of 5.

### Surgery
- **Bilateral NSS** (partial nephrectomy or enucleation); frozen section; lymph node sampling; avoid spill.
- Compressed normal parenchyma is often underestimated, so try NSS in every case. Enucleation: positive margins 23–36%, good outcome with radiotherapy.
- Unresectable both sides: bilateral nephrectomy, dialysis, transplant after **1–2 years** tumour-free.

### Adjuvant
- Guided by worst histology and margins: anaplasia or positive margin → intensified chemotherapy and flank radiotherapy.

### Outcomes
- AREN0534: surgery by week 12 in 84%; bilateral NSS 39%; **4-year EFS (event-free survival) 82.1%, OS (overall survival) 94.9%**, against 56% and 80.8% in NWTS-5.

### SIOP (International Society of Paediatric Oncology)
- Same principle (no biopsy, NSS by week 12) but **two drugs, VCR + AMD**, if non-metastatic.

**Follow-up:** long term: blood pressure, proteinuria, creatinine, imaging (late relapse occurs).

**Diagram to draw:** bilateral tumours with planned partial nephrectomies.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Stage V, 5–7%; no upfront nephrectomy or biopsy.
> - COG: three drugs for 6 weeks, surgery by week 12; SIOP: two drugs.
> - AREN0534: 4-year OS about 95%.

## III-3d. Asymptomatic Hematuria
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definitions
- **MH (microhaematuria)**: **3 or more RBCs/HPF (red blood cells per high-power field)** on microscopy of one properly collected sample (AUA/SUFU (American Urological Association/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction) 2020). A dipstick alone is not enough (myoglobin, semen and peroxidases give false positives).
- **GH (gross haematuria)**: visible; exclude pigmenturia and menstrual blood.
- Prevalence about 6.5%. Malignancy: about 3% of MH; 14–26% of GH (DETECT I, IDENTIFY).

### Causes
- **Neoplasm**: bladder (over 85% of cancers found), UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma), RCC (renal cell carcinoma) about 1 in 1000 each, prostate.
- **Benign**: BPE (benign prostatic enlargement), stones, UTI (urinary tract infection), stricture, TB (tuberculosis), schistosomiasis.
- **Medical renal** (IgA nephropathy): dysmorphic RBCs, casts, proteinuria.
- **Others**: exercise, endometriosis, sickle cell. Anticoagulants do not explain haematuria; evaluate as usual.

### Initial assessment
- History (smoking, occupation, cyclophosphamide, pelvic radiation, Lynch), examination, blood pressure, creatinine, culture.
- UTI → treat → repeat urinalysis; persistence means full evaluation. Glomerular picture → nephrology **and** urological evaluation.

### AUA/SUFU risk stratification
| | Low (all) | Intermediate (any) | High (any) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Age | Women under 50, men under 40 | Women 50–59, men 40–59 | 60 or over |
| Smoking (pack-years) | Under 10 | 10–30 | Over 30 |
| RBCs/HPF | 3–10 | 11–25 | Over 25 |
| Gross haematuria history | No | No | **Yes** |
| Cancer risk | 0–0.4% | 1–2.5% | About 6% |
| **Work-up** | Repeat urinalysis in 6 months **or** cystoscopy + RUS (renal ultrasound) | Cystoscopy + RUS | Cystoscopy + **CT urography** |

- CT (computed tomography) contraindicated → MR (magnetic resonance) urography, or retrograde pyelograms.
- Cytology and urine markers are **not** used initially; cytology if CIS (carcinoma in situ) is suspected.
- **Gross haematuria**: cystoscopy, CT urography and cytology at any age.

### Follow-up
- Negative evaluation → repeat urinalysis within 12 months; release if negative.
- Re-evaluate for GH, rising MH or new symptoms.

**Diagram to draw:** AUA/SUFU flowchart from MH to risk-based work-up.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - MH: 3 or more RBCs/HPF on microscopy, one sample.
> - Low: optional; intermediate: cystoscopy + ultrasound; high: cystoscopy + CT urography.
> - Any gross haematuria = high risk.

## III-3e. Prenatal and Neonatal assessment of neurogenic bladder dysfunction
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- Commonest cause: **myelomeningocele** (open NTD (neural tube defect)); also occult dysraphism, sacral agenesis, anorectal malformation.
- **Bony or motor level does not predict bladder function**, so every newborn needs testing.

### Prenatal assessment
- **Maternal serum AFP (alpha-fetoprotein)** raised in open defects; amniotic AFP and acetylcholinesterase confirm.
- **Ultrasound**: spinal defect; "lemon" and "banana" signs of Chiari II; ventriculomegaly; hydronephrosis, thick bladder. Fetal MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) for level.
- Multidisciplinary counselling at a fetal centre.
- **MOMS (Management of Myelomeningocele Study) 2011**: closure **before 26 weeks** reduced shunt need (about 40% vs 82%) and improved motor and cognitive scores, but caused prematurity (34 vs 37 weeks) and uterine thinning. **No proven bladder benefit**: CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) and reconstruction rates unchanged.

### Neonatal assessment (baseline)
| Test | Timing / purpose |
|---|---|
| Examination | Spine, legs, anal tone, perineal sensation, bladder |
| **Renal and bladder ultrasound** | Before discharge, then 6-monthly for 2 years; hydronephrosis, wall thickness |
| Residual urine | Start CIC if not emptying |
| Creatinine | Within 3 months (cystatin C later) |
| **VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) or video-UDS** | Reflux; dilating VUR (vesicoureteric reflux) in about 7% |
| **UDS (urodynamics)** with EMG (electromyography) | Within 3 months, once stable after closure |
| DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid) | Baseline scarring (EAU (European Association of Urology)) |

### Urodynamic risk (UMPIRE (Urologic and Renal Protocol for the Newborn and Young Child with Spina Bifida))
| Risk | DLPP (detrusor leak point pressure) or end-fill pressure |
|---|---|
| High (hostile) | 40 cm H2O or more (McGuire 1981) |
| Intermediate | 25–39, or detrusor overactivity |
| Low | Under 25 |
- Poor compliance and DSD (detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia) also predict upper-tract damage.

### Action
- High risk → **early CIC plus oxybutynin**: hydronephrosis 0–15% versus 18–80% expectant; ESRD (end-stage renal disease) about 30% falls to under 2%.
- Repeat studies: the lesion can change (re-tethering).

**Diagram to draw:** neonatal UDS trace with DLPP over 40 cm H2O.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Level does not predict bladder; UDS within 3 months.
> - DLPP 40 cm H2O or more = hostile; early CIC + antimuscarinic.
> - MOMS: fewer shunts, no bladder benefit.

## III-3f. Marginal Donor
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- A donor whose kidney carries **more DGF (delayed graft function) and shorter graft survival** than a standard donor; used because of organ shortage.

### Categories
**1. ECD (expanded criteria donor), UNOS (United Network for Organ Sharing) 2002**: age **60 or over**, or **50–59 with 2 or more** of: hypertension, **creatinine over 1.5 mg/dL**, death from **CVA (cerebrovascular accident)**. Graft-loss risk about 70% higher.

**2. KDPI (Kidney Donor Profile Index) over 85%**: replaced ECD in the US (2014). **Longevity matching**: lowest-KDPI kidneys go to recipients with the best EPTS (Estimated Post-Transplant Survival).

**3. DCD (donation after circulatory death), Maastricht**
| Category | Description | Type |
|---|---|---|
| I / II | Dead on arrival / failed resuscitation | Uncontrolled |
| III | Arrest after withdrawal of support | **Controlled (commonest)** |
| IV | Arrest in a brain-dead donor | Either |
| V | Euthanasia | Controlled |
- Warm ischaemia raises DGF (roughly 40–50%); long-term survival is similar to DBD (donation after brain death).

**4. Others**: AKI (acute kidney injury) donors; **HCV (hepatitis C virus)-viraemic** donors with antivirals; **paediatric donors under 15 kg (en bloc)**; older living donors.
- **Excluded**: metastatic or haematological cancer, melanoma, untreatable viral infection.

### Assessment and use
- **Remuzzi biopsy score** (glomeruli, tubules, interstitium, vessels, 0–3 each): **0–3 single**, **4–6 dual kidney transplant**, **7 or more discard**.
- **HMP (hypothermic machine perfusion)** reduces DGF versus cold storage (Moers, 2009), without long-term survival difference; normothermic perfusion tests viability.
- **ESP (Eurotransplant Senior Program)**: donors 65 or over to recipients 65 or over, with short cold ischaemia.
- Recipient: informed consent; induction and delayed CNI (calcineurin inhibitor) if DGF.

### Urological relevance
- Older and ECD kidneys have more **urine leak and ureteric stricture** (ischaemic distal ureter), as do multiple arteries and DGF.

- **Ojo (2001)**: ECD transplantation improves survival over staying on dialysis.

**Diagram to draw:** Maastricht categories on a timeline of circulatory arrest.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - ECD: 60 or over, or 50–59 with 2 of hypertension, creatinine over 1.5, CVA death.
> - Remuzzi 0–3 single, 4–6 dual, 7 or more discard.
> - HMP reduces DGF; Maastricht III is the commonest DCD.
