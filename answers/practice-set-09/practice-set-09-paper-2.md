## Q1. Diagnosis and Treatment of renal trauma
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2017-Summer Paper 2, Q2; grade IV in Practice Set 3 Paper 2, Q3c)

### Introduction
- The kidney is the **commonest urological organ injured** (up to 5% of all trauma). **Blunt injury causes 80–90%**; firearms cause most penetrating injuries.
- **Rapid deceleration** stretches the kidney at its fixed points (hilum and UPJ, ureteropelvic junction): arterial intimal tear and thrombosis, vein disruption, pedicle or UPJ avulsion.

### Diagnosis
**History and examination**
- **Mechanism is the key history**. Signs: flank bruise or tenderness, **fractured ribs 9–12**, flank or low chest wound.

**Haematuria**
- Test the **first urine sample**. Significant: gross haematuria, or microhaematuria (>5 RBC [red blood cells] per high-power field) with **shock (SBP [systolic blood pressure] <90 mmHg)**.
- Degree does **not** match severity. It may be **absent in pedicle injury** and in about half of UPJ injuries.

**Indications for imaging (AUA [American Urological Association] and EAU [European Association of Urology])**
1. Blunt trauma with gross haematuria, or microhaematuria with SBP <90 mmHg at any time.
2. Rapid deceleration, even without haematuria.
3. Penetrating trauma with likely renal injury, any haematuria.
4. Children: image liberally (hypotension appears late).
- Microhaematuria alone without shock: observe.

**Imaging**
- **Contrast-enhanced CT (computed tomography) is the gold standard.** The arterial or nephrographic phase (~80 s) shows bleeding; the **delayed excretory phase (5–10 min)** shows urinary extravasation.

| CT finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Medial haematoma | Pedicle (vascular) injury |
| Medial extravasation, no contrast in distal ureter | Renal pelvis or UPJ avulsion |
| Absent enhancement (cortical rim sign) | Main renal artery occlusion |
| Active contrast blush; haematoma rim >4 cm | Bleeding likely to need intervention |

- **One-shot IVP (intravenous pyelography)** on the table if unstable: 2 mL/kg, one film at 10 min; confirms a **functioning contralateral kidney**.
- **Also know:** FAST (focused assessment with sonography for trauma) is non-specific; CT shows renal vein injury poorly.

### Classification: AAST (American Association for the Surgery of Trauma) 2018
| Grade | Injury |
|---|---|
| I | Subcapsular haematoma and/or contusion, no laceration |
| II | Perirenal haematoma within Gerota's fascia; laceration ≤1 cm, no extravasation |
| III | Laceration >1 cm, no collecting-system injury; vascular injury or active bleeding **contained within Gerota's fascia** |
| IV | Laceration into collecting system with extravasation; renal pelvis laceration or UPJ disruption; **segmental artery or vein injury**; active bleeding beyond Gerota's fascia; segmental or complete infarction |
| V | **Main renal artery or vein laceration or hilar avulsion**; devascularised kidney with active bleeding; shattered kidney |
- Advance one grade for multiple injuries, up to grade III.

### Treatment
```
Unstable despite resuscitation → laparotomy (± damage control)
                                  or angioembolisation if team ready
Stable → CT staging → non-operative for grade I-V (selected IV-V)
   Active blush → angioembolisation
   Extravasation → observe; stent ± nephrostomy if urinoma
                   enlarges, fever, or pelvis/UPJ injury
```
**Non-operative management**
- Bed rest, **haemoglobin every 4–6 h**. Succeeds in **>90% of blunt injuries** and selected penetrating ones.
- Failure: instability, or falling haemoglobin despite 2–3 units of blood.
- **Reimage at ~48 h** for grade IV–V with deep lacerations, or with fever, pain or falling haematocrit.
- Urinary extravasation heals spontaneously in **76–90%**.

**Angioembolisation (AE)**
- Superselective coils for active bleeding, pseudoaneurysm, AVF (arteriovenous fistula) or delayed bleeding.

**Indications for exploration**
| Absolute | Relative |
|---|---|
| Instability with no or transient response | Extravasation with >25% devitalised parenchyma |
| Expanding or pulsatile retroperitoneal haematoma | Arterial thrombosis |
| Suspected pedicle avulsion; UPJ avulsion | Associated colon or pancreatic injury |

**Operative steps**
1. Midline laparotomy. **Control renal vessels before opening Gerota's fascia**: incise the retroperitoneum over the aorta, find the left renal vein crossing it, loop the vein and arteries.
2. **Renorrhaphy**: debride, suture-ligate vessels, close the collecting system watertight, close the defect over a bolster, omental cover, drain. Polar injury: partial nephrectomy.
3. **Nephrectomy** if unreconstructable or unstable. **Damage control**: pack, resuscitate in ICU (intensive care unit), return at 24–48 h.

**Renovascular injury**
- Unilateral artery thrombosis with a normal other kidney: observe. **Solitary or bilateral: revascularise** (warm ischaemia ideally <120 min) or stent an intimal flap.
- Left renal vein can be ligated (collaterals); the right cannot.

### Complications and follow-up
- **Delayed bleeding** (usually within 21 days): AE. Urinoma or abscess: stent, nephrostomy, drain.
- **Hypertension**: Goldblatt (arterial stenosis), **Page kidney** (haematoma compression), AVF.
- **Also know:** AKI (acute kidney injury); hydronephrosis.
- Follow-up: blood pressure, urinalysis, creatinine; EAU: DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid) scan for salvaged grade III–V kidneys.

**Diagram to draw:** left renal vein crossing the aorta, with vessel loops on the vein and artery.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - AAST 2018: segmental vessel injury grade IV; main vessel grade V.
> - Image for gross haematuria, microhaematuria with SBP <90, deceleration or penetrating injury.
> - Stable patients, even grade IV–V: non-operative ± angioembolisation.
> - Revascularise only a solitary kidney or bilateral injuries.

## Q2. Discuss etiopathogenesis of uric acid calculi. Describe rationale of medical management,
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- Uric acid stones form **8–10%** of stones in the USA and are increasing with obesity and diabetes. They are **radiolucent** and can be **dissolved medically**.

### Etiopathogenesis
**1. Why humans form uric acid stones**
- Humans and apes lack hepatic **uricase**, which converts uric acid to allantoin (10–100 times more soluble). Blood and urine uric acid are therefore high.

**2. Physical chemistry: pH is the key**
- Uric acid is a weak acid, **pKa about 5.35–5.5**. At pH 5.5 half is free uric acid and half is urate.
- Free uric acid is poorly soluble (about 96–100 mg/L); sodium urate is about 20 times more soluble. At **pH 6.5, over 90% is ionised**.
- **No natural inhibitor** of uric acid crystallisation is known, so stone formation depends almost entirely on supersaturation.

**3. The three determinants**
| Determinant | Causes | Importance |
|---|---|---|
| **Low urine pH (<5.5)** | Insulin resistance, obesity, type 2 diabetes, gout, high animal-protein diet, chronic diarrhoea, ileostomy | **Most important**; most formers have normal uric acid excretion |
| **Low urine volume** | Hot climate or occupation, diarrhoea, dehydration | Concentrates uric acid |
| **Hyperuricosuria (>600 mg/day)** | Purine-rich diet, gout, myeloproliferative disorders, tumour lysis, uricosuric drugs, inherited defects | Least often the driver in idiopathic cases |

**4. Why urine is too acid: insulin resistance**
- Insulin normally stimulates **ammonia production** from glutamine in the proximal tubule and the Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger NHE3.
- Insulin resistance reduces ammonium excretion. Acid is then buffered as titratable acid, so urine pH falls.
- **Renal lipotoxicity:** fat accumulates in the proximal tubule, which then burns fatty acids instead of glutamine, so ammoniagenesis falls further.
- Endogenous acid production is also higher. Uric acid stones are thus a **renal manifestation of the metabolic syndrome**; about a third of stones in diabetics are uric acid.

**5. Aetiological categories**
- **Congenital:** HGPRT (hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase) deficiency (Lesch–Nyhan), PRPP (phosphoribosylpyrophosphate) synthetase overactivity, hereditary renal hypouricaemia (URAT1, the urate transporter).
- **Idiopathic:** low urine pH without gout ("gouty diathesis").

**6. Uric acid and calcium stones**
- In **hyperuricosuric calcium oxalate stone disease**, urate promotes calcium oxalate by removing inhibitors, by "salting out" and by **epitaxy** (urate crystals act as a nidus).
- **Also know:** ammonium acid urate stones (laxative abuse, diarrhoea, ileostomy); theobromine (cocoa) inhibits uric acid crystallisation in vitro.

### Diagnosis in brief
- Radiolucent on X-ray; CT **200–400 HU (Hounsfield units)**; **dual-energy CT** identifies uric acid.
- Urine pH persistently <5.5; serum uric acid; 24-hour urine; screen for diabetes.

### Rationale of medical management
**Principle:** uric acid stones are a **"pH disease" more than a uric acid disease**. Raising pH turns insoluble uric acid into soluble urate. Diluting urine lowers supersaturation. Lowering uric acid production helps only when excretion is high.

**1. Fluids**
- Urine output **at least 2.5 L/day** lowers uric acid concentration.

**2. Diet and lifestyle**
- Limit non-dairy **animal protein** (it gives purines and an acid load; dietary purines provide about 30% of urinary uric acid).
- More fruit and vegetables (alkali load). Weight loss, exercise and glucose control correct the underlying insulin resistance.

**3. Urinary alkalinisation (mainstay)**
- **Potassium citrate 30–60 mEq/day** in two or three doses. Citrate is metabolised to bicarbonate, raising urine pH.
- **Target pH 6.0–6.5** for prevention (EAU [European Association of Urology]: 6.2–6.8); **avoid pH >7.0**, which risks calcium phosphate stones.
- **Why potassium, not sodium:** sodium alkali raises urinary calcium and can cause calcium stones. Sodium bicarbonate or sodium citrate is used if potassium is unsafe (renal impairment, hyperkalaemia).
- Patients **monitor urine pH with dipsticks**.

**4. Dissolution (oral chemolysis)**
- Pure uric acid stones dissolve over weeks to months. **EAU: pH 7.0–7.2**; stent or nephrostomy if obstructed or infected; monitor by imaging.

**5. Xanthine oxidase inhibitors**
- **Allopurinol 300 mg/day** lowers uric acid production. It is **not first-line**, because low pH, not hyperuricosuria, is the usual cause.
- Indications: hyperuricosuria with hyperuricaemia (e.g. gout), recurrence despite alkalinisation (AUA, American Urological Association), hyperuricosuric calcium oxalate stones.
- Reduce the dose in renal impairment. Risks: rash; **allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome** (2–8% have reactions; may be fatal); Stevens–Johnson syndrome.
- **Febuxostat** is an alternative. **Rasburicase** (recombinant uricase) prevents uric acid nephropathy in tumour lysis.

**Landmark trial:** **Ettinger (1986)**, randomised placebo-controlled trial: allopurinol more than halved recurrence in hyperuricosuric, normocalciuric calcium oxalate stone formers.

**Diagram to draw:** sigmoid curve of uric acid solubility against urine pH, marking pKa 5.5 and target 6.0–6.5.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Low urine pH is the main cause; pKa 5.5; no inhibitor exists.
> - Insulin resistance reduces ammoniagenesis, so urine is acid.
> - Potassium citrate to pH 6.0–6.5 (dissolution 7.0–7.2); avoid >7.0.
> - Allopurinol only for hyperuricosuria or failed alkalinisation.

## Q3a. Methods for GFR estimation in prospective tx donor.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 1, Q3a)

*Assumed meaning: tx = transplant (prospective living kidney donor).*

### Why it matters
- The donor loses half the nephron mass; function settles at 60–80% of the pre-donation level. GFR (glomerular filtration rate) is assessed by at least two methods, **including a measured one**.

### Methods
**1. Serum creatinine and eGFR (estimated GFR)**
- Creatinine is freely filtered but depends on **muscle mass, diet and age**, and is also secreted, so it overestimates GFR.
- **CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) 2021** equation: age and sex, **no race term**. MDRD (Modification of Diet in Renal Disease) and Cockcroft–Gault are older.
- Less accurate above 60 mL/min, where donors lie: a screening test only.

**2. Cystatin C**
- 13-kDa protein from all nucleated cells; freely filtered, then reabsorbed and broken down. Independent of muscle mass; affected by thyroid state, steroids, inflammation.
- The **combined creatinine–cystatin C equation** is more accurate than either alone.

**3. 24-hour creatinine clearance**
- (Urine creatinine × volume) ÷ serum creatinine; collection errors common.

**4. Measured GFR (mGFR): reference**
| Marker | Method |
|---|---|
| **Inulin** | Urinary clearance; gold standard, research only |
| **Iohexol** | Plasma clearance; safe and accurate |
| **Iothalamate** | Urinary or plasma clearance |
| **⁵¹Cr-EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid)** | Plasma clearance |
| **⁹⁹ᵐTc-DTPA (diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid)** | Plasma sampling, or gamma camera (Gates method); purely filtered |

**5. Split function**
- DTPA renogram (or DMSA, dimercaptosuccinic acid) gives each kidney's share. If the difference exceeds 10%, **leave the better kidney with the donor**.

### KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) 2017 decision
| GFR (mL/min/1.73 m²) | Decision |
|---|---|
| **≥90** | Accept |
| **60–89** | Individualise (age, lifetime risk) |
| **<60** | Decline |
- Initial eGFR is confirmed by mGFR, measured creatinine clearance or creatinine–cystatin C eGFR.
- Indian practice: DTPA GFR with split function, plus CT (computed tomography) angiography.

**Diagram to draw:** plasma tracer clearance curve after a single injection.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - CKD-EPI 2021 is race-free; cystatin C adds accuracy.
> - Inulin is the gold standard; iohexol or DTPA in practice.
> - KDIGO 2017: ≥90 accept, 60–89 individualise, <60 decline.

## Q3b. Measures to decrease complications of TURP
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(TUR syndrome also answered in 2017-Winter Paper 3, Q3f)

### Introduction
- TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate) is the benchmark; overall morbidity approaches one-fifth, mostly preventable.

### Preoperative measures
- **Selection:** AUA (American Urological Association) 2023: TURP for glands up to 80 mL; larger glands need HoLEP (holmium laser enucleation) or simple prostatectomy.
- **Sterile urine:** treat UTI (urinary tract infection) first; single-dose prophylaxis covering gram-negatives and enterococci.
- **Anticoagulants:** stop after advice from the prescriber; if they cannot be stopped, choose HoLEP, ThuLEP or PVP (photoselective vaporisation).
- **Finasteride** for recurrent BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) bleeding.
- Spinal anaesthesia shows early TUR syndrome; pad the peroneal nerve.

### Intraoperative measures
| Complication | Prevention |
|---|---|
| **TUR syndrome** (hypo-osmolar fluid absorbed, dilutional hyponatraemia) | **Bipolar TURP with saline**; else irrigant ≤60 cm high, resection <90 min, avoid capsular perforation, check sodium in long resections |
| **Bleeding** | Systematic resection to capsule; control arterial bleeders as you go; bipolar "cut and seal"; catheter traction for venous ooze |
| **Ureteric orifice injury** | Identify orifices first; take a large median lobe down first |
| **Perforation** | Respect the capsule; visible fat means perforation |
| **Incontinence** | **Stop at the verumontanum**; resect apex last in a bloodless field |
| **Stricture** | Calibrate or dilate meatus, lubricate generously, correct sheath size |
| **Bladder neck contracture** | Avoid over-resection and excess fulguration; TUIP (transurethral incision of the prostate) for small glands |

### Postoperative measures
- Large-bore three-way catheter; careful **continuous bladder irrigation** (blocked outflow distends the bladder and worsens bleeding).
- Even routine cases absorb 800–1000 mL of irrigant; check sodium if confused.
- TUR syndrome treatment: **3% saline 100 mL** boluses; raise sodium by no more than 12 mEq/L in 24 h (pontine myelinolysis).
- Stool softeners; no cycling for 4–6 weeks; counsel about **retrograde ejaculation** (about two-thirds).

**Diagram to draw:** resection field from bladder neck to verumontanum with the external sphincter marked.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Bipolar saline TURP removes TUR syndrome risk; fewer transfusions.
> - Monopolar: irrigant ≤60 cm, resection <90 min, gland <45 mL safer.
> - Verumontanum is the distal limit.

## Q3c. What is optical coherence tomography. What is its potential role in management of CA bladder?
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in Practice Set 5 Paper 4, Q3c and 2017-Summer Paper 4, Q3c)

### What is OCT (optical coherence tomography)?
- An **"optical biopsy"** technique: the **optical analogue of B-mode ultrasound**. It measures the echo delay of back-scattered **near-infrared light**, using low-coherence interferometry.
- Gives real-time cross-sectional images with about 10–20 µm resolution but only **1–2 mm depth**. First described in 1991 (ophthalmology).
- **Technique:** a fibre-optic probe passes through the cystoscope working channel and is placed **in contact** with the lesion found by WLC (white-light cystoscopy) or blue light.

### Image interpretation
| Tissue | OCT appearance |
|---|---|
| Normal | Three layers: thin low-scatter urothelium, bright lamina propria, darker muscularis |
| Inflammation | Layers preserved but thickened |
| Ta papillary tumour | Thick urothelium; lamina propria boundary intact |
| T1 tumour | **Loss of the urothelium–lamina propria boundary** |

### Potential role in carcinoma bladder
1. **Diagnosis:** separates benign from malignant flat lesions in real time; fewer unnecessary biopsies.
2. **Staging aid:** suggests Ta versus T1 during TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour).
3. **Resection margins:** checks completeness of TURBT.
4. **Surveillance** of NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer), including after BCG (bacille Calmette–Guérin).
5. **Upper tract:** through the ureteroscope, OCT correlates well with final grade and stage of UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma).

### Evidence and limitations
- Small studies: **high sensitivity, lower specificity**. Inflammation, scar and post-BCG change mimic tumour; adding OCT to blue light improves specificity.
- Small field; too shallow for muscle invasion; cannot grade; cost; learning curve. **Investigational.**

### Related techniques
- **Blue-light (hexaminolevulinate) cystoscopy:** detects more CIS (carcinoma in situ).
- **NBI (narrow-band imaging):** 415/540 nm light absorbed by haemoglobin.
- **CLE (confocal laser endomicroscopy):** cellular images after fluorescein.

**Diagram to draw:** OCT image of three bladder layers beside a tumour with loss of layering.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Near-infrared "ultrasound with light"; depth 1–2 mm.
> - Loss of layering suggests tumour; boundary loss suggests T1.
> - Sensitive but false positives from inflammation; investigational.

## Q3d. Tubeless PCNL
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2025-Winter Paper 2, Q3e and 2016-Summer Paper 4, Q3e)

### Definition
- PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) that ends **without a nephrostomy tube**.
- **Tubeless:** drainage by a ureteral stent (double-J, or externalised stent or string). **Totally tubeless:** no stent and no tube.
- **Wickham (1984)** did the first tubeless PCNL; **Bellman (1997)** popularised it.

### Rationale
- A large nephrostomy tube causes pain, more analgesia, longer stay and higher cost. Most tracts stop bleeding without tamponade.

### Selection
| Suitable | Keep a nephrostomy |
|---|---|
| Complete clearance, single tract | Pyonephrosis or infected stone |
| Minimal bleeding | Significant bleeding |
| Intact collecting system | Significant perforation |
| Supracostal and bilateral cases (shown safe) | Residual stone needing a second look |

### Technique (exit strategy)
1. Confirm clearance by rigid and **flexible nephroscopy** and fluoroscopy.
2. Antegrade double-J stent, or externalised stent or string.
3. Withdraw the sheath under vision; **safety wire removed last**.
4. Watch the tract; apply pressure. Fibrin glue did not reduce bleeding in randomised trials; some use haemostatic sealants or tract cautery.
5. Urethral catheter overnight.

### Evidence
- Meta-analyses of randomised trials: shorter stay and less pain.
- Pain benefit is over large tubes (**>16 Fr**); small tubes (≤10 Fr) give similar comfort without more bleeding.
- No difference in fever, transfusion or stone-free rate.
- Stent discomfort: worse WiSQoL (Wisconsin Stone Quality of Life) scores than nephrostomy in two trials. Externalised stents come out before discharge without cystoscopy.

### Disadvantages
- Loss of tract for a second look.
- Stent symptoms; a further procedure for removal.
- Urine leak or urinoma.
- Bleeding without tamponade: reinsert a balloon (Kaye) catheter, then **angioembolisation**.

### Current position
- AUA (American Urological Association): choose tubeless, small tube or stent case by case. Pairs well with mini-PCNL.

**Diagram to draw:** kidney with sealed tract and double-J stent.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Tubeless = stent only; totally tubeless = neither.
> - Benefit is mainly over large (>16 Fr) tubes.
> - Keep a tube for bleeding, perforation, infection or second look.

## Q3e. Medullary sponge kidney
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **MSK (medullary sponge kidney; precalyceal canalicular ectasia):** dilated (ectatic) distal **collecting ducts** with small cysts and diverticula **confined to the medullary pyramids**. Nephrons are initially normal.
- Usually **non-heritable** (sporadic); usually bilateral.

### Pathogenesis of stones and nephrocalcinosis
- **Stasis and infection** in the ectatic ducts favour crystal deposition.
- **Tubular defects:**
  - **Hypercalciuria** in one-third to one-half (mostly absorptive; sometimes renal leak).
  - **Incomplete distal RTA (renal tubular acidosis)** in 30–40%, shown on acid loading. This causes **hypocitraturia** and alkaline urine.
  - Impaired urinary concentration.
  - Mutations in H⁺-ATPase genes (ATP6V1B1, ATP6V0A4) support an MSK–distal RTA link.
- Result: calcium stones within dilated ducts and medullary **nephrocalcinosis**.

### Clinical features
- Often silent for life; presents **after 20 years**.
- **Renal colic 50–60%**, UTI (urinary tract infection) 20–33%, gross haematuria 10–18%.
- Found in **2.6–21% of stone formers**, more often women.

### Diagnosis
- **Urographic triad:**
  1. Enlarged kidneys, with or without papillary calcification.
  2. Elongated papillary tubules or cavities filling with contrast: **"bristles on a brush"** (paintbrush); when calcified, a **"bouquet of flowers"**.
  3. Papillary blush and persistent medullary opacification.
- CT urography now replaces IVU (intravenous urography); plain CT shows medullary nephrocalcinosis.
- **Metabolic work-up:** 24-hour urine (calcium, citrate, pH), serum bicarbonate and potassium.
- **Differentials:** papillary necrosis, other medullary nephrocalcinosis (distal RTA, hyperparathyroidism), calyceal diverticulum.

### Management
| Agent | Target | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Potassium citrate** | Hypocitraturia, hypercalciuria | **First choice**; reduces calciuria and recurrence |
| Thiazide | Hypercalciuria | Useful even without hypercalciuria |
| Inorganic phosphate | Hypercalciuria | If thiazide unsuitable; avoid with urease infection |
| Fluids, low-sodium diet | Volume, calciuria | All patients |
- Treat infection; stones by SWL (shock-wave lithotripsy) or ureteroscopy; stones inside ducts may be unreachable.
- **Prognosis:** usually benign; renal failure is rare.

**Diagram to draw:** pyramid with ectatic ducts filled with contrast ("brush") and small calculi.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Ectatic collecting ducts; sporadic; "bristles on a brush".
> - Hypercalciuria, incomplete distal RTA, hypocitraturia.
> - Potassium citrate is first-line prevention.

## Q3f. Amendments to THOA: 2011.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Indian law; not covered in the chapter notes.*

### Background
- **THOA (Transplantation of Human Organs Act) 1994** regulated organ transplantation, banned commercial dealing and legalised **brain-stem death**.
- Organ trade continued and deceased donation stayed low. The **Transplantation of Human Organs (Amendment) Act 2011** responded; **Rules followed in 2014**.

### Main amendments
| Area | Change |
|---|---|
| **Scope** | **Tissues** added; renamed the **Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act (THOTA)** |
| **Near relatives** | **Grandparents and grandchildren** added to spouse, children, parents and siblings |
| **Swap donation** | **Paired exchange** between near-relative pairs allowed, with Authorisation Committee approval |
| **Retrieval centres** | Hospitals with ICU (intensive care unit) facilities can register as **non-transplant organ retrieval centres**; **tissue banks** registered |
| **Transplant coordinator** | Mandatory in every registered transplant hospital |
| **Mandatory ("required") request** | ICU doctor must ask relatives of a potential brain-dead donor about donation and inform the retrieval centre |
| **Brain-death board** | If a neurologist or neurosurgeon is unavailable, a surgeon, physician, anaesthetist or intensivist nominated by the hospital can serve |
| **Eye donation** | Trained technicians may remove corneas |
| **Registry and network** | National human organ and tissue removal and storage network and **national registry** of donors and recipients (now run by NOTTO, the National Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation) |
| **Penalties** | Raised (see below) |

### Penalties under the amendment
- **Removal of organs without authority:** imprisonment up to **10 years** and fine up to ₹20 lakh.
- **Commercial dealing in organs:** imprisonment **5–10 years** and fine **₹20 lakh to ₹1 crore**.
- Registered medical practitioner: name removed from the register for **3 years** for a first offence and **permanently** for a later offence.

### Safeguards retained
- Unrelated donors and foreigners need **Authorisation Committee** approval.
- Minors and mentally ill persons cannot be living donors except in exceptional, approved cases.

**Diagram to draw:** flowchart: potential donor in ICU → required request → transplant coordinator → brain-stem death board → retrieval → national registry and allocation.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 2011: tissues added; grandparents and grandchildren became near relatives.
> - Swap donation, retrieval centres, transplant coordinators, required request.
> - Commercial dealing: 5–10 years and ₹20 lakh to ₹1 crore.

#### Q3c. What is optical coherence tomography. What is its potential role in management of CA bladder? (SAQ)

- Near-infrared "ultrasound with light"; depth 1–2 mm.
- Loss of layering suggests tumour; boundary loss suggests T1.
- Sensitive but false positives from inflammation; investigational.

#### Q3d. Tubeless PCNL (SAQ)

- Tubeless = stent only; totally tubeless = neither.
- Benefit is mainly over large (>16 Fr) tubes.
- Keep a tube for bleeding, perforation, infection or second look.

#### Q3e. Medullary sponge kidney (SAQ)

- Ectatic collecting ducts; sporadic; "bristles on a brush".
- Hypercalciuria, incomplete distal RTA, hypocitraturia.
- Potassium citrate is first-line prevention.

#### Q3f. Amendments to THOA: 2011. (SAQ)

- 2011: tissues added; grandparents and grandchildren became near relatives.
- Swap donation, retrieval centres, transplant coordinators, required request.
- Commercial dealing: 5–10 years and ₹20 lakh to ₹1 crore.
