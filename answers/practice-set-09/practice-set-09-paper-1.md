## Q1. Describe in detail, anatomy of prostate with special reference to concepts in surgical management if localised CA prostate.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in Practice Set 2 Paper 4, Q1)

*"if localised" is read as "of localised" prostate cancer.*

### Introduction
- A 20-g fibromuscular gland around the proximal urethra, from bladder neck (**base**) to urogenital diaphragm (**apex**).
- RP (radical prostatectomy) aims at the "trifecta" of cancer control, continence and potency. Each anatomical step either secures a clear margin or saves a named structure (nerves, sphincter, anterior support).

### Zonal anatomy (McNeal, 1981)
| Zone | Share of glands | Surgical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Peripheral (PZ) | About 70% | **70% of cancers**; posterolateral, so ECE (extracapsular extension) runs along the nerves |
| Central (CZ) | About 25% | Cone around the ejaculatory ducts; few cancers |
| Transition (TZ) | About 5% | BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia); anterior cancers missed by DRE (digital rectal examination) |
| AFMS (anterior fibromuscular stroma) | No glands | Anterior tumours reach it; margin risk in posterior approaches |
- There is **no true capsule**, so cancer breaching the gland is "extraprostatic extension".

### Fascial planes and nerve sparing
| Plane | Lies | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Intrafascial** | Between capsule and prostatic fascia | Most nerves kept; more positive margins |
| **Interfascial** | Between prostatic and levator fascia | **Standard** for organ-confined disease |
| **Extrafascial** | Outside levator and Denonvilliers fascia | Widest margin; ED (erectile dysfunction) likely |
- **Denonvilliers fascia** stays on the rectum when ECE risk is low; it is excised when posterolateral ECE is suspected.

### Neurovascular bundle (NVB)
- Walsh and Donker (1982). The pelvic plexus (S2–S4 parasympathetic plus hypogastric sympathetic) gives the **cavernous nerves**. They run posterolaterally **3–5 mm from the capsule**, marked by capsular vessels.
- At the apex they lie at **5 and 7 o'clock**; most nerve injuries occur here.
- Nerve distribution is diffuse (posterolateral bundle, posterior "hammock", anterolateral fibres), which is the basis of high anterior release.
- Injury comes from incision, entrapment, **heat** and **traction**, so dissect athermally without traction.

### Vessels and lymphatics
- Inferior vesical artery (urethral and capsular branches). Preserve **accessory pudendal arteries**; they may be the main penile supply.
- DVC (dorsal venous complex): CO₂ pneumoperitoneum tamponades it, so robotic blood loss is low.
- Lymph: obturator, external and internal iliac, common iliac, presacral.

### Continence anatomy
| Passive | Active |
|---|---|
| Smooth muscle of bladder neck and urethra; anterior support (endopelvic fascia, detrusor apron, puboprostatic ligaments, DVC) | Horseshoe striated sphincter at the apex, supplied by pudendal nerve branches |

### Concepts in surgical management of localised cancer
1. **Selection:** RP suits localised disease (mainly intermediate and selected high risk) with a life expectancy of more than 10 years; low risk is usually offered active surveillance.
2. **RARP (robot-assisted RP):** 3D, magnified view and wristed instruments removed the suturing barrier. **Yaxley 2016** RCT (randomised controlled trial): equal early function versus open; less blood loss and shorter stay. Surgeon matters more than platform.
3. **Risk-graded nerve sparing:** each side is graded from full (intrafascial) through partial to none, using grade, positive cores and mpMRI (multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging) capsular contact. **NeuroSAFE** (intraoperative frozen section of the posterolateral surface) aims to allow more nerve sparing without more positive margins.
4. **Anterior-support preservation:**
   - **Retzius-sparing** (Bocciardi, 2010): fully posterior through the pouch of Douglas. Earlier continence at 3 months, equal at 12; more positive margins, so avoid for anterior tumours.
   - **Hood technique** (Tewari, 2021): anterior approach keeping detrusor apron, puboprostatic ligaments and DVC.
5. **Apical dissection:** the apex is the commonest positive-margin site. Keep maximal urethral length and divide sharply without cautery. Posterior (Rocco) reconstruction may aid early continence.
6. **PLND (pelvic lymph node dissection) templates:**

| Template | Nodes | Drainage captured |
|---|---|---|
| Limited | Obturator | Least |
| Standard | + External iliac | About 38% |
| **Extended** | + Internal iliac | About 63% |
| + Common iliac | + Common iliac | About 75% |
- Extended PLND when nodal risk exceeds 5% (EAU [European Association of Urology]). It stages but does not improve survival; lymphocele is the commonest complication.
7. **Focal therapy anatomy:** ablate the **index lesion**. Transrectal HIFU (high-intensity focused ultrasound) suits posterior PZ lesions; anterior lesions need transperineal cryotherapy. Spare sphincter, NVB and rectum.
- **Also know:** single-port robot (extraperitoneal); PSMA PET (prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography) staging; ERAS (enhanced recovery after surgery).

**Diagram to draw:** axial prostate showing the three fasciae, the NVB at 5 and 7 o'clock and the intra-, inter- and extrafascial planes.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - PZ gives 70% of cancers; no true capsule.
> - Interfascial plane is the standard nerve-sparing plane; apex is the commonest margin and nerve-injury site.
> - Retzius-sparing: earlier continence, more positive margins.
> - Extended PLND captures about 63% of drainage.

## Q2. Describe anatomy of pelvic floor in females. Discuss fertility preserving treatment options for procidentia
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in Practice Set 2 Paper 1, Q1)

*"Fertility preserving" means uterus-preserving treatment for a woman who wants future pregnancy.*

### A. Anatomy of the female pelvic floor
Support comes from the **levator ani**, the **endopelvic fascia and ligaments**, and the **perineal membrane and perineal body**, anchored to the bony pelvis.

**1. Pelvic diaphragm**

| Muscle | Attachment | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Puborectalis | Back of pubis, loops behind anorectal junction | Anorectal angle |
| Pubococcygeus | Pubis and arcus tendineus to viscera and anococcygeal raphe | Slings urethra and vagina |
| Iliococcygeus | Arcus tendineus and ischial spine to coccyx | Horizontal **levator plate** |
| Coccygeus (not levator) | Ischial spine to sacrum and coccyx | Lies on sacrospinous ligament |

- Nerve to levator ani **(S3–S5)**; **pudendal nerve (S2–S4)** to sphincters and perineal muscles.
- Tonic contraction closes the **urogenital hiatus**; the uterus and upper vagina rest on the levator plate.

**2. Perineal membrane and perineal body**
- The membrane spans the pubic arch and is pierced by urethra and vagina; the striated urogenital sphincter lies above it. The perineal body anchors the distal vagina.

**3. Ligaments and surgical anchors**
- **Cardinal ligament** (side wall to cervix; carries the uterine artery) and **uterosacral ligament** (sacrum to cervix). The ureter is 4 cm from the uterosacral ligament near the sacrum but 0.9 cm near the cervix.
- Pubocervical fascia attaches to the **ATFP (arcus tendineus fasciae pelvis)**, pubis to ischial spine.
- **Sacrospinous ligament (SSL)**, ischial spine to sacrum: the pudendal nerve and vessels pass behind the spine.
- **Sacral promontory**: left common iliac vein and right ureter each about 2.7 cm away.

**4. DeLancey levels (1992)**

| Level | Structures | Loss produces |
|---|---|---|
| I suspension | Cardinal–uterosacral complex | **Uterine or vault prolapse**, enterocele |
| II attachment | Paravaginal attachment to ATFP | Anterior or posterior wall prolapse |
| III fusion | Perineal membrane, perineal body | Urethral hypermobility, distal rectocele |

**5. Why prolapse occurs**
- Muscle fails first (birth trauma, levator avulsion, denervation). The hiatus widens and the ligaments carry the whole load and stretch. Other risks: vaginal birth, forceps, age, obesity, constipation, collagen disorders.

**Diagram to draw:** sagittal female pelvis with levator plate and DeLancey levels I–III.

### B. Procidentia: definition and assessment
- **Procidentia**: complete uterine (or vault) prolapse outside the hymen, **POP-Q (pelvic organ prolapse quantification) stage 4**.
- Assess all compartments, occult SUI (stress urinary incontinence) with prolapse reduced, PVR (post-void residual), hydronephrosis (ureteric kinking at the hiatus), cervical length and ulceration.
- Abnormal bleeding or cervical pathology needs gynaecological evaluation first, because it may argue against keeping the uterus.

### C. Fertility-preserving options
**Principle:** restore **level I** support while keeping uterus and cervix. Defer definitive surgery until the family is complete where possible, because pregnancy and delivery cause recurrence.

**1. Conservative (first line)**
- PFMT (pelvic floor muscle training), weight loss, treat constipation.
- **Pessary**: space-filling type (Gellhorn, cube) for procidentia; removed for conception; regular review for discharge and erosion.

**2. Uterus-preserving surgery**

| Operation | Technique | Fertility points |
|---|---|---|
| **Sacrospinous hysteropexy** (vaginal) | Cervix sutured into the SSL **1.5–3 cm medial to the ischial spine** | Non-inferior to vaginal hysterectomy with uterosacral suspension (SAVE U trial); later data suggest superiority. Buttock pain, pudendal injury |
| **Uterosacral hysteropexy** (vaginal or laparoscopic) | Shorten and reattach uterosacral ligaments to cervix | No mesh; cystoscopy for ureteric kinking |
| **Sacrohysteropexy** (open, laparoscopic, robotic) | Mesh from posterior cervix to anterior longitudinal ligament over the promontory | Most durable apical support; later delivery usually by caesarean |
| **Manchester (Fothergill)** | Cervical amputation, cardinal ligaments sutured in front of the stump, anterior colporrhaphy | Suits elongated cervix, but risks **cervical stenosis, incompetence, miscarriage, preterm labour**: avoid if pregnancy wanted |

- **Also know:** Indian cervicopexy slings for young nulliparous prolapse (Shirodkar, Purandare, Khanna, Virkud). **Le Fort colpocleisis** keeps the uterus but not fertility (elderly only).

**3. Choosing**
```
Procidentia, wants pregnancy -> PFMT + pessary
  -> fails -> sacrospinous hysteropexy or
     laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy (avoid Manchester)
  -> recurrence after childbearing -> hysterectomy
     + vault suspension or sacrocolpopexy
```

### D. Complications and follow-up
- Recurrence (mostly posterior), de novo SUI, mesh exposure (3.4–5.4% vaginal mesh exposure in meta-analyses), ureteric kinking. The retained uterus needs cervical screening.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Levator ani = puborectalis, pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus; S3–S5.
> - DeLancey level I (uterosacral–cardinal) failure causes uterine prolapse.
> - Pessary first; sacrospinous hysteropexy 1.5–3 cm medial to the ischial spine.
> - Manchester amputates the cervix: poor choice for future pregnancy.

## Q3a. Describe contribution of a. Prof. Herald Hopkins b. Dr Charles Huggins c. Prof. Roy Caine d. Dr D D Gaue
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Names read as Prof. Harold Hopkins, Dr Charles Huggins, Prof. Sir Roy Calne and Dr D. D. Gaur.*

### a. Prof. Harold Hopkins (British physicist)
- **Fibreoptics (1954, with Kapany):** coherent bundles of glass fibres transmit an image by total internal reflection (Tyndall's principle). This made flexible endoscopes possible.
- **Rod-lens system (1966):** the old scope used thin glass lenses separated by long air spaces. Hopkins replaced them with **long glass rods separated by short air gaps**. Result: more light transmission, a brighter and wider image, and a **smaller calibre**. Developed commercially with Karl Storz.
- It underpins all modern rigid endoscopes and enabled the first rigid ureteroscope (1979).

### b. Dr Charles Huggins (urologist, University of Chicago)
- **1941, with Hodges:** showed that prostate cancer is **androgen-dependent**. Castration (or oestrogen) relieved far-advanced disease; injected androgens aggravated it.
- Used serum **acid phosphatase** to monitor response, an early tumour marker.
- **1945:** bilateral adrenalectomy for castration-resistant disease (modest, brief responses).
- **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 1966**: the basis of ADT (androgen deprivation therapy) and of all hormonal cancer therapy.

### c. Prof. Sir Roy Calne (Cambridge transplant surgeon)
- Showed in dogs that **6-mercaptopurine and then azathioprine** prolong renal allograft survival; azathioprine with steroids became standard immunosuppression.
- Performed the **first liver transplant in the UK (1968)**.
- **First clinical use of ciclosporin** in kidney transplantation (late 1970s), the calcineurin-inhibitor era; acute rejection fell markedly.

### d. Dr D. D. Gaur (Mumbai, India)
- **1992: balloon dissection of the retroperitoneum.** A size 7 surgeon's glove tied on an 8-Fr red rubber catheter, inflated inside the retroperitoneum, creates a working space.
- This made **retroperitoneoscopy** practical and cheap; used for ureterolithotomy, nephrectomy and other renal surgery.

**Diagram to draw:** conventional lens scope versus Hopkins rod-lens scope (glass rods with short air gaps).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Hopkins: rod-lens system 1966; fibreoptics 1954.
> - Huggins and Hodges 1941: androgen dependence; Nobel 1966.
> - Calne: azathioprine, ciclosporin, first UK liver transplant 1968.
> - Gaur 1992: glove-balloon retroperitoneoscopy.

## Q3b. EMG
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*EMG is taken as urodynamic electromyography of the striated urethral/anal sphincter and pelvic floor.*

### Definition and principle
- EMG (electromyography) records the electrical potentials of depolarising striated muscle membranes, here the **external urethral sphincter and pelvic floor**, during filling and voiding.
- It shows **coordination** between detrusor and sphincter, not sphincter strength. The bladder neck (smooth muscle) is invisible to EMG.
- External anal sphincter activity mirrors the urethral sphincter, except in some neurological diseases.

### Electrodes
| Surface (patch) | Concentric needle |
|---|---|
| Perianal patches at 3 and 9 o'clock (ICS [International Continence Society] standard: two skin electrodes plus reference) | Needle into the sphincter (girls beside the meatus; boys transperineal) |
| Comfortable; poorer signal; movement artefact | Best signal; painful; dislodges |
| Coordination only | Also motor unit analysis: acute versus chronic denervation |

### Normal pattern
- Filling: activity **rises gradually** (recruitment, guarding reflex).
- Voiding: EMG **falls silent before** detrusor pressure rises and stays silent until flow ends.
- **Lag time** (uroflow with patch EMG in children): relaxation to flow 2–6 s is normal; <2 s suggests DO (detrusor overactivity); >6 s suggests primary bladder neck dysfunction.

### Abnormal patterns
| Finding | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **DSD (detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia)** | Sphincter contracts during detrusor contraction; needs a **suprasacral** cord lesion (SCI [spinal cord injury], MS [multiple sclerosis]). Blaivas types 1–3 |
| Dysfunctional voiding | Same tracing without neurological lesion (learned) |
| Pseudodyssynergia | EMG rise from straining or trying to suppress a contraction |
| Denervation potentials | Sacral cord or cauda equina lesion; MSA (multiple system atrophy) |
| Complex repetitive discharges, decelerating bursts | **Fowler syndrome** (retention in young women); predicts SNM (sacral neuromodulation) response |

### Clinical uses
- Neurogenic bladder (spina bifida, SCI); voiding dysfunction in children; female retention; before sphincter surgery.
- **Limitation:** surface EMG misdiagnoses some dysfunctional voiders; **VUDS (videourodynamics)** is more accurate.

**Diagram to draw:** tracing of Pdet, flow and EMG: EMG silent during normal voiding versus active in DSD.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - EMG judges coordination, not strength.
> - True DSD needs a suprasacral lesion.
> - Lag time 2–6 s normal.
> - Fowler syndrome: complex repetitive discharges on needle EMG.

## Q3c. Secondary malignancies in Uro
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in Practice Set 3 Paper 1, Q3f)

*Read as second cancers caused by treatment (radiotherapy, chemotherapy, urinary diversion, immunosuppression).*

### Definition
- A **second (treatment-related) malignancy** is a new, histologically distinct cancer caused by treatment of a first disease. It appears after a latency of years, often **in the radiation field**.

### Causes and risks
| Cause | Second cancer | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Pelvic radiotherapy** | **Bladder**, rectal cancer, sarcoma (in-field) | Bladder RR (relative risk) 2–4 after cervical EBRT (external beam radiotherapy), 1.5–2 after prostate EBRT; little with IMRT (intensity-modulated radiotherapy). Latency 15–30 years |
| **Cyclophosphamide** | **Bladder cancer**, UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma) | Only chemotherapy proven to cause bladder cancer (acrolein, phosphoramide mustard); about 4.5-fold, dose-dependent |
| **Urinary diversion** | Adenocarcinoma at the uro-intestinal junction | Ureterosigmoidostomy 6–29% after 10–20 years; cystoplasty 1.6%; ileal conduit 0.02% |
| **Transplant immunosuppression** | Skin cancer, PTLD (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder), RCC (renal cell carcinoma), bladder | RCC mostly in native kidneys; bladder about 3-fold |
| **Testicular GCT (germ cell tumour) treatment** | Solid tumours, leukaemia | Excess after cisplatin or radiotherapy, highest after both; etoposide leukaemia |

### Mechanisms
- Radiation and alkylating drugs: mutagenic DNA damage.
- Immunosuppression: lost immune surveillance, oncogenic viruses (EBV [Epstein–Barr virus], HPV [human papillomavirus]).
- Diversion: urine–faeces contact at the anastomosis.

### Prevention and surveillance
- IMRT, smaller fields; **mesna** with cyclophosphamide.
- **Haematuria after pelvic radiotherapy or cyclophosphamide: always cystoscopy.**
- Endoscopy from 5 years after ureterosigmoidostomy; augmentation surveillance from about 10 years; excise a defunctioned ureterosigmoid anastomosis.
- Testis cancer: fewer treatment modalities; low-dose CT (computed tomography) in surveillance.
- **Also know:** paediatric tumours (Wilms 1% at 10 years); pioglitazone, aristolochic acid, phenacetin.

**Diagram to draw:** ureterosigmoid anastomosis with adenocarcinoma at the junction.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Pelvic EBRT raises bladder cancer 1.5–4-fold; IMRT little.
> - Cyclophosphamide: only chemotherapy proven to cause bladder cancer.
> - Ureterosigmoidostomy cancer 6–29% after 10–20 years.
> - Transplant recipients: bladder cancer about 3-fold.

## Q3d. Hematospermia
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2025-Winter Paper 1, Q3c)

### Definition
- Blood in the ejaculate. Usually **benign and self-limiting**, most often from non-specific prostatic or seminal vesicle inflammation.
- **Primary (idiopathic):** no cause found, often young men. **Secondary:** a cause is identified. **Pseudo-haematospermia:** partner's blood (condom test) or haematuria.

### Causes
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Iatrogenic (commonest identifiable)** | Prostate biopsy (about 10–50%, lasts about a month), TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate), radiotherapy |
| Inflammation and infection | Prostatitis, vesiculitis, STI (sexually transmitted infection), **genitourinary tuberculosis**, schistosomiasis |
| Obstruction and cysts | Ejaculatory duct obstruction or calculi; seminal vesicle or utricle cyst |
| Tumours | **Prostate cancer**; rarely urethral, bladder, seminal vesicle |
| Vascular and systemic | Prostatic varices, hypertension, bleeding disorders, anticoagulants |

### Evaluation
- **History and examination:** biopsy, LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms), haematuria, TB (tuberculosis) contact, anticoagulants; blood pressure, genitalia, **DRE (digital rectal examination)**.
- **Basic tests:** urinalysis and culture, STI screen, **PSA (prostate-specific antigen)** over 40, coagulation if indicated.
- **Full work-up if:** age **≥40**, persistent or recurrent, haematuria, abnormal DRE or PSA, TB or cancer risk.
  - **TRUS (transrectal ultrasound):** seminal vesicles >1.5 cm or ejaculatory ducts >2.3 mm suggest obstruction.
  - **MRI (magnetic resonance imaging):** best for seminal vesicle haemorrhage, cysts, cancer.
  - **Cystoscopy and cytology**; early-morning urine for acid-fast bacilli.

### Management
- Young, single episode, normal tests: **reassure**.
- Treat the cause: antibiotics or antitubercular drugs; control BP (blood pressure); correct coagulopathy.
- Persistent prostatic bleeding: 5-ARI (5-alpha-reductase inhibitor).
- Refractory or obstructive: TURED (transurethral resection of ejaculatory ducts), cyst aspiration or unroofing, **seminal vesiculoscopy** (small scope through the ejaculatory duct or utricle; irrigation, laser for stones).

**Diagram to draw:** algorithm: <40 and single episode → reassure; ≥40 or persistent → PSA, TRUS/MRI, cystoscopy.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Usually benign; prostate biopsy is the commonest identifiable cause.
> - Full work-up if ≥40, persistent or with haematuria.
> - Exclude prostate cancer and genitourinary tuberculosis.
> - TRUS: seminal vesicle >1.5 cm or ejaculatory duct >2.3 mm suggests obstruction.

## Q3e. Botulimum toxin
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2025-Winter Paper 2, Q3d)

*"Botulimum" is read as botulinum toxin.*

### Introduction
- Neurotoxin of *Clostridium botulinum*, serotypes A–G. Urology uses **onabotulinumtoxinA (BoNT-A)**: a heavy chain (100 kDa) and a zinc-dependent light chain (50 kDa).

### Mechanism
1. Heavy chain binds the cholinergic nerve terminal and the toxin is internalised.
2. Light chain **cleaves SNAP-25** (synaptosomal-associated protein 25), a SNARE docking protein. Vesicles cannot fuse, so **acetylcholine release stops**.
- **Afferent action:** less ATP (adenosine triphosphate), substance P and CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide) release; fewer TRPV1 and P2X3 sensory receptors, so urgency falls.
- Effect wanes as nerve terminals sprout (6–9 months), so injections are repeated.

### Technique
- Cystoscopic detrusor injection, **20 sites (100 U) or 30 sites (200 U)**, usually sparing trigone; check PVR (post-void residual) at 2 weeks.

### Urological uses
| Indication | Dose and evidence |
|---|---|
| **Idiopathic OAB (overactive bladder)**, third line | **100 U**. ABC trial: equal to antimuscarinic, more complete dryness. ROSETTA: comparable to SNM (sacral neuromodulation), more UTI (urinary tract infection) |
| **NDO (neurogenic detrusor overactivity)** | **200 U** (DIGNITY): less incontinence, larger capacity, lower pressure |
| Paediatric NDO (spina bifida) | About 10 U/kg (maximum 360 U) in published series; FDA (Food and Drug Administration) label 6 U/kg, maximum 200 U |
| **DSD (detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia)** | Intrasphincteric injection |
| IC/BPS (interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome) | Off-label option |
| BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) | Intraprostatic: **no benefit** over placebo |

### Complications
- **Retention needing CISC (clean intermittent self-catheterisation)**, commoner in NDO; each 10 mL of baseline PVR raises risk about 27%. The patient must accept CISC beforehand.
- UTI, haematuria; rarely generalised weakness. Contraindications: active UTI, myasthenia gravis, pregnancy.

**Diagram to draw:** cholinergic synapse showing SNAP-25 cleavage by the light chain.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Cleaves SNAP-25; efferent and afferent action.
> - 100 U idiopathic OAB; 200 U NDO.
> - Main risks: retention needing CISC, UTI.
> - Children: FDA label 6 U/kg, maximum 200 U.

## Q3f. Retrograde endopyelotomy
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and principle
- Endoscopic **full-thickness incision of the UPJ (ureteropelvic junction)** from inside the ureter, through to peripelvic fat, followed by stenting so the ureter heals with a wider lumen (Davis "intubated ureterotomy" concept, 1943).
- **Retrograde ureteroscopic holmium laser endopyelotomy** is now the standard endourological route: direct vision without a percutaneous tract.

### Indications and contraindications
| Favourable | Unfavourable / contraindicated |
|---|---|
| Functionally significant UPJO (UPJ obstruction); short segment | **Long stenotic segment** |
| **Secondary UPJO** (failed pyeloplasty) | **Upper-tract stones** (treat percutaneously instead) |
| Moderate hydronephrosis, fair function | **Crossing vessel**, **gross hydronephrosis**, poor function |
- Pre-operative CT (computed tomography) angiography or Doppler to look for crossing vessels.

### Technique
1. General anaesthesia; retrograde pyelogram; hydrophilic safety wire into the pelvis.
2. Access sheath; flexible ureteroscope to the UPJ (semirigid in some women). Dilate a narrow intramural ureter or pre-stent.
3. Holmium laser, 200-µm fibre, 0.8–1.2 J, 10–15 Hz.
4. **Lateral incision** (no crossing vessels laterally), deepened into fat and extended into normal ureter and pelvis.
5. Optional balloon dilation to 24 Fr; DJ (double-J) stent and Foley catheter.
6. Stent out after some weeks; **diuretic renogram 4 weeks later**; follow up for 24–36 months.

### Results
- Holmium series 70–80% at 5 years (range 44–89%). Excluding crossing vessels >4 mm raises success to about 90%.
- Crossing vessel present versus absent: 42% versus 86% (antegrade series).
- Still below pyeloplasty (about 95%), but most cost-effective endourological option.

### Complications
- Urine leak, stent migration, infection, bleeding (lateral cut spares vessels), stricture (rare with small scopes); ureteroarterial fistula reported.
- **Also know:** antegrade (percutaneous) endopyelotomy for UPJO with stones; cautery wire balloon (more haemorrhage); balloon dilation alone (about 42% success); failure then needs pyeloplasty.

**Diagram to draw:** UPJ with lateral full-thickness incision and crossing vessel lying anterior/posterior.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Lateral full-thickness incision over a stent.
> - Crossing vessel and gross hydronephrosis predict failure.
> - Success 70–80%, below pyeloplasty (about 95%).

- Full work-up if ≥40, persistent or with haematuria.
- Exclude prostate cancer and genitourinary tuberculosis.
- TRUS: seminal vesicle >1.5 cm or ejaculatory duct >2.3 mm suggests obstruction.

#### Q3e. Botulimum toxin (SAQ)

- Cleaves SNAP-25; efferent and afferent action.
- 100 U idiopathic OAB; 200 U NDO.
- Main risks: retention needing CISC, UTI.
- Children: FDA label 6 U/kg, maximum 200 U.

#### Q3f. Retrograde endopyelotomy (SAQ)

- Lateral full-thickness incision over a stent.
- Crossing vessel and gross hydronephrosis predict failure.
- Success 70–80%, below pyeloplasty (about 95%).
