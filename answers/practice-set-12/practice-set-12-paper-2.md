## Q1. 65 yr male underwent RP for stage T1c Gleason's score (4+3). At 6 months post surgery, his S. PSA has risen to 2 ng/ml above his preop PSA value. Discuss treatment and prognosis
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(biochemical recurrence also discussed in 2019-Summer Paper 4, Q2)

*Assumed meaning: 6 months after RP (radical prostatectomy) the PSA (prostate-specific antigen) never became undetectable and is now ≥2 ng/mL, i.e. PSA persistence with early rise, suggesting residual or systemic disease.*

### Introduction
- After RP, PSA (half-life about 3 days) should become undetectable (<0.1 ng/mL) within about 6 weeks.
- **PSA persistence:** failure to become undetectable after RP. **BCR (biochemical recurrence):** a rise after an undetectable nadir.
- **Gleason 4+3 = grade group 3** (unfavourable intermediate risk).

### Definitions of BCR after RP
| Body | Definition |
|---|---|
| AUA (American Urological Association) | PSA ≥0.2 ng/mL |
| NCCN (National Comprehensive Cancer Network) | Failure to reach undetectable, two consecutive rises, or PSA ≥0.1 ng/mL |
| **EAU (European Association of Urology) risk groups** | **Low risk:** PSADT (PSA doubling time) >1 year **and** grade group <4. **High risk:** PSADT ≤1 year **or** grade group 4–5 |

### Why is the PSA high? (sources)
1. **Residual local disease:** positive margin (the only modifiable factor), residual benign tissue (low, stable PSA).
2. **Occult nodal metastases** (limited or no lymph node dissection).
3. **Distant micrometastases:** likeliest with persistent PSA and a fast rise.

### Evaluation
- **Repeat PSA** to confirm; calculate PSADT.
- **Review pathology:** pT stage, margin (length >1 mm, Gleason 4 at margin), seminal vesicles, nodes. **Also know:** CAPRA-S (Cancer of the Prostate Risk Assessment–Surgery) nomogram; Decipher genomic classifier.
- **PSMA (prostate-specific membrane antigen) PET/CT (positron emission tomography/computed tomography):** best test; detection about half below 0.5 ng/mL and nearly 100% above 2 ng/mL. Choline or fluciclovine if PSMA is unavailable.
- **mpMRI (multiparametric MRI) pelvis:** best for local recurrence in the prostatic fossa.
- CT and bone scan are rarely positive below PSA 10 ng/mL.

### Treatment
```
Confirm PSA, PSADT, pathology → PSMA PET ± mpMRI
 ├ Negative or fossa only → salvage RT to bed + ADT
 ├ Pelvic nodes only      → bed + pelvic node RT + ADT
 ├ Oligometastatic (≤3)   → ADT ± metastasis-directed therapy
 └ Polymetastatic         → treat as mHSPC: ADT + ARPI ± docetaxel
```
**1. Salvage RT (radiotherapy): the only curative option**
- Dose **≥64–66 Gy** to the bed (SAKK 09/10: 70 Gy gave no gain over 64 Gy); 40–45 Gy to pelvic nodes.
- Best at the lowest PSA: each 0.1 ng/mL costs about 2.6% recurrence-free survival.
- **RADICALS-RT, RAVES, GETUG-AFU 17 (ARTISTIC meta-analysis):** early salvage at PSA 0.1–0.2 equals adjuvant RT and spares ≥60% from RT. These apply to men who become undetectable, not to this patient.

**2. Add ADT (androgen deprivation therapy) to salvage RT: essential here**
| Trial | Arms | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **RTOG 9601** (Radiation Therapy Oncology Group) | RT ± 2 years bicalutamide 150 mg; PSA 0.2–4.0 | 12-year OS (overall survival) 76.3% vs 71.3% |
| **GETUG-AFU 16** | RT ± 6 months goserelin; PSA 0.2–2.0 | 10-year BCR-free 64% vs 49%; MFS (metastasis-free survival) better; OS unchanged |
| **SPPORT (RTOG 0534)** | Bed / bed + 4–6 months ADT / pelvis + ADT | 5-year freedom from progression 70.9% / 81.3% / 87.4% |
| **RADICALS-HD** | None vs 6 vs 24 months ADT | 24 months better MFS than 6 months |
- At PSA ≥2 ng/mL with grade group 3, give whole-pelvis RT with **long-course (about 2 years) ADT**.

**3. Systemic therapy**
- **PSMA-positive metastases:** ADT + ARPI (androgen receptor pathway inhibitor: abiraterone, enzalutamide, apalutamide) ± docetaxel (LATITUDE, ARCHES, PEACE-1, ARASENS).
- **Oligometastases (≤3):** MDT (metastasis-directed therapy) by SBRT (stereotactic body RT) delays ADT (**STOMP:** ADT-free survival 21 vs 13 months; **ORIOLE:** progression 19% vs 61%).
- **High-risk BCR not fit for salvage:** **EMBARK** (PSADT ≤9 months, PSA ≥1 ng/mL): enzalutamide + leuprolide, MFS HR (hazard ratio) 0.42.
- **Also know:** castrate testosterone <50 ng/dL; intermittent ADT acceptable (PR.7); observation only for EAU low-risk BCR or short life expectancy; salvage lymph node dissection is investigational.

### Prognosis
- **Untreated BCR (Pound):** metastasis in about one-third, median 8 years; death a median 5 years later. Predictors: **BCR <2 years, PSADT <10 months, Gleason 8–10.**
- **This patient is poor-risk:** persistent PSA, high level within 6 months, likely short PSADT. Persistent PSA carries a higher risk of metastasis than late BCR.
- **Salvage RT:** one-third to one-half BCR-free at 4–5 years; poorer above PSA 0.5–1 ng/mL. Cancer-specific survival gain mainly with PSADT <6 months treated within 2 years.
- Lifelong PSA follow-up.

**Diagram to draw:** the treatment flowchart above, with PSA kinetics curve after RP.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - EAU high-risk BCR: PSADT ≤1 year or grade group 4–5.
> - PSMA PET first: almost 100% detection above PSA 2 ng/mL.
> - Salvage RT ≥64 Gy + ADT: RTOG 9601 (OS), GETUG-AFU 16, SPPORT (add pelvis).
> - Pound: BCR <2 years, PSADT <10 months, Gleason 8–10 predict death.

## Q2. Outline management of muscle invasive TCC. give critical evaluation of various types of neobladder.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(neobladder also answered in Practice Set 4 Paper 2, Q3e and Practice Set 3 Paper 4, Q2)

### A. Management of MIBC (muscle-invasive bladder cancer; TCC = transitional cell carcinoma)
**Staging**
- Re-TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour) with muscle; **MRI with VI-RADS** (Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System; ≥3 suggests invasion); CT (computed tomography) urogram, chest CT; bone scan if symptomatic.

**1. Cystectomy pathway (standard for cT2–T4a N0 M0)**
- **Cisplatin-based NAC (neoadjuvant chemotherapy):** ddMVAC (dose-dense methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, cisplatin) or GC (gemcitabine–cisplatin) ×4. **ABC meta-analysis:** 5% absolute 5-year survival gain. **SWOG 8710:** pT0 38% vs 15%. **VESPER:** ddMVAC better PFS (progression-free survival) than GC.
- **NIAGARA:** perioperative durvalumab + GC improved event-free survival and OS (overall survival).
- **Cisplatin-ineligible:** GFR (glomerular filtration rate) <60 mL/min, ECOG (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group) ≥2, hearing loss, neuropathy, NYHA (New York Heart Association) III–IV failure → direct cystectomy.
- **RC (radical cystectomy) + standard bilateral PLND (pelvic lymph node dissection)** within 12 weeks of diagnosis. Men: cystoprostatectomy. Women: anterior exenteration; organ-sparing in selected cases. **SWOG S1011:** extended PLND gave no survival gain.
- **Adjuvant:** cisplatin chemotherapy if no NAC; **nivolumab** for pT3–4 or N+ (**CheckMate 274:** disease-free survival 20.8 vs 10.8 months).

**2. Bladder preservation: TMT (trimodality therapy)**
- Maximal TURBT + RT (radiotherapy) + radiosensitiser (**BC2001:** mitomycin C + 5-FU [fluorouracil]). Select cT2, solitary, **no hydronephrosis, no CIS (carcinoma in situ)**. Salvage cystectomy 10–30%.
- **Partial cystectomy:** solitary dome tumour, no CIS.

**3. Metastatic disease**
- **EV (enfortumab vedotin) + pembrolizumab** first line (EV-302: about doubled OS vs platinum). Alternatives: platinum chemotherapy then **avelumab maintenance** (JAVELIN Bladder 100); erdafitinib for FGFR (fibroblast growth factor receptor) alterations.

### B. Urinary diversion options
- Ileal conduit (simplest, commonest), continent cutaneous pouch, or **orthotopic neobladder**. No diversion is proven superior for QoL (quality of life).

### C. Neobladder: principles
1. **Reliable outlet:** intact rhabdosphincter, tumour-free urethra (frozen section).
2. **Low pressure:** complete **detubularisation** and cross-folding abolish peristalsis; a **sphere** gives most volume (Laplace).
3. **Capacity:** 44 cm of folded ileum holds <200 mL at first, **500–600 mL by 1 year**.
- **Contraindications:** positive urethral margin, stricture, eGFR (estimated GFR) <35–40 mL/min, liver failure, inability to self-catheterise. Age, obesity and nodes are not bars.

### D. Critical evaluation of neobladder types
| Type | Bowel; ureteric technique | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Studer** | 40–44 cm ileum + isoperistaltic afferent limb; refluxing | Simple, no staples, takes short ureters; stricture <3%; good long-term function | Night incontinence; reflux on cystogram (harmless) |
| **Hautmann W** | About 70 cm ileum; refluxing since 1997 | Largest capacity, early continence; stricture fell 9.5% → 1% | More retention, acidosis, B12 loss |
| **T pouch** (Stein–Skinner) | 44 cm + tunnelled T-limb flap valve | Antireflux; accepts dilated ureters | Complex; more secondary procedures (USC [University of Southern California] RCT) |
| **Abol-Enein serous tunnel** | 40 cm W, serosal troughs | Antireflux; stricture about 3.8% | Long ureters needed; no dilated ureters |
| **Hemi-Kock** | Intussuscepted nipple | Good early results | Late nipple stenosis, stones; abandoned |
| **Camey II; Padua (VIP)** | 65 cm or 42 cm ileum; Le Duc tunnel | Early designs | Le Duc stricture 7–29% |
| **Mainz III; right colon; sigmoid (Reddy)** | Colon ± ileum | When ileum short or diseased | High-pressure contractions, poor compliance, more leakage |

**Critical points**
- **Reflux vs obstruction:** Valsalva voiding pressurises the whole system equally, so reflux is benign; every antireflux valve can obstruct. **USC RCT** (484 patients): Studer = T pouch for renal function and infection; T pouch needed more secondary procedures. Hence **Studer and Hautmann** are the most popular.
- **Segment:** ileum is most compliant; colon and stomach give high pressures.
- **Capacity trade-off:** a larger pouch gives earlier continence but more retention and metabolic load.
- **Robotic intracorporeal neobladder:** comparable outcomes; long-term data awaited.

### E. Outcomes and complications
- **Day continence 80–90%; night 20–50%.** Retention: men 4–10%, **women 20–30%**.
- **Late:** hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis, B12 deficiency, mucus, stones, ureteroileal stricture, pouch rupture, urethral recurrence (men about 7% at 5 years).
- **Follow-up:** blood gas, B12, upper-tract imaging, urethral cytology if CIS.

**Diagram to draw:** Studer pouch with afferent limb and urethral anastomosis.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - NAC cisplatin: 5% absolute survival gain; NIAGARA adds durvalumab.
> - Studer and Hautmann (refluxing) dominate: obstruction, not reflux, destroys kidneys.
> - USC RCT: Studer = T pouch; T pouch more reoperations.
> - Day continence 80–90%, night 20–50%; women retain 20–30%.

## Q3a. Describe the principles of vascular anastomosis. Enumerate and describe options for vascular access surgery for HD
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in Practice Set 5 Paper 2, Q2)

*Assumed meaning: HD = haemodialysis.*

### Principles of vascular anastomosis
| Principle | Why |
|---|---|
| Proximal and distal control; systemic heparin, heparinised flush | Prevents thrombosis in the static column |
| Atraumatic handling (adventitia only); clamp clear of atheroma | Avoids intimal dissection |
| Healthy ends, no tension, twist or kink | Prevents stenosis and leak |
| **Intima-to-intima eversion** | Smooth endothelium faces the lumen |
| Wide opening: spatulate, or end-to-side elliptical arteriotomy | Avoids stricture |
| **Monofilament polypropylene**, double-armed (5-0/6-0 transplant; 6-0/7-0 wrist fistula) | Smooth, low drag, low thrombogenicity |
| **Inside-out on the artery** | Tacks down plaque and intima |
| Carrel triangulation or parachute; interrupted in children | Clear back wall; allows growth |
| Flush before last knot; leave "growth factor"; release venous clamp first | Removes air and clot; avoids purse-string |

### Options for HD vascular access
| Option | Description |
|---|---|
| **1. Non-tunnelled CVC** (central venous catheter) | Dual-lumen 10–12 Fr in internal jugular or femoral vein; emergencies, days only |
| **2. Tunnelled cuffed CVC** | Right internal jugular; bridge or last resort; infection, central stenosis |
| **3. Native AVF** (arteriovenous fistula), **first choice** | Best patency, fewest infections; matures 6–8 weeks |
| a. Radiocephalic (Brescia–Cimino) | Wrist, most distal; preserves proximal sites |
| b. Brachiocephalic | Elbow; higher flow, more steal |
| c. Brachiobasilic transposition | Deep basilic vein moved superficially |
| **4. AVG** (arteriovenous graft) | PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) loop; usable in 2–3 weeks; more stenosis and thrombosis |
| **5. HeRO graft** (Haemodialysis Reliable Outflow) | Bypasses central vein occlusion into right atrium |
| **6. Endovascular AVF** | Percutaneous forearm anastomosis |
- **Planning:** distal before proximal, non-dominant arm; Allen test, Doppler vein mapping; create AVF about 6 months before dialysis; preserve arm veins.
- **Complications:** non-maturation, stenosis, thrombosis, **steal** (banding, DRIL [distal revascularisation–interval ligation]), aneurysm, infection.

**Diagram to draw:** radiocephalic end-to-side fistula with triangulation sutures.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Intima-to-intima, polypropylene, inside-out on the artery, flush before tying.
> - AVF first, distal, non-dominant arm, 6 months ahead.
> - Rule of 6s: flow >600 mL/min, >6 mm wide, <6 mm deep, 6 weeks.

## Q3b. Describe medical management of renal calculi
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q2)

### Aims
- Relieve pain, dissolve suitable stones and **prevent recurrence** (up to 50% at 5 years).

### 1. Acute care
- **NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs)** first line; opioids if refractory. Urgent drainage if infection with obstruction.
- **MET (medical expulsive therapy):** α-blocker (tamsulosin) for distal ureteric stones 5–10 mm after migration from the kidney; trial 4–6 weeks.

### 2. Dissolution (chemolysis)
- **Uric acid** (radiolucent): potassium citrate to alkalinise (EAU [European Association of Urology]: pH 7.0–7.2 for dissolution); allopurinol if hyperuricosuric.
- **Cystine:** alkalinise to pH 7.0–7.5 with thiol drugs. **Struvite:** acetohydroxamic acid occasionally dissolves.

### 3. Prevention: general measures (all formers)
- **Fluid for urine ≥2.5 L/day** (Borghi 1996: recurrence halved).
- **Normal calcium**, low salt, less animal protein, more fruit and vegetables (Borghi 2002).

### 4. Metabolic evaluation
- Stone analysis, serum calcium, uric acid, creatinine, PTH (parathyroid hormone) if needed; one or two **24-hour urines** in high-risk or recurrent formers.

### 5. Selective therapy
| Abnormality | Drug and dose |
|---|---|
| **Hypercalciuria** | **Thiazide:** hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg twice daily, chlorthalidone 25–50 mg, indapamide; add potassium citrate (thiazide lowers potassium and citrate) |
| **Hypocitraturia**, distal RTA (renal tubular acidosis) | **Potassium citrate 30–60 mEq/day**: alkali load reduces tubular citrate reabsorption |
| Hyperuricosuria | Allopurinol 300 mg/day |
| Uric acid stones | Potassium citrate to **pH 6.0–6.5**; avoid >7.0 (calcium phosphate) |
| Enteric hyperoxaluria | Calcium with meals, low fat and oxalate, magnesium |
| Primary hyperoxaluria type 1 | Pyridoxine; **lumasiran** |
| **Cystinuria** | Urine >3 L, pH 7.0–7.5; **tiopronin** or D-penicillamine; cystine <250 mg/L |
| **Struvite** | Complete clearance; AHA (acetohydroxamic acid) 250 mg two to three times daily (urease inhibitor) |
- **Also know:** AUA (American Urological Association): thiazide or citrate for recurrent calcium formers even without abnormality; NOSTONE (2023): hydrochlorothiazide did not reduce recurrence; repeat 24-hour urine within 6 months of therapy, then yearly.

**Diagram to draw:** flowchart from stone analysis to selective therapy.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Urine ≥2.5 L/day; normal calcium, low salt.
> - Thiazide for hypercalciuria; potassium citrate 30–60 mEq/day for hypocitraturia.
> - Uric acid pH 6.0–6.5; cystine pH 7.0–7.5 and <250 mg/L.

## Q3c. Embryogenesis of urinary bladder
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 3, Q1)

### 1. Division of the cloaca (weeks 4–6)
- The **urorectal septum** (Tourneux and Rathke folds) divides the endodermal cloaca into a dorsal **anorectal canal** and a ventral **UGS (urogenital sinus)**.
- **Mesonephric (Wolffian) duct entry** is the landmark:
  - **Cranial vesicourethral canal** (continuous with the allantois) → **bladder and pelvic urethra**.
  - **Caudal (definitive) UGS** → phallic urethra (male); distal vagina and vestibule (female).

### 2. Layers of the wall
- **Urothelium:** UGS endoderm.
- **Lamina propria, detrusor, serosa:** surrounding splanchnic mesenchyme; epithelium and mesenchyme induce each other.

### 3. Trigone (weeks 5–6)
- The **common excretory ducts** (mesonephric duct distal to the ureteric bud) dilate and are absorbed into the UGS.
- **Apoptosis** separates ureters from mesonephric ducts. Ureteric orifices move **cranially and laterally**; mesonephric ducts end caudally as ejaculatory ducts.
- **Classic view:** trigone muscle is ureteric. **Current view** (mouse lineage tracing): mostly bladder smooth muscle.
- **Weigert–Meyer rule:** bud too low → lateral orifice, short tunnel → VUR (vesicoureteral reflux); too high → caudal, medial, ectopic orifice.

### 4. Allantois and urachus
- **Months 4–5:** the bladder descends into the pelvis; the allantoic duct elongates and obliterates as the **urachus** → median umbilical ligament.

### 5. Function
- Urine production starts at **week 10**. Fetal bladder seen in about half at 10 weeks, almost all by 13 weeks; empties every 15–20 minutes. After **16 weeks** amniotic fluid is mainly fetal urine.

### Anomalies explained by embryology
| Anomaly | Basis |
|---|---|
| **Exstrophy** | Cloacal membrane not reinforced by infraumbilical mesoderm |
| Urachal (patent urachus, sinus, cyst, diverticulum) | Failed obliteration at different levels |
| Agenesis, duplication | UGS or trigone failure; septation |
| Congenital (Hutch) diverticulum | Detrusor deficiency at the ureteric hiatus |
| VUR, ectopic ureter | Abnormal bud position |

**Diagram to draw:** cloaca divided by the urorectal septum, labelled vesicourethral canal, UGS, allantois.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Urorectal septum weeks 4–6; mesonephric duct entry is the landmark.
> - Trigone: common excretory duct absorption; mostly bladder muscle (current view).
> - Urachus forms at months 4–5; urine from week 10; amniotic fluid mainly urine after 16 weeks.

## Q3d. Tamsulosin
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- A **uroselective α1-adrenergic antagonist**, first-line drug for bothersome male LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) due to BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) (AUA [American Urological Association], EAU [European Association of Urology]).

### Receptor basis
| Subtype | Site | Effect of blockade |
|---|---|---|
| **α1a** (about 70% in prostate) | Prostate, bladder neck, prostatic urethra, iris dilator | Relieves dynamic obstruction; ejaculatory dysfunction; IFIS (intraoperative floppy iris syndrome) |
| α1b | Blood vessels | Hypotension (tamsulosin has 10-fold less affinity) |
| α1d | Detrusor, spinal afferents | Storage symptom relief |

### Mechanism
- Blocks noradrenaline at α1a receptors → relaxes prostatic and bladder-neck smooth muscle → lowers the **dynamic component** of BOO (bladder outlet obstruction). It does not shrink the prostate.

### Pharmacology and dose
- **0.4 mg once daily** after the same meal (up to 0.8 mg); no titration.
- Modified release; hepatic metabolism (CYP3A4, CYP2D6); avoid strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (ketoconazole).
- Flow rises within 4–8 hours, symptoms by 1 week; 0.2 mg is not better than placebo.

### Indications
1. **BPH/LUTS:** IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score) falls about 30–40%; Qmax rises; storage and nocturia improve.
2. **Combination with 5ARI** (5α-reductase inhibitor) for prostate >30–40 mL: **CombAT** (dutasteride + tamsulosin) and MTOPS (combination cut progression by 66%).
3. **MET (medical expulsive therapy):** distal ureteric stones 5–10 mm; SUSPEND (2015) negative overall but included all sizes.
4. **AUR (acute urinary retention):** before trial without catheter.
5. **Also know:** ureteric stent symptoms; female functional outlet obstruction.

### Adverse effects
- **Ejaculatory dysfunction** (dose-dependent; less than silodosin): retrograde ejaculation from bladder-neck relaxation, or anejaculation (emission failure).
- **IFIS:** floppy, billowing iris, iris prolapse, progressive miosis during cataract surgery; **highest risk with tamsulosin**. Stopping the drug does not remove the risk, so warn the ophthalmologist.
- Dizziness, rhinitis, headache; less orthostatic hypotension than non-selective agents. Caution: sulfonamide allergy.

### Comparison
| | Tamsulosin | Silodosin | Alfuzosin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Selectivity | α1a/α1d | Most α1a | Clinical only |
| Ejaculatory effect | Moderate | Highest | Lowest |

**Diagram to draw:** prostate cross-section showing α1a receptors in stroma and bladder neck.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 0.4 mg once daily; α1a/α1d selective; no titration.
> - IFIS highest with tamsulosin; persists after stopping.
> - MET benefit in distal stones 5–10 mm.

## Q3e. Valve bladder.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 2, Q3e)

### Definition
- **Valve bladder syndrome** (Mitchell, 1982): upper tracts and renal function worsen after successful PUV (posterior urethral valve) ablation, without residual obstruction, because of bladder dysfunction.

### Pathophysiology (vicious cycle)
```
Detrusor hypertrophy, collagen, low compliance → poor sensation, rising residual
+ polyuria (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus) → bladder never empty
→ pressure transmitted to dilated upper tracts → they refill the bladder → renal damage
```
- Three processes: **polyuria, poor sensation, residual urine.**
- Age pattern: hyperreflexia in infancy → better compliance in childhood → large atonic bladder (myogenic failure) after puberty.

### Clinical features
- Late continence (about 5.5 years), incontinence, UTI (urinary tract infection), worsening hydronephrosis, rising creatinine. **New incontinence after being dry is a red flag.**

### Evaluation
- Uroflowmetry and PVR (post-void residual) at each visit; ultrasound; creatinine; voiding diary.
- **Videourodynamics** when the picture changes and before transplantation.

### Management (escalating ladder)
1. **Behavioural:** adequate fluids, timed and double voiding, biofeedback.
2. **Drugs:** anticholinergic (oxybutynin) for a small high-pressure bladder; α-blocker for high residual. Stop oxybutynin if residual rises.
3. **CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation)** if residual rises despite drugs.
4. **Overnight bladder drainage** for worsening hydronephrosis, UTI or renal function; it gives bladder and kidneys a daily rest.
5. **Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy** if urethral CIC is difficult (sensate urethra, high bladder neck).
6. **Augmentation** (rare) for a small, high-pressure bladder despite the above: **ureterocystoplasty** with the dilated ureter is preferred, avoiding bowel (mucus, acidosis, stones).
- Reflux is treated through the bladder; reimplantation is rarely needed. Manage CKD (chronic kidney disease).

### Transplantation
- ESRD (end-stage renal disease) in 20–50%. Optimise the bladder first (videourodynamics, CIC or overnight drainage); graft survival is then similar.

**Diagram to draw:** vicious-cycle diagram of the valve bladder.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Polyuria, poor sensation, residual urine.
> - Overnight drainage breaks the cycle.
> - Ureteric augmentation rather than bowel; optimise before transplant.

## Q3f. Access techniques in dialysis
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(CAPD also answered in Practice Set 1 Paper 2, Q3a)

*Assumed meaning: access for PD (peritoneal dialysis) and HD (haemodialysis); emphasis on PD catheter insertion, since HD vascular access is answered in full in Practice Set 12 Paper 2, Q3a.*

### A. Peritoneal dialysis access
**Catheter:** **Tenckhoff** silicone catheter with **two Dacron cuffs**. The deep cuff sits in the rectus muscle; the superficial cuff about 2–4 cm from the exit. The tip lies in the **pelvis (rectovesical or rectouterine pouch)**; the exit site points **downwards** to reduce infection.

**Insertion techniques**
| Technique | Steps | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Open (mini-laparotomy)** | Paramedian incision → split rectus → open posterior sheath and peritoneum → catheter to pelvis on a stylet → purse-string round deep cuff → tunnel → exit site | Simple; general or local anaesthesia |
| **Laparoscopic (advanced)** | Rectus sheath tunnel; **omentopexy**, adhesiolysis, hernia repair as needed | Best function; fixes causes of outflow failure |
| **Percutaneous (Seldinger)** | Needle, guidewire, dilator and peel-away sheath under local anaesthesia, ± ultrasound or fluoroscopy | Bedside; avoid after major abdominal surgery |
| Peritoneoscopic | Small-scope guided placement | Variant of percutaneous |
- **Break-in:** delay full-volume exchanges about 2 weeks to prevent leak; urgent start uses small supine volumes.
- **Complications:** outflow failure (constipation, omental wrap, migration), leak, hernia, exit-site or tunnel infection, peritonitis, bowel perforation.
- **Contraindications:** peritoneal scarring, uncorrectable hernia, inability to perform exchanges.

### B. Haemodialysis access (brief)
- **Non-tunnelled CVC (central venous catheter):** dual-lumen 10–12 Fr, internal jugular or femoral vein by **ultrasound-guided Seldinger**; for AKI (acute kidney injury); avoid subclavian (central stenosis).
- **Tunnelled cuffed CVC:** right internal jugular, tip at the cavoatrial junction under fluoroscopy; bridge to AVF.
- **AVF (arteriovenous fistula)** first choice, then **AVG (arteriovenous graft)**; HeRO and endovascular AVF in selected patients (see Practice Set 12 Paper 2, Q3a).

**Diagram to draw:** Tenckhoff catheter with two cuffs, tunnel, downward exit and pelvic tip.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Tenckhoff: two cuffs, pelvic tip, downward exit.
> - Laparoscopic placement with omentopexy gives best function.
> - Avoid subclavian lines; AVF first for long-term HD.

#### Q1. 65 yr male underwent RP for stage T1c Gleason's score (4+3). At 6 months post surgery, his S. PSA has risen to 2 ng/ml above his preop PSA value. Discuss treatment and prognosis (LAQ)

- EAU high-risk BCR: PSADT ≤1 year or grade group 4–5.
- PSMA PET first: almost 100% detection above PSA 2 ng/mL.
- Salvage RT ≥64 Gy + ADT: RTOG 9601 (OS), GETUG-AFU 16, SPPORT (add pelvis).
- Pound: BCR <2 years, PSADT <10 months, Gleason 8–10 predict death.

#### Q2. Outline management of muscle invasive TCC. give critical evaluation of various types of neobladder. (LAQ)

- NAC cisplatin: 5% absolute survival gain; NIAGARA adds durvalumab.
- Studer and Hautmann (refluxing) dominate: obstruction, not reflux, destroys kidneys.
- USC RCT: Studer = T pouch; T pouch more reoperations.
- Day continence 80–90%, night 20–50%; women retain 20–30%.

#### Q3a. Describe the principles of vascular anastomosis. Enumerate and describe options for vascular access surgery for HD (SAQ)

- Intima-to-intima, polypropylene, inside-out on the artery, flush before tying.
- AVF first, distal, non-dominant arm, 6 months ahead.
- Rule of 6s: flow >600 mL/min, >6 mm wide, <6 mm deep, 6 weeks.

#### Q3b. Describe medical management of renal calculi (SAQ)

- Urine ≥2.5 L/day; normal calcium, low salt.
- Thiazide for hypercalciuria; potassium citrate 30–60 mEq/day for hypocitraturia.
- Uric acid pH 6.0–6.5; cystine pH 7.0–7.5 and <250 mg/L.

#### Q3c. Embryogenesis of urinary bladder (SAQ)

- Urorectal septum weeks 4–6; mesonephric duct entry is the landmark.
- Trigone: common excretory duct absorption; mostly bladder muscle (current view).
- Urachus forms at months 4–5; urine from week 10; amniotic fluid mainly urine after 16 weeks.

#### Q3d. Tamsulosin (SAQ)

- 0.4 mg once daily; α1a/α1d selective; no titration.
- IFIS highest with tamsulosin; persists after stopping.
- MET benefit in distal stones 5–10 mm.

#### Q3e. Valve bladder. (SAQ)

- Polyuria, poor sensation, residual urine.
- Overnight drainage breaks the cycle.
- Ureteric augmentation rather than bowel; optimise before transplant.

#### Q3f. Access techniques in dialysis (SAQ)

- Tenckhoff: two cuffs, pelvic tip, downward exit.
- Laparoscopic placement with omentopexy gives best function.
- Avoid subclavian lines; AVF first for long-term HD.
