## Q1. Discuss treatment of CRPC
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 2, Q3a)

### Definition (EAU [European Association of Urology])
- CRPC (castration-resistant prostate cancer): castrate testosterone **<50 ng/dL (1.7 nmol/L)** plus either:
  - **Biochemical progression:** three consecutive PSA (prostate-specific antigen) rises 1 week apart, two of them 50% above nadir, PSA >2 ng/mL; or
  - **Radiological progression:** ≥2 new bone lesions, or soft-tissue progression by RECIST (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours).

### Why it happens: the AR still drives growth
- Castration-resistant is **not** androgen-independent, so treatment first targets the AR (androgen receptor) more deeply.
- Mechanisms: AR overexpression and amplification; AR mutation (activated by other steroids); **splice variants lacking the ligand-binding domain**; adrenal and intratumoural androgen synthesis (CYP17 overexpressed); bypass pathways (PI3K/AKT, PTEN loss in ~40%).
- **Lineage plasticity:** neuroendocrine change with low PSA, visceral and lytic disease.

### Evaluation
- Testosterone (exclude non-compliance), PSA, **PSADT (PSA doubling time)**; CT and bone scan; PSMA (prostate-specific membrane antigen) PET.
- Prognosis: performance status, pain, haemoglobin, ALP (alkaline phosphatase), LDH (lactate dehydrogenase), visceral disease.
- **Germline testing** in all metastatic disease; somatic sequencing of tumour tissue for HRR (homologous recombination repair) genes and MSI (microsatellite instability).
- Biopsy a metastasis if progression is fast with a low PSA.
- PCWG3 (Prostate Cancer Clinical Trials Working Group 3): early bone-scan lesions may be **flare**; re-baseline at 12–16 weeks, then progression = ≥2 new lesions.

**Clinical states model (PCWG3):**
```
Localised → rising PSA → mCSPC (castration-sensitive)
                  ↓ (on ADT)
              nmCRPC → mCRPC 1st line → 2nd line → 3rd line
```

### General principles
1. **Continue ADT (androgen deprivation therapy) lifelong**, including during chemotherapy.
2. **Antiandrogen withdrawal** (e.g. bicalutamide): watch PSA 4–8 weeks; brief responses in a minority.
3. Choose by prior therapy, symptoms, visceral disease, genomics and comorbidity.
4. **Switch mechanism.** Cross-resistance means a second ARPI (AR-pathway inhibitor) helps only 15–30%.

### nmCRPC (non-metastatic)
- **PSADT ≤10 months:** add an ARPI. Each improved MFS (metastasis-free survival) and overall survival:
  - Apalutamide 240 mg (**SPARTAN**: MFS HR 0.28); enzalutamide 160 mg (**PROSPER**); darolutamide 300 mg twice daily (**ARAMIS**: MFS 40.4 vs 18.4 months).
- Slower PSADT: continue ADT and monitor.

### mCRPC: life-prolonging options
| Class | Agent and dose | Key trial (overall survival) |
|---|---|---|
| ARPI | **Abiraterone** 1000 mg fasting + prednisone | COU-AA-301: 14.8 vs 10.9 months; COU-AA-302 (pre-chemotherapy) |
| | **Enzalutamide** 160 mg | AFFIRM: 18.4 vs 13.6 months; PREVAIL |
| Taxane | **Docetaxel** 75 mg/m² 3-weekly + prednisone | TAX 327: 18.9 vs 16.4 months (mitoxantrone) |
| | **Cabazitaxel** 20–25 mg/m² after docetaxel | TROPIC: 15.1 vs 12.7; CARD: beats a second ARPI |
| Radioligand | **Radium-223** 50 kBq/kg 4-weekly ×6 (symptomatic bone-only, no visceral) | ALSYMPCA: 14.9 vs 11.3 months |
| | **¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA-617** (PSMA-positive, after ARPI + taxane) | VISION: 15.3 vs 11.3 months |
| PARP (poly-ADP-ribose polymerase) inhibitor | Olaparib (HRR genes); rucaparib (BRCA) | PROfound; TRITON3 |
| | Olaparib + abiraterone; niraparib + abiraterone; talazoparib + enzalutamide (first line) | PROpel; MAGNITUDE; TALAPRO-2 |
| Immunotherapy | **Sipuleucel-T** (asymptomatic, no visceral disease) | IMPACT: 25.8 vs 21.7 months |
| | Pembrolizumab for MSI-high/dMMR (mismatch repair-deficient), 2–5% | Tumour-agnostic approval |

- **Abiraterone** blocks CYP17, which also lowers cortisol. ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone) then rises and causes mineralocorticoid excess (hypokalaemia, hypertension, oedema), so prednisone is co-prescribed. Never combine with radium-223 (more deaths and fractures).
- **Enzalutamide:** seizures (~1%), fatigue, falls. Apalutamide: rash, hypothyroidism.
- **PARP inhibitors** cause synthetic lethality in HRR-deficient cells; benefit is greatest with BRCA2 and weak with ATM.

### Sequencing by prior mCSPC therapy
| Prior therapy | Next option |
|---|---|
| Docetaxel only | ARPI |
| ARPI only | Docetaxel, radium-223, sipuleucel-T |
| Both | Cabazitaxel, ¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA, PARP inhibitor if HRR mutation |

### Neuroendocrine/aggressive variant
- Low PSA, liver or brain metastases, lytic bone lesions with hypercalcaemia. Treat with **platinum + etoposide** (or carboplatin + taxane) ± radiotherapy; survival <12 months.

### Supportive and palliative care
- **Bone health:** denosumab 120 mg SC or zoledronic acid 4 mg IV (renal dose adjustment) every 4 weeks, with calcium and vitamin D. Denosumab delayed the first SRE (skeletal-related event) longer (20.7 vs 17.1 months); neither prolongs life. Risk: osteonecrosis of the jaw.
- Focal bone pain: external-beam radiotherapy.
- **Cord compression:** high-dose dexamethasone → urgent MRI → radiotherapy ± decompression (Patchell trial: surgery + radiotherapy better).
- Ureteric obstruction: stent or nephrostomy. Anaemia; hospice care.

### Recent advances
- PSMA-ligand therapy earlier (PSMAfore); AR degraders (ARV-110); AKT inhibition in PTEN loss; bipolar androgen therapy; antibody–drug conjugates.

**Diagram to draw:** clinical states model with drugs placed at each state.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Testosterone <50 ng/dL with PSA or radiological progression; keep ADT going.
> - nmCRPC with PSADT ≤10 months: apalutamide, enzalutamide or darolutamide.
> - mCRPC: ARPI, docetaxel, cabazitaxel, radium-223, ¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA, PARP inhibitors, sipuleucel-T.
> - Switch mechanism; never radium-223 with abiraterone.

## Q2. Classification and treatment of lower track trauma in # pelvis
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

*Assumed meaning: lower urinary tract trauma (bladder and urethra) in pelvic fracture.*

### Introduction
- Pelvic fracture accompanies **83–95% of bladder injuries**, yet only **5–10% of pelvic fractures** injure the bladder. Urethral injury occurs in about **10% of male** and up to 6% of female pelvic fractures.
- 80–94% have other major injuries, which cause the deaths; long-term disability comes from wrong management.

### Mechanism
- **EPR (extraperitoneal rupture):** pelvic disruption tears the bladder at its fascial attachments, or bone spicules lacerate it.
- **IPR (intraperitoneal rupture):** a blow on a **full bladder** bursts the dome, the weakest part covered by peritoneum.
- **PFUI (pelvic fracture urethral injury):** the posterior urethra is fixed by the puboprostatic ligaments, so displacement tears it at the **bulbomembranous junction**. The sphincter is usually avulsed but stays functional. Highest risk: straddle fracture (all four rami), vertically unstable fractures.

### Classification
**Bladder: AAST (American Association for the Surgery of Trauma)**
| Grade | Injury |
|---|---|
| I | Contusion, intramural haematoma, partial-thickness laceration |
| II | Extraperitoneal wall laceration <2 cm |
| III | Extraperitoneal ≥2 cm, or intraperitoneal <2 cm |
| IV | Intraperitoneal ≥2 cm |
| V | Laceration extending into bladder neck or ureteric orifice (trigone) |
- Practical: contusion, EPR (simple or complex), IPR, combined.

**Urethra: Goldman**
| Type | Injury |
|---|---|
| I | Posterior urethra stretched, intact |
| II | Disruption above the urogenital diaphragm |
| III | Disruption of membranous urethra through the urogenital diaphragm (commonest) |
| IV | Bladder neck injury extending into urethra (IVa: bladder-base injury mimicking IV) |
| V | Anterior urethral injury |
- Chapter's practical grading: **partial injury, minor distraction, major distraction** (a cystoscope cannot pass into the bladder). Stricture risk rises with each.

### Clinical features
- Bladder: **gross haematuria (nearly all)**, suprapubic pain, inability to void, free fluid.
- Urethra: **triad: blood at the meatus, inability to void, palpable bladder**. A "high-riding" prostate and butterfly perineal haematoma are unreliable. **Vaginal examination** in every female.

### Investigations
- **RUG (retrograde urethrogram) first if blood at the meatus** (AUA [American Urological Association]). Method: 16 Fr catheter in fossa navicularis, 3 mL balloon, penile traction, oblique position, 20 mL contrast, film during injection. Females: urethroscopy.
- **Cystography:** absolute indication: **gross haematuria + pelvic fracture** (rupture in ~29%). Relative: gross haematuria alone; microscopic haematuria with fracture.
  - **CT cystography:** retrograde fill with 350 mL of 2–4% contrast; no drainage film needed.
  - Plain: scout, full-bladder and **drainage films** (posterior leaks otherwise missed).
  - Clamping the catheter after IV contrast is inadequate.
- **EPR:** flame-shaped pelvic contrast. **IPR:** contrast outlines bowel loops.

### Treatment
**Resuscitation first (ATLS [Advanced Trauma Life Support]); pelvic stabilisation.**

**Bladder**
- **Uncomplicated EPR:** 22 Fr urethral catheter 2–3 weeks; cystogram before removal; repair if leak persists beyond 4 weeks.
- **Immediate repair:** IPR; penetrating injury; bladder neck injury; bone fragments in the bladder; rectal or vaginal injury; poor drainage or clots; laparotomy for other reasons.
- IPR: open or laparoscopic two-layer closure; inspect ureteric orifices; cystogram at 7–10 days. At laparotomy, repair EPR from inside the bladder, leaving the pelvic haematoma undisturbed.
- Suprapubic tube adds nothing over urethral catheter (AUA). Antibiotics only around IPR repair.
- Rectal or vaginal injury: separate the walls, no overlapping suture lines, interpose tissue.

**Urethra: acute**
```
Blood at meatus → RUG
  Partial → one gentle catheter attempt (confirm position)
  Complete → SPC (suprapubic catheter): percutaneous 16 Fr
             or open (if bladder repair needed)
             ± brief primary realignment if stable
             (flexible cystoscopy, antegrade + retrograde)
  Bladder neck/rectal injury → immediate repair
```
- Avoid small "punch" tubes. SPC is safe with pelvic plating (AUA); place it high.
- Realignment: catheter out at 4–6 weeks, keep SPC. It does **not** lower urethroplasty rates; most still form a 1–2 cm stenosis.

**Urethra: delayed (2–3 months)**
- **Up-and-down-o-gram** (cystogram + RUG) for gap length; MRI for complex cases; flexible cystoscopy if bladder neck does not open.
- **Perineal excision and primary anastomosis (EPA)** is the treatment of choice: lithotomy → mobilise bulbar urethra → excise all scar → identify prostatic apex → tension-free spatulated anastomosis.
- Escalating steps for long gaps: **corporal splitting → inferior pubectomy → supracrural rerouting**. Abdominoperineal approach for fistula, bladder neck injury, failed repair, children.
- **Not** "cut-to-the-light" or repeated DVIU (direct visual internal urethrotomy) for gaps ≥1 cm.

### Complications
- ED (erectile dysfunction) up to 82%, mainly from the injury (nerve, arterial, venous).
- Restenosis 5–15% (DVIU usually works); incontinence <4%.
- Missed IPR: ascites, acidosis, azotaemia (peritoneal reabsorption).
- Fistula, abscess, neurogenic voiding dysfunction; follow up ≥1 year.

**Diagram to draw:** pelvis with EPR (flame-shaped) vs IPR, and bulbomembranous distraction defect.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Gross haematuria + pelvic fracture = retrograde CT cystogram (350 mL).
> - EPR: catheter 2–3 weeks; IPR: immediate repair.
> - PFUI: SPC, then perineal anastomotic urethroplasty at 2–3 months.
> - Bladder neck or rectal injury: repair at once.

## Q3a. Pre-emptive renal transplant
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- Kidney transplantation **before starting maintenance dialysis** in a patient with advanced CKD (chronic kidney disease).

### Rationale
- Every year on dialysis adds cardiovascular disease, vascular calcification and bone–mineral damage that the graft later inherits. Pre-emptive transplantation **avoids this exposure**.
- Transplantation beats dialysis: Wolfe (1999) showed early risk is higher, equal at 106 days, then 68% lower long term.

### Advantages
- Better patient and graft survival than transplantation after dialysis; longer dialysis time worsens outcome (registry data).
- No dialysis access surgery, access infection or catheter complications.
- Fewer transfusions, so less HLA (human leucocyte antigen) sensitisation.
- Better quality of life, employment; lower cost.
- Children: better growth and development.

### Frequency
- 28.7% of US transplants in 2021 (living donor 51%, deceased donor 21%, children 56%). Commonest with a **living donor**.

### Timing and evaluation
- Evaluate early, ideally before dialysis. KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) 2020: refer when eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) is <30 mL/min/1.73 m², 6–12 months before expected dialysis. Living-donor transplant is usually done at eGFR <20 with progressive decline.
- Deceased-donor listing (US) at eGFR ≤20.
- Full recipient work-up: cardiac, vascular (iliac calcification), infection (TB [tuberculosis], hepatitis B and C, HIV [human immunodeficiency virus]), malignancy, psychosocial; recurrence risk (primary FSGS [focal segmental glomerulosclerosis]).
- **Urological:** bladder function and lower tract (valves, neurogenic bladder); native nephrectomy only for specific indications.
- India: mainly living related donors under THOA (Transplantation of Human Organs Act).

### Limitations
- Needs early referral and a ready donor.
- Transplant may come earlier than needed, using up dialysis-free native function; adherence untested.
- In advanced CKD, bowel augmentation may speed decline.

**Also know:** ABO-incompatible or paired exchange programmes widen pre-emptive options.

**Diagram to draw:** Wolfe curve: relative risk of death after transplant vs dialysis over time.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Transplant before dialysis = best outcome.
> - Refer at eGFR <30; transplant usually at eGFR <20.
> - Commonest with living donors and children.

## Q3b. What are the causes of bleeding in PCNL? How will you manage persistent hematuria 6 days after PCNL!?
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(PCNL complications also in Practice Set 2 Paper 2, Q3e)

### Introduction
- Bleeding is the commonest serious complication of PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy). Transfusion was historically ~7%, now nearer 1% with smaller tracts. Angioembolisation is needed in <1%.

### Causes
| Timing | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Intraoperative** | Parenchymal or venous tract bleeding; infundibular artery injury; through-and-through puncture; posterior segmental artery injury (upper-pole access); torn infundibulum or pelvis |
| **Early postoperative** | Tract bleeding after sheath removal; clot retention |
| **Delayed (about 1 week)** | **Pseudoaneurysm**, **AV (arteriovenous) fistula** |
- **Risk factors:** infundibular or upper-pole puncture, multiple or large tracts, staghorn stone, long operation, antithrombotic drugs, coagulopathy, diabetes.
- **Prevention:** puncture through the **calyceal fornix** along a posterior calyx (Sampaio: no arterial injury); correct clotting; mini-tracts; tranexamic acid reduces transfusion (Cochrane).

### Mechanism of delayed bleeding
- **Pseudoaneurysm:** clot seals an arterial tear, then ruptures intermittently.
- **AV fistula:** artery and vein heal together; the thin vein cannot withstand arterial pressure.

### Management at day 6
- At day 6, assume an **arterial lesion** until proved otherwise.
```
Resuscitate: IV access, crossmatch, transfuse; Hb, clotting
→ Stop anticoagulants; catheter, irrigate clots
→ Stable, mild: bed rest, clamp nephrostomy, serial Hb
→ Persistent or recurrent bleeding or Hb fall
   → CT angiography (arterial blush) or direct angiography
→ SUPERSELECTIVE ANGIOEMBOLISATION (coils)
→ Fails or unstable after embolisation
   → open exploration, partial or total nephrectomy
```
- **Angiography** diagnoses >90% and treats in the same sitting. It may miss intermittent bleeds; non-selective embolisation loses renal tissue.
- Clot colic or obstruction: double-J stent or nephrostomy.
- Follow-up: Hb and creatinine; check for infection and abscess.

### Intraoperative ladder (for completeness)
- Pressure on tract → nephrostomy clamped (tamponade) → balloon reinflation or **Kaye tamponade catheter** → angioembolisation.

**Diagram to draw:** angiogram sketch: pseudoaneurysm before and after coiling.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Early bleeding venous: tamponade.
> - Bleeding at about 1 week arterial: pseudoaneurysm or AV fistula.
> - Treatment: superselective angioembolisation.

## Q3c. Partial nephrectomy – indications and approaches.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(nephron-sparing methods also in 2022-Summer Paper 3, Q1)

### Definition
- PN (partial nephrectomy): complete removal of a tumour with maximal preservation of functioning parenchyma. Equal cancer control to RN (radical nephrectomy) with less CKD (chronic kidney disease) and cardiovascular risk.

### Indications
| Category | Situations |
|---|---|
| **Imperative** | Solitary kidney; bilateral tumours; RN would cause dialysis |
| **Relative** | Contralateral kidney threatened (stones, renal artery stenosis, diabetes, hypertension); hereditary RCC (renal cell carcinoma) |
| **Elective** | **cT1a with normal contralateral kidney: standard of care**; cT1b–T2 when feasible |
| **Benign** | Symptomatic AML (angiomyolipoma); focal atrophy in duplex kidney; infected calyceal diverticulum |
- **Relative contraindications:** <20% nephron mass left; pedicle encasement; venous thrombus; cT4; nodes.
- Planning: **R.E.N.A.L. nephrometry score** (Radius, Exophytic, Nearness to collecting system, Anterior/posterior, Location) predicts complexity; 3D models help.

### Approaches
| Approach | Features |
|---|---|
| **Open, extraperitoneal flank** (supracostal 11th/12th rib, 8 cm mini-flank) | Posterior exposure; solitary kidney, complex or central tumours, cold ischaemia |
| **Open transperitoneal** (subcostal, chevron, midline) | Bilateral or large tumours, vascular reconstruction |
| **Laparoscopic transperitoneal** | Large space, easier suturing; anterior tumours |
| **Laparoscopic retroperitoneal** | No bowel mobilisation, direct hilum; posterior tumours |
| **Robot-assisted (RAPN)** | Easier suturing, shorter warm ischaemia, fewer conversions |

### Key technical steps
1. Early hilar control; **intraoperative ultrasound** for borders and depth.
2. Vascular control: artery ± vein clamp; off-clamp or selective clamping for small tumours; ice slush if cold ischaemia needed.
3. Excision: rim of normal tissue, or **enucleation** (hereditary, multifocal). Margin width is unimportant if negative.
4. Renorrhaphy: inner layer (vessels, collecting system) + capsular layer (sliding-clip); drain.
- **Trifecta:** negative margin, no major complication, warm ischaemia ≤25 minutes.
- Function depends mainly on **parenchyma preserved**, less on clamp time.

### Complications
- Urine leak (drain → stent); bleeding, **pseudoaneurysm or AV (arteriovenous) fistula** (angioembolisation); AKI (acute kidney injury); positive margin; local recurrence 1–2% elective, 3–5% imperative.

**Diagram to draw:** tumour excised with rim and two-layer renorrhaphy.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Imperative, relative, elective; PN standard for cT1a.
> - Retroperitoneal for posterior, transperitoneal for anterior tumours.
> - Trifecta: negative margin, no major complication, warm ischaemia ≤25 min.

## Q3d. Adrenal Sx.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(preoperative phaeochromocytoma management also in 2020-Summer Paper 2, Q3c)

*Assumed meaning: surgery of the adrenal gland (adrenalectomy).*

### Surgical anatomy
- Right: pyramidal; **short vein into posterior IVC (inferior vena cava)**, so it is the riskier side. Left: crescentic; long vein into left renal vein. Three arteries (inferior phrenic, aorta, renal), one vein.

### Indications (AAES [American Association of Endocrine Surgeons] 2022)
- **Functional, any size:** aldosteronoma (Conn; after AVS [adrenal venous sampling]), cortisol-secreting adenoma, phaeochromocytoma, sex-hormone tumour, failed pituitary surgery for Cushing disease.
- **Malignant potential:** ≥4 cm; growth; >10 HU (Hounsfield units) unenhanced; washout <50%; suspected ACC (adrenocortical carcinoma); solitary metastasis.
- Myelolipoma >7 cm or symptomatic; cysts.
- **Contraindications (correct first):** uncontrolled hypertension, coagulopathy, peritonitis (transperitoneal); pregnancy relative.

### Preoperative preparation
- Biochemistry: metanephrines in **every** case before surgery.
- **Phaeochromocytoma:** α-blockade 2 weeks (phenoxybenzamine or doxazosin), β-blocker only after α, salt and fluids.
- Cushing: hydrocortisone 100 mg IV on the day of surgery. Conn: correct potassium.

### Approaches
| Approach | Use |
|---|---|
| **Laparoscopic transperitoneal** (gold standard since 1992) | Familiar view, larger tumours |
| **Retroperitoneoscopic** (lateral or prone) | Prior abdominal surgery, small tumours |
| Robotic | Either route |
| **Open** (subcostal, chevron, thoracoabdominal, flank) | **ACC**, large or invasive tumours |

### Steps (laparoscopic transperitoneal)
- **Right:** elevate liver → Kocher manoeuvre → follow IVC → **control adrenal vein early** (warn anaesthetist) → free gland to psoas → bag intact.
- **Left:** reflect colon and spleen → follow left renal vein to adrenal vein → clip → free from pancreas and splenic vessels.
- Kidney attachment last (handle). ACC: open en bloc, no rupture.
- **Partial adrenalectomy:** bilateral or hereditary phaeochromocytoma, solitary gland.

### Complications
- Bleeding (adrenal vein avulsion, IVC); spleen, pancreatic tail, liver, bowel injury; pneumothorax.
- **Phaeochromocytoma:** surges during handling, **hypotension after vein ligation**, rebound hypoglycaemia.
- **Adrenal insufficiency** after Cushing surgery (~99.7%): steroid taper.
- Conn: **hyperkalaemia** for weeks (high-salt diet).

**Diagram to draw:** right and left adrenal veins and relations.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Function (any size) or malignancy risk (≥4 cm, >10 HU) indicates surgery.
> - Control the vein early; right vein short into IVC.
> - Open en bloc surgery for ACC.

## Q3e. Malakoplakia
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- "Soft plaque": a rare chronic granulomatous inflammation caused by **defective macrophage killing of bacteria**, mostly *E. coli*. Described by Michaelis and Gutmann (1902) and von Hansemann (1903).
- Commonest in the **bladder**; also ureter, kidney, prostate, testis, GI (gastrointestinal) tract, skin, lung.

### Pathogenesis
- Macrophages ingest bacteria but cannot digest them (lysosomal defect, low intracellular cGMP [cyclic guanosine monophosphate]).
- Bacterial remnants become mineralised with calcium phosphate, forming laminated **Michaelis–Gutmann bodies**.
- Hosts are often immunocompromised, diabetic, chronically ill or **renal transplant recipients**.

### Pathology
- Gross: soft yellow-brown plaques or nodules; renal tumour-like masses.
- Micro: sheets of large foamy histiocytes (**von Hansemann cells**) with **Michaelis–Gutmann bodies** (pathognomonic; "owl's eye" targetoid; stain with von Kossa, PAS [periodic acid–Schiff] and Prussian blue).
- MG bodies may be absent early; **α1-antitrypsin** immunostaining helps.

### Clinical features
- Mostly >50 years; urinary tract **female:male 4:1**.
- Bladder: irritative LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms), haematuria, recurrent *E. coli* UTI (urinary tract infection); plaques or masses mimicking cancer; distal ureteric stricture.
- Kidney: masses, fever, flank pain; renal vein or IVC thrombosis. Testis: epididymo-orchitis. Prostate: mimics carcinoma.

### Investigations
- Urine culture (*E. coli*). Cystoscopy and **biopsy is diagnostic**.
- Imaging: enlarged kidneys with filling defects, **no stones, calcification or hydronephrosis** (unlike XGP).

| | XGP (xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis) | Malakoplakia |
|---|---|---|
| Cell | Lipid-laden foamy macrophages | Von Hansemann cells with MG bodies |
| Stone/obstruction | Usually (staghorn) | Absent |
| Laterality | Unilateral | Uni- or multifocal, bilateral |
| Treatment | Nephrectomy | Antibiotics; nephrectomy if unilateral, symptomatic |

### Management
- **Long-term antibiotics active inside macrophages:** fluoroquinolones (concentrate in macrophages), trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, rifampicin, doxycycline.
- **Bethanechol + ascorbic acid** raise intracellular cGMP and correct the degradative defect.
- Reduce immunosuppression where possible.
- Surgery if progression despite medical therapy: TUR (transurethral resection) of bladder lesions; **nephrectomy** for symptomatic unilateral renal disease.

### Prognosis
- Bilateral or transplant-kidney disease: death often within 6 months; overall mortality can exceed 50%. Unilateral: long survival after nephrectomy.

**Diagram to draw:** histiocyte with Michaelis–Gutmann bodies.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Defective macrophage digestion of *E. coli*.
> - Michaelis–Gutmann bodies are pathognomonic.
> - Fluoroquinolones + bethanechol/vitamin C; nephrectomy for unilateral renal disease.

## Q3f. Medical treatment of BPH
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2015-Summer Paper 2, Q1)

### Rationale
- BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) obstruction has a **dynamic** part (α1A-mediated smooth-muscle tone) and a **static** part (DHT [dihydrotestosterone]-driven gland bulk). Drugs target each.

### Before treatment
- IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score), DRE (digital rectal examination), urinalysis, PSA (prostate-specific antigen), PVR (post-void residual); PVR >300 mL = chronic retention.
- **Watchful waiting** with lifestyle advice for mild or non-bothersome LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms).

### Drugs
| Class | Doses | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **α1-blockers** (first line) | Tamsulosin 0.4 mg; alfuzosin 10 mg; silodosin 8 mg; doxazosin 4–8 mg (titrate) | Act in days; do not prevent AUR (acute urinary retention) or surgery. Hypotension, ejaculatory dysfunction (silodosin most), **IFIS (intraoperative floppy iris syndrome)** |
| **5-ARIs (5α-reductase inhibitors)** | Finasteride 5 mg; dutasteride 0.5 mg | Prostate >30–40 mL. Shrink gland ~20%, halve PSA, act after 6 months. PLESS: AUR or surgery ↓51%. Low libido, ED (erectile dysfunction). Also BPH haematuria |
| **Tadalafil 5 mg daily** | PDE5 (phosphodiesterase-5) inhibitor | LUTS ± ED; not with nitrates |
| **Antimuscarinic or β3-agonist** (mirabegron, vibegron) | Add-on | Persisting storage symptoms; PVR <150 mL |
| **Desmopressin** | — | Nocturnal polyuria; monitor sodium |

### Combination therapy
- **MTOPS (Medical Therapy of Prostatic Symptoms):** progression ↓39% doxazosin, 34% finasteride, **66% combination**. Only finasteride-containing arms reduced AUR and surgery.
- **CombAT (Combination of Avodart and Tamsulosin):** combination reduced AUR or surgery by ~66% vs tamsulosin.
- Combine when **PV (prostate volume) >30–40 mL and PSA >1.5 ng/mL**; α-blocker may stop after 6 months (SMART).
- Tadalafil + finasteride fixed dose approved (FDA [Food and Drug Administration] 2021).

### Important cautions
- **IFIS:** billowing iris, prolapse, miosis; stopping the drug does not prevent it.
- 5-ARIs: double the PSA; not for cancer prevention (more high-grade tumours in PCPT [Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial] and REDUCE).
- **AUR:** catheter, α-blocker, then TWOC (trial without catheter).
