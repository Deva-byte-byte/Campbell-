## Q1. Describe Pathogenesis of Congenital Pelvi Ureteric Junction Obstruction its diagnosis and Management.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(related: 2021-Summer Paper 1, Q3e; 2016-Summer Paper 3, Q2)

### Definition
- PUJO (pelviureteric junction obstruction) is impaired flow of urine from the pelvis into the ureter. **Koff:** obstruction is "any restriction to urinary outflow that, if left untreated, will lead to progressive kidney damage". **Dilatation is not obstruction.**
- About 1 in 1500 live births; commonest cause of antenatal hydronephrosis. Male and left predominance; **bilateral in 10–40%**.

### Pathogenesis

| Type | Lesion | Typical patient |
|---|---|---|
| **Intrinsic** (commonest) | Short adynamic segment: interrupted circular muscle, excess collagen, fewer pacemaker (interstitial Cajal-like) cells. The peristaltic wave cannot cross, so the pelvis empties poorly | Neonate or infant, antenatal finding |
| **Extrinsic** | **Lower-pole crossing vessel** anterior to the PUJ; the ureter kinks over it | Older child or adult with intermittent pain |
| Secondary | Kinking by a tortuous ureter in high-grade VUR (vesicoureteral reflux); fibroepithelial polyp; high insertion | Any age |

- **Crossing vessels** are persistent embryonic renal arteries. They occur at a fifth to a quarter of **normal** PUJs, so a vessel alone does not prove cause.
- **Also know:** proposed incomplete ureteric recanalisation; horseshoe kidney and duplex lower moiety associations.

### Effects of obstruction (pathophysiology)
- Pelvic pressure rises; filtration and perfusion fall.
- **Immature kidney:** apoptosis and impaired nephrogenesis. **Mature kidney:** hypertrophy, then fibrosis (TGF-β [transforming growth factor-beta] signalling). Fibrosis, not dilatation, causes loss of function.
- **Also know:** concentrating defect, salt wasting; rarely renin-mediated hypertension.

### Clinical features
- **Antenatal ultrasound** (commonest); infants: UTI (urinary tract infection), flank mass, failure to thrive.
- Older children and adults: **intermittent loin pain with vomiting (Dietl crisis)**, haematuria after minor trauma, stones, infection.
- **Associated anomalies (up to 50%):** contralateral PUJO, dysplasia or MCDK (multicystic dysplastic kidney), VUR (up to 40%).

### Diagnosis
**1. Antenatal grading (APD, anteroposterior renal pelvic diameter)**

| | Second trimester | Third trimester |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | 4 to <7 mm | 4 to <9 mm |
| Moderate | 7–10 mm | 9–15 mm |
| Severe | >10 mm | >15 mm |
- **UTD (urinary tract dilation) 2014 consensus:** six parameters (APD, central/peripheral calyces, parenchymal thickness and appearance, ureter, bladder). Prenatal A1 (low) or A2–3; postnatal **P1** (APD 10–<15 mm, central calyces), **P2** (≥15 mm or peripheral calyces or ureter), **P3** (abnormal parenchyma).
- **SFU (Society for Fetal Urology) grades 0–4:** 1 pelvis just seen; 2 pelvis plus major calyces; 3 all calyces, normal parenchyma; **4 parenchymal thinning**.

**2. Postnatal ultrasound** after **48 hours** (neonatal dehydration under-reads dilatation): large pelvis, rounded calyces, ureter not seen.

**3. MAG3 (technetium-99m mercaptoacetyltriglycine) diuretic renogram** ("well-tempered": hydrated, catheterised, furosemide)
- **DRF (differential renal function)** (normal 45–55%); drainage half-time <10 min normal, 10–20 equivocal, **>20 min obstructed** (adult chapter: >40 min).

**4. Others:** VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) by UTI risk; MRU (magnetic resonance urography) or CT (computed tomography) angiography for vessels; **Whitaker test** (10 mL/min; >15–20 cm H₂O obstructed) if equivocal.

### Management
**Conservative:** most resolve, about **one-third need surgery**; serial ultrasound and renogram.

**Indications for pyeloplasty**
- **DRF <40%** or a **fall of >10%** on serial scans.
- **Increasing APD** or SFU 4 / P3 with poor drainage.
- **Breakthrough UTI**, symptoms, stones, hypertension.
- Bilateral or solitary kidney.

**Anderson–Hynes dismembered pyeloplasty: gold standard (~95% success)**
1. Expose the PUJ; free the lower pole and look for a crossing vessel.
2. Stay sutures; transect the pelvis; reduce a redundant pelvis.
3. **Spatulate the ureter laterally** into healthy ureter; keep the narrow segment as a "no-touch" handle.
4. **Transpose** the PUJ anterior to any vessel (never divide it).
5. Watertight, tension-free, dependent anastomosis (5-0/6-0 absorbable); excise the segment.
6. Double-J or externalised stent (1–4 weeks equivalent); Foley 24–48 h with a double-J.

- **Route:** open preferred in infants; laparoscopic equals open with less pain; **robotic** (easier suturing, shorter learning curve) is now primary at most major centres.

- **Alternatives:** Foley Y-V plasty (high insertion); Culp spiral or vertical flap (long segment); **vascular hitch** (crossing vessel with normal PUJ); **endopyelotomy** (73% overall; **42% with vs 86% without a crossing vessel**), mainly for secondary PUJO.
- **Nephrectomy:** DRF **<15% in adults** (children: no agreed cut-off).

### Complications and follow-up
- Urine leak, stent problems, UTI; early failure from oedema or clot (temporary nephrostomy).
- **Late failure:** redo pyeloplasty (best), endopyelotomy or ureterocalicostomy. Renogram and ultrasound after stent removal.

### Recent advances
- Robotic redo with buccal graft and ICG (indocyanine green) fluorescence; pyramidal thickness ≤3 mm predicts surgery; urinary biomarkers.

**Diagram to draw:** Anderson–Hynes steps: transection, lateral spatulation, transposition anterior to the crossing vessel, dependent anastomosis.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Intrinsic adynamic segment (infants) vs crossing vessel (older children, intermittent pain).
> - SFU 4 = parenchymal thinning; UTD P3 = abnormal parenchyma.
> - Operate for DRF <40%, fall >10%, rising APD, UTI or symptoms.
> - Anderson–Hynes ~95%; never divide a crossing vessel.

## Q2. Nepron Sparing Surgery in renal malignancy.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2022-Summer Paper 3, Q1; 2017-Summer Paper 1, Q1)

*"Nepron" is read as nephron.*

### Definition and rationale
- NSS (nephron-sparing surgery) is complete local removal of the tumour with **maximal preservation of functioning parenchyma**. PN (partial nephrectomy) is the standard form; thermal ablation is the non-excisional form.
- **Why:** RN (radical nephrectomy) causes CKD (chronic kidney disease), linked to cardiovascular events and death. PN gives equal cancer control for small masses and keeps about **90%** of global function (RN 65–70%).

### Indications

| Category | Situations |
|---|---|
| **Imperative** | Bilateral RCC (renal cell carcinoma); solitary functioning kidney; RN would mean dialysis |
| **Relative** | Contralateral kidney threatened: stones, renal artery stenosis, hydronephrosis, diabetes, severe hypertension; hereditary RCC |
| **Elective** | **cT1a (≤4 cm)** with a normal contralateral kidney: **standard of care** |

- Also used for selected cT1b–T2, complex or hilar tumours when function matters.
- **AUA (American Urological Association) 2021:** RN is preferred only if **all three** apply: high complexity; no CKD or proteinuria; normal contralateral kidney with new eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) likely **>45** after RN. Otherwise PN.
- **Relative contraindications:** **<20% of nephron mass retained**; pedicle encasement; collecting-system invasion; **venous tumour thrombus**; cT4; cN1.

### Preoperative evaluation
- Multiphase CT (computed tomography) or MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) with 3D (three-dimensional) reconstruction; chest imaging; eGFR and proteinuria.
- **R.E.N.A.L. nephrometry** (Radius, Exophytic/endophytic, Nearness to collecting system, Anterior/posterior, Location to polar lines): **4–6 low, 7–9 moderate, 10–12 high** complexity. PADUA and C-index are alternatives.
- Biopsy when it changes management; genetic counselling if age ≤46, multifocal or bilateral.

### Techniques of PN
**Approach:** open (flank extraperitoneal or transperitoneal), laparoscopic, or **RAPN (robot-assisted PN)**: transperitoneal for anterior and retroperitoneal for posterior tumours.

**Steps**
1. **Early hilar control** of artery, vein and accessory vessels, even if off-clamp is planned.
2. Defat the kidney; **intraoperative ultrasound** maps borders and depth.
3. Clamp the artery (± vein). Use **ice slush** if ischaemia is expected to be **≥25–30 minutes** (solitary kidney, CKD).
4. Excise with a thin rim of normal parenchyma. **A negative margin matters; its width does not.**
5. **Two-layer renorrhaphy:** inner layer closes vessels and collecting system; outer capsular layer over a haemostatic bolster.
6. Unclamp; check haemostasis; close Gerota fascia; drain.

**Excision options**

| | Standard (wedge) resection | Enucleation |
|---|---|---|
| Plane | Through normal parenchyma, thin rim | Along the pseudocapsule |
| Advantage | Fewer positive margins | Less bleeding, maximal parenchyma |
| Use | Default | Hereditary RCC (VHL [von Hippel–Lindau]), multifocal, severe CKD |
- Polar resection or heminephrectomy for large polar tumours.

**Vascular control (ischaemia)**
- Traditional warm ischaemia **<25–30 minutes**. Function depends mainly on the **quantity of parenchyma preserved**; ischaemia is secondary within accepted limits.
- **Off-clamp, selective or early unclamping:** randomised trials show no long-term eGFR advantage. Hypothermia and mannitol give no benefit with a normal contralateral kidney.

### Ablative NSS
- **Cryoablation** (double freeze–thaw), **RFA (radiofrequency ablation)**, **MWA (microwave ablation)**; mostly percutaneous.
- AUA: alternative for **cT1a <3 cm**, especially elderly or comorbid; biopsy before or at ablation. Recurrence is higher than PN but treatable by repeat ablation.
- **Also know:** SBRT (stereotactic body radiotherapy) for non-surgical candidates; active surveillance for frail patients.

### Special situations
- **Hereditary RCC:** observe until the largest tumour is **3 cm**, then enucleate (except HLRCC [hereditary leiomyomatosis and RCC]: wide excision).
- **Bilateral tumours:** bilateral PN, usually staged; if one side needs RN, do the PN first.
- **Solitary kidney:** temporary dialysis about 3.5%; local recurrence 3–5%.
- **Also know:** neoadjuvant TKI (tyrosine kinase inhibitor) downsizing in imperative cases; bilateral Wilms tumour; kidney-sparing upper-tract urothelial cancer surgery.

### Outcomes and evidence
- **Trifecta:** negative margin + no major complication + warm ischaemia ≤25 minutes. RAPN: shorter ischaemia than laparoscopy, less blood loss than open.
- Elective PN: **local recurrence 1–2%**, cancer-free survival >90%.
- **EORTC (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer) 30904 (2011):** PN vs RN for ≤5 cm: better function and similar oncology with PN; 10-year overall survival 81% RN vs 76% PN, but the trial is flawed.

### Complications
- **Urine leak** (drain, stent); **haemorrhage, pseudoaneurysm or AV (arteriovenous) fistula** (delayed haematuria: angioembolisation); acute kidney injury.
- **Positive margin 0–7%:** surveillance, not reoperation.
- **Hyperfiltration injury** if >50% of a solitary kidney is removed: proteinuria, FSGS (focal segmental glomerulosclerosis); ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitor.
- **Follow-up:** AUA risk-based imaging; eGFR and urine protein at every visit.

### Recent advances
- 3D models (randomised: shorter ischaemia, less blood loss); ICG (indocyanine green) fluorescence for selective clamping; single-port RAPN.

**Diagram to draw:** clamped renal artery, tumour excised with a rim, two-layer renorrhaphy.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Imperative, relative, elective; PN is standard for cT1a.
> - AUA: RN only if complex + no CKD + eGFR likely >45.
> - Preserved parenchyma, not clamp time, governs function.
> - EORTC 30904: better function, similar cancer control.

## Q3a. Extended Criteria for Selection of Live Renal Donor
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(donor assessment also answered in 2022-Summer Paper 3, Q3a; marginal donor in 2024-Winter Paper 4, Q3e)

*Read as the expanded (marginal, "medically complex") living donor.*

### Definition and principle
- A living donor with a risk factor that would once have excluded donation; used because of organ shortage.
- **KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) 2017** replaced fixed cut-offs with a **risk-based approach**: the donor's estimated lifetime ESKD (end-stage kidney disease) risk is compared with the centre's acceptable threshold. Donor safety comes first.
- Counsel with figures: donor lifetime ESKD 90 vs 14 per 10,000 matched non-donors (Muzaale 2014); mortality 3 per 10,000.

### Extended criteria

| Factor | Standard | Extended (individualised) | Exclude |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Age** | Adult | Older donors (often >60) acceptable; no upper limit | Under legal age |
| **GFR (glomerular filtration rate)**, mL/min/1.73 m² | ≥90 | **60–89** | <60 |
| **Albuminuria**, mg/day | <30 | **30–100** | >100 |
| **Hypertension** | None | Controlled on 1–2 drugs, no target-organ damage | Uncontrolled, organ damage |
| **Obesity** | BMI (body mass index) <30 | **30–35** | Many centres: >35 |
| **Glucose** | Normal | Prediabetes; selected type 2 diabetes | Type 1 diabetes |
| **Stones** | None | Single past stone, low recurrence risk; small stone removed ex vivo | Bilateral or recurrent; cystinuria, primary hyperoxaluria |
| **Anatomy** | Single vessels | Multiple arteries, simple cyst, mild FMD (fibromuscular dysplasia) | Major anomaly |

- **Why older donors are acceptable:** their remaining lifespan is short, so lifetime ESKD risk is low. Young donors carry the highest lifetime risk.
- **Also know:** isolated microscopic haematuria after negative work-up; treated low-risk cancer; APOL1 (apolipoprotein L1) high-risk genotype; future pregnancy (pre-eclampsia risk).

### Safeguards
- Measured GFR and split function; **the better kidney stays with the donor**.
- CT (computed tomography) angiography; laparoscopic retrieval.
- India: **THOA (Transplantation of Human Organs Act)** authorisation; independent donor advocate.
- **Outcome:** grafts from older living donors do about as well as standard deceased-donor kidneys.

**Diagram to draw:** risk-threshold scale (accept, individualise, decline).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - KDIGO 2017: risk-based lifetime ESKD threshold.
> - GFR 60–89 and albuminuria 30–100 are individualised.
> - Controlled hypertension on 1–2 drugs may donate; type 1 diabetes cannot.

## Q3b. Metabolic Evaluation in Stone Disease
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2015-Summer Paper 2, Q2)

### Rationale
- Stones are a chronic metabolic disease: about **50% recur within 10 years**. Evaluation finds a treatable cause and directs targeted therapy, which reduces stone events.

### Step 1: Screening evaluation (every first-time former; AUA [American Urological Association], EAU [European Association of Urology])

| Component | What is assessed |
|---|---|
| History | GI (gastrointestinal) disease, gout, sarcoidosis, recurrent UTI (urinary tract infection), family history; diet; drugs (calcium, vitamins C and D, acetazolamide, steroids) |
| Blood | Creatinine, sodium, potassium, calcium, uric acid, bicarbonate; PTH (parathyroid hormone) if calcium is high |
| Urine | pH, crystals (hexagonal = cystine), culture |
| **Stone analysis** | Infrared spectroscopy or X-ray diffraction; uric acid, cystine and struvite point to a cause |
| Imaging | NCCT (non-contrast computed tomography): burden |

### Step 2: Extended evaluation (high-risk or recurrent formers)
- **High risk:** children, recurrent stones, solitary kidney, GI disease or bariatric surgery, hyperparathyroidism, RTA (renal tubular acidosis), metabolic syndrome, gout, family history.
- **One or two 24-hour urines** on a usual diet, ≥4 weeks after an episode, stone- and infection-free; creatinine confirms completeness.

| Parameter | Target per day | Abnormality |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | **>2.5 L** | Low volume (commonest) |
| Calcium | <4 mg/kg (about 250 mg women, 300 mg men) | Hypercalciuria |
| Oxalate | **<40 mg** | Dietary, enteric, primary |
| Citrate | **>320 mg** | Hypocitraturia (RTA, diarrhoea) |
| Uric acid | <600 mg | Hyperuricosuria |
| pH | 5.8–6.2 | <5.5 uric acid; >6.5 calcium phosphate or infection |
| Sodium | <100 mmol | Raises urine calcium |
| Cystine | Negative screen | Cystinuria |

- **Special tests:** ammonium chloride load (urine pH stays >5.5 in distal RTA); quantitative cystine.
- Fast and calcium-load subtyping of hypercalciuria is no longer advised (does not change treatment).

### Follow-up
- Repeat 24-hour urine **within 6 months** of starting therapy, then yearly; blood tests for drug side effects.
- Pregnancy: defer 24-hour urine.

**Diagram to draw:** flowchart: first stone → screening → low risk (general advice) or high risk (24-hour urine → targeted therapy).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Screening for all first-time formers; 24-hour urine for high-risk.
> - Targets: volume >2.5 L, oxalate <40 mg, citrate >320 mg.
> - Repeat 24-hour urine within 6 months of therapy.

## Q3c. Hematospermia
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- Blood in the ejaculate. It alarms patients but is usually **benign and self-limiting**, most often from non-specific prostatic or seminal vesicle inflammation.
- **Primary (idiopathic):** no cause found, often young men. **Secondary:** a cause is identified.
- **Pseudo-haematospermia:** blood from the partner (condom test) or haematuria.

### Causes

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Iatrogenic (commonest identifiable)** | Prostate biopsy (10–50%, lasts about a month), TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate), radiotherapy, BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) procedures |
| Inflammation and infection | Prostatitis, vesiculitis, urethritis, STI (sexually transmitted infection), **genitourinary tuberculosis**, schistosomiasis |
| Obstruction and cysts | Ejaculatory duct obstruction or calculi; seminal vesicle cyst (Zinner syndrome) or utricle cyst |
| Tumours | **Prostate cancer**; rarely urethral, bladder or seminal vesicle |
| Vascular | Prostatic varices, BPH vessels, AV (arteriovenous) malformation |
| Systemic | Hypertension, bleeding disorders, anticoagulants, liver disease; also abstinence, trauma |

### Evaluation
- **History:** episodes, recent biopsy, LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms), haematuria, TB (tuberculosis) contact, anticoagulants.
- **Examination:** blood pressure, genitalia, **DRE (digital rectal examination)**.
- **Basic tests:** urinalysis and culture, semen culture, STI screen, **PSA (prostate-specific antigen)** in men over 40, coagulation if indicated.

**Who needs full work-up:** age **≥40**, persistent beyond several weeks or recurrent, haematuria, abnormal DRE or PSA, risk factors for TB or cancer, or a distressed patient.
- **TRUS (transrectal ultrasound):** cysts, calculi, dilated seminal vesicles (>1.5 cm) or ejaculatory ducts (>2.3 mm).
- **MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) pelvis:** best for seminal vesicle haemorrhage, cysts and prostate cancer.
- **Cystoscopy and urine cytology**; early-morning urine for acid-fast bacilli.

### Management
- **Young, single episode, normal tests:** reassure.
- **Treat the cause:** antibiotics or antitubercular drugs; control BP (blood pressure); correct coagulopathy.
- **Persistent prostatic bleeding:** 5-ARI (5-alpha-reductase inhibitor), e.g. finasteride.
- **Refractory or obstructive:** TURED (transurethral resection of ejaculatory ducts), cyst unroofing or aspiration, **seminal vesiculoscopy** (4–6 Fr scope through the utricle, irrigation, laser for stones).

**Diagram to draw:** algorithm: <40 and single episode → reassure; ≥40 or persistent → PSA, TRUS/MRI, cystoscopy.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Usually benign; prostate biopsy is the commonest identifiable cause.
> - Full work-up if ≥40, persistent or recurrent, or associated haematuria.
> - Exclude prostate cancer and genitourinary tuberculosis.

## Q3d. Vascular Complications after Renal Transplantation in Recipient
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- Uncommon but a major cause of **early graft loss**. Doppler ultrasound is the first test.
- **Concept:** no inflow gives anuria; blocked outflow gives a swollen, tender graft with haematuria; reduced inflow gives hypertension and fluid retention.

### Main complications

| | Arterial thrombosis | Venous thrombosis | TRAS (transplant renal artery stenosis) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incidence | 0.2–5% | 0.1–8.2% | 1–23% (commonest) |
| Timing | First days | First week | Months (usually 3 months–2 years) |
| Presentation | Sudden anuria | Painful swollen graft, haematuria, oliguria | Refractory hypertension, rising creatinine, flash pulmonary oedema |
| Doppler | No arterial flow | **Reversed diastolic flow**, clot in vein | High velocity at stenosis; **parvus-tardus** waveform distally |
| Treatment | Immediate exploration; thrombectomy (catheter thrombolysis described); nephrectomy if non-viable | Immediate exploration, thrombectomy; nephrectomy if necrotic | **Angioplasty or stenting** (first choice); surgical revision if that fails |

- **Thrombosis risks:** extreme donor or recipient age, **cold ischaemia >24 hours**, multiple arteries, technical error (kink, intimal flap), hypovolaemia, rejection, thrombophilia (factor V Leiden, antiphospholipid antibodies).
- **TRAS causes:** anastomotic technique, clamp injury, recipient atherosclerosis (iliac inflow stenosis mimics it). Contrast angiography confirms.

### Other vascular complications
- **Haemorrhage:** hilar vessels or anastomosis; re-explore if unstable.
- **Pseudoaneurysm (<1%):** technical or **mycotic**; rupture risk; endovascular or open repair, often graft nephrectomy, prolonged antibiotics.
- **Post-biopsy AV (arteriovenous) fistula:** persistent haematuria; usually closes; large or with steal: **coil embolisation**. Subcapsular haematoma can cause a **Page kidney**.
- **Graft torsion:** intraperitoneal grafts; prevent with nephropexy.
- **DVT (deep vein thrombosis)** of the iliac vein; **lymphocele** compressing the iliac or renal vein.
- **Limb ischaemia** from iliac dissection; internal iliac use: gluteal claudication, erectile dysfunction.

### Prevention
- Anastomose away from atheroma; check the lie for kinking.
- Extend a short right renal vein with donor IVC (inferior vena cava).
- Short cold ischaemia; anticoagulate thrombophilic patients; avoid hypovolaemia.
- At reperfusion, a soft kidney suggests poor inflow; a tense, engorged kidney suggests outflow block.

**Diagram to draw:** Doppler waveforms: normal, parvus-tardus (TRAS) and reversed diastolic flow (venous thrombosis).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - TRAS is the commonest; angioplasty or stent first.
> - Venous thrombosis: reversed diastolic flow, swollen tender graft.
> - Arterial thrombosis: sudden anuria; explore immediately.

## Q3e. Management of Acute Hyperkalemia
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and physiology
- Serum potassium **>5.5 mmol/L**; mild 5.5–5.9, moderate 6.0–6.4, **severe ≥6.5** (UK Kidney Association). Above 6.0 is generally urgent.
- About 98% of potassium is intracellular; the ratio sets the resting membrane potential, so a high level causes **arrhythmia and cardiac arrest**.
- Defence: rapid shift into cells (insulin, β₂-agonists via Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase), then renal excretion (needs distal sodium delivery, urine flow, aldosterone).

### Causes

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| Pseudohyperkalaemia | Haemolysis, tourniquet, fist clenching, thrombocytosis |
| Reduced excretion | AKI (acute kidney injury), CKD (chronic kidney disease), **obstructive uropathy** (type 4 RTA [renal tubular acidosis]), adrenal insufficiency; drugs: ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors, ARBs (angiotensin receptor blockers), spironolactone, NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), tacrolimus |
| Shift out of cells | Acidosis, insulin deficiency, β-blockers, suxamethonium |
| Cell release | **Rhabdomyolysis** (prolonged lithotomy or flank position), tumour lysis, crush |
| Urological | Jejunal conduit (reabsorbs urinary potassium) |

### Assessment
- Repeat the sample to **exclude pseudohyperkalaemia** if the ECG (electrocardiogram) is normal.
- **ECG changes:** peaked T (earliest), prolonged PR, wide QRS, loss of P, sine wave, ventricular fibrillation. Ascending weakness.
- Creatinine, glucose, blood gas, CK (creatine kinase); stop potassium sources.

### Emergency treatment (in order)

| Step | Drug and dose | Onset and effect |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Protect the heart** | **Calcium gluconate 10%, 10 mL IV (intravenous)** over 5–10 min; repeat if ECG unchanged | 1–3 min; lasts 30–60 min; does not lower potassium |
| **2. Shift into cells** | **Insulin 10 units regular IV with 50 mL of 50% dextrose** | 15–30 min; lowers about 0.6–1 mmol/L; check glucose (hypoglycaemia) |
| | **Salbutamol 20 mg nebulised** | Additive to insulin |
| | Sodium bicarbonate | Only with metabolic acidosis |
| **3. Remove** | Loop diuretic if passing urine; **binders**: sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (about 1 h), patiromer (about 7 h), sodium polystyrene sulfonate (slow; colonic necrosis) | Hours |
| | **Haemodialysis** | Refractory, oliguric, ESKD (end-stage kidney disease) |

- **Relieve obstruction** (catheter, nephrostomy); watch for post-obstructive diuresis.
- Monitor ECG, potassium and glucose; treat the cause.

**Diagram to draw:** flowchart: ECG change or K⁺ ≥6.5 → calcium → shift → remove.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Calcium stabilises the membrane but does not lower potassium.
> - Insulin 10 units + 50 mL 50% dextrose; salbutamol 20 mg nebulised.
> - Binders, diuretics and dialysis remove potassium.

## Q3f. BCG therapy in Bladder Cancer
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(intravesical therapy also answered in 2017-Summer Paper 4, Q1)

### Introduction
- BCG (bacille Calmette-Guérin), a live attenuated mycobacterium (Morales, 1976), is the only intravesical agent that reduces both **recurrence and progression** of NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer).

### Mechanism
- BCG binds **fibronectin** on the urothelium; cytokines (IFN-γ [interferon-gamma], IL-2 [interleukin-2] and TNF-α [tumour necrosis factor-alpha]) and immune cells follow.
- A **Th1 (T-helper 1)** response gives cell-mediated tumour kill. It needs repeated stimulation, which is why **maintenance** is essential.

### Indications
- **High-risk NMIBC:** HG (high-grade) T1, large/multifocal/recurrent HG Ta, **CIS (carcinoma in situ)** (initial complete response up to 84%).
- **Intermediate risk:** option. Not for low risk.

### Technique and schedule
- Start **2–4 weeks after TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour)**; urinalysis first; atraumatic catheter; retain **1–2 hours**.
- **Induction:** weekly × 6.
- **Maintenance (SWOG [Southwest Oncology Group], Lamm 2000):** weekly × 3 at 3 and 6 months, then every 6 months to **3 years** (recurrence-free survival 76.8 vs 35.7 months).
- **EORTC (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer) 30962:** intermediate risk, full dose for 1 year; high risk, full dose for 3 years. **NIMBUS:** reduced frequency was inferior.

### Contraindications
- **Absolute:** immediately after TURBT, gross haematuria, traumatic catheterisation (all risk **intravasation**); previous BCG sepsis; total incontinence; immunosuppression (now debated).
- **Relative:** UTI (urinary tract infection), liver disease, prior TB (tuberculosis).

### Toxicity

| Grade | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cystitis, fever <38.5 °C, <48 h | Symptomatic |
| 2 | Severe or >48 h | Isoniazid + rifampicin; resume at ½–⅓ dose |
| 3 | Sepsis, organ involvement | Stop BCG; add ethambutol; steroids only with cover |
- BCG is resistant to **pyrazinamide**.

### BCG failure
- **BCG-unresponsive:** persistent or recurrent CIS or HG Ta/T1 despite **adequate BCG** (≥5 of 6 induction + ≥2 of 3 maintenance), or HG relapse within 6 months (Ta/T1) or 12 months (CIS). **Timely radical cystectomy** is standard.
- **If unfit or unwilling:** pembrolizumab, nadofaragene firadenovec, N-803 + BCG, gemcitabine–docetaxel.
- **Recent:** PD-1/PD-L1 (programmed death-1/ligand-1) inhibitor + BCG in BCG-naive high risk improved event-free survival (CREST, POTOMAC); TAR-200 gemcitabine-releasing device.

**Diagram to draw:** SWOG schedule timeline.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Only BCG reduces progression; maintenance is essential.
> - Never immediately after TURBT; start at 2–4 weeks.
> - Unresponsive: timely cystectomy.

## Rapid Revision Sheet – 2025-Winter Paper 1

#### Q1. Describe Pathogenesis of Congenital Pelvi Ureteric Junction Obstruction its diagnosis and Management. (LAQ)

- Intrinsic adynamic segment (infants) vs crossing vessel (older children, intermittent pain).
- SFU 4 = parenchymal thinning; UTD P3 = abnormal parenchyma.
- Operate for DRF <40%, fall >10%, rising APD, UTI or symptoms.
- Anderson–Hynes ~95%; never divide a crossing vessel.

#### Q2. Nepron Sparing Surgery in renal malignancy. (LAQ)

- Imperative, relative, elective; PN is standard for cT1a.
- AUA: RN only if complex + no CKD + eGFR likely >45.
- Preserved parenchyma, not clamp time, governs function.
- EORTC 30904: better function, similar cancer control.

#### Q3a. Extended Criteria for Selection of Live Renal Donor (SAQ)

- KDIGO 2017: risk-based lifetime ESKD threshold.
- GFR 60–89 and albuminuria 30–100 are individualised.
- Controlled hypertension on 1–2 drugs may donate; type 1 diabetes cannot.

#### Q3b. Metabolic Evaluation in Stone Disease (SAQ)

- Screening for all first-time formers; 24-hour urine for high-risk.
- Targets: volume >2.5 L, oxalate <40 mg, citrate >320 mg.
- Repeat 24-hour urine within 6 months of therapy.

#### Q3c. Hematospermia (SAQ)

- Usually benign; prostate biopsy is the commonest identifiable cause.
- Full work-up if ≥40, persistent or recurrent, or associated haematuria.
- Exclude prostate cancer and genitourinary tuberculosis.

#### Q3d. Vascular Complications after Renal Transplantation in Recipient (SAQ)

- TRAS is the commonest; angioplasty or stent first.
- Venous thrombosis: reversed diastolic flow, swollen tender graft.
- Arterial thrombosis: sudden anuria; explore immediately.

#### Q3e. Management of Acute Hyperkalemia (SAQ)

- Calcium stabilises the membrane but does not lower potassium.
- Insulin 10 units + 50 mL 50% dextrose; salbutamol 20 mg nebulised.
- Binders, diuretics and dialysis remove potassium.

#### Q3f. BCG therapy in Bladder Cancer (SAQ)

- Only BCG reduces progression; maintenance is essential.
- Never immediately after TURBT; start at 2–4 weeks.
- Unresponsive: timely cystectomy.
