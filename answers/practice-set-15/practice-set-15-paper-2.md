## Q1. Describe classification and management of Wilm's bladder
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(bilateral Wilms tumour also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 3, Q3e and 2016-Summer Paper 3, Q3c; Wilms tumour in Practice Set 10 Paper 2, Q3b)

*Read as: "Wilms tumour" (nephroblastoma); "bladder" is taken as a typo.*

### Introduction
- WT (Wilms tumour) is an embryonal tumour of metanephric blastema. It forms 95% of kidney cancers under 15 years; median age 3.5 years; over 80% present before 5 years.
- **Genetics:** WT1 (11p13), IGF2/11p15 (commonest change, about 70%), WTX, CTNNB1; **TP53 in most anaplastic tumours**. Familial 1–2%.
- **Syndromes:** WAGR (Wilms, aniridia, genitourinary anomalies, intellectual disability) and Denys–Drash (46,XY DSD [disorder of sex development], mesangial sclerosis): risk 50%. Beckwith–Wiedemann and hemihypertrophy: 5–10%. Screen by ultrasound every 3–4 months until 7 years when risk is >1%.
- **Nephrogenic rests** (precursors) occur in over one-third of WT kidneys and over 90% of bilateral cases.

### Classification

**A. Histological**

| Group | Features | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Favourable histology** | Triphasic: blastema, epithelium (tubules), stroma; pseudocapsule | Survival >90% |
| **Anaplasia** (unfavourable) | Nuclei ≥3× neighbours, hyperchromasia, abnormal mitoses; **focal** or **diffuse** (worse) | Chemoresistant; poor even at stage I |

- **SIOP (International Society of Paediatric Oncology) post-chemotherapy risk groups:**

| Risk | Histology |
|---|---|
| Low | Completely necrotic |
| Intermediate | All others (regressive, mixed, epithelial, stromal, focal anaplasia) |
| High | **Blastemal-predominant**; diffuse anaplasia |

- Residual blastema predicts relapse; stromal and epithelial tumours shrink little but are cured by excision.

**B. Staging: COG (Children's Oncology Group), surgical-pathological, before chemotherapy**

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Confined to kidney, completely resected, capsule intact, no renal sinus vessel invasion |
| II | Beyond kidney (renal sinus vessels, perirenal fat, vein thrombus) but completely resected |
| III | Residual abdominal disease: **positive nodes, any spill, prior biopsy**, positive margin, peritoneal implants |
| IV | Haematogenous metastases (lung commonest, liver, bone, brain) |
| V | Bilateral at diagnosis (each side staged separately) |

- **SIOP staging** uses the same five stages but is applied **after preoperative chemotherapy**. Necrotic, chemotherapy-induced change in the sinus or perirenal fat does not upstage.
- **Biology:** combined **1p and 16q LOH (loss of heterozygosity)** predicts relapse; 1q gain is the next stratifier.

### Evaluation
- Abdominal mass; haematuria about 20%; hypertension about 25% (renin). Varicocele or hepatomegaly suggests IVC (inferior vena cava) thrombus. Acquired von Willebrand disease in up to 8%.
- Ultrasound with Doppler → contrast CT (computed tomography) or MRI abdomen → **chest CT** (before anaesthesia). Blood count, renal and liver function, coagulation.

### Management: two philosophies

| | COG (North America) | SIOP (Europe) |
|---|---|---|
| Sequence | **Upfront radical nephrectomy**, then chemotherapy | **Preoperative VCR + AMD** (4 weeks; add DOX for 6 weeks if metastatic), no biopsy, then nephrectomy |
| Advantage | Untreated histology and biology for risk assignment | Tumour shrinks; spill almost zero |
| Risk basis | Stage + histology + LOH | Post-chemotherapy histology + stage |

(VCR = vincristine; AMD = actinomycin D; DOX = doxorubicin.)

**Surgical principles (radical nephrectomy)**
- Transperitoneal approach; palpate renal vein and IVC before ligation; spare a tumour-free adrenal.
- **Avoid spill** (it makes the tumour stage III).
- **Lymph node sampling of at least 5 nodes** (hilar, paracaval, para-aortic, interaortocaval) is essential; formal dissection is not. Omitted sampling is the commonest protocol violation.
- IVC thrombus at or above the hepatic veins → preoperative chemotherapy to avoid bypass.

**Postoperative therapy (COG, favourable histology)**

| Group | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Very low risk (<2 years, <550 g, stage I) | Nephrectomy alone (AREN0532) |
| Stage I–II | VCR + AMD |
| Stage III | VCR + AMD + DOX + flank RT (radiotherapy) |
| Stage IV | Three drugs + whole-lung RT; lung RT omitted if nodules clear by week 6 (AREN0533) |
| Anaplasia or 1p/16q LOH | Intensified regimens (regimen M adds cyclophosphamide + etoposide) |

### Bilateral WT (stage V) and nephron sparing
- Bilateral WT occurs in 5–7%. **No upfront nephrectomy or biopsy.** The aim is cure while preserving nephrons.
```
VCR + AMD + DOX × 6 weeks → reimaging
  ├─ resectable → bilateral NSS (nephron-sparing surgery)
  └─ not → 6 more weeks → surgery by week 12
       poor response → bilateral open biopsy
```
- **Why by week 12:** in NWTS-4 (National Wilms Tumor Study-4) prolonged chemotherapy delayed surgery and some tumours became anaplastic.
- **AREN0534:** 4-year EFS (event-free survival) 82.1% and OS (overall survival) 94.9%.
- Bilateral nephrectomy and dialysis are rare; transplant after 1–2 years.
- **Unilateral NSS:** for syndromic children and solitary kidney; SIOP UMBRELLA criteria are volume <300 mL, a substantial remnant and negative nodes.

### Relapse and late effects
- Salvage survival: 70–80% after VCR/AMD only; about 10% for anaplastic or blastemal relapse.
- **Late effects:** cardiotoxicity (DOX), second cancers (RT), infertility, renal failure (bilateral).

**Diagram to draw:** COG staging diagram showing capsule, renal sinus, nodes and spill.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Anaplasia (≥3× nuclei, hyperchromasia, abnormal mitoses) is unfavourable; TP53.
> - Any spill, biopsy or positive node = stage III; sample ≥5 nodes.
> - COG operates first; SIOP gives VCR + AMD first.
> - Bilateral: three drugs, surgery by week 12, NSS.

## Q2. Discuss management of organ confined CA prostate
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- Organ-confined prostate cancer is **cT1–T2 N0 M0**. Treatment must balance cancer mortality against **competing mortality**: life expectancy, comorbidity, baseline urinary, sexual and bowel function, and patient preference (shared decision-making).

### Risk stratification (NCCN, National Comprehensive Cancer Network)

| Risk group | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Very low | All of: T1c, GG (grade group) 1, PSA (prostate-specific antigen) <10 ng/mL, <3 positive cores with ≤50% cancer in each, PSA density <0.15 |
| Low | All of: T1–T2a, GG1, PSA <10 |
| Favourable intermediate | 1 intermediate factor (T2b–c, GG2–3, PSA 10–20), GG1–2, <50% cores positive |
| Unfavourable intermediate | 2–3 intermediate factors, GG3, or ≥50% cores positive |
| High | Any of: T3a, GG4–5, PSA >20 |

- **Grade groups:** GG1 = 3+3; GG2 = 3+4; GG3 = 4+3; GG4 = 8; GG5 = 9–10.

### Evaluation
- PSA, DRE (digital rectal examination), mpMRI (multiparametric MRI) with targeted and systematic biopsy.
- **Staging imaging** from unfavourable intermediate risk: bone scan + CT/MRI, or PSMA (prostate-specific membrane antigen) PET (positron emission tomography). **proPSMA:** accuracy 92% vs 65% for conventional imaging.
- Life expectancy (life tables, Charlson index); IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score) and erectile function.
- **Also know:** Partin tables, Kattan nomogram, CAPRA (Cancer of the Prostate Risk Assessment) score; genomic tests (Decipher, Prolaris, Oncotype DX) for borderline surveillance decisions.

### Options by risk

| Risk | Preferred options |
|---|---|
| Very low / low | **Active surveillance (AS)**; watchful waiting if life expectancy short |
| Favourable intermediate | AS in selected men; RP (radical prostatectomy); EBRT (external-beam radiotherapy) or brachytherapy alone |
| Unfavourable intermediate | RP + PLND (pelvic lymph node dissection); EBRT + **4–6 months ADT** (androgen deprivation therapy) |
| High (organ-confined, e.g. GG4–5 or PSA >20) | RP + extended PLND (multimodal); EBRT + **18–36 months ADT** |

### 1. Active surveillance vs watchful waiting
- **AS:** deferred treatment with **curative intent**. Monitor by PSA every 3–6 months, DRE, **confirmatory biopsy or MRI within 6–12 months**, then periodic MRI and biopsy. Trigger for treatment: grade progression (GG ≥2–3), increasing volume or patient choice.
- Entry criteria: Johns Hopkins (strict, Epstein), PRIAS (Prostate Cancer Research International Active Surveillance), Toronto (inclusive). About half are treated by 10–15 years.
- **Watchful waiting:** no curative intent; ADT for symptomatic progression. For limited life expectancy.

### 2. Radical prostatectomy
- Open retropubic or **robot-assisted** (similar outcomes in the randomised trial). Nerve sparing when there is no same-side extracapsular risk.
- **PLND** when nodal risk is significant on nomograms (extended template).
- **Complications:** incontinence, ED (erectile dysfunction), anastomotic stricture, rectal injury.
- **Advantages:** pathological staging; relieves obstruction; RT kept for salvage.

### 3. Radiotherapy
- **EBRT:** IMRT (intensity-modulated RT) with image guidance. Fractionation: conventional 78 Gy in 39; **moderate hypofractionation 60 Gy in 20** (CHHiP: non-inferior); **SBRT (stereotactic body RT) 36.25 Gy in 5** (PACE-B: similar toxicity).
- **Why add ADT:** synergistic cell kill and target shrinkage. **RTOG 94-08:** 4 months ADT improved OS in intermediate risk; **DFCI 95-096:** 6 months ADT improved 8-year OS (61% → 74%).
- **Brachytherapy:** LDR (low-dose-rate) seeds for low and favourable intermediate risk. Relative contraindications: gland >60 mL, high IPSS, recent TURP. HDR (high-dose-rate) or LDR **boost** with EBRT for higher risk (ASCENDE-RT: half the biochemical failure).
- **Toxicity:** proctitis, irritative urinary symptoms, late ED, second cancers (controversial).

### 4. Focal and whole-gland ablation
- HIFU (high-intensity focused ultrasound), cryotherapy, focal laser, IRE (irreversible electroporation), VTP (vascular-targeted photodynamic therapy).
- Based on the **index lesion hypothesis**. Best for MRI-visible, biopsy-confirmed, unifocal **intermediate-risk (GG2)** disease. Investigational; ideally in trials. VTP is the only modality with a randomised trial (versus AS in low risk).

### Landmark trials

| Trial | Comparison | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **SPCG-4** (pre-PSA era) | RP vs watchful waiting | Cancer death RR (relative risk) 0.56 at 23 years; NNT 8; benefit <65 years |
| **PIVOT** | RP vs observation (PSA era) | No mortality difference; benefit only in intermediate risk |
| **ProtecT** | Monitoring vs RP vs RT + ADT | 15-year cancer mortality 2.7%, 2.2%, 2.9% (no difference); more metastases with monitoring |

- **ProtecT function:** RP worst for continence and sexual function; RT transient bowel effects.

### Algorithm
```
Organ-confined CaP → risk group + life expectancy
 ├─ short life expectancy → watchful waiting
 ├─ very low / low → active surveillance
 ├─ favourable intermediate → AS (selected), RP, EBRT, brachytherapy
 └─ unfavourable intermediate / high → RP + PLND
                                     or EBRT + ADT (4–6 or 18–36 months)
```

### Follow-up
- PSA every 6–12 months. **Biochemical recurrence:** after RP, PSA ≥0.2 ng/mL confirmed; after RT, **nadir + 2 ng/mL (Phoenix)**.

### Recent advances
- PSMA PET staging; MRI-led surveillance; SBRT; genomic classifiers.

**Diagram to draw:** stepwise decision tree (treat or not → partial or whole gland → surgery or radiation).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Organ-confined = cT1–T2 N0 M0; NCCN risk groups drive choice.
> - AS is preferred for low risk; ProtecT: no mortality difference at 15 years.
> - EBRT + ADT: 4–6 months (intermediate), 18–36 months (high).
> - Focal therapy rests on the index lesion hypothesis; still investigational.

## Q3a. Monopolar v/s Bipolar TURP.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate) removes transition-zone tissue from bladder neck to verumontanum, down to the surgical capsule; the benchmark BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) operation.
- **AUA (American Urological Association):** no preference; choose by expertise.

### Principles
- **Monopolar (m-TURP):** current runs from the loop through the patient to a grounding pad. Saline would disperse the current, so a **non-conducting, hypo-osmolar irrigant** (glycine 1.5%, sorbitol–mannitol, water) is needed.
- **Bipolar (b-TURP):** active and return poles are on the same instrument, so current stays local and **normal saline** can be used. The Plasma system turns saline into a plasma layer (orange glow) that cuts at lower temperature and seals vessels.

### Comparison

| Feature | Monopolar | Bipolar |
|---|---|---|
| Current path | Loop → patient → pad | Within the loop |
| Irrigant | Glycine, sorbitol, water | Normal saline |
| TUR syndrome | Yes | Practically eliminated |
| Resection time | Limited (speed over haemostasis) | No time pressure; large glands |
| Loop | Tungsten | Platinum–iridium (Plasma) |
| Bleeding, transfusion, clot retention | Higher | Lower |
| Catheter time, hospital stay | Longer | Shorter |
| Obturator reflex | Risk | Reduced |
| IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score), QoL, PVR | Equivalent (Qmax marginally better with bipolar) | Equivalent |
| Stricture, BNC (bladder neck contracture), sexual function, retreatment | Similar | Similar |

### TUR syndrome (monopolar)
- Irrigant absorbed through open sinuses (venous pressure about 10 mmHg) → **dilutional hyponatraemia**: confusion, visual disturbance, nausea, bradycardia, hypertension, seizures; glycine also yields ammonia.
- **Risk factors:** gland >45 mL, resection >90 minutes, high irrigation bag.
- **Treatment:** stop surgery, check sodium; 3% saline 100 mL boluses for neurological symptoms; avoid over-rapid correction (central pontine myelinolysis).

### Evidence
- Meta-analyses: equal efficacy up to 5 years; b-TURP safer (NNT about 50 to prevent one TUR syndrome, about 20 to prevent one clot retention).
- **Also know:** TURis (sheath as return) caused strictures; bipolar enucleation and vaporisation (PlasmaButton).

**Diagram to draw:** monopolar circuit (loop → patient → pad) versus bipolar (loop to return electrode on the same resectoscope).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Bipolar allows saline; TUR syndrome practically eliminated.
> - Equal efficacy; bipolar has less bleeding and shorter catheter time.
> - TUR syndrome risk: >45 mL gland, >90 minutes.

## Q3b. Overactive bladder
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(OAB also answered in Practice Set 4 Paper 1, Q2; detrusor relaxants in Practice Set 1 Paper 1, Q3e)

### Definition
- **OAB (overactive bladder), ICS (International Continence Society):** **urgency**, usually with frequency and nocturia, with or without UUI (urgency urinary incontinence), in the absence of UTI (urinary tract infection) or other obvious pathology.
- **OAB dry** vs **wet** (with UUI). OAB is a symptom diagnosis; DO (detrusor overactivity) is urodynamic, found in only 44–69% with urgency.

### Pathophysiology
- **Afferent:** silent C fibres become active and hypersensitive.
- **Urothelial:** urothelium releases ATP (adenosine triphosphate) and acetylcholine onto suburothelial afferents.
- **Myogenic:** patchy denervation → spontaneous micromotions that spread.
- **Central:** loss of forebrain inhibition of the pontine micturition centre.
- **Associations:** ageing, BOO (bladder outlet obstruction), metabolic syndrome.

### Evaluation
- **Minimum:** history, examination, **urinalysis**. Bladder diary (3 days), questionnaires, PVR (post-void residual) when indicated.
- Urodynamics and cystoscopy only for complicated or refractory cases.

### Management (AUA/SUFU [American Urological Association/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction]; not a compulsory sequence)

| Line | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Behavioural | Education; reduce fluids by about 25%; cut caffeine; weight loss; **bladder training** (timed voids, interval increased by 15–30 minutes); **PFMT (pelvic floor muscle training)** with urge suppression: 50–80% fewer leaks |
| Drugs | **Antimuscarinics** (oxybutynin, solifenacin, extended-release trospium, darifenacin, fesoterodine); **β3-agonists** (mirabegron, vibegron); combinations |
| Third line | **PTNS (percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation)** weekly × 12; **onabotulinumtoxinA 100 U** intradetrusor (about 25% dry; UTI about 20%; retention); **SNM (sacral neuromodulation)** at S3 (success about 70–80%; revision >30% at 5 years) |
| Last resort | Augmentation cystoplasty or urinary diversion |

- **Antimuscarinic use >3 months raises dementia risk** → prefer β3-agonists in the elderly. Men: α-blocker first; add antimuscarinic if PVR is low.
- **Trials:** ROSETTA (Botox beat SNM at 6 months, more UTI); SUmiT (PTNS 55% vs sham 21%).

**Diagram to draw:** bladder showing urothelial afferents and C fibres with brainstem and cortical inhibition.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - OAB is defined by urgency; DO is urodynamic.
> - Behavioural therapy first; antimuscarinic or β3-agonist next.
> - Botox 100 U, PTNS, SNM for refractory OAB.
> - Long-term antimuscarinics → dementia risk.

## Q3c. Fracture penis
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and mechanism
- **Penile fracture** is a tear of the **tunica albuginea** with rupture of the corpus cavernosum, in the **erect** penis.
- Abnormal bending of the erect penis causes an abrupt rise in intracavernosal pressure beyond tunical strength → **transverse tear**.
- The tunica (inner circular, outer longitudinal layers) is **thinnest ventrolaterally**, so most tears are ventral or lateral, proximal to mid shaft.
- **Causes:** intercourse (commonest; rear-entry), masturbation, rolling over; self-inflicted "taqaandan" (Middle East); collagenase injection for Peyronie disease.
- **Bilateral tears in about 10%**; urethral injury about 20% in the West vs about 3% in Asia and the Middle East.

### Clinical features
- **Cracking or popping sound → pain → rapid detumescence → swelling and discoloration.**
- **"Eggplant deformity"** when Buck's fascia is intact; if torn, haematoma spreads to scrotum, perineum and suprapubic area.
- Penis deviates **away from the tear**; tunical defect may be palpable.
- **Urethral injury:** blood at meatus, haematuria, retention.

### Investigations
- **Diagnosis is clinical** (AUA [American Urological Association] Urotrauma Guideline).
- **Ultrasound** for equivocal cases (localises the tear); **MRI** (magnetic resonance imaging) if still equivocal (100% negative predictive value). Cavernosography is discouraged.
- Urethra: **intraoperative flexible cystoscopy** (AUA and EAU [European Association of Urology] preference) or RUG (retrograde urethrogram).
- **"False fracture":** dorsal vein or artery rupture.

### Management
- **Immediate surgical repair** (less curvature, ED [erectile dysfunction] and nodules than conservative care).
1. Incision: ventral penoscrotal or lateral over the defect; subcoronal degloving if site unclear (preputial ischaemia risk).
2. Evacuate haematoma; find the tear (artificial erection helps).
3. Close the tunica with **interrupted 2-0/3-0 absorbable sutures**; avoid debriding erectile tissue.
4. Urethra: partial → close over catheter; complete → tension-free repair; catheter 2–3 weeks.
- Abstinence about 1 month. Delay up to 7 days does not worsen outcome. After collagenase injection without urethral injury, conservative care is acceptable.

### Complications
- About 20%: nodule, curvature, ED, painful erection, infection, stricture or fistula.

**Diagram to draw:** cross-section of penis showing tunical tear, Buck's fascia and haematoma spread.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Snap, detumescence, eggplant deformity.
> - Urethral injury about 20% (West); suspect if bilateral.
> - Immediate exploration and interrupted absorbable tunical closure.

## Q3d. Describe various penile prosthesis in ED
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2023-Winter Paper 4, Q3c and 2018-Summer Paper 2, Q3d; surgery for ED in 2020-Summer Paper 2, Q2)

### Introduction
- Most effective, but last-line, treatment of ED (erectile dysfunction); **satisfaction 80–90%**. First devices: Scott inflatable (1972), Small–Carrion rod (1978).
- **Indications:** failure of, contraindication to or refusal of PDE5Is (phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors), injections and vacuum devices; ED with Peyronie disease; after ischaemic priapism (early implant preserves length).
- **Contraindications:** active infection, uncontrolled diabetes, unrealistic expectations.

### Types

| Type | Devices | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Malleable (semirigid) rods** | Tactra (nitinol core), Genesis (braided silver), Rigi 10 (stainless steel) | Two bendable rods; simple, cheap; mechanical failure exceedingly rare; permanently firm; suited to poor dexterity |
| Mechanical rods | Spectra (discontinued) | Articulating segments |
| Two-piece inflatable | Ambicor (discontinued) | Cylinders + scrotal pump; no reservoir; poor flaccidity |
| **Three-piece inflatable** | AMS 700 CX/LGX/CXR; Coloplast Titan, Titan Touch, Titan Narrow; Rigicon Infla 10 | Two cylinders, scrotal pump, abdominal reservoir; **best rigidity and true flaccidity**; most popular |

### Three-piece inflatable details
- **Expansion:** girth only (CX, Titan) or girth and length (LGX, up to 25%; Infla 10 AX). **Narrow** cylinders (CXR, Titan Narrow) for fibrotic corpora.
- **Material:** AMS and Rigicon use silicone–fabric–silicone layers; Titan uses polyurethane (Bioflex).
- **Coatings:** InhibiZone (rifampicin + minocycline) on AMS; hydrophilic coating on Titan, dipped in antibiotic.
- **Reservoir:** space of Retzius, or **ectopic submuscular** after pelvic surgery; lockout valve prevents autoinflation.

### Surgical principles
- **Approach:** penoscrotal (commonest) or infrapubic (dorsal nerve at risk).
- Corporotomy → dilate (instruments dorsolateral) → check for crossover or perforation → measure → cylinders sized to corporal length. Rods are placed slightly shorter.
- Antibiotics, glucose control, no-touch technique.

### Complications
- **Infection 1–3%** (biofilm): remove all components; **Mulcahy salvage** (washout and immediate replacement).
- **Mechanical failure** (inflatables): repair about 15% at 5 years, 30% at 10.
- Crural perforation, urethral injury, erosion, glans necrosis, shortening, autoinflation.

**Diagram to draw:** three-piece inflatable: cylinders, scrotal pump, reservoir in the space of Retzius.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Three-piece inflatable: best rigidity and flaccidity.
> - Malleable rods: nitinol (Tactra), silver (Genesis); almost never fail.
> - Infection 1–3%; satisfaction 80–90%.

## Q3e. Ovarian vein syndromes
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **Ovarian vein syndrome** (Clark, 1964) is extrinsic compression of the **right ureter** by a dilated or thrombosed **right ovarian vein**, causing intermittent hydroureteronephrosis. It is a **controversial** entity; many cases are now attributed to other causes.
- The plural also covers ovarian vein thrombosis and pelvic congestion.

### Anatomy (basis)
- Ovarian veins run in the infundibulopelvic ligaments. The **right** enters the IVC (inferior vena cava) obliquely; the **left** enters the left renal vein at a right angle.
- Gonadal vessels **cross anterior to the ureter**; ovarian veins dilate greatly in pregnancy.

### Types

| Syndrome | Mechanism | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Ovarian vein (ureteric) syndrome** | Dilated right ovarian vein compresses the ureter at the crossing | Multiparous women; right flank pain (premenstrual or in pregnancy); recurrent UTI (urinary tract infection) |
| **Ovarian vein thrombosis** | Postpartum hypercoagulability and venous stasis; mostly right-sided | Fever and right lower-quadrant or flank pain after delivery; may obstruct ureter; risk of pulmonary embolism |
| **Pelvic congestion syndrome** | Incompetent, refluxing ovarian veins with pelvic varices; sometimes secondary to nutcracker (left renal vein compression) | Chronic pelvic pain worse on standing, dyspareunia, post-coital ache |

### Controversy
- Right-sided dilatation in pregnancy is mostly **physiological hydronephrosis** (progesterone plus uterine compression; stops at the pelvic brim). Venous compression is rarely proven, so diagnosis is by **exclusion**.

### Investigations
- Ultrasound; **CT urography** or IVU (intravenous urography): right hydroureter to a smooth oblique extrinsic indentation, normal ureter below.
- **Diuretic renogram** to prove obstruction; retrograde pyelography.
- CT or MR (magnetic resonance) venography, Doppler; **gonadal venography** (gold standard for reflux).
- **Exclude:** stone, retrocaval ureter, iliac vessel crossing, retroperitoneal fibrosis, endometriosis.

### Management
- **Asymptomatic:** observe. **Obstruction:** stent or nephrostomy if infected or pregnant; definitive **ureterolysis with ligation and division of the ovarian vein** (open or laparoscopic).
- **Thrombosis:** anticoagulation and antibiotics.
- **Pelvic congestion:** progestogens or GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) analogues; **coil or sclerotherapy embolisation**; ligation.

**Diagram to draw:** right ovarian vein crossing anterior to the right ureter, with proximal hydroureteronephrosis.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Right ovarian vein compresses right ureter; controversial, diagnosis of exclusion.
> - Postpartum ovarian vein thrombosis: right side, anticoagulate.
> - Treatment: ureterolysis with vein division, or embolisation.

## Q3f. Megameatus intact prepuce
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **MIP (megameatus intact prepuce)** is a hypospadias variant (up to 5%) with a **wide, "fish-mouth" glanular or coronal meatus**, a **completely formed prepuce** and **no significant curvature**. Described by Duckett and Keating (1989).

### Embryology
- Normally the prepuce closes ventrally only after the urethral folds fuse (about 20 weeks), so 95% of hypospadias have a hooded, incomplete foreskin.
- In MIP the foreskin is complete, showing that **preputial and urethral development can dissociate**.

### Clinical features
- Hidden under a normal foreskin; often found on **retraction before circumcision** or during circumcision.
- Wide, deep glans groove with a large, patulous meatus; well-developed spongiosum.
- Symptoms: spraying or deflected stream; cosmetic concern.
- **Advice:** retract the foreskin before every newborn circumcision. If found, the circumcision may be completed; prior circumcision does not harm later repair.

### Evaluation
- Examine meatal site and size, glans and plate, curvature (artificial erection at surgery). GMS (glans–meatus–shaft) score.
- No routine upper-tract imaging for isolated distal hypospadias.

### Management
- **Indication:** cosmesis and a straight stream; repair is optional. Usual timing 6–18 months.
- **Pyramid procedure (Duckett and Keating):**
  1. Glans traction suture; retract prepuce.
  2. Incise around the megameatus in a **pyramid shape**, apex on the ventral shaft.
  3. Dissect the wide distal urethra free of the glans wings.
  4. **Excise a ventral wedge** of redundant urethra to taper it.
  5. Tubularise over a catheter; cover with dartos.
  6. Approximate glans wings in two layers (glansplasty); preserve or reconstruct the prepuce.
- **Alternatives:** GAP (glans approximation procedure; pyramid = GAP with intact prepuce), TIP (tubularised incised plate) urethroplasty, Thiersch–Duplay tubularisation, Mathieu.

### Complications
- Fistula, meatal stenosis or regression, glans dehiscence; generally low after distal repair.

**Diagram to draw:** fish-mouth glanular meatus with intact prepuce, and the pyramid incision.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Wide glanular meatus, intact prepuce, no curvature; up to 5% of hypospadias.
> - Often found at circumcision; retract foreskin first.
> - Pyramid repair: dissect, taper and tubularise; glansplasty.

#### Q1. Describe classification and management of Wilm's bladder (LAQ)

- Anaplasia (≥3× nuclei, hyperchromasia, abnormal mitoses) is unfavourable; TP53.
- Any spill, biopsy or positive node = stage III; sample ≥5 nodes.
- COG operates first; SIOP gives VCR + AMD first.
- Bilateral: three drugs, surgery by week 12, NSS.

#### Q2. Discuss management of organ confined CA prostate (LAQ)

- Organ-confined = cT1–T2 N0 M0; NCCN risk groups drive choice.
- AS is preferred for low risk; ProtecT: no mortality difference at 15 years.
- EBRT + ADT: 4–6 months (intermediate), 18–36 months (high).
- Focal therapy rests on the index lesion hypothesis; still investigational.

#### Q3a. Monopolar v/s Bipolar TURP. (SAQ)

- Bipolar allows saline; TUR syndrome practically eliminated.
- Equal efficacy; bipolar has less bleeding and shorter catheter time.
- TUR syndrome risk: >45 mL gland, >90 minutes.

#### Q3b. Overactive bladder (SAQ)

- OAB is defined by urgency; DO is urodynamic.
- Behavioural therapy first; antimuscarinic or β3-agonist next.
- Botox 100 U, PTNS, SNM for refractory OAB.
- Long-term antimuscarinics → dementia risk.

#### Q3c. Fracture penis (SAQ)

- Snap, detumescence, eggplant deformity.
- Urethral injury about 20% (West); suspect if bilateral.
- Immediate exploration and interrupted absorbable tunical closure.

#### Q3d. Describe various penile prosthesis in ED (SAQ)

- Three-piece inflatable: best rigidity and flaccidity.
- Malleable rods: nitinol (Tactra), silver (Genesis); almost never fail.
- Infection 1–3%; satisfaction 80–90%.

#### Q3e. Ovarian vein syndromes (SAQ)

- Right ovarian vein compresses right ureter; controversial, diagnosis of exclusion.
- Postpartum ovarian vein thrombosis: right side, anticoagulate.
- Treatment: ureterolysis with vein division, or embolisation.

#### Q3f. Megameatus intact prepuce (SAQ)

- Wide glanular meatus, intact prepuce, no curvature; up to 5% of hypospadias.
- Often found at circumcision; retract foreskin first.
- Pyramid repair: dissect, taper and tubularise; glansplasty.
