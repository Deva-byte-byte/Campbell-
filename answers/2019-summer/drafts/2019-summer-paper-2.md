## Q1. Describe in detail the management of T1G3 TCC of urinary bladder?
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- T1G3 is high-grade (HG) urothelial carcinoma (the current name for TCC, transitional cell carcinoma) that invades the lamina propria but not the muscularis propria.
- T1 is 20–30% of NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer) and its most dangerous form. Recurrence is >80%. Progression to muscle invasion is 30–50%, up to half within 3 years without effective treatment.
- Grade, not stage, drives progression. So every HG T1 is **high risk** (AUA [American Urological Association]/SUO and EAU [European Association of Urology]).

### Risk factors for progression
| Factor | Significance |
|---|---|
| Concurrent CIS (carcinoma in situ) | Second most important factor after grade |
| Depth of lamina propria invasion (substaging) | Invasion deep to the muscularis mucosae is worse. T1a/T1b proposed; no universally accepted method |
| LVI (lymphovascular invasion) | Suggests occult spread |
| Variant histology | Micropapillary (any amount), plasmacytoid, sarcomatoid, nested |
| Size >3 cm, multifocal, recurrent | More residual tumour and progression |
| Prostatic urethra, bladder neck, diverticulum | Poor BCG contact; a diverticulum has no muscle layer |
| Residual T1 at re-TURBT | Progression up to 80% |
| HG T1 at 3 months after BCG | Progression about 82% |
- **Also know:** EORTC (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer) and CUETO risk tables; hydronephrosis or a sessile, nodular tumour suggests muscle invasion.

### Evaluation
- Cystoscopy, urine cytology (sensitive for HG and CIS), CT urography for the upper tract (tumour in <5%; risk higher with T1 and trigonal tumours).
- Bimanual examination under anaesthesia before and after resection.

### Step 1: Complete, good-quality TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour)
- Resect all visible tumour **with detrusor muscle** in the specimen; send the base separately.
- Blue-light (hexaminolevulinate) cystoscopy or NBI (narrow-band imaging) detects more CIS and lowers recurrence. **En bloc resection** keeps orientation, which helps substaging and LVI reporting.
- Biopsy suspicious areas. Biopsy the prostatic urethra if the tumour involves the bladder neck or trigone, or if CIS or positive cytology is present.

### Step 2: Re-TURBT (restaging) in all T1
- Within **2–6 weeks**, before BCG (bacille Calmette-Guérin).
- **Why:** residual tumour in ≥40%; upstaging to muscle invasion in 25–30%, and in about half when the first specimen had no muscle. Treatment changes in one-third. Divrik's RCT (randomised controlled trial) showed better recurrence-free and progression-free survival.

```
HG T1 on TURBT → re-TURBT at 2–6 weeks
   ├─ ≥T2                       → treat as MIBC (NAC + radical cystectomy)
   ├─ residual T1 / very high risk → offer early radical cystectomy
   └─ T0, Ta or CIS              → BCG induction + 3-year maintenance
```
(MIBC: muscle-invasive bladder cancer; NAC: neoadjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy.)

### Step 3a: BCG – standard for most patients
- **Mechanism:** BCG attaches to fibronectin and is taken up by urothelium. It triggers cytokines (IFN-γ [interferon-gamma], IL [interleukin]-2, IL-12) and a **Th1 (T-helper 1)** response that kills tumour. It is the **only intravesical agent that reduces progression**.
- **Schedule:** start 2–4 weeks after TURBT. Induction: weekly ×6.
- **Maintenance (SWOG, Lamm 2000):** 3 weekly doses at 3 and 6 months, then every 6 months to **3 years**. Median recurrence-free survival 76.8 vs 35.7 months. **EORTC 30962:** full dose for 3 years in high risk. **NIMBUS:** fewer instillations were inferior.
- **Contraindications:** within 2 weeks of TURBT, gross haematuria, traumatic catheterisation, symptomatic UTI (urinary tract infection), previous BCG sepsis.
- **Toxicity:** cystitis is common; a short quinolone course after each dose reduces it. BCG sepsis: stop BCG; isoniazid, rifampicin and ethambutol plus steroids (BCG resists pyrazinamide).
- A single post-TURBT chemotherapy instillation is mainly for low-risk tumours; never give it after perforation.

### Step 3b: Early (immediate) radical cystectomy
- **Rationale:** clinical T1 is understaged in about one-third to one-half at cystectomy. Patients who progress under surveillance do worse than those presenting with muscle invasion. Immediate cystectomy gives about 92% 10-year disease-free survival, but only 64% if muscle invasion was missed.
- **AUA:** offer to fit patients with persistent HG T1 on re-resection, or T1 with CIS, LVI or variant histology.
- **EAU very-high-risk group (2021 model; risk factors: age >70, multiple papillary tumours, diameter ≥3 cm):** T1 HG with CIS plus ≥1 risk factor; T1 HG without CIS but with all three; also LVI, variant histology, CIS of the prostatic urethra. Offer immediate cystectomy; give BCG to those who decline or are unfit.
- Also: diverticular tumour, deep lamina propria invasion, tumour not controllable endoscopically.
- Operation: radical cystectomy, pelvic lymph node dissection and diversion.

### Step 4: BCG failure
- **Adequate BCG for T1:** ≥5 of 6 induction doses.
- **BCG-unresponsive:** HG T1 at the 3-month check after induction; persistent or recurrent CIS or HG Ta/T1 despite adequate BCG; or HG relapse within 6 months (Ta/T1) or 12 months (CIS) of the last dose.
- **Standard:** timely radical cystectomy (EAU, AUA).
- **Unfit or refusing:** pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-057: CIS complete response 41% at 3 months); nadofaragene firadenovec (53%); N-803 with BCG; sequential gemcitabine–docetaxel; TAR-200; clinical trials; chemoradiation in selected patients.
- **Also know:** BCG-intolerant, BCG-exposed, delayed relapse (may respond to BCG rechallenge).

### Follow-up (AUA/SUO high risk)
- Cystoscopy and cytology every 3–4 months for 2 years, 6-monthly for the next 2 years, then yearly for life. Restart the schedule after each recurrence.
- Upper-tract imaging yearly for 2 years, then less often.
- Positive cytology with normal cystoscopy: check the prostatic urethra (recurrence 24–30%) and the upper tracts.

### Recent advances
- En bloc resection; checkpoint inhibitors added to BCG in BCG-naive high-risk disease (trials); urine markers to predict BCG response (CyPRIT, Oncuria).

**Diagram to draw:** bladder wall layers showing Ta, T1a/T1b (above and below the muscularis mucosae) and T2.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Re-TURBT in all T1 at 2–6 weeks; residual T1 means progression up to 80%.
> - BCG induction + 3-year maintenance (SWOG); only agent that reduces progression.
> - Very high risk (CIS, LVI, variants, prostatic urethra): offer early cystectomy.
> - BCG-unresponsive: cystectomy; pembrolizumab or nadofaragene if unfit.

## Q2. Describe indication, mechanism of stone fragmentation and current status of ESWL?
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- ESWL (extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy), now called SWL, breaks stones with focused acoustic shock waves generated outside the body. It is the only truly non-invasive stone treatment.
- First human treatment in 1980 (Dornier HM1). The HM3 set the standard for efficacy; later machines traded efficacy for lower cost, smaller size and less need for anaesthesia.
- A shock wave is a short pressure pulse: peak positive pressure 30–100 MPa, followed by a negative (tensile) tail of about −10 MPa.

### Components of a lithotripter
- Energy source at the first focus (F1); focusing system; coupling medium (water or gel); imaging (fluoroscopy and/or ultrasound) to place the stone at the second focus (F2).
- A focal zone wider than the stone improves breakage and allows for breathing movement.

| Energy source | Principle | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electrohydraulic (spark gap; HM3) | Underwater spark at F1; ellipsoid reflector focuses wave | Most effective fragmentation | Electrode wears out (<40,000 shocks); shot-to-shot variation |
| Piezoelectric | Thousands of ceramic elements on a spherical dish | Long life; often no anaesthesia | Low energy; very small focal zone |
| Electromagnetic (all modern machines) | Electromagnetic coil moves a metal membrane; lens or reflector focuses | Consistent output; broad skin entry, high energy at F2 | — |

### Mechanisms of stone fragmentation
- Each shock creates stress inside the stone. Above a critical value, cracks form; repeated shocks make them grow and join (**fatigue**).

| Mechanism | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Spallation** | Wave reflects at the back surface of the stone (stone–fluid interface) and inverts; tension breaks off the distal face |
| **Shear and tear (superfocusing)** | Waves inside the stone reflect and focus at internal points; cracks form where shear and tension peak |
| **Quasistatic squeezing** | Wave travels faster in stone than in fluid; a ring of pressure compresses the stone (needs focal zone wider than stone) |
| **Dynamic squeezing** | Combination of the above at the stone edges; the strongest force in initial breakage |
| **Cavitation** | Negative phase creates bubbles; their collapse produces microjets that erode the surface. Too many bubbles shield the stone and injure tissue |

### Indications
- **Renal stones (AUA/EAU):** <2 cm outside the lower pole (SWL or URS [ureteroscopy]); lower pole <1 cm. EAU: <10 mm first-line; 10–20 mm equal to endourology; >20 mm second-line after PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy).
- **Ureteric stones:** an option at any level; best for upper ureteric stones <10 mm. Emergency SWL within 48 hours of colic works well.
- Patients wishing to avoid general anaesthesia and stents; children.
- **Poor choices:** stones >2 cm or staghorn; lower pole >1 cm (**Lower Pole I trial**: stone-free 21% with SWL vs 91% with PCNL); cystine, brushite or calcium oxalate monohydrate stones; infection stones; stones in several locations.

### Contraindications
- **Absolute:** pregnancy; uncorrected coagulopathy or antithrombotic drugs that cannot be stopped; untreated UTI (urinary tract infection); obstruction distal to the stone; arterial aneurysm near the stone (aortic aneurysm >4 cm); severe uncontrolled hypertension.
- **Relative:** gross obesity or skeletal deformity preventing targeting; pacemaker (reprogramme).

### Predictors of success
- **Stone:** size (most important); density <900 HU (Hounsfield units) best, >1000 HU predicts failure; composition; impaction (a "ring" of thick ureteric wall around the stone on CT).
- **Patient:** SSD (skin-to-stone distance) >10 cm predicts failure.
- **Lower pole anatomy (EAU):** steep infundibulopelvic angle, long infundibulum (>10 mm) and narrow infundibulum (<5 mm) hinder fragment clearance.
- **Triple D score:** stone density, stone volume and skin-to-stone distance.

### Technique to optimise results
1. Urine culture; treat infection. Stop antithrombotics; control blood pressure. No routine stent.
2. Analgesia, sedation or general anaesthesia; stone movement of 10–15 mm reduces breakage.
3. **Coupling:** gel applied as a mound, free of air. Air pockets cut fragmentation by up to two-fifths.
4. **Rate 60–90 shocks/min** (1–1.5 Hz) gives better results than 120/min, because slower rates let cavitation bubbles clear and cause less renal injury.
5. **Ramping:** start at low energy and increase step by step. This causes protective renal vasoconstriction and better breakage.
6. Monitor fragmentation with imaging; respect the maximum shock count. More than two sessions add little.
7. **Adjuncts:** α-blockers after SWL; percussion, diuresis and inversion for lower pole fragments; potassium citrate.

### Complications
- Renal haematoma: rare, mostly mild. Risk factors are age, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, anticoagulation, high energy and many shocks.
- Steinstrasse ("stone street"): risk rises several-fold when stone burden is >2 cm².
- Colic, haematuria, UTI and sepsis; arrhythmia (treat by ECG gating); rare injury to liver, spleen, pancreas or bowel.
- Long-term hypertension, diabetes and reduced fertility are **not** proven.

### Current status
- **Declining use worldwide.** URS has grown because of small digital scopes and holmium/thulium lasers, which give a higher single-session stone-free rate.
- **SWL vs URS:** SWL has fewer complications, no stent, no general anaesthesia and lower cost, but needs more retreatment. Ureteric stone-free rates are similar at 3 months.
- Guidelines still list SWL as first-line for small renal and ureteric stones.

### Recent advances
- **Burst wave lithotripsy:** bursts of 5–100 ultrasound cycles at 4–8 MPa; less cavitation; usable while awake; gives uniform small fragments.
- **Ultrasonic propulsion** moves fragments to help them pass.
- Wide-focus, low-pressure machines.

**Diagram to draw:** ellipsoid reflector with F1 (spark) and F2 (stone), with spallation and squeezing arrows on the stone.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Spallation, squeezing, shear, cavitation; dynamic squeezing strongest.
> - Failure: >1000 HU, SSD >10 cm, lower pole >1 cm (Lower Pole I: 21% vs 91%).
> - 60–90 shocks/min with ramping; good coupling.
> - Use is declining as URS grows; burst wave lithotripsy is coming.

## Q3a. Management of Urethral meatal stenosis.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and causes
- Narrowing of the external urethral meatus below the normal calibre for age.

| Age | Normal calibre |
|---|---|
| Under 4 months | 6–10 Fr |
| 1–12 months | 10–12 Fr |
| 1–10 years | 12–14 Fr |
| Over 10 years | 14–18 Fr |

- **Children:** almost always after **neonatal circumcision**. The exposed meatus is irritated by wet nappies (ammoniacal meatitis) and the ventral lips fuse into a thin membrane. Also frenular artery damage; congenital with hypospadias.
- **Adults:** LS (lichen sclerosus, formerly BXO [balanitis xerotica obliterans]); catheter or instrument trauma; failed hypospadias repair; urethritis. In adults it is usually the tip of a **fossa navicularis stricture**.

### Clinical features
- Thin, forceful stream deflected **upwards**; straining, frequency, incomplete emptying; pain at the start of voiding; spraying; meatal bleeding; UTI (urinary tract infection) occasionally.

### Evaluation
- Watch the child void and calibrate the meatus with a bougie à boule.
- Uroflowmetry and post-void residual. Urethrography only for UTI, incontinence or a suspected proximal lesion.
- Adults: **retrograde urethrogram before any meatal surgery**, as a proximal stricture often coexists, especially in LS.

### Management
| Situation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Meatitis, early narrowing (child) | Topical 0.05% clobetasol; gentle dilation; nappy hygiene |
| Symptomatic stenosis (child) | **Ventral meatotomy** (topical EMLA in clinic or general anaesthesia). Parents spread the edges with a dilator or ointment tip 3 times a day for 7–10 days |
| Simple adult stenosis | Ventral meatotomy with 3 sutures (apex and each side) uniting mucosa to glans skin; meatal dilator afterwards |
| Scarred or recurrent | Dorsal meatotomy (Y-V plasty after excising the scar ridge); effective but poorer cosmesis |
| Fossa navicularis involved | Urethroplasty: penile skin island flap (Jordan, De Sy), not in LS |
| **LS** | Topical steroid; extended meatotomy; **buccal mucosa graft** (never genital skin); staged repair for long strictures; perineal urethrostomy for pananterior disease |
| Unfit or elderly | Self-dilation with clobetasol as lubricant |

- Sutured meatotomy lowers restenosis; tailoring with a clamp without sutures is equally effective in children.

### Complications
- Bleeding, restenosis (commoner in LS), spraying, poor cosmesis after dorsal meatotomy.

**Also know:** biopsy LS that looks atypical (squamous carcinoma risk up to 8%); circumcision in infancy has lower complication rates.

**Diagram to draw:** ventral meatotomy: crush, incise ventrally, suture mucosa to glans at apex and both sides.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Child: post-circumcision ventral membrane; ventral meatotomy and parental dilation.
> - Adult: RUG first; usually a fossa navicularis stricture.
> - LS: steroid, buccal mucosa, never genital skin.

## Q3b. Flexible Ureteroscopy.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Covered in part in 2017-Summer Paper 1, Q3b; written in full here.*

### Introduction
- Flexible ureteroscopy (fURS), also called RIRS (retrograde intrarenal surgery), examines and treats the whole upper tract through the natural route. Marshall first saw a ureteric stone with a fibreoptic scope in the 1960s.

### Instrument
| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Tip | About 4.5–7.5 Fr (shaft larger) |
| Working channel | 3.6 Fr (laser fibre, basket) |
| Deflection | ≥270° both ways |
| Types | **Fibreoptic** (image degrades with fibre breakage); **digital** (chip on tip, better image); **single-use** (no repair or sterilisation cost) |

### Indications
- **Stones:** renal <2 cm; lower pole <1 cm (or larger as an alternative to PCNL [percutaneous nephrolithotomy]); patients who must continue anticoagulants; failed SWL (shock wave lithotripsy); obesity; bleeding disorders.
- **Others:** diagnosis and laser ablation of low-grade UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma); filling defects, positive cytology; strictures and endopyelotomy; calyceal diverticulum; essential haematuria; foreign bodies.

### Technique
1. Sterile urine; single-dose antibiotic.
2. Cystoscopy, **safety guidewire**, retrograde pyelogram.
3. **Ureteral access sheath** (often 12/14 Fr): lowers intrarenal pressure and allows repeated passes, but injures the ureter in nearly half (severe in about 1 in 8).
4. Low-pressure irrigation. **Holmium:YAG or thulium fibre laser:** dusting or fragmenting and basketing.
5. Lower pole stones: move them to an upper calyx with a basket.
6. Inspect the ureter on withdrawal. Omit the stent if the case was uncomplicated with normal anatomy and no ureteric injury.

### Outcomes
- Stone-free rate is higher than SWL for renal stones <2 cm but falls with size and lower pole location. Larger stones may need staged procedures.
- Reports vary with the imaging used (CT is the strictest).

### Complications
- Stent symptoms (commonest); sepsis about 5%; stricture about 3%; perforation a few per cent; bleeding or subcapsular haematoma <1%; avulsion <1% (nephrostomy, delayed reconstruction); scope damage.

### Recent advances
- Suction access sheaths; intrarenal pressure sensors; thulium fibre laser; robotic fURS.

**Diagram to draw:** flexible scope through an access sheath, deflected into the lower pole calyx.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 3.6 Fr channel; ≥270° deflection.
> - Access sheath lowers pressure but may injure ureter.
> - First choice when anticoagulants cannot be stopped.

## Q3c. Testicular aspiration of sperms and assisted reproduction
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- TESA (testicular sperm aspiration) retrieves sperm from the testis with a needle for ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection). ICSI needs only one sperm per oocyte, so even a few sperm can lead to pregnancy.

### Indications
- **OA (obstructive azoospermia)** with normal spermatogenesis: CBAVD (congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens), unreconstructable obstruction, failed vasectomy reversal, or when epididymal aspiration fails.
- Anejaculation (spinal cord injury) after failed vibratory stimulation or electroejaculation.
- **NOA (non-obstructive azoospermia):** poor yield; micro-TESE preferred.

### Technique
1. Local anaesthesia with spermatic cord block.
2. A 23 G needle on a high-suction syringe enters the testis; many passes are made with suction to draw tubules into the needle.
3. Fresh wet examination for sperm; 3–4 aspirations until sperm are found (often 10–20 passes).
4. Cryopreserve spare sperm.

### Comparison of retrieval methods
| Technique | Site | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| PESA (percutaneous epididymal sperm aspiration) | Caput epididymis | OA | Few sperm; may obstruct epididymis |
| MESA (microsurgical epididymal sperm aspiration) | Single tubule at 16–25× | OA (best) | Many motile sperm; multiple frozen vials |
| **TESA** | Testis, needle | OA | Least invasive; immotile, few sperm |
| TESE (testicular sperm extraction) | Open biopsy | OA, NOA | Gold standard for diagnosis |
| **Micro-TESE** | Larger tubules chosen under microscope | **NOA (best)** | Sperm in about half |

### Assisted reproduction
- **IUI (intrauterine insemination):** processed sperm placed in the uterus; for mild male factor.
- **IVF (in vitro fertilisation):** oocytes and sperm combined in the laboratory.
- **ICSI:** single sperm injected into each oocyte. It is essential for surgically retrieved sperm, which have poor motility.
- Sperm preparation: density gradient or swim-up.
- Frozen retrieved sperm work as well as fresh, except possibly in spinal cord injury.

### Outcomes and concerns
- OA: retrieval almost always succeeds and ICSI pregnancy rates are good.
- NOA: retrieval depends on the cause (AZFc [azoospermia factor c] deletion 70%, Klinefelter 68%, Sertoli-cell-only 30%, **AZFa/AZFb deletion 0%**). More miscarriages occur.
- ICSI bypasses natural selection, so there is a risk of passing on genetic defects and more birth defects. Offer karyotype, Y-microdeletion and CFTR testing with counselling.
- Female burden: ovarian stimulation, multiple pregnancy, egg collection risks.
- **Complications of TESA:** haematoma, pain, vascular injury.

**Diagram to draw:** needle in testis with syringe suction; flow to ICSI.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - TESA: 23 G needle, suction, local anaesthesia; for OA.
> - NOA: micro-TESE best; AZFa/AZFb deletion means no sperm.
> - All surgically retrieved sperm need ICSI; cryopreserve extras.

## Q3d. Management of detrusor underactivity
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **DUA (detrusor underactivity), ICS (International Continence Society) 2002:** "a contraction of reduced strength and/or duration, resulting in prolonged bladder emptying and/or failure to achieve complete bladder emptying within a normal time span." **Acontractile detrusor:** no contraction.
- **UAB (underactive bladder):** the symptom complex: slow stream, hesitancy, straining, with or without incomplete emptying and storage symptoms.
- Found in about 40–48% of men over 70 having urodynamics.

### Causes
- **Neurogenic:** cauda equina, disc prolapse, spinal injury, diabetes, multiple system atrophy, early stroke, radical pelvic surgery.
- **Myogenic:** long-standing BOO (bladder outlet obstruction), diabetes, ischaemia, ageing.
- **Drugs:** anticholinergics, opioids. **Functional:** Fowler syndrome, dysfunctional voiding.

### Evaluation
- Bladder diary, flow rate, ultrasound PVR (post-void residual), neurological examination (S2–S4), spinal MRI if indicated, drug review, constipation.
- **Pressure-flow study** is the only accepted test. **BCI (bladder contractility index) = Pdet@Qmax + 5 Qmax:** <100 weak, 100–150 normal, >150 strong. Low Pdet with low Qmax.
- Ambulatory urodynamics if no contraction on standard study (the "bashful bladder").

### Management
- **Goals:** protect kidneys, prevent UTI (urinary tract infection) and stones, improve symptoms.

| Option | Details |
|---|---|
| Conservative | Timed and double voiding; treat constipation; stop causative drugs |
| **CISC (clean intermittent self-catheterisation)** | **Mainstay** for high PVR; fewer infections than indwelling catheter. Suprapubic catheter if CISC impossible |
| α-blockers | Lower outlet resistance; modest benefit |
| Parasympathomimetics (bethanechol, distigmine) | **Weak evidence**, systemic side effects (bronchospasm, rarely cardiac arrest); not routine |
| **SNM (sacral neuromodulation)** | For non-obstructive retention, especially Fowler syndrome; needs some detrusor function |
| Sacral anterior root stimulator (Brindley) | Complete spinal cord injury, with posterior rhizotomy |
| Intrasphincteric botulinum toxin | Relaxes sphincter; experimental |
| Outlet surgery (TURP, transurethral resection of the prostate) | Little help in low-PVR men; may free catheter-dependent fit men |
| Diversion | Mitrofanoff when urethral CISC fails; rarely ileal conduit |

- Credé or Valsalva voiding: avoid unless the sphincter is incompetent (risk of reflux).

### Investigational
- Intravesical electrotherapy, intravesical prostaglandins, TAC-302, latissimus dorsi detrusor myoplasty, muscle-derived stem cells.

**Diagram to draw:** pressure-flow plot on the Schäfer nomogram with BCI lines at 100 and 150.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - BCI = Pdet@Qmax + 5Qmax; <100 weak.
> - CISC is the mainstay; no effective drug.
> - SNM helps non-obstructive retention; intact sensation predicts response.

## Q3e. Retro peritoneal fibrosis medical management
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- RPF (retroperitoneal fibrosis): inflammatory fibrous tissue around the distal aorta (L4–L5) that encases the ureters. Age 40–60; male:female 2–3:1.
- **Idiopathic (about 70%):** chronic periaortitis, often autoimmune. Many cases are **IgG4-related disease** (raised serum IgG4, IgG4-positive plasma cells, storiform fibrosis).
- **Secondary (about 30%):** drugs (methysergide, ergots, β-blockers), malignancy (lymphoma commonest), radiotherapy, asbestos, infection (TB, tuberculosis).

### Evaluation before treatment
- Raised ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) and CRP (C-reactive protein), anaemia, renal failure; serum IgG4.
- CT/MRI: mass enveloping aorta and IVC (inferior vena cava), medial deviation of ureters. High T2 signal shows active disease. **PET-CT** is the most sensitive test of activity and useful for follow-up.
- Biopsy if malignancy is suspected; may be omitted when imaging is typical.

### Medical management
1. **Relieve obstruction first:** stents or nephrostomy (nephrostomy if uraemic and anuric); manage post-obstructive diuresis.
2. **Stop the culprit drug;** treat any secondary cause.
3. **Steroids are first line:**
   - Prednisone about 60 mg daily (about 1 mg/kg), tapered to 5 mg, total course up to 2 years.
   - About 80% respond: pain and symptoms settle in days, ESR falls, the mass shrinks.
   - Relapse on tapering is 25–50%. Active inflammation predicts response; burnt-out fibrosis responds poorly.
4. **Tamoxifen:** thought to inhibit fibroblasts. **Vaglio RCT (randomised controlled trial; Lancet 2011):** relapse 17% with prednisone vs 50% with tamoxifen, so steroids remain first line.
5. **Steroid-sparing or second line:** mycophenolate mofetil (with steroids), azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, ciclosporin, colchicine; medroxyprogesterone.
6. **Rituximab:** for refractory or IgG4-related disease.

### Monitoring
- Symptoms, ESR/CRP, creatinine, IgG4, ultrasound for hydronephrosis, CT/MRI or PET-CT every few months. Remove stents when the ureters drain.
- Watch for steroid side effects (diabetes, osteoporosis: give calcium, vitamin D).

### When medical therapy fails
- About two-thirds clear obstruction with drainage plus drugs; about 15% need **ureterolysis**: bilateral, with biopsies, and the ureters protected by intraperitonealisation or omental wrap (open, laparoscopic or robotic). Autotransplantation is rarely needed.

**Diagram to draw:** mass around aorta at L4–L5 pulling both ureters medially.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Drain first, then steroids (about 80% respond).
> - Vaglio 2011: prednisone better than tamoxifen (relapse 17% vs 50%).
> - Rituximab for IgG4-related or refractory disease; ureterolysis for about 15%.

## Q3f. Female Circumcision
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Assumed to mean female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C). This topic is not covered in the reference chapters; the answer follows WHO (World Health Organization) definitions.*

### Definition
- **FGM (female genital mutilation):** all procedures that partly or totally remove the external female genitalia, or injure the female genital organs, for non-medical reasons (WHO).
- More than 200 million living women and girls are affected, mainly in Africa, the Middle East and Asia. It is usually done in childhood. In India it is practised in some communities (e.g. "khatna" among Dawoodi Bohras).

### WHO classification
| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| I (clitoridectomy) | Partial or total removal of the clitoral glans and/or prepuce (hood) |
| II (excision) | Removal of the clitoral glans and labia minora, with or without the labia majora |
| III (infibulation) | Narrowing of the vaginal opening by cutting and joining the labia to form a seal, with or without clitoral excision |
| IV (other) | All other harmful procedures: pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, cauterisation |

### Complications
- **Immediate:** severe pain, bleeding, shock, infection (including tetanus), **acute urinary retention**, urethral injury, death.
- **Urological (late):** slow or obstructed stream, dysuria, **recurrent UTI (urinary tract infection)**, urethral stenosis, meatal obstruction under the infibulation scar, incontinence, **vesicovaginal fistula** after obstructed labour.
- **Gynaecological:** epidermoid inclusion cysts, keloid, clitoral neuroma, dysmenorrhoea, haematocolpos, infertility.
- **Sexual:** dyspareunia, apareunia.
- **Obstetric:** prolonged or obstructed labour, perineal tears, postpartum haemorrhage, caesarean section, newborn death.
- **Psychological:** anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder.

### Evaluation
- Sensitive, non-judgemental history; examination to classify the type; urinalysis, flow rate and post-void residual if voiding is affected; urethrocystoscopy for suspected stenosis or fistula.

### Management
- **Deinfibulation (type III):** midline anterior incision of the scar under local or regional anaesthesia to uncover the urethral meatus and vaginal opening. Edges are sutured separately.
  - **Timing:** before marriage or pregnancy, in the antenatal period, or during labour. It relieves retention, recurrent UTI and dyspareunia.
  - **Reinfibulation should not be done** (illegal in some countries, e.g. UK).
- Excise inclusion cysts; treat urethral stenosis (dilation or meatoplasty); repair fistula.
- Psychological and sexual counselling. Clitoral reconstruction is offered in some centres (limited evidence).

### Legal and ethical aspects
- WHO and the UN (United Nations) condemn FGM as a violation of human rights. WHO opposes "medicalisation" (FGM by health workers).
- It is illegal in many countries (e.g. UK FGM Act 2003, with mandatory reporting by health professionals for girls under 18). India has no specific FGM law; the practice has been challenged in the Supreme Court.
- 6 February: International Day of Zero Tolerance for FGM.
