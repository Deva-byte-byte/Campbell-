## Q1. Describe in detail the management of T1G3 TCC of urinary bladder?
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- T1G3 is high-grade (HG) urothelial carcinoma (the current name for TCC, transitional cell carcinoma) that invades the lamina propria but not the muscularis propria.
- T1 is 20–30% of NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer) and its most dangerous form: recurrence >80%, progression to muscle invasion 30–50%.
- Grade, not stage, drives progression. So every HG T1 is **high risk** (AUA [American Urological Association]/SUO [Society of Urologic Oncology] and EAU [European Association of Urology]).

### Risk factors for progression
| Factor | Significance |
|---|---|
| Concurrent CIS (carcinoma in situ) | Second most important factor after grade |
| Depth of invasion (substaging) | Invasion deep to the muscularis mucosae is worse; T1a/T1b proposed, no agreed method |
| LVI (lymphovascular invasion) | Suggests occult spread |
| Variant histology | Micropapillary (any amount), plasmacytoid, sarcomatoid, nested |
| Size >3 cm, multifocal, recurrent | More residual tumour and progression |
| Prostatic urethra, diverticulum | Poor BCG contact; diverticulum lacks muscle |
| Residual T1 at re-TURBT | Progression up to 80% |
| HG T1 at 3 months after BCG | Progression about 82% |
- **Also know:** EORTC (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer) and CUETO risk tables; hydronephrosis suggests muscle invasion.

### Evaluation
- Cystoscopy, urine cytology, CT urography (upper tract tumour <5%), bimanual examination under anaesthesia.

### Step 1: Complete, good-quality TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour)
- Resect all visible tumour **with detrusor muscle** in the specimen; send the base separately.
- Blue-light cystoscopy or NBI (narrow-band imaging) finds more CIS. **En bloc resection** keeps orientation for substaging.
- Biopsy the prostatic urethra if tumour is at the bladder neck or trigone, or with CIS.

### Step 2: Re-TURBT (restaging) in all T1
- Within **2–6 weeks**, before BCG (bacille Calmette-Guérin).
- **Why:** residual tumour in ≥40%; upstaging to muscle invasion in 25–30% (about half if no muscle in the first specimen). Divrik's RCT (randomised controlled trial) showed better recurrence-free and progression-free survival.

```
HG T1 on TURBT → re-TURBT at 2–6 weeks
   ├─ ≥T2                          → neoadjuvant chemotherapy + cystectomy
   ├─ residual T1 / very high risk → offer early radical cystectomy
   └─ T0, Ta or CIS                → BCG induction + 3-year maintenance
```

### Step 3a: BCG – standard for most patients
- **Mechanism:** BCG attaches to fibronectin, triggers cytokines (IFN-γ [interferon-gamma], IL [interleukin]-2) and a **Th1 (T-helper 1)** response that kills tumour. It is the **only intravesical agent that reduces progression**.
- Start 2–4 weeks after TURBT; induction weekly ×6.
- **Maintenance (SWOG [Southwest Oncology Group], Lamm 2000):** 3 weekly doses at 3 and 6 months, then every 6 months to **3 years**. Median recurrence-free survival 76.8 vs 35.7 months. **EORTC 30962:** full dose for 3 years in high risk.
- **Contraindications:** gross haematuria, traumatic catheterisation, symptomatic UTI (urinary tract infection), previous BCG sepsis.
- **Toxicity:** cystitis common; BCG sepsis needs isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol and steroids.

### Step 3b: Early (immediate) radical cystectomy
- **Rationale:** clinical T1 is understaged in one-third to one-half. Patients who progress under surveillance do worse than those presenting with muscle invasion. Immediate cystectomy gives about 92% 10-year disease-free survival.
- **AUA:** offer to fit patients with persistent HG T1 on re-resection, or T1 with CIS, LVI or variant histology.
- **EAU very-high-risk group (2021 model; risk factors: age >70, multiple papillary tumours, diameter ≥3 cm):** T1 HG with CIS plus ≥1 risk factor; T1 HG without CIS but with all three; also LVI, variant histology, CIS of the prostatic urethra. Offer immediate cystectomy; BCG if declined or unfit.
- Also: diverticular tumour, tumour not controllable endoscopically.

### Step 4: BCG failure
- **Adequate BCG for T1:** ≥5 of 6 induction doses.
- **BCG-unresponsive:** HG T1 at the 3-month check after induction; persistent or recurrent CIS or HG Ta/T1 despite adequate BCG; or HG relapse within 6 months (Ta/T1) or 12 months (CIS) of the last dose.
- **Standard:** timely radical cystectomy (EAU, AUA).
- **Unfit or refusing:** pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-057: CIS complete response 41%); nadofaragene firadenovec (53%); N-803 with BCG; gemcitabine–docetaxel; TAR-200; trials.
- **Also know:** BCG-intolerant; delayed relapse may respond to BCG rechallenge.

### Follow-up (AUA/SUO high risk)
- Cystoscopy and cytology every 3–4 months for 2 years, 6-monthly for 2 years, then yearly for life.
- Upper-tract imaging yearly for 2 years.
- Positive cytology with normal cystoscopy: check prostatic urethra and upper tracts.

### Recent advances
- Checkpoint inhibitors with BCG in BCG-naive disease (trials); urine markers predicting BCG response.

**Diagram to draw:** bladder wall layers showing Ta, T1a/T1b (above and below the muscularis mucosae) and T2.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Re-TURBT in all T1 at 2–6 weeks; residual T1 means progression up to 80%.
> - BCG induction + 3-year maintenance (SWOG); only agent that reduces progression.
> - Very high risk (CIS, LVI, variants, prostatic urethra): offer early cystectomy.
> - BCG-unresponsive: cystectomy; pembrolizumab or nadofaragene if unfit.

## Q2. Describe indication, mechanism of stone fragmentation and current status of ESWL?
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- ESWL (extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy), now SWL, breaks stones with focused acoustic shock waves generated outside the body. First human use 1980 (Dornier HM1).
- A shock wave is a short pulse: peak positive pressure 30–100 MPa, then a negative tail of about −10 MPa.

### Components of a lithotripter
- Energy source at F1 (first focus); focusing system; coupling (water or gel); fluoroscopy and/or ultrasound to place the stone at F2. A focal zone wider than the stone improves breakage.

| Energy source | Principle | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electrohydraulic (HM3) | Underwater spark; ellipsoid reflector | Most effective | Electrode wear; variable shocks |
| Piezoelectric | Ceramic elements on a spherical dish | Long life; little pain | Low energy; tiny focal zone |
| Electromagnetic (modern machines) | Coil moves a metal membrane; lens focuses | Consistent; high energy at F2 | — |

### Mechanisms of stone fragmentation
- Each shock creates stress inside the stone. Above a critical value, cracks form; repeated shocks make them grow and join (**fatigue**).

| Mechanism | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Spallation** | Wave reflects and inverts at the back surface of the stone; tension breaks off the distal face |
| **Shear and tear (superfocusing)** | Waves inside the stone reflect and focus; cracks form where shear peaks |
| **Quasistatic squeezing** | Wave travels faster in stone than fluid; a ring of pressure compresses the stone |
| **Dynamic squeezing** | Shear waves at the stone edges combine; strongest force in initial breakage |
| **Cavitation** | Negative phase forms bubbles; collapse erodes the surface. Excess bubbles shield the stone and injure tissue |

### Indications
- **Renal (AUA/EAU):** <2 cm outside the lower pole and lower pole <1 cm (SWL or URS [ureteroscopy]); EAU: 10–20 mm equal to endourology; >20 mm only after PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy).
- **Ureteric:** any level, best <10 mm; emergency SWL within 48 hours works well. Patients avoiding anaesthesia and stents; children.
- **Poor choices:** >2 cm or staghorn; lower pole >1 cm (**Lower Pole I**: stone-free 21% SWL vs 91% PCNL); cystine, brushite, calcium oxalate monohydrate; infection stones.

### Contraindications
- **Absolute:** pregnancy; uncorrected coagulopathy or antithrombotics; untreated UTI (urinary tract infection); obstruction distal to the stone; nearby arterial aneurysm (aortic >4 cm); severe uncontrolled hypertension.
- **Relative:** obesity or skeletal deformity preventing targeting; pacemaker.

### Predictors of success
- **Stone:** size (most important); density <900 HU (Hounsfield units) clears best, >1000 HU predicts failure; composition; impaction.
- **Patient:** SSD (skin-to-stone distance) >10 cm predicts failure.
- **Lower pole anatomy:** steep infundibulopelvic angle, long (>10 mm) or narrow (<5 mm) infundibulum.
- **Triple D score:** density, volume and skin-to-stone distance.

### Technique to optimise results
1. Treat infection; stop antithrombotics; control blood pressure. No routine stent: it does not improve clearance.
2. Good analgesia or sedation, because stone movement reduces breakage.
3. **Coupling:** gel as a mound, free of air; air pockets cut fragmentation by up to two-fifths.
4. **Rate 60–90 shocks/min** beats 120/min: bubbles clear between shocks, so less shielding and renal injury.
5. **Ramping:** start at low energy and step up; this causes protective vasoconstriction.
6. Monitor with imaging; more than two sessions add little.
7. **Adjuncts:** α-blockers; percussion and inversion for lower pole fragments.

### Complications
- Renal haematoma (risk: age, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, high energy, many shocks); steinstrasse (risk rises with burden >2 cm²); colic, sepsis; arrhythmia (ECG [electrocardiogram] gating); rare liver, spleen or bowel injury.
- Long-term hypertension, diabetes and infertility are **not** proven.

### Current status
- **Declining use worldwide.** URS has grown with small digital scopes and lasers, giving higher single-session stone-free rates. Newer lithotripters are also less effective than the HM3.
- **SWL vs URS:** SWL has fewer complications, no stent or general anaesthesia, lower cost, but more retreatment. Guidelines still list it first-line for small renal and ureteric stones.

### Recent advances
- **Burst wave lithotripsy:** bursts of 5–100 ultrasound cycles at 4–8 MPa; less cavitation; done awake; uniform fragments.
- **Ultrasonic propulsion** moves fragments to help them pass.

**Diagram to draw:** ellipsoid reflector with F1 (spark) and F2 (stone), with spallation and squeezing arrows on the stone.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Spallation, squeezing, shear, cavitation; dynamic squeezing strongest.
> - Failure: >1000 HU, SSD >10 cm, lower pole >1 cm (Lower Pole I: 21% vs 91%).
> - 60–90 shocks/min with ramping; good coupling.
> - Use is declining as URS grows; burst wave lithotripsy is coming.

## Q3a. Management of Urethral meatal stenosis.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and causes
- Narrowing of the external urethral meatus below the normal calibre for age.

| Age | Normal calibre |
|---|---|
| Under 4 months | 6–10 Fr |
| 1–12 months | 10–12 Fr |
| 1–10 years | 12–14 Fr |
| Over 10 years | 14–18 Fr |

- **Children:** almost always after **neonatal circumcision**: wet nappies irritate the exposed meatus (ammoniacal meatitis) and the ventral lips fuse into a thin membrane. Rarely congenital with hypospadias.
- **Adults:** LS (lichen sclerosus, formerly BXO [balanitis xerotica obliterans]); catheter or instrument trauma; failed hypospadias repair. Usually the tip of a **fossa navicularis stricture**.

### Clinical features
- Thin, forceful stream deflected **upwards**; straining, frequency, incomplete emptying; pain at the start of voiding.

### Evaluation
- Watch the child void; calibrate with a bougie à boule. Uroflowmetry. Urethrography only for UTI (urinary tract infection) or suspected proximal lesion.
- Adults: **retrograde urethrogram (RUG) before meatal surgery**, as a proximal stricture often coexists, especially in LS.

### Management
| Situation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Meatitis, early narrowing (child) | Topical 0.05% clobetasol; gentle dilation; nappy hygiene |
| Symptomatic stenosis (child) | **Ventral meatotomy** (EMLA [eutectic mixture of local anaesthetics] cream in clinic, or general anaesthesia); parents spread the edges with a dilator tip 3 times daily for 7–10 days |
| Simple adult stenosis | Ventral meatotomy with 3 sutures (apex and each side), mucosa to glans; dilator afterwards |
| Scarred or recurrent | Dorsal meatotomy (Y-V plasty after excising the scar ridge); effective but poorer cosmesis |
| Fossa navicularis involved | Urethroplasty: penile skin island flap (Jordan, De Sy), not in LS |
| **LS** | Topical steroid; extended meatotomy; **buccal mucosa graft** (never genital skin); perineal urethrostomy for pananterior disease |
| Unfit or elderly | Self-dilation with clobetasol as lubricant |

### Complications
- Bleeding, restenosis (commoner in LS), spraying.

**Also know:** biopsy atypical LS (squamous carcinoma risk up to 8%).

**Diagram to draw:** ventral meatotomy: crush, incise ventrally, suture mucosa to glans at apex and both sides.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Child: post-circumcision ventral membrane; ventral meatotomy and parental dilation.
> - Adult: RUG first; often a fossa navicularis stricture.
> - LS: steroid, buccal mucosa, never genital skin.

## Q3b. Flexible Ureteroscopy.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Covered in part in 2017-Summer Paper 1, Q3b; written in full here.*

### Introduction
- Flexible ureteroscopy (fURS), or RIRS (retrograde intrarenal surgery), examines and treats the whole upper tract through the natural route.

### Instrument
| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Tip | About 4.5–7.5 Fr (shaft larger) |
| Working channel | 3.6 Fr (laser fibre, basket) |
| Deflection | ≥270° both ways |
| Types | **Fibreoptic** (image degrades with fibre breakage); **digital** (chip on tip, better image); **single-use** (no repair or sterilisation cost) |

### Indications
- **Stones:** renal <2 cm; lower pole <1 cm (or larger as an alternative to PCNL [percutaneous nephrolithotomy]); patients who must continue anticoagulants; failed SWL (shock wave lithotripsy); obesity; bleeding disorders.
- **Others:** diagnosis and laser ablation of low-grade UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma); filling defects, positive cytology; strictures and endopyelotomy; calyceal diverticulum; essential haematuria; foreign bodies.

### Technique
1. Sterile urine; single-dose antibiotic.
2. Cystoscopy, **safety guidewire**, retrograde pyelogram.
3. **Ureteral access sheath** (often 12/14 Fr): lowers intrarenal pressure and allows repeated passes, but injures the ureter in nearly half (severe in about 1 in 8).
4. Low-pressure irrigation. **Holmium:YAG (yttrium-aluminium-garnet) or thulium fibre laser:** dusting or fragmenting and basketing.
5. Lower pole stones: move them to an upper calyx with a basket.
6. Inspect the ureter on withdrawal. Omit the stent if the case was uncomplicated with normal anatomy and no ureteric injury.

### Outcomes
- Stone-free rate is higher than SWL for renal stones <2 cm but falls with size and lower pole location. Larger stones may need staged procedures.
- Reported rates depend on the imaging used (CT strictest).

### Complications
- Stent symptoms (commonest); sepsis about 5%; stricture about 3%; perforation a few per cent; bleeding or subcapsular haematoma <1%; avulsion <1% (nephrostomy, delayed reconstruction); scope damage.

### Recent advances
- Suction access sheaths; intrarenal pressure sensors; thulium fibre laser; robotic fURS.

**Diagram to draw:** flexible scope through an access sheath, deflected into the lower pole calyx.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 3.6 Fr channel; ≥270° deflection.
> - Access sheath lowers pressure but may injure ureter.
> - First choice when anticoagulants cannot be stopped.

## Q3c. Testicular aspiration of sperms and assisted reproduction
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- TESA (testicular sperm aspiration) retrieves sperm from the testis by needle for ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection), which needs only one sperm per oocyte.

### Indications
- **OA (obstructive azoospermia)** with normal spermatogenesis: CBAVD (congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens), unreconstructable obstruction, failed vasectomy reversal, or when epididymal aspiration fails.
- Anejaculation (spinal cord injury) after failed vibratory stimulation or electroejaculation.
- **NOA (non-obstructive azoospermia):** poor yield; micro-TESE preferred.

### Technique
1. Local anaesthesia with spermatic cord block.
2. 23 G needle on a high-suction syringe; many passes draw tubules into the needle.
3. Wet examination at once; repeat until sperm are found. Cryopreserve spare sperm.

### Comparison of retrieval methods
| Technique | Site | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| PESA (percutaneous epididymal sperm aspiration) | Caput epididymis | OA | Few sperm; may obstruct epididymis |
| MESA (microsurgical epididymal sperm aspiration) | Single tubule at 16–25× | OA (best) | Many motile sperm; multiple frozen vials |
| **TESA** | Testis, needle | OA | Least invasive; immotile, few sperm |
| TESE (testicular sperm extraction) | Open biopsy | OA, NOA | Gold standard for diagnosis |
| **Micro-TESE** | Larger tubules chosen under microscope | **NOA (best)** | Sperm in about half |

### Assisted reproduction
- **IUI (intrauterine insemination):** washed sperm into the uterus; mild male factor.
- **IVF (in vitro fertilisation):** oocytes and sperm combined in the laboratory.
- **ICSI:** one sperm injected into each oocyte; essential for surgically retrieved sperm (poor motility).
- Frozen retrieved sperm work as well as fresh, except possibly in spinal cord injury.

### Outcomes and concerns
- OA: retrieval almost always succeeds; good pregnancy rates.
- NOA: retrieval depends on the cause (AZFc [azoospermia factor c] deletion 70%, Klinefelter 68%, Sertoli-cell-only 30%, **AZFa/AZFb deletion 0%**). More miscarriages occur.
- ICSI bypasses natural selection: risk of transmitting genetic defects. Offer karyotype, Y-microdeletion and CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) testing.
- Female burden: ovarian stimulation, multiple pregnancy.
- **TESA complications:** haematoma, pain, vascular injury.

**Diagram to draw:** needle in testis with syringe suction; flow to ICSI.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - TESA: 23 G needle, suction, local anaesthesia; for OA.
> - NOA: micro-TESE best; AZFa/AZFb deletion means no sperm.
> - All surgically retrieved sperm need ICSI; cryopreserve extras.

## Q3d. Management of detrusor underactivity
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **DUA (detrusor underactivity), ICS (International Continence Society) 2002:** "a contraction of reduced strength and/or duration, resulting in prolonged bladder emptying and/or failure to achieve complete bladder emptying within a normal time span." **Acontractile detrusor:** no contraction.
- **UAB (underactive bladder):** symptoms of slow stream, hesitancy and straining, with or without incomplete emptying. Found in 40–48% of men over 70 having urodynamics.

### Causes
- **Neurogenic:** cauda equina, disc prolapse, spinal injury, diabetes, multiple system atrophy, early stroke, radical pelvic surgery.
- **Myogenic:** long-standing BOO (bladder outlet obstruction), diabetes, ischaemia, ageing.
- **Drugs:** anticholinergics, opioids. **Functional:** Fowler syndrome, dysfunctional voiding.

### Evaluation
- Bladder diary, flow rate, PVR (post-void residual), sacral neurological examination, spinal MRI if indicated, drug review.
- **Pressure-flow study** is the only accepted test. **BCI (bladder contractility index) = Pdet@Qmax (detrusor pressure at maximum flow) + 5 × Qmax:** <100 weak, 100–150 normal, >150 strong. Low Pdet with low Qmax.
- Ambulatory urodynamics if no contraction on standard study (the "bashful bladder").

### Management
- **Goals:** protect kidneys, prevent UTI (urinary tract infection) and stones, improve symptoms.

| Option | Details |
|---|---|
| Conservative | Timed and double voiding; treat constipation; stop causative drugs |
| **CISC (clean intermittent self-catheterisation)** | **Mainstay** for high PVR; fewer infections than indwelling catheter. Suprapubic catheter if CISC impossible |
| α-blockers | Lower outlet resistance; modest benefit |
| Parasympathomimetics (bethanechol, distigmine) | **Weak evidence**, systemic side effects (bronchospasm, rarely cardiac arrest); not routine |
| **SNM (sacral neuromodulation)** | For non-obstructive retention, especially Fowler syndrome; needs some detrusor function |
| Sacral anterior root stimulator (Brindley) | Complete spinal cord injury, with posterior rhizotomy |
| Intrasphincteric botulinum toxin | Experimental |
| Outlet surgery (TURP, transurethral resection of the prostate) | Little help in low-PVR men; may free catheter-dependent fit men |
| Diversion | Mitrofanoff when urethral CISC fails; rarely ileal conduit |

- Avoid Credé or Valsalva voiding (reflux risk).

### Investigational
- Intravesical electrotherapy, intravesical prostaglandins, TAC-302, latissimus dorsi detrusor myoplasty, muscle-derived stem cells.

**Diagram to draw:** pressure-flow plot on the Schäfer nomogram with BCI lines at 100 and 150.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - BCI = Pdet@Qmax + 5Qmax; <100 weak.
> - CISC is the mainstay; no effective drug.
> - SNM helps non-obstructive retention; intact sensation predicts response.

## Q3e. Retro peritoneal fibrosis medical management
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- RPF (retroperitoneal fibrosis): inflammatory fibrous tissue around the distal aorta (L4–L5) that encases the ureters. Age 40–60; male:female 2–3:1.
- **Idiopathic (about 70%):** chronic periaortitis, often autoimmune. Many cases are **IgG4-related disease** (raised serum IgG4, IgG4-positive plasma cells, storiform fibrosis).
- **Secondary (about 30%):** drugs (methysergide, ergots, β-blockers), malignancy (lymphoma commonest), radiotherapy, asbestos, infection (TB, tuberculosis).

### Evaluation before treatment
- Raised ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) and CRP (C-reactive protein), anaemia, renal failure; serum IgG4.
- CT/MRI: mass around aorta and IVC (inferior vena cava), medially deviated ureters; high T2 signal means activity. **PET-CT** is the most sensitive test of activity.
- Biopsy if malignancy is suspected.

### Medical management
1. **Relieve obstruction first:** stents or nephrostomy (nephrostomy if uraemic and anuric); manage post-obstructive diuresis.
2. **Stop the culprit drug;** treat any secondary cause.
3. **Steroids are first line:**
   - Prednisone about 60 mg daily (about 1 mg/kg), tapered to 5 mg, total course up to 2 years.
   - About 80% respond: pain and symptoms settle in days, ESR falls, the mass shrinks.
   - Relapse on tapering is 25–50%. Active inflammation predicts response; burnt-out fibrosis responds poorly.
4. **Tamoxifen:** thought to inhibit fibroblasts. **Vaglio RCT (randomised controlled trial; Lancet 2011):** relapse 17% with prednisone vs 50% with tamoxifen, so steroids remain first line.
5. **Steroid-sparing or second line:** mycophenolate mofetil (with steroids), azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, ciclosporin, colchicine; medroxyprogesterone.
6. **Rituximab:** for refractory or IgG4-related disease.

### Monitoring
- Symptoms, ESR/CRP, creatinine, ultrasound, periodic CT/MRI or PET-CT. Remove stents when ureters drain.
- Watch steroid side effects (diabetes, osteoporosis).

### When medical therapy fails
- About two-thirds clear obstruction with drainage plus drugs; about 15% need **ureterolysis**: bilateral, with biopsies, and the ureters protected by intraperitonealisation or omental wrap (open, laparoscopic or robotic). Autotransplantation is rarely needed.

**Diagram to draw:** mass around aorta at L4–L5 pulling both ureters medially.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Drain first, then steroids (about 80% respond).
> - Vaglio 2011: prednisone better than tamoxifen (relapse 17% vs 50%).
> - Rituximab for IgG4-related or refractory disease; ureterolysis for about 15%.

## Q3f. Female Circumcision
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Assumed to mean female genital mutilation/cutting. Not covered in the reference chapters; answer follows WHO (World Health Organization) definitions.*

### Definition
- **FGM (female genital mutilation):** all procedures that partly or totally remove the external female genitalia, or otherwise injure them, for non-medical reasons (WHO).
- Over 200 million living women are affected, mainly in Africa, the Middle East and Asia; usually in childhood. Practised in some Indian communities ("khatna").

### WHO classification
| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| I (clitoridectomy) | Partial or total removal of the clitoral glans and/or prepuce |
| II (excision) | Removal of the clitoral glans and labia minora, with or without the labia majora |
| III (infibulation) | Narrowing of the vaginal opening by cutting and joining the labia into a seal, with or without clitoral excision |
| IV (other) | Pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, cauterisation |

### Complications
- **Immediate:** pain, haemorrhage, shock, infection (tetanus), **acute urinary retention**, urethral injury, death.
- **Urological:** slow stream, dysuria, **recurrent UTI (urinary tract infection)**, meatal obstruction under the scar, urethral stenosis, incontinence, **vesicovaginal fistula** after obstructed labour.
- **Gynaecological and sexual:** inclusion cysts, keloid, dyspareunia, infertility.
- **Obstetric:** obstructed labour, perineal tears, postpartum haemorrhage, neonatal death.
- **Psychological:** anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder.

### Evaluation
- Sensitive history; examination to classify type; flow rate, residual urine; cystoscopy if fistula suspected.

### Management
- **Deinfibulation (type III):** anterior midline incision of the scar under local or regional anaesthesia to expose the urethral meatus and introitus; raw edges oversewn.
  - **Timing:** ideally before pregnancy, or antenatally, or in labour. Relieves retention, UTI and dyspareunia.
  - **Never reinfibulate** (illegal in the UK).
- Excise cysts; treat stenosis; repair fistula; counselling. Clitoral reconstruction has limited evidence.

### Legal and ethical aspects
- WHO and the UN (United Nations) condemn FGM as a human rights violation; WHO opposes its performance by health workers.
- Illegal in many countries (e.g. UK FGM Act 2003, with mandatory reporting in girls under 18). India has no specific law.

**Diagram to draw:** vulva showing types I–III and the deinfibulation incision line.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - WHO types: I clitoridectomy, II excision, III infibulation, IV others.
> - Urological: retention, recurrent UTI, stenosis, fistula.
> - Deinfibulation for type III; never reinfibulate.

- All surgically retrieved sperm need ICSI; cryopreserve extras.

#### Q3d. Management of detrusor underactivity (SAQ)

- BCI = Pdet@Qmax + 5Qmax; <100 weak.
- CISC is the mainstay; no effective drug.
- SNM helps non-obstructive retention; intact sensation predicts response.

#### Q3e. Retro peritoneal fibrosis medical management (SAQ)

- Drain first, then steroids (about 80% respond).
- Vaglio 2011: prednisone better than tamoxifen (relapse 17% vs 50%).
- Rituximab for IgG4-related or refractory disease; ureterolysis for about 15%.

#### Q3f. Female Circumcision (SAQ)

- WHO types: I clitoridectomy, II excision, III infibulation, IV others.
- Urological: retention, recurrent UTI, stenosis, fistula.
- Deinfibulation for type III; never reinfibulate.
