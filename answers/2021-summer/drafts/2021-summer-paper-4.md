## Q1. Role and impact of Radiotherapy methods for management of focal and advanced carcinoma prostate?
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2017-Summer Paper 2, Q3f)

*"Focal" is taken to mean localised (organ-confined and locally advanced) disease.*

### Radiobiology
- RT (radiotherapy) causes DNA double-strand breaks. Prostate cancer has a **low α/β ratio (~1.5 Gy)**, lower than the rectum (~3 Gy). So larger fractions kill more tumour for the same late toxicity: the basis of **hypofractionation**.

### EBRT (external beam radiotherapy) techniques
| Step | Advance |
|---|---|
| 3D-CRT (three-dimensional conformal RT) | CT-planned beams shaped to the prostate |
| **IMRT (intensity-modulated RT)/VMAT (volumetric modulated arc therapy)** | Inverse planning, steep dose gradients; less rectal toxicity; now standard |
| **IGRT (image-guided RT)** | Daily fiducials or cone-beam CT (computed tomography); margins 3–7 mm |
| MRI fusion, PSMA (prostate-specific membrane antigen) PET (positron emission tomography) | Better targeting; PSMA PET changes plans in up to half of high-risk men |

| Schedule | Dose per fraction | Example | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conventional | 1.8–2 Gy | 78 Gy in 39 | Dose escalation (RTOG 0126): better PSA control, no survival gain |
| **Moderate hypofractionation** | 2.25–4 Gy | **60 Gy in 20** | **CHHiP, PROFIT**: non-inferior |
| **SBRT (stereotactic body RT)** | ≥5 Gy | 36.25 Gy in 5 | **HYPO-RT-PC** non-inferior; **PACE-B** equal toxicity |

### Brachytherapy
| Feature | LDR (low dose rate) | HDR (high dose rate) |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Permanent ¹²⁵I (145 Gy) or ¹⁰³Pd seeds | Temporary ¹⁹²Ir via afterloader |
| Monotherapy | Low and favourable intermediate risk | 27 Gy in 2 or 34–38 Gy in 4 (single 19 Gy fraction fails) |
| Quality | Post-implant CT: **D90** (dose covering 90% of gland) **>90%** of prescription | Real-time planning |

- **Technique:** TRUS (transrectal ultrasound)-guided perineal template; peripheral loading spares the urethra.
- **Boost** (EBRT 45 Gy + implant) for unfavourable intermediate/high risk: **ASCENDE-RT** halved biochemical failure, with more grade 3 urinary toxicity (18% vs 5%). **RTOG 0232:** adding EBRT to implant gave no benefit in intermediate risk.
- **Contraindications:** IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score) >20, Qmax <10 mL/s, recent TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate), pubic arch interference, prior pelvic RT, inflammatory bowel disease.

### ADT (androgen deprivation therapy) with RT
- **Why:** ADT radiosensitises (less DNA repair) and causes apoptosis; RT up-regulates androgen-receptor signalling, which ADT blocks.

| Risk group | ADT with EBRT |
|---|---|
| Low, favourable intermediate | None |
| Unfavourable intermediate | **4–6 months** |
| High/locally advanced | **18–36 months** (EORTC 22863, PCS IV); add **abiraterone** if very high risk (STAMPEDE) |

- **RT added to ADT** improves survival in locally advanced disease: **SPCG-7, NCIC PR.3/MRC PR07** (about 8–10% absolute).

### After radical prostatectomy
- **Adjuvant RT** (pT3/positive margin): EORTC 22911, ARO 96-02 (biochemical), **SWOG 8794** (metastasis-free and overall survival).
- **RADICALS-RT, RAVES, GETUG-AFU 17** (ARTISTIC meta-analysis): **early salvage at PSA (prostate-specific antigen) 0.1–0.2 ng/mL** equals adjuvant RT and spares over 60% of men RT.
- Salvage dose ≥64–66 Gy. **RTOG 9601:** 2 years bicalutamide improved overall survival. **SPPORT:** pelvic nodes + short ADT improve freedom from progression.

### Advanced and metastatic disease
| Setting | RT role | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Low-volume de novo mHSPC (metastatic hormone-sensitive) | **RT to prostate** + ADT | **STAMPEDE arm H**: overall survival HR (hazard ratio) 0.68 (3-year 73% → 81%); none in high volume |
| Oligometastases (≤3–5) | SBRT to all lesions (MDT, metastasis-directed therapy) | STOMP, **ORIOLE** (less progression), SABR-COMET |
| Painful bone metastases | Palliative EBRT, e.g. **8 Gy single fraction**; cord compression | Equal pain relief to multiple fractions |
| mCRPC (metastatic castration-resistant), bone-only symptomatic | **Radium-223** (α-emitter, calcium mimic, range <100 μm) 50 kBq/kg 4-weekly × 6 | **ALSYMPCA**: overall survival 14.9 vs 11.3 months; fewer skeletal events |
| mCRPC, PSMA-positive, after taxane and ARPI (androgen-receptor pathway inhibitor) | **¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA-617** (β-emitter radioligand) | **VISION**: survival 15.3 vs 11.3 months (HR 0.62); **TheraP**: better PSA response than cabazitaxel |

- **Never combine radium-223 with abiraterone** (more deaths and fractures).
- **Also know:** protons (no proven gain over IMRT); strontium-89, samarium-153 (pain palliation); elective pelvic nodal RT (RTOG 94-13, GETUG-01: no survival gain).

### Response assessment
- **Phoenix failure:** PSA nadir + 2 ng/mL. **Benign bounce** after brachytherapy in about a third: watch, do not salvage.
- Biopsy-proven local failure: salvage prostatectomy, brachytherapy, HIFU (high-intensity focused ultrasound) or cryotherapy.

### Toxicity
| Type | Features and management |
|---|---|
| Acute (weeks) | Frequency, dysuria, diarrhoea; α-blockers |
| Late GI (gastrointestinal) | **Radiation proctitis** at 6–18 months: sucralfate/steroid enemas, argon plasma coagulation, hyperbaric oxygen |
| Late GU (genitourinary) | Cystitis, haematuria, stricture |
| Sexual | ED (erectile dysfunction), vascular; worse with ADT; PDE5 (phosphodiesterase type 5) inhibitors work in most |
| Rare | Second cancers, fistula |

### Recent advances
- **Hydrogel rectal spacer** (late rectal toxicity 2% vs 7%); MR-linac adaptive SBRT; **FLAME** focal boost to the MRI lesion; PSMA radioligands moving earlier (PSMAfore).

**Diagram to draw:** TRUS-guided perineal template brachytherapy.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - α/β ~1.5 Gy: basis of 60 Gy/20 (CHHiP) and SBRT 36.25 Gy/5.
> - ADT 4–6 months unfavourable intermediate; 18–36 months high risk.
> - Early salvage at PSA 0.1–0.2 = adjuvant (RADICALS, RAVES, GETUG-AFU 17).
> - STAMPEDE H: prostate RT for low-volume M1; ALSYMPCA, VISION for mCRPC.

## Q2. Define Azoospermia and describe types of testicular biopsies and surgical sperm retrival.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 1, Q3d and 2019-Summer Paper 2, Q3c)

### Definition
- **Azoospermia:** no sperm in the **centrifuged pellet** of the ejaculate on **at least two** semen analyses. Found in about 1% of men and 10–15% of infertile men.
- Not **cryptozoospermia** (rare sperm in pellet) or **aspermia** (no ejaculate).

### Classification
| Type | Causes | Clues |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-testicular | Kallmann syndrome, pituitary disease, **exogenous testosterone** | Low FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone), LH (luteinising hormone) |
| Testicular: **NOA (non-obstructive azoospermia)** | Klinefelter, Y microdeletion, cryptorchidism, chemotherapy, mumps | Small testes, high FSH |
| Post-testicular: **OA (obstructive azoospermia)** | CBAVD (congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens), vasectomy, epididymal or ejaculatory duct obstruction | Normal testes and FSH |
| Ejaculatory | Retrograde ejaculation, anejaculation | Low volume; sperm in post-ejaculate urine |

### Evaluation (brief)
- Absent vasa → CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) testing of both partners. Volume <1 mL → post-ejaculate urine, then TRUS (transrectal ultrasound).
- **FSH ≤7.6 IU/L and testis long axis >4.6 cm → obstruction likely;** FSH >7.6 and axis ≤4.6 cm → spermatogenic failure.
- **Karyotype and Y microdeletion** in NOA. **AZF (azoospermia factor) a or b complete deletion: no sperm, do not operate.**

### Testicular biopsy
**Indications**
- Azoospermia with **normal testes, palpable vasa and normal FSH**: separates OA from NOA (AUA/ASRM, American Urological Association/American Society for Reproductive Medicine).
- Since ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection) needs one sperm, biopsy is now **diagnostic and therapeutic**: plan it as TESE (testicular sperm extraction) and **cryopreserve** sperm.
- **Not needed:** CBAVD with normal FSH (spermatogenesis always present).

**Types**
| Type | Method | Use | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open biopsy** | Small incision in the albuginea, pea-sized tissue | **Gold standard** for histology and retrieval | Open procedure |
| **Percutaneous core biopsy** | 14 G biopsy gun under cord block | Fresh retrieval in OA | Blind; may injure epididymis or testicular artery; avoid in scarred scrotum |
| **FNA (fine-needle aspiration)**, i.e. TESA (testicular sperm aspiration) | 23 G needle, suction, several passes | OA retrieval; backup to epididymal methods | Few tubules, poor architecture; low yield in NOA |
| **Microdissection biopsy** (micro-TESE) | Operating microscope | NOA | Long operation |

- **Also know:** multi-site FNA "mapping" in NOA.

**Open biopsy technique**
1. Local anaesthesia with cord block (or general/spinal); choose the larger testis.
2. 2 cm transverse incision; open tunica vaginalis.
3. 3–4 mm albugineal incision avoiding surface vessels (loupes).
4. Excise extruding tubules with iris scissors, **no forceps**.
5. Fix in **Bouin's, Zenker's or glutaraldehyde, never formalin** (distorts histology).
6. **Touch or wet prep** at once for sperm; negative → contralateral biopsy.
7. Close albuginea and tunica vaginalis separately; no antibiotics.

**Histology patterns**
| Pattern | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Normal | Suggests obstruction |
| Hypospermatogenesis | All stages, fewer cells |
| Maturation arrest | Stops at a germ-cell stage |
| SCO (Sertoli cell–only) | No germ cells |
| Tubular sclerosis/hyalinisation | End-stage (e.g. Klinefelter) |

- **Complications:** haematoma (commonest); **inadvertent epididymal biopsy** (most serious: causes obstruction); infection rare.

### Surgical sperm retrieval
**Indications:** CBAVD; unreconstructable obstruction; declined or failed vasectomy reversal; backup at reconstruction; NOA; anejaculation.

| Technique | Method | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PESA** (percutaneous epididymal sperm aspiration) | Fine needle into caput | OA | Few sperm; may obstruct epididymis |
| **MESA** (microsurgical epididymal sperm aspiration) | Single tubule opened at 16–25×; micropipette by **capillary action**, never suction | **OA (best)** | Many motile sperm, multiple frozen vials |
| **TESA** | 23 G needle, suction | OA | Least invasive; immotile sperm |
| **Conventional TESE** | Open biopsy, one or more sites | OA, NOA | Blind sampling |
| **Micro-TESE** (Schlegel 1999) | Testis opened in an avascular plane; **larger, opaque tubules** excised under the microscope | **NOA (best)** | Most sperm, least tissue |

**Key principles**
- **MESA:** start distally and move **proximally** until motile sperm appear; the most motile sperm are often in the efferent ductules.
- **Micro-TESE:** spermatogenesis in NOA is **focal**. Thin white tubules are Sertoli cell–only; plump ones hold sperm. Embryologist examines tissue in theatre; map positive sites; negative → other testis.

**Micro-TESE retrieval by diagnosis**
| Condition | Retrieval |
|---|---|
| Post-orchidopexy | 74% |
| AZFc deletion | 70% |
| Klinefelter | 68% |
| Post-chemotherapy | 53% |
| Maturation arrest | 40% |
| SCO | 30% |
| **AZFa/AZFb deletion** | **0%** |

- Overall sperm found in **about half** of NOA.
- **Before retrieval in NOA:** stop testosterone; repair clinical varicocele; off-label clomiphene, anastrozole or hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin).

### Use of retrieved sperm
- **ICSI is essential** (poor motility). Frozen sperm equal fresh, except in spinal cord injury.
- NOA: more miscarriage (sperm DNA damage); genetic counselling. No sperm: donor sperm or adoption.
- **Also know:** post-mortem retrieval needs prior consent; microfluidic sperm sorting.

**Diagram to draw:** micro-TESE: bivalved testis with plump spermatogenic tubules beside thin Sertoli cell–only tubules.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Azoospermia: no sperm in centrifuged pellet on two samples.
> - Open biopsy is the gold standard; fix in Bouin's, never formalin.
> - OA: MESA; NOA: micro-TESE (sperm in about half).
> - AZFa/b deletion: 0% retrieval, do not operate.

## Q3a. Operating room safety : the role of surgeon.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- About half of hospital adverse events involve surgery; half of these are preventable. The surgeon is **team leader** and ultimately responsible.
- **Swiss cheese model (Reason):** harm occurs when holes in several barriers line up, so safety needs layered defences.

### WHO (World Health Organization) Surgical Safety Checklist (2008, 19 items)
| Phase | Key checks |
|---|---|
| **Sign in** (before induction) | Identity, site, procedure, consent; site marked; allergy; airway; blood loss >500 mL |
| **Time out** (before incision) | Team introductions; confirm patient, site, procedure; surgeon states critical steps, duration, blood loss; antibiotic within 60 min; imaging displayed |
| **Sign out** (before leaving theatre) | Procedure recorded; counts correct; specimen labelled; equipment problems; recovery concerns |

- Pilot in 8 hospitals: deaths fell 47%, major complications 36%.

### Surgeon's responsibilities
1. **Universal Protocol** (Joint Commission, 2004): verification, **site marking** by the operating surgeon, patient awake, correct side only, time-out. Wrong-site surgery is a never event.
2. **Lead the time-out:** whole team engaged; repeat when procedure or surgeon changes.
3. **Retained surgical items** (sponges commonest): a count discrepancy raises risk about 100-fold; do not close until resolved; X-ray or RF (radiofrequency) tags.
4. **Fire safety:** the surgeon owns the **ignition source** (diathermy, light cables, laser). Let alcohol prep dry; FiO₂ (inspired oxygen fraction) ≤30% near the airway.
5. **Sharps:** double gloves, blunt needles, neutral zone (no hand-to-hand passing).
6. **Distractions:** "sterile cockpit" during critical steps (e.g. hilar clamping).
7. **Communication** (failure underlies up to 70% of events): Use **TeamSTEPPS** (Team Strategies and Tools to Enhance Performance and Patient Safety) tools: brief, debrief, **SBAR** (situation, background, assessment, recommendation), closed-loop check-back, **CUS** ("concerned, uncomfortable, safety issue"), two-challenge rule. Flatten hierarchy so juniors speak up.
8. **Self-monitoring:** **I'M SAFE** (illness, medication, stress, alcohol, fatigue, eating).

- **Also know:** positioning and padding; VTE (venous thromboembolism) prophylaxis; simulation.

**Diagram to draw:** Swiss cheese model with aligned holes.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - WHO checklist: sign in, time out, sign out.
> - Universal Protocol: verify, mark, time-out.
> - Count discrepancy: ~100-fold risk of a retained item.
> - Fire triangle: surgeon owns ignition.

## Q3b. Detrussor underactivity.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 2, Q3d)

### Definition
- **DUA (detrusor underactivity), ICS (International Continence Society) 2002:** a contraction of reduced strength and/or duration, causing prolonged or incomplete emptying. **Acontractile detrusor:** no contraction.
- **UAB (underactive bladder):** the symptom complex (slow stream, hesitancy, straining, incomplete emptying). DUA is common in elderly men and women having urodynamics.

### Pathophysiology and causes
- Failure may be **myogenic** (myocyte loss, fibrosis), **efferent** (poor nerve activation) or **afferent** (reduced sensation cannot sustain the voiding reflex).

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Neurogenic | Cauda equina, disc prolapse, spinal injury, diabetic neuropathy, radical pelvic surgery |
| Myogenic | Long-standing BOO (bladder outlet obstruction), diabetes, ischaemia, ageing |
| Drugs | Anticholinergics, opioids |
| Functional | Fowler syndrome, dysfunctional voiding |

### Evaluation
- Bladder diary, flow rate, **PVR (post-void residual)**, sacral neurological examination, spinal MRI if deficit, drug review.
- **Pressure-flow study is diagnostic:** low detrusor pressure with low flow.
- **BCI (bladder contractility index) = Pdet@Qmax (detrusor pressure at maximum flow) + 5 Qmax:** <100 weak, 100–150 normal, >150 strong. Also PIP (projected isovolumetric pressure), Watts factor, stop test.
- Ambulatory urodynamics if no contraction is seen.

### Management
- **Goals:** protect kidneys; prevent UTI (urinary tract infection) and stones; relieve symptoms. No drug reliably improves contraction.

| Aim | Options |
|---|---|
| Drainage | Timed and double voiding; **CISC (clean intermittent self-catheterisation), the mainstay**; suprapubic catheter if CISC impossible |
| Lower outlet resistance | α-blockers; pelvic-floor biofeedback; outlet surgery (TURP, transurethral resection of the prostate) in selected obstructed men |
| Improve contraction | **SNM (sacral neuromodulation)** for non-obstructive retention (Fowler); **sacral anterior root stimulator** with rhizotomy in complete spinal injury |
| Not recommended | Bethanechol, distigmine (weak evidence; bronchospasm, rarely cardiac arrest); Credé or Valsalva voiding (reflux risk) |
| Experimental | Intravesical prostaglandins, intravesical electrotherapy, TAC-302, latissimus dorsi myoplasty, stem cells |

- SNM and parasympathomimetics need some intact detrusor and sensory function; a fibrosed detrusor responds to nothing.

**Diagram to draw:** pressure-flow plot with BCI lines at 100 and 150.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - BCI = Pdet@Qmax + 5Qmax; <100 weak.
> - CISC is the mainstay; parasympathomimetics not recommended.
> - SNM for Fowler syndrome.

## Q3c. Surgical management of primary scrotal cancer.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- Rare. **SCC (squamous cell carcinoma)** is commonest. **Percivall Pott (1775)** linked it to chimney soot: the first occupational cancer.
- **Risks:** soot, tar, mineral oils, arsenic, PUVA (psoralen + ultraviolet A), HPV (human papillomavirus), chronic inflammation.
- **Other types:** EMPD (extramammary Paget disease), BCC (basal cell carcinoma), melanoma, sarcoma.
- Lymph drains to **superficial inguinal nodes**, crossing the midline: both groins at risk.

### Evaluation
- Nodule or ulcer on the anterolateral scrotum, often late; **biopsy** confirms.
- Examine both groins; FNAC (fine-needle aspiration cytology) of palpable nodes; CT (computed tomography) for staging.

### Staging (Lowe modification of Ray and Whitmore)
| Stage | Extent |
|---|---|
| A1 | Confined to scrotum |
| A2 | Invades testis, cord, penis, pubis or perineum |
| B | Inguinal nodes only |
| C | Pelvic nodes, no distant spread |
| D | Distant metastases |

### Management of the primary
1. **Wide local excision with a 2 cm margin**, full thickness including dartos; frozen sections for margins.
2. Testes and cord preserved unless invaded. Hemi- or total scrotectomy for large tumours.
3. **Reconstruction:** scrotal laxity usually allows primary closure. Defect >50%: local or thigh flaps, split-skin graft, or thigh pouches for the testes.
4. Stage A2: en bloc removal of involved testis, penis, perineum or pubis.

### Management of nodes (extrapolated from penile cancer)
- **Palpable, cytology positive:** ILND (inguinal lymph node dissection) ± pelvic dissection. Nodal status is the main prognostic factor.
- **Impalpable:** surveillance; dynamic sentinel node biopsy or modified ILND for high-grade/invasive tumours. Routine prophylactic dissection is not advised.
- **Bulky/fixed nodes:** neoadjuvant cisplatin-based chemotherapy, then surgery; RT (radiotherapy) for palliation.

### Other histologies
- **EMPD:** wide excision (up to 3 cm) guided by **mapping biopsies**; screen for associated GU (genitourinary) and other cancers.
- **Also know:** BCC (excision or Mohs); melanoma (excision by Breslow depth ± sentinel node); sarcoma (wide excision).

**Diagram to draw:** elliptical wide excision with 2 cm margin and inguinal node drainage.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Pott 1775: chimney sweeps, first occupational cancer.
> - Wide local excision, 2 cm margin; testes spared unless invaded.
> - Nodes: managed as in penile cancer; nodes decide prognosis.

## Q3d. Painful bladder syndrome management.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2017-Winter Paper 2, Q3b)

### Definition
- **IC/BPS (interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome), AUA (American Urological Association):** unpleasant bladder-related sensation (pain, pressure, discomfort) with LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) for **>6 weeks**, without infection or other cause. ESSIC (European Society for the Study of Interstitial Cystitis) uses >6 months.
- **Phenotypes:** Hunner lesion (HL) disease (true inflammation) versus non-HL; also pelvic-floor-dominant and widespread (centralised) pain.

### Before treatment
- Exclude UTI (urinary tract infection), CIS (carcinoma in situ), stones, **ketamine cystitis**, OAB (overactive bladder), radiation cystitis; PVR (post-void residual), urinalysis, culture.
- **Office cystoscopy** if over 50 or HL suspected.

### Principles (AUA 2022)
- **Old AUA (2011/2014): six tiers**, from self-care to surgery. **AUA 2022 dropped the tiers:** phenotype-guided, shared decisions, multimodal care; major surgery stays last.

### Treatment options
| Group | Options |
|---|---|
| Behavioural | Education; avoid trigger foods (coffee, citrus, spicy food, alcohol); stress management; flare plans |
| Physical | **Myofascial pelvic-floor physiotherapy** if tender (Standard); **avoid Kegel exercises** |
| Oral | **Amitriptyline** 10 mg at night, up to ~50 mg; hydroxyzine; cimetidine; **PPS (pentosan polysulfate)** 100 mg three times daily (**pigmentary maculopathy**: retinal checks) |
| Intravesical | **DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide) 50%, 50 mL, 15–20 min**; heparin; alkalinised lidocaine (flares) |
| Procedures | **HL: fulguration and/or triamcinolone injection** (75–90% improve); short (1–3 min) low-pressure (60–80 cmH₂O) hydrodistension; **onabotulinumtoxinA 100 U** (risk of retention); SNM (sacral neuromodulation) |
| Refractory HL | **Ciclosporin A** |
| Last resort (<10%) | Diversion ± cystectomy, or augmentation; pain may persist |

- **Do not offer:** long-term antibiotics, intravesical BCG (bacille Calmette–Guérin), long-term oral steroids.
- Pain management: gabapentinoids; opioids only via pain specialists.
- Monitor with symptom scores; stop ineffective treatments. Most hydrodistension responses last under 2 months.
- Treat associated pain conditions (IBS (irritable bowel syndrome), fibromyalgia) with other specialists.

**Diagram to draw:** cystoscopic Hunner lesion with radiating vessels.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - AUA 2022: no tiers; phenotype-based.
> - HL: fulguration/triamcinolone; ciclosporin if refractory.
> - PPS: maculopathy; antibiotics and BCG not offered.

## Q3e. Secondary tumors after urinary diversion.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- New cancers arising in or next to bowel used for urinary diversion or bladder augmentation, usually after a **long latency** (10–20 years or more). Distinct from recurrent urothelial cancer.

### Risk by diversion (German registry, Kälble 2011)
| Diversion | Risk | Surveillance |
|---|---|---|
| **Ureterosigmoidostomy** | 2.6% (6–29% in long-term series) | Regular colonoscopy **from 5 years** |
| Augmentation cystoplasty | 1.6% | Endoscopy, often from 10 years |
| Colonic neobladder | 1.3% | — |
| Ileocaecal pouch | 0.14% | If symptomatic |
| Ileal neobladder | 0.05% | No routine |
| Ileal conduit | 0.02% | No routine |

- **Pattern:** risk is highest where urothelium meets colon and is bathed in faeces; purely ileal diversions are lowest.

### Pathogenesis
- **Urothelium–colonic junction** exposed to urine and faeces; tumours arise at the ureterocolic anastomosis.
- **N-nitrosamines** from bacterial action on urinary nitrates (probably enhancers, not sole cause); chronic inflammation; growth factors (EGF, epidermal growth factor); ornithine decarboxylase; abnormal mucin.
- Removing the urothelium prevents tumours experimentally.
- **Defunctioned anastomoses still form cancer:** when converting a ureterosigmoidostomy, excise the old anastomosis.

### Pathology
- Mostly **adenocarcinoma**; also adenomatous polyps, urothelial carcinoma, sarcoma.
- **Augmentation:** adenocarcinoma and aggressive urothelial carcinoma, often advanced at diagnosis; earliest at 4 years. **Gastrocystoplasty** carries relatively high risk. Exstrophy and immunosuppression add risk.
- **Nephrogenic adenoma:** benign; commonest tumour after cystoplasty.

### Presentation
- Haematuria, rectal bleeding, change in bowel habit, ureteric obstruction (hydronephrosis), recurrent infection, mass.

### Management
- Biopsy and staging.
- **Ureterosigmoidostomy:** resection of the segment with the anastomosis; conversion to conduit or continent diversion.
- **Augmentation:** partial or total cystectomy with new diversion.
- **Prevention:** prefer ileal segments; counsel patients; lifelong follow-up.

**Also know:** after cystectomy, recurrent urothelial cancer may arise in the upper tracts or a retained urethra (urethral recurrence about 7–9%); needs separate surveillance.

**Diagram to draw:** ureterosigmoid anastomosis with tumour at the urothelial–colonic junction.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Ureterosigmoidostomy: highest risk; colonoscopy from year 5.
> - Mostly adenocarcinoma at the anastomosis.
> - Excise defunctioned anastomoses.

## Q3f. Role of open and laparoscopic stone surgeries in modern era of endourology.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- SWL (shock wave lithotripsy), URS (ureteroscopy) and PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) treat almost all stones. Open surgery is now a small minority in centres with full endourology, but remains in resource-limited settings.
- **AUA (American Urological Association)/Endourological Society 2016:** consider laparoscopic, robotic or open surgery **only when SWL, URS and PCNL fail or are expected to fail**; not first line.

### Indications
| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Endourology failed or unlikely | Very complex staghorn needing many PCNL sessions; failed multiple procedures |
| Anatomical problem treated together | **PUJ (pelviureteric junction) obstruction + stone:** pyeloplasty with pyelolithotomy; anterior calyceal diverticulum; infundibular stenosis |
| Difficult access | Pelvic or ectopic kidney (laparoscopy-assisted PCNL); morbid obesity; skeletal deformity |
| Poor renal function | **Nephrectomy** for a non-functioning kidney; partial nephrectomy for a non-functioning pole |
| Large impacted ureteric stone | Laparoscopic ureterolithotomy after failed URS/SWL |
| Concomitant surgery | Other open or laparoscopic procedure |
| Bladder | Very large stone; with open prostatectomy, diverticulectomy or augmentation; small bladder; urethral stricture |

### Procedures
- **Pyelolithotomy:** simple, extended (Gil-Vernet, sinus dissection) or coagulum.
- **Anatrophic nephrolithotomy (Smith and Boyce):** for complex staghorn. Clamp renal artery; cool with ice slush; give mannitol; incise along **Brödel's avascular line**; remove stone; repair infundibula; close.
- Radial nephrotomy, ureterolithotomy, cystolithotomy.

### Laparoscopic and robotic surgery
- Transperitoneal or retroperitoneal pyelolithotomy and ureterolithotomy; robotic anatrophic nephrolithotomy reported.
- Best for large pelvic stone in an extrarenal pelvis: high single-stage clearance, less bleeding than PCNL. Drawbacks: longer operation, calyceal stones need a flexible scope.

| Feature | Open | Laparoscopic/robotic |
|---|---|---|
| Morbidity | Large incision, pain, long stay | Less pain, shorter stay |
| Kidney | Ischaemic loss of function (anatrophic) | Similar repair, less wound morbidity |
| Later surgery | Adhesions make redo difficult | Fewer |

### Complications
- Bleeding, urinary leak, infection, loss of renal function, residual fragments.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Open/laparoscopic only when endourology fails or is unsuitable.
> - Laparoscopic pyeloplasty + pyelolithotomy for PUJ obstruction with stone.
> - Anatrophic nephrolithotomy: Brödel's line, cold ischaemia.
