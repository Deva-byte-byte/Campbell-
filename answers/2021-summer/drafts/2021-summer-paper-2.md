## Q1. Describe factors affecting outcome of ESWL. Discuss complications of ESWL and strategies to avoid them.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(Mechanism, indications and current status are answered in 2019-Summer Paper 2, Q2.)

### Introduction
- ESWL (extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy, now SWL) breaks stones with shock waves (peak 30–100 MPa, negative tail about −10 MPa) generated at F1 and focused on the stone at F2.
- Stones break by spallation, shear, squeezing and cavitation. **Cavitation** also causes most tissue injury, so steps that improve outcome usually reduce complications too.
- Outcome = SFR (stone-free rate) and need for retreatment.

### Factors affecting outcome
**1. Stone factors**

| Factor | Favourable | Unfavourable |
|---|---|---|
| Size (most important) | <10 mm | Renal >20 mm (PCNL [percutaneous nephrolithotomy] first); ureteric ≥10 mm favours URS (ureteroscopy) |
| Density on NCCT (non-contrast computed tomography) | <900 HU (Hounsfield units) | >1000 HU |
| Composition | Calcium oxalate dihydrate, uric acid | Cystine, brushite, calcium oxalate monohydrate (dense, homogeneous) |
| Impaction (ureter) | Recent, mobile | Chronic impaction (soft-tissue ring of thick ureteral wall on CT, unchanged position): may be the strongest predictor |
| Burden and site | Single, renal pelvis or upper calyx | Multiple sites; lower pole >1 cm |

- Infection stones and stones in several sites: prefer a more efficient modality.

**2. Renal anatomical factors**
- **Lower pole:** fragments drain against gravity. **Lower Pole I (Albala, 2001):** stones >1 cm, SFR 21% SWL vs 91% PCNL. A steep infundibulopelvic angle and long, narrow infundibulum reduce clearance.
- Obstruction distal to the stone (UPJO [ureteropelvic junction obstruction], infundibular stenosis, calyceal diverticulum) traps fragments. Horseshoe and ectopic kidneys drain poorly.

**3. Patient factors**
- **SSD (skin-to-stone distance) >10 cm** (obesity) predicts failure. Skeletal deformity can prevent targeting.
- Respiratory movement of 10–15 mm moves the stone out of F2.
- **Triple D score:** stone density, stone volume and skin-to-stone distance together predict success.

**4. Machine and technique factors**
- **Energy source:** electrohydraulic (HM3) breaks best; piezoelectric has low energy and a tiny focal zone; modern machines are electromagnetic. A focal zone wider than the stone breaks better.
- **Coupling:** air pockets in the gel cut fragmentation by up to two-fifths.
- **Rate 60–90 shocks/min** beats 120/min, because the bubble cloud clears between shocks. **Ramping** energy up also helps.
- **Analgesia:** GA (general anaesthesia) or sedation keeps the stone in focus.
- Positioning (prone for mid-ureteric stones over the sacrum), continuous imaging, experienced team. Little gain beyond two sessions.
- **Adjuncts:** α-blockers (faster fragment clearance); potassium citrate; percussion, diuresis and inversion for lower pole fragments.

### Complications

| Group | Complication | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Fragment-related | Renal colic; **steinstrasse** ("stone street" of fragments in the ureter); residual fragments | Steinstrasse rises several-fold with burden >2 cm² |
| Infective | UTI (urinary tract infection), pyelonephritis, urosepsis | Infection stones, stents |
| Renal tissue injury | Haematuria (transient, common); **subcapsular or perirenal haematoma** (minority; rarely embolisation) | Risk: age, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, coronary disease, thrombocytopenia, high energy, many shocks, narrow-focus machines |
| Other organs | Liver, spleen, pancreas, colon, lung (children), skeletal muscle | Very rare |
| Cardiac | Arrhythmia during treatment | Pause or ECG (electrocardiogram) gating |
| Skin | Petechiae, bruising | Minor |

- **Refuted long-term effects:** new hypertension, diabetes, decline in renal function and infertility.

### Strategies to avoid complications
1. **Select.** Absolute contraindications: pregnancy; uncorrected coagulopathy or ongoing antithrombotics; untreated UTI; obstruction distal to the stone; aneurysm near the stone (aortic >4 cm); severe uncontrolled hypertension. Send large, dense, lower pole or impacted stones to URS or PCNL.
2. **Prepare.** Urine culture; treat bacteriuria. Prophylaxis only with risk factors (stent, nephrostomy, struvite, recent UTI, bowel reconstruction). Stop antithrombotics for the day and 48 hours after. Control blood pressure; reprogramme pacemakers.
3. **Protect the kidney.** Slow rate; ramp over at least 4 minutes (induces protective renal vasoconstriction); fewest shocks and lowest effective energy.
4. **Prevent steinstrasse.** PCNL or URS for large burdens. Routine stenting does not improve SFR, though it may reduce steinstrasse with large stones. α-blockers after SWL.
5. **Treat early.** Steinstrasse: MET (medical expulsive therapy), SWL to the lead fragment or URS; **nephrostomy if fever or obstruction**. Haematoma: bed rest, transfusion, embolisation if bleeding continues.
6. **Follow up** with KUB (kidney-ureter-bladder radiograph) and ultrasound.

### Recent advances
- **Burst wave lithotripsy** (ultrasound bursts at 4–8 MPa: less cavitation, no anaesthesia, uniform fragments); **ultrasonic propulsion** of fragments.

**Diagram to draw:** ellipsoid reflector with F1, F2, stone and gel coupling; box of failure predictors.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Size is the main predictor; HU >1000 and SSD >10 cm predict failure.
> - Lower Pole I: SFR 21% SWL vs 91% PCNL for lower pole stones >1 cm.
> - Slow rate (60–90/min), ramping and good coupling improve breakage and reduce injury.
> - Steinstrasse risk rises with burden >2 cm²; no routine stent.

## Q2. Describe selection criteria for cadaver renal donor. Describe in details kidney retrieval surgery.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(Marginal donor in 2016-Summer Paper 3, Q3f; brain death certification in 2018-Summer Paper 4, Q3c.)

### Introduction
- A cadaver (deceased) donor is **DBD** (donation after brain death: circulation maintained until cold perfusion) or **DCD** (donation after circulatory death: warm ischaemia).
- India: **THOA (Transplantation of Human Organs Act) 1994**, amended 2011, Rules 2014, recognises brain-stem death; **NOTTO** (National Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation) allocates organs.

### Selection criteria
**1. Legal death and consent**
- **DBD:** apnoeic coma of known irreversible cause; exclude drugs, hypothermia, metabolic causes. All brainstem reflexes absent (pupillary, corneal, oculocephalic, oculovestibular, cranial motor response, gag and cough). **Apnoea test:** no breathing effort at PaCO₂ (arterial carbon dioxide tension) ≥60 mmHg.
- India: **4-member Board** (hospital in-charge, independent specialist, neurologist or neurosurgeon, treating doctor; none from the transplant team); **two tests at least 6 hours apart**; Form 10.
- **DCD (Maastricht):** I dead on arrival; II failed resuscitation (both uncontrolled); **III arrest after withdrawal of support (controlled, commonest)**; IV arrest in a brain-dead donor; V euthanasia.
- Next-of-kin consent after a **mandatory request** by ICU (intensive care unit) staff.

**2. Donor evaluation**
- Cause of death, medical and social (high-risk behaviour) history; kidney: terminal creatinine, urine output, hypertension, diabetes, ultrasound.
- Laboratory: ABO and HLA (human leucocyte antigen) typing; serology and NAT (nucleic acid testing) for HIV (human immunodeficiency virus), hepatitis B and C, CMV (cytomegalovirus), EBV (Epstein–Barr virus), syphilis, toxoplasma.

**3. Absolute contraindications**
- Active or metastatic cancer, haematological malignancy, history of melanoma, untreatable viral infection.
- Treated bacterial infection (even bacterial meningitis) is acceptable; viral encephalitis needs care.

**4. Standard versus marginal donor**

| Category | Definition |
|---|---|
| **ECD (expanded criteria donor), UNOS (United Network for Organ Sharing)** | Age ≥60, or 50–59 with 2 of: hypertension, creatinine >1.5 mg/dL, death from CVA (cerebrovascular accident) |
| **KDPI (Kidney Donor Profile Index)** | Replaced ECD in the US (2014); >85% = marginal; longevity matching with EPTS (Estimated Post-Transplant Survival) |
| Paediatric <15 kg | En bloc transplant |

- **Remuzzi biopsy score** for marginal kidneys: 0–3 single, 4–6 dual, ≥7 discard.
- **Donor management (rule of 100s):** systolic pressure >100 mmHg, urine >100 mL/h, PaO₂ (arterial oxygen tension) >100 mmHg, haemoglobin >100 g/L. Vasopressin treats diabetes insipidus.

### Kidney retrieval surgery (multi-organ, DBD)
1. **Checks:** death certificate, consent, blood group; coordinate thoracic, liver, pancreas and kidney teams.
2. **Incision:** midline xiphoid-to-pubis with median sternotomy.
3. **Exposure:** mobilise right colon and small-bowel mesentery off the retroperitoneum (Cattell–Braasch manoeuvre); expose aorta and IVC (inferior vena cava).
4. **Prepare cannulation:** encircle the distal aorta above the bifurcation; ligate the inferior mesenteric artery; prepare the supracoeliac aorta for clamping. **Look for aberrant renal arteries**; keep accessory arteries from the iliacs.
5. **Cold perfusion:** systemic heparin; cannulate the distal aorta; clamp the supracoeliac aorta; **vent** the IVC (or right atrium); flush cold UW (University of Wisconsin) or HTK (histidine–tryptophan–ketoglutarate) solution; crushed ice; open Gerota's fascia laterally to cool the kidneys.
6. **Remove heart, lungs, liver and pancreas first.**
7. **Kidneys en bloc:** mobilise both with perinephric fat off the posterior wall; keep periureteric tissue; divide ureters low. Divide aorta and IVC (upper limit set by the liver team, lower at the bifurcation); lift everything anteriorly and divide prevertebral tissue.
8. **Extras:** iliac vessels for reconstruction; spleen and lymph nodes for crossmatch.

**DCD:** after death and a no-touch period, **super-rapid** laparotomy, direct aortic cannulation and IVC venting limit warm ischaemia; kidneys usually go on machine perfusion.

### Back-bench preparation
- Open the aorta posteriorly to see the renal ostia and divide it between them; keep the IVC with the right kidney to lengthen the short right renal vein.
- Keep an aortic **Carrel patch**; ligate tributaries and lymphatics (prevents lymphocele); trim fat without decapsulation; spare the lower pole–ureter triangle.
- Document anatomy and injuries; biopsy marginal kidneys; triple-bag in cold solution on ice; label.

### Preservation
- Cooling to 4 °C slows metabolism. **Hypothermic machine perfusion** reduces DGF (delayed graft function) versus static cold storage (Moers, 2009), not long-term survival. Keep cold ischaemia short (thrombosis risk >24 hours).

- **Pitfalls:** polar artery or intimal injury, short right vein, ureteric devascularisation, capsular tears.
- **Also know:** HCV (hepatitis C virus)-viraemic donors with antivirals; normothermic perfusion (experimental).

**Diagram to draw:** aorta and IVC with distal cannula, supracoeliac clamp, IVC vent and en bloc kidneys.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - THOA: 4-member Board, two tests ≥6 h apart; apnoea test at PaCO₂ ≥60 mmHg.
> - ECD: ≥60, or 50–59 with 2 of hypertension, creatinine >1.5, CVA death; KDPI >85%.
> - Distal aortic cannula, supracoeliac clamp, IVC vent, cold UW/HTK and ice.
> - Kidneys en bloc last; Carrel patch, IVC for right vein; machine perfusion reduces DGF.

## Q3a. Post prostatectomy incontinence.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and incidence
- PPI (post-prostatectomy incontinence) is mainly **SUI (stress urinary incontinence)** after RP (radical prostatectomy), the commonest cause of male SUI. Rates vary with definition ("no pad" vs "safety pad"); expert no-pad continence is in the mid-90s % at 1 year.

### Pathophysiology
- RP removes the internal sphincter (bladder neck, prostatic urethra), so continence depends on the external **rhabdosphincter**.
- Causes: apical injury to rhabdosphincter or pudendal branches, short membranous urethra, afferent denervation; also detrusor overactivity, low compliance, **VUAS (vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis)**.
- Nerve sparing and posterior reconstruction (Rocco stitch) give **earlier, not better final** continence.

### Evaluation
- History (type, severity, radiotherapy, cancer status), bladder diary, **24-hour pad weight**: mild <200 g, moderate 200–400 g, severe >400 g.
- **Standing cough test**, grade 0 to 4 (leak reported but not seen; delayed drops; early drops; delayed stream; early persistent stream).
- Urinalysis, PVR (post-void residual), PSA (prostate-specific antigen). **Cystoscopy** excludes VUAS and judges residual sphincter coaptation; urodynamics when needed.

### Management
1. **Wait 6–12 months** (improves up to 2 years). Supervised **PFMT (pelvic floor muscle training)** with biofeedback, ideally preoperative; pads, condom catheter or clamp.
2. **Duloxetine** off-label. **Bulking agents are not advised.**
3. **Surgery** (AUA/SUFU [American Urological Association/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction] 2019, EAU [European Association of Urology]):

| Situation | Choice |
|---|---|
| Mild–moderate, no radiotherapy | **Male sling**: AdVance (repositions the bulbar urethra; needs a coapting sphincter) or adjustable systems (ATOMS [adjustable transobturator male system]) |
| Severe or any radiotherapy | **AUS (artificial urinary sphincter), AMS 800**: gold standard; cuff 61–70 cm H₂O |
| ProACT balloons | Avoid after radiotherapy |

- **MASTER trial:** male sling non-inferior to AUS at 12 months.
- AUS: social continence (0–1 pad) 55–77%; revision 10–29% at 3 years; lasts 8–10 years. Erosion risks: radiotherapy, prior erosion, urethroplasty (use a **transcorporal cuff**).

**Diagram to draw:** AMS 800 with cuff, scrotal pump and pressure-regulating balloon.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Wait 6–12 months with PFMT before surgery.
> - Sling for mild–moderate; AUS for severe or irradiated.
> - MASTER: sling non-inferior to AUS at 12 months.

## Q3b. Early versus delayed VVF repair.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- VVF (vesicovaginal fistula): an epithelialised bladder–vagina communication causing continuous leakage.
- Causes: **obstetric** (over 90% in low-resource settings; obstructed labour) and **iatrogenic** (over 75% in high-resource settings, mainly hysterectomy); radiation; malignancy.
- Common mechanism: **ischaemic necrosis**, then a tract that epithelialises with time.
- Presents: cystotomy, days; thermal injury, 7–10 days; radiation, months to years.

### The debate

| | Early repair | Delayed repair |
|---|---|---|
| Definition (varies) | Days, <6 weeks or <3 months | Traditionally ≥3 months |
| Rationale | Clean iatrogenic tissue heals well; ends wetness and distress early | Inflammation settles, necrotic tissue sloughs, margins become clear |
| Evidence | **Comparable closure** in most series; Waaldijk: high first-attempt closure with immediate obstetric repair | One small series: more recurrence when repaired <6 weeks |
| Drawback | Friable tissue if inflamed | Prolonged distress, no better outcome |

- Some authors **avoid 4–12 weeks** (active healing phase).
- **EAU (European Association of Urology) consensus:** no minimum delay if **no active infection and no recent irradiation**. 

### Recommended timing by cause
- **Clean post-hysterectomy:** early repair. **Obstetric with necrosis:** catheter trial, wait about 3 months.
- **Radiation fistula:** wait **≥12 months** after radiotherapy; biopsy for recurrence; interposition flap; diversion if the bladder is poor.

### Conservative trial (during the wait)
- Catheter drainage cures about 15%: best if small (<1 cm, ideally <3 mm), started **within 3 weeks**, fresh and non-irradiated. Once epithelialised (≥6 weeks), unlikely. EAU: if tried, at least 12 weeks.
- **Also know:** fulguration of tiny tracts; fibrin glue.

### Principles at repair
- Treat infection, optimise nutrition; ureteric stents if within 1 cm of a ureter.
- Vaginal or abdominal (open, laparoscopic, robotic): about 90% first-attempt closure. Tension-free, multilayer closure; **interposition flap** (Martius, omentum, peritoneum) if irradiated or recurrent; catheter often ≥2 weeks.

**Diagram to draw:** timeline: early window, 4–12 week zone, 3 months (obstetric), 12 months (radiation).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Early repair for clean iatrogenic VVF; closure comparable to delayed.
> - Delay ~3 months obstetric, ≥12 months radiation.
> - Catheter trial works only if small, fresh and started within 3 weeks.

## Q3c. Perineal urethrostomy.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- PU (perineal urethrostomy): the proximal bulbar urethra is opened onto perineal skin as a permanent or staged new meatus; the patient voids sitting. It **bypasses** rather than reconstructs a diseased anterior urethra.

### Indications
- **Complex anterior strictures:** LS (lichen sclerosus) panurethral disease, multiple failed urethroplasties, failed hypospadias repair.
- **Elderly or high-risk patients** who do not want or cannot withstand staged reconstruction.
- **First stage** of a staged (Johanson-type) urethroplasty.
- After **penectomy** or distal urethrectomy for penile or urethral cancer.
- Temporary: stricture after phalloplasty; resectoscope access in morbid obesity; Fournier gangrene.
- AUA (American Urological Association) guideline (2016): PU is an acceptable long-term alternative to urethroplasty.
- LS spares the proximal urethra, so PU is easy in LS.

### Contraindications and limits
- Disease reaching the proximal bulbar or membranous urethra; prior pelvic radiation and LS increase restricture.

### Techniques
**1. Classical (Blandy) PU:** inverted-U perineal skin flap advanced into a ventral bulbar urethrotomy (a random-pattern advancement flap).

**2. BMG (buccal mucosal graft) PU, steps:**
1. High lithotomy; inverted-U incision; divide bulbospongiosus in the midline.
2. Free the spongiosum; **transect** it; **dorsal urethrotomy** until healthy mucosa.
3. Bring the healthy ventral urethra to perineal skin; calibrate proximally to 30 Fr; close the distal urethra.
4. Quilt a BMG on the corpora to complete the dorsal wall up to the skin.
- The graft prevents the circumferential scar that narrows a deep PU.

### Complications and outcomes
- **Stenosis of the urethrostomy** (main failure): revision or periodic dilation.
- Spraying, dribbling, perineal wetness, wound infection, body-image concerns.
- Most succeed at mid-term with high satisfaction in selected patients.

**Diagram to draw:** sagittal perineum showing inverted-U flap sutured into the opened bulbar urethra.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - PU bypasses, rather than reconstructs, the diseased anterior urethra.
> - Ideal for LS, failed repairs and elderly patients; patient voids sitting.
> - BMG PU prevents circumferential scarring; calibrate to 30 Fr.

## Q3d. Medical expulsive therapy in urinary calculi.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- MET (medical expulsive therapy): drugs that help a ureteric stone pass into the bladder. **α-blockers** are the main agents.

### Rationale
- Spontaneous passage depends on **width**, size and site:

| Width | Passage | Location | Passage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 mm | 98% | Vesicoureteric junction | 92% (small stones) |
| 5 mm | 65% | Distal ureter | 75% |
| >6.5 mm | 9% | Proximal ureter | 48% |

### Mechanism
- **α1A and α1D receptors** dominate in the distal ureter and VUJ (vesicoureteric junction). Blockade reduces spasm and peristaltic force and frequency, so a larger urine bolus pushes the stone.
- Calcium-channel blockers (nifedipine); PDE5 (phosphodiesterase-5) inhibitors under study. **NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) do not aid passage.**

### Drugs
- **Tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily** (most studied); **silodosin 8 mg daily** (better expulsion in meta-analyses; twice the retrograde ejaculation); alfuzosin, doxazosin.

### Evidence

| Study | Result |
|---|---|
| **SUSPEND (Pickard, 2015)**: 1136 patients, tamsulosin, nifedipine or placebo | No difference in need for intervention at 4 weeks (19% vs 20%) |
| **STONE (Meltzer, 2018)**: stones <9 mm | Tamsulosin no better than placebo |
| Meta-analyses and a large distal-stone RCT (randomised controlled trial) | Benefit for **distal stones >5 mm** |

- Stratified, the trials agree: benefit lies in **distal stones of 5–10 mm** (smaller pass anyway; larger rarely pass).

### Indications and guideline position
- AUA (American Urological Association) and EAU (European Association of Urology): offer MET for **ureteric stones <10 mm**, especially distal, when immediate intervention is not needed (EAU: distal >5 mm).
- **After SWL (shock wave lithotripsy)** for faster fragment clearance. Children: off-label but effective.

### Contraindications (need urgent drainage instead)
- Infection with obstruction or sepsis, solitary or bilateral obstruction, renal deterioration, refractory pain or vomiting.

### Protocol
- NSAID analgesia; counsel on off-label use, dizziness, hypotension, retrograde ejaculation.
- Periodic KUB (kidney-ureter-bladder radiograph) or ultrasound; **intervene after 4–6 weeks** if not passed.

**Diagram to draw:** ureter with α1 receptors at the distal ureter and VUJ and a stone above it.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Tamsulosin 0.4 mg; best for distal stones 5–10 mm.
> - SUSPEND 2015: no benefit in an unselected population.
> - Trial of passage 4–6 weeks, then intervene.

## Q3e. TOT versus TVT in SUI.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(TOT answered in 2020-Summer Paper 2, Q3f.)

### Introduction
- Both are polypropylene **MUS (mid-urethral slings)** for SUI (stress urinary incontinence), based on the **integral theory** (Petros and Ulmsten, 1990): a loose mid-urethral tape replaces the pubourethral ligaments as a backboard on straining.
- **TVT (tension-free vaginal tape, retropubic):** Ulmsten, 1995–1998. **TOT (transobturator tape):** Delorme, 2001, to avoid bladder, bowel and vessel injury.

### Technique differences

| | TVT (retropubic) | TOT (transobturator) |
|---|---|---|
| Path | Vagina → retropubic space → suprapubic skin, hugging the pubis; cystoscopy essential | Labiocrural fold → obturator membrane → vagina (outside-in), or reverse (inside-out) |
| Structures at risk | Bladder, bowel, iliac and epigastric vessels | Obturator nerves and vessels, vaginal fornix |

### Outcomes
- **TOMUS (Richter, 2010):** 597 women; 12-month objective cure **80.8% retropubic vs 77.7% transobturator** (not different); voiding dysfunction needing surgery 2.7% vs 0%.
- TOMUS at 5 years: 51.3% vs 43.4%; equivalence not shown; satisfaction similar.
- **Cochrane (Ford, 2017):** cure similar up to and beyond 5 years; repeat surgery possibly commoner after TOT.

### Complications (Cochrane, 2017)

| Complication | TVT | TOT |
|---|---|---|
| Bladder perforation | **4.5%** | **0.6%** |
| Groin pain | 1.3% | **6.4%** |
| Voiding dysfunction, blood loss, vascular injury | More | Less |
| Mesh exposure | About 2% | About 2% |

- Urethral injury during either: **do not place mesh** (AUA [American Urological Association] guideline).

### Choosing
- **TVT preferred:** **ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency)** (ALPP [abdominal leak point pressure] <60 cm H₂O or MUCP [maximum urethral closure pressure] <20 cm H₂O), fixed urethra, recurrent SUI. Failure is 12 times likelier after TOT with low leak point pressure (O'Connor, 2006).
- **TOT preferred:** previous retropubic surgery, obesity, avoiding bladder injury.
- AUA: either may be offered to the index patient.
- **Also know:** single-incision mini-slings (non-inferior to TOT); mesh warnings (FDA [Food and Drug Administration] 2010; UK pause 2018): counsel about alternatives (colposuspension, autologous sling, bulking).

**Diagram to draw:** coronal pelvis: U-shaped retropubic TVT versus flat TOT through the obturator foramina.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - TOMUS: 80.8% vs 77.7%, equivalent at 1 year.
> - TOT: less bladder injury (0.6% vs 4.5%), more groin pain (6.4% vs 1.3%).
> - Prefer retropubic TVT in ISD or fixed urethra.

## Q3f. Hereditary urological malignancies.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Principles
- **Knudson two-hit hypothesis:** every cell carries one inherited mutant tumour-suppressor allele, so one more hit causes cancer: **early, bilateral, multifocal** tumours.
- **4–6% of RCC (renal cell carcinoma)** is familial; all familial RCC syndromes are **autosomal dominant**; only hereditary papillary RCC (HPRC) involves an activated oncogene.

### Hereditary kidney cancer syndromes

| Syndrome | Gene (chromosome) | Kidney tumour | Other features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VHL** (von Hippel–Lindau) | VHL (3p25) | Clear cell, bilateral, multifocal (~50%) | Retinal angioma, CNS (central nervous system) haemangioblastoma, phaeochromocytoma, pancreatic cysts |
| **HPRC** | MET (7q31) oncogene | Multiple bilateral papillary (type 1-like) | None |
| **HLRCC** (hereditary leiomyomatosis and RCC) | FH (fumarate hydratase, 1q42–43) | **Aggressive**, often solitary papillary | Skin and uterine leiomyomas |
| **BHD** (Birt–Hogg–Dubé) | FLCN (folliculin, 17p11.2) | Chromophobe, hybrid oncocytic, oncocytoma | Fibrofolliculomas, lung cysts, pneumothorax |
| **TSC** (tuberous sclerosis complex) | TSC1 (9q34), TSC2 (16p13) | Multiple AMLs (angiomyolipomas); RCC 1–3% | Angiofibromas, cortical tubers |

- **VHL type 1** (deletions, truncations): no phaeochromocytoma; **type 2** (missense): phaeochromocytoma.
- **Also know:** SDH (succinate dehydrogenase)-deficient RCC (paraganglioma); BAP1 (uveal melanoma, mesothelioma); Cowden (PTEN; breast, thyroid).

### Management principles for kidney
- **3 cm rule:** watch tumours <3 cm; nephron-sparing surgery at ≥3 cm (VHL, HPRC, BHD) to save function for repeat operations. **Exception: HLRCC**: excise promptly, wide margins.
- TSC AML: everolimus; embolisation or partial nephrectomy if ≥5 cm. **Belzutifan** (HIF-2α [hypoxia-inducible factor 2α] inhibitor, FDA [Food and Drug Administration]-approved 2021) for VHL.

### Other urological cancers
- **Prostate:** **BRCA2**, BRCA1, ATM, CHEK2, PALB2, HOXB13, mismatch repair genes; father plus brother: 9-fold risk. Earlier screening, cautious active surveillance, **PARP (poly ADP-ribose polymerase) inhibitors**.
- **UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma): Lynch syndrome** (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2): 14-fold risk, about 75-fold with MSH2. Suspect if age <60 or Lynch cancer history; MMR (mismatch repair) immunohistochemistry, then germline testing.
- **Testis:** affected brother RR (relative risk) 8–12, father 2–4.
- **Also know:** Wilms tumour (WT1 gene: WAGR [Wilms, aniridia, genitourinary anomalies, retardation] and Denys–Drash; Beckwith–Wiedemann).
- **Approach:** genetic counselling, germline testing, screening relatives.

**Diagram to draw:** VHL loss → HIF accumulation → VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) → angiogenesis.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - All familial RCC is autosomal dominant; 3 cm rule except HLRCC.
> - VHL 3p25, MET 7q31, FH 1q42, FLCN 17p11.
> - Lynch (MSH2) → UTUC; BRCA2 → aggressive prostate cancer.
