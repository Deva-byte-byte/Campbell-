## Q1. Anatomy of lymphatic drainage of testis and scrotum. Describe management implications of penile malignancies.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2018-Summer Paper 2, Q2 [testis and scrotum] and 2017-Summer Paper 2, Q1 [inguinal nodes in penile cancer])

*Second part taken as the management implications of lymphatic drainage in penile cancer, i.e. management of the inguinal and pelvic nodes.*

### Embryological basis
- The testis develops at L1–L2 and descends with its vessels and lymphatics, so its lymph returns to the **retroperitoneum**. The scrotum is skin and drains to the **groin**.

### Lymphatic drainage of the testis
- Lymphatics of the testis, caput and corpus epididymis run up the **spermatic cord** with the gonadal vessels to the **para-aortic and interaortocaval nodes** near the renal hilum; then cisterna chyli, thoracic duct, **left supraclavicular** nodes.
- **Cauda epididymis** drains with the vas to the **external iliac** nodes.

| | Right testis | Left testis |
|---|---|---|
| Primary landing zone | **Interaortocaval** | **Left para-aortic** |
| Secondary | Paracaval, precaval | Pre-aortic, interaortocaval |
| Crossover | Right to left occurs | Rare to right (bulky disease only) |

- Crossover is right to left because lymph flows that way.

### Lymphatic drainage of the scrotum
- Scrotal lymphatics **do not cross the median raphe**; they drain to the **ipsilateral superficial inguinal nodes**.

### Lymphatic drainage of the penis
| Superficial system | Deep system |
|---|---|
| Prepuce and shaft skin | Glans, frenulum, corpora |
| Dorsal network divides at the base → both superficial inguinal groups | Trunks circle the corona, run under Buck's fascia → presymphyseal plexus → femoral triangle nodes |

- **Two rules:** drainage is **bilateral and unpredictable in side** (presymphyseal crossover), but **orderly in sequence**: superficial inguinal → deep inguinal → external iliac and obturator. **No skip metastases.**
- **Superficial nodes** (4–25, above fascia lata) lie in **Daseler's five zones**; sentinel nodes are mostly **superomedial**, then central. **Deep nodes** (1–3) lie medial to the femoral vein; **Cloquet's node** is the highest.

### Clinical implications: testis and scrotum
- **Never operate trans-scrotally** on a testicular tumour. Scrotal violation opens skin lymphatics, so tumour can reach inguinal and pelvic nodes; use **radical inguinal orchidectomy** with high cord clamping.
- Inguinal or pelvic spread from the testis means altered drainage: scrotal violation, previous orchidopexy or hernia repair, scrotal invasion.
- RPLND (retroperitoneal lymph node dissection) templates and para-aortic or "dog-leg" radiotherapy fields follow the landing zones.
- Scrotal skin cancer is staged at the ipsilateral groin.

### Management implications in penile cancer
**1. Prognosis and timing**
- Nodal status is the **strongest prognostic factor**. Penile SCC (squamous cell carcinoma) has a long **locoregional phase**, so the groin is a **curable station**.
- **Kroon (2005):** early ILND (inguinal lymph node dissection) for impalpable nodes gave 3-year disease-specific survival about **84% vs 35%** when delayed until palpable.
- Manage both groins **within 3 months**; remove **at least 8 nodes per side**.

**2. Bilateral management**
- Crossover means **both groins** are staged or treated; with one palpable groin, the other holds micrometastasis in about 30%.

**3. Impalpable groin (cN0): risk-based**
| Risk | EAU-ASCO (European Association of Urology–American Society of Clinical Oncology) 2023 | Groin |
|---|---|---|
| Low | pTis, pTa, pT1a G1 | Surveillance |
| Intermediate | pT1a G2 | Surveillance if compliant, or surgical staging |
| High | **pT1b or higher**, G3 | **DSNB (dynamic sentinel node biopsy)**; ILND if DSNB unavailable |
- NCCN (National Comprehensive Cancer Network): pT1b G1–2 is intermediate risk and is staged surgically (ILND first).
- **DSNB:** ultrasound ± FNAC (fine-needle aspiration cytology) first, because a tumour-replaced node diverts flow (false negative). Then technetium-99m nanocolloid, blue dye and gamma probe.
- **No-skip spread and superomedial sentinels** justify **modified ILND (Catalona)**: smaller field, saphenous vein preserved; positive frozen section → complete radical ILND.

**4. Palpable nodes (cN+)**
- About half are reactive, but do not delay with antibiotics. Ultrasound-guided **FNAC**; positive → **radical ILND** on that side, stage the other.
- **Bulky (≥4 cm), fixed or bilateral nodes:** neoadjuvant **TIP** (paclitaxel, ifosfamide, cisplatin) × 4, then surgery in responders.

**5. Pelvic nodes**
- Stepwise spread means a negative groin needs no pelvic dissection.
- **PLND (pelvic lymph node dissection)**, ipsilateral, if **more than 2 inguinal nodes or ENE (extranodal extension)**. ENE in any node is pN3.

**6. Complications of ILND**
- Wound infection, **flap necrosis**, **lymphocele**, **lymphoedema**, DVT (deep vein thrombosis). Reduced by modified templates, saphenous sparing and **VEIL (video-endoscopic inguinal lymphadenectomy)**.

**Also know:** adjuvant therapy for pN2–3; Cabanas sentinel biopsy abandoned.

**Diagram to draw:** retroperitoneal landing zones (right interaortocaval, left para-aortic) beside the femoral triangle with Daseler's zones and Cloquet's node.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Right testis → interaortocaval; left → para-aortic; crossover right to left only.
> - Scrotum and penis → inguinal nodes; never operate trans-scrotally.
> - Penile drainage is bilateral and stepwise without skips: stage both groins, PLND only if inguinal nodes positive.
> - pT1b or higher: DSNB or ILND within 3 months; Kroon 84% vs 35%.

## Q2. Describe IVU & its applications in Urology.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Definition and principle
- IVU (intravenous urography; also IVP, intravenous pyelography or excretory urography) is a series of timed abdominal radiographs after intravenous iodinated contrast.
- Contrast is **freely filtered by the glomerulus and not reabsorbed**. As water is reabsorbed it is concentrated in the tubules (**nephrogram**) and then outlines the calyces, pelvis, ureters and bladder (**pyelogram**).
- So IVU shows **anatomy plus a rough, qualitative idea of function and drainage** of each kidney.
- **Current status:** largely replaced by CTU (CT [computed tomography] urography). It is still used where CT is unavailable (common in India) or when dynamic excretion and drainage must be seen.

### Contrast media
| Type | Example | Feature |
|---|---|---|
| High-osmolar ionic | Diatrizoate | More reactions; obsolete for IV use |
| **Low-osmolar non-ionic** (standard) | Iohexol, iopamidol | Fewer reactions |
| Iso-osmolar non-ionic | Iodixanol | High-risk renal patients |

### Preparation
- Check creatinine or eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate), allergy, asthma, pregnancy, diabetes on metformin, myeloma.
- Laxative the night before (bowel gas hides the kidneys); short fast but **no dehydration**.
- Premedicate high-risk patients, e.g. prednisone 50 mg at 13, 7 and 1 h plus diphenhydramine 50 mg at 1 h.

### Technique: film sequence
| Film | Timing | Shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Scout (control) KUB** (kidney–ureter–bladder) | Before contrast | Calcification, bones, gas, technique |
| Contrast | Low-osmolar, about 1 mL/kg (usually 50–100 mL) IV | — |
| **Nephrogram / nephrotomogram** | About 1 min (60–90 s) | Renal size, outline, parenchyma |
| Pyelogram | 5 min | Calyces; then apply **ureteric compression** |
| Compression film | 10 min | Well-distended calyces and pelvis |
| **Release (full-length) film** | About 15 min | Whole ureters |
| Bladder and **post-void** films | 20–30 min | Bladder outline, residue, lower ureters |
| Delayed films | Up to 24 h | Level of obstruction; **no excretion at 24 h = non-functioning kidney** |
- **Also know:** films in expiration; prone film fills the ureters; oblique views; erect film for nephroptosis.
- Compression is avoided in suspected obstruction or colic, aortic aneurysm, recent abdominal surgery and trauma.

### Contraindications
- Previous severe contrast reaction; significant renal impairment; pregnancy (relative); dehydration, myeloma.

### Nephrogram patterns
| Pattern | Causes |
|---|---|
| Absent | Renal artery occlusion, agenesis, avulsion |
| **Delayed, increasingly dense** | **Acute obstruction**, renal artery stenosis |
| Striated | Acute pyelonephritis, obstruction |
| **Rim (cortical rim) sign** | Renal infarction (capsular collaterals) |
| Bilateral persistent dense | Hypotension, ATN (acute tubular necrosis) |

### Applications in urology
| Condition | IVU features |
|---|---|
| **Calculi** | Confirms an opacity is in the tract; **radiolucent stone = filling defect**; site, obstruction, calyceal anatomy before SWL (shock wave lithotripsy) or PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) |
| **Acute obstruction** | Delayed dense nephrogram, delayed excretion, dilatation down to the block, **standing column** of contrast, forniceal rupture |
| **PUJ (pelviureteric junction) obstruction** | Ballooned pelvis, delayed drainage, non-dilated ureter; crossing-vessel indentation |
| **Duplex system** | Upper moiety obstructed or non-functioning: lower moiety displaced, "**drooping lily**" |
| **Ureterocele** | "**Cobra head**" (spring onion) at the bladder |
| **Horseshoe kidney** | Low, medially rotated; lower calyces point medially |
| **Retrocaval ureter** | "Reverse J" or **fish-hook** at L3 |
| **Medullary sponge kidney** | "**Paintbrush**" papillary striations |
| **Papillary necrosis** | **Ring sign**, "lobster claw" calyces |
| **Genitourinary TB** (tuberculosis) | **Moth-eaten** calyx, **phantom calyx**, Kerr's kink, **pipestem** or beaded ureter, contracted bladder, autonephrectomy |
| **Urothelial tumour** | Irregular filling defect, non-visualised calyx |
| Retroperitoneal fibrosis | Medial deviation and tapering of ureters with hydronephrosis |
| Pelvic lipomatosis | Pear-shaped bladder |
| Schistosomiasis | Ureteric striations, calcified bladder |
- **Trauma: one-shot intraoperative IVP:** 2 mL/kg IV push, single film at 10 min. It confirms a functioning contralateral kidney before exploration in the unstable patient.
- **Also know:** nephroptosis (descent >5 cm or two vertebral bodies supine to erect); urinary fistulae (ureterovaginal); renal masses (distortion); post-operative assessment; diverticula.

### Complications
- **Contrast reactions:** immediate (urticaria to anaphylaxis) or delayed (rash). Severe reaction: stop contrast, **IM (intramuscular) adrenaline 0.3–0.5 mg** every 5–15 min (maximum 1 mg), oxygen, β2-agonist. Observe 30 min.
- **CA-AKI (contrast-associated acute kidney injury):** prevent with hydration, low or iso-osmolar contrast, lowest dose.
- Radiation; metformin-related lactic acidosis in renal impairment.

### Limitations and current role
- Two-dimensional; bowel gas; poor parenchymal detail; misses small masses; poor in renal failure.
- CTU is more sensitive for stones, masses and urothelial lesions, but gives a higher radiation dose than IVU. IVU remains useful in resource-limited settings.

### Recent advances
- Split-bolus CTU (fewer phases); dual-energy CT (virtual unenhanced images); MR urography without radiation.

**Diagram to draw:** normal IVU with minor and major calyces, pelvis, ureter and the three narrowings; beside it a "drooping lily".

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Contrast is filtered, not reabsorbed: nephrogram then pyelogram.
> - Delayed, increasingly dense nephrogram = acute obstruction; no excretion at 24 h = non-functioning.
> - Classic signs: drooping lily, cobra head, fish-hook, paintbrush, ring sign, phantom calyx.
> - Severe reaction: IM adrenaline 0.3–0.5 mg; CTU has replaced IVU.

## Q3a. Premalignant conditions in urology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- A premalignant (precursor) lesion is altered tissue with a raised risk of invasive cancer, often **intraepithelial neoplasia with an intact basement membrane**. Treating it prevents cancer.

### Penis
| Lesion | Key points |
|---|---|
| **PeIN** (penile intraepithelial neoplasia) | HPV (human papillomavirus)-associated (commonest) or differentiated (HPV-independent). WHO (World Health Organization) 2022: all PeIN is high grade |
| Erythroplasia of Queyrat | Red velvety glans plaque; **progresses in 10–33%** |
| Bowen disease | Scaly shaft plaque; about 5% progress |
| Bowenoid papulosis | Shaft papules in young men; does not progress |
| **Lichen sclerosus** (BXO, balanitis xerotica obliterans) | SCC (squamous cell carcinoma) in **2.3–9%**, HPV-independent type |
| Giant condyloma (Buschke–Löwenstein) | HPV 6/11; local invasion; coexisting SCC |
| **Also know** | Cutaneous horn, micaceous balanitis, phimosis, genital lichen planus |
- **Mechanism (HPV):** E6 inactivates p53, E7 inactivates retinoblastoma protein; p16 is the marker.
- **Treatment:** biopsy first; circumcision for preputial lesions; topical **5-fluorouracil or 5% imiquimod**; laser; **glans resurfacing**. Lichen sclerosus: clobetasol 0.05%, lifelong follow-up.

### Bladder
- **CIS** (carcinoma in situ): flat high-grade lesion, umbrella cells lost; untreated, **40–83%** invade muscle. Treat with BCG (bacillus Calmette–Guérin).
- **Urothelial dysplasia:** 14–19% progress; 60% to CIS with prior cancer.
- **Cystitis glandularis** (intestinal type): low risk of adenocarcinoma; resect and survey.
- **Leukoplakia** (keratinising squamous metaplasia): traditionally premalignant; Campbell now says **not** (no TP53 changes). Schistosomiasis and chronic catheters predispose to SCC.

### Prostate
- **HGPIN** (high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia): precursor; isolated HGPIN no longer needs repeat biopsy. IDC-P (intraductal carcinoma of the prostate) usually means invasive cancer nearby.

### Testis
- **GCNIS** (germ cell neoplasia in situ): precursor of all postpubertal GCTs (germ cell tumours) except spermatocytic tumour; **50% invasive within 5 years**. Contralateral GCNIS 5–9%. Treat by orchidectomy or **radiotherapy ≥20 Gy**.
- Cryptorchidism; **gonadoblastoma** in dysgenetic gonads with Y material.

### Kidney and others
- **Also know:** papillary adenoma; acquired cystic kidney disease on dialysis; urethral diverticulum; bowel in augmentation.

**Diagram to draw:** normal urothelium vs dysplasia vs CIS (umbrella cells lost).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - PeIN is all high grade; Queyrat 10–33% vs Bowen 5% progression.
> - Untreated bladder CIS: 40–83% invade.
> - GCNIS: 50% invasive in 5 years; radiotherapy ≥20 Gy.

## Q3b. Bacteriuria in pregnancy.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 3, Q3b)

### Definition
- **ASB (asymptomatic bacteriuria):** ≥10⁵ CFU (colony-forming units)/mL of one uropathogen in a midstream sample without urinary symptoms (≥10² CFU/mL on a catheter sample).

### Epidemiology
- Prevalence is **not raised** by pregnancy (about 2–10%); higher with low socioeconomic status, multiparity, sickle cell trait. Organisms as in non-pregnant women (mainly *E. coli*).

### Why it matters (pathophysiology)
- **Progesterone** relaxes ureteric smooth muscle; the **uterus compresses the ureters at the pelvic brim** (right more). Dilatation from about 15 weeks; normal by 6 weeks postpartum.
- Result: **stasis and hydroureteronephrosis**, so bacteriuria persists and ascends.
- GFR (glomerular filtration rate) rises 30–50%: creatinine and drug levels fall.
- **Untreated ASB → pyelonephritis in 15–45%** (historical), 60–75% in the third trimester. Treatment cuts this to **1–4%**.
- Pyelonephritis causes **preterm birth, low birth weight** and maternal sepsis. **Cochrane 2019:** treating ASB reduces all three.

### Screening
- **Urine culture at 12–16 weeks** or the first visit (USPSTF [US Preventive Services Task Force], IDSA [Infectious Diseases Society of America]). Dipstick misses many cases.

### Treatment
| Safe | Restricted or avoided |
|---|---|
| **β-lactams** (amoxicillin, cephalexin): throughout | **Nitrofurantoin:** stop from 35 weeks (neonatal haemolysis, G6PD [glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase] deficiency); not for pyelonephritis |
| Fosfomycin: lower tract only | **Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole:** avoid 1st trimester (folate, neural tube); caution near term (kernicterus) |
| Gentamicin: pyelonephritis | **Fluoroquinolones**, **tetracyclines**, chloramphenicol: avoid |
- ASB and cystitis: culture-directed, **4–7 days** (cures more than a single dose); **repeat culture** after treatment.

### Pyelonephritis
- **Admit**; IV (intravenous) β-lactam (± gentamicin) until afebrile, then oral to complete ≥14 days. No improvement by **72 h → renal ultrasound** (stone, obstruction, abscess).

### Prophylaxis
- After one pyelonephritis or two cystitis episodes: nightly nitrofurantoin or cephalexin until delivery; close culture surveillance is an alternative (Cochrane 2015).
- **Also know:** urinary diversion or augmentation, SCI (spinal cord injury): most treat ASB in pregnancy.

**Diagram to draw:** right hydroureter compressed at the pelvic brim.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Prevalence unchanged; risk of pyelonephritis raised (15–45% untreated).
> - Screen by culture at 12–16 weeks; treat 4–7 days.
> - β-lactams safe; no fluoroquinolones or tetracyclines; nitrofurantoin not after 35 weeks.

## Q3c. Complications if DJS
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Taken as complications of a DJ (double-J) ureteric stent.*

### Introduction
- The **double-J (double-pigtail)** stent (Finney 1978) is a self-retaining polymer tube with coils in the pelvis and bladder. Consent should cover its symptoms and complications.

### Classification of complications
| Timing | Complications |
|---|---|
| **At insertion** | **Misplacement**: proximal end below the obstruction; **submucosal** passage or perforation (suspect when force is needed) |
| **Early** | **Stent-related symptoms**; haematuria; infection and urosepsis; **vesicoureteric reflux** up the stent (flank pain on voiding) |
| **Late** | **Migration** (proximal or distal; short stent); **encrustation**; **stent failure** (obstruction); biofilm and colonisation; **fracture**; **forgotten stent**; rarely **ureteroarterial fistula** |

### Stent-related symptoms
- About **4 in 5 patients**: urgency, frequency, dysuria, haematuria, flank and groin pain; sexual and work problems.
- **Mechanism:** the **intravesical coil irritates the trigone**, and **reflux up the stent** distends the kidney on voiding.
- Measured by **USSQ** (Ureteral Stent Symptom Questionnaire).
- **Reduced by:** **α-blockers and antimuscarinics**; intraureteral placement; hydrocoated silicone; shortest dwell. Diameter does not matter.

### Encrustation and forgotten stent
- Minerals and biofilm deposit on the stent. Risk rises with **dwell time**, infection, stone-forming urine and pregnancy. Leads to obstruction, sepsis, fracture, **ureteric avulsion on removal**, renal loss.
- **FECal grading (Forgotten, Encrusted, Calcified):** I minimal linear; II circular; III partial on one or both coils; IV complete on one coil; V both coils.
- **Management:** non-contrast CT (computed tomography); never pull by force; clear **bladder coil** (cystolithotripsy), then **ureter** (ureteroscopy), then **renal coil** (flexible ureteroscopy or PCNL [percutaneous nephrolithotomy]).

### Stent failure
- Extrinsic compression (malignancy, radiation), debris, encrustation. Obstructive symptoms with a stent → image; change stent, or **PCN** (percutaneous nephrostomy) or **metallic stent**.

### Prevention
- Indication-based stenting; correct length; **stent register** and removal date; long-term stents changed every 3–6 months (local practice); **pregnancy: change about every 4 weeks**.

**Diagram to draw:** DJ stent in situ with bladder coil on the trigone and arrows showing reflux.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Symptoms in about 80%: trigonal irritation and reflux; α-blockers and antimuscarinics help.
> - Encrustation rises with dwell time; FECal I–V.
> - Encrusted stent: bladder, then ureter, then kidney; keep a stent register.

## Q3d. Proteinuria
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2017-Summer Paper 1, Q3f)

### Definition
- Normal excretion is **80–150 mg/day** (albumin <30 mg/day). The glomerulus holds back albumin; the proximal tubule reabsorbs filtered small proteins.
- **Nephrotic range:** >3.5 g/day with hypoalbuminaemia, oedema and hyperlipidaemia. Pregnancy: abnormal only above 300 mg/24 h.

### Types
| Type | Mechanism | Amount | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Glomerular** (commonest) | Leaky filter | Often >2 g; albumin about 70% | Diabetes, glomerulonephritis, amyloid |
| **Tubular** | Failed reabsorption of small proteins | Seldom >2–3 g; globulins | Fanconi syndrome, heavy metals, drugs |
| **Overflow** | Excess plasma protein | 0.3–2 g | **Myeloma** (Bence Jones), myoglobin, haemoglobin |

| By timing | Features |
|---|---|
| **Transient** | Fever, exercise, heart failure; commonest in children |
| **Orthostatic** | Young men, upright only, <1 g; benign. Normal first-morning sample, raised after hours upright |
| **Persistent** | Mostly glomerular; needs full work-up |

### Detection and quantification
- **Dipstick** (tetrabromophenol blue) detects mainly albumin from 20–30 mg/dL; misses dilute urine and **light chains**. **Sulfosalicylic acid** precipitates all proteins; dipstick negative but sulfosalicylic acid positive → suspect myeloma.
- KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) categories by ACR (albumin-to-creatinine ratio): **A1 <30, A2 30–300, A3 >300 mg/g**. Alternatives: PCR (protein-to-creatinine ratio), 24-h urine.

### Evaluation
```
Positive dipstick → repeat; exclude UTI, fever, exercise
→ persistent → quantify (ACR, PCR or 24-h)
→ creatinine/eGFR, sediment (dysmorphic RBCs, casts), ultrasound
→ glomerular (>2 g, albumin) → nephrology ± biopsy; C3, C4, ANA if systemic
→ globulins → electrophoresis, immunofixation (myeloma)
```
- UTI: urinary tract infection; eGFR: estimated glomerular filtration rate; RBC: red blood cell; ANA: antinuclear antibody.

### Urological relevance
- **Haematuria work-up:** urological bleeding rarely raises protein much; proteinuria with casts or dysmorphic cells suggests medical renal disease.
- **Reflux nephropathy:** proteinuria marks progression to renal failure.
- **After nephrectomy:** hyperfiltration causes proteinuria and FSGS (focal segmental glomerulosclerosis); favour nephron sparing.
- **Living donors:** significant proteinuria excludes donation. **Also know:** mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin) inhibitors cause it.

### Management
- Treat the cause; control blood pressure; **ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitor or ARB (angiotensin receptor blocker)**; SGLT2 (sodium–glucose cotransporter-2) inhibitors.

**Diagram to draw:** glomerulus and proximal tubule marking glomerular, tubular and overflow sites.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Normal <150 mg/day; nephrotic >3.5 g/day.
> - Glomerular, tubular, overflow; orthostatic is benign.
> - Dipstick misses light chains; use sulfosalicylic acid or immunofixation.

## Q3e. Radionuclide studies for PUJ obstruction.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- Dilatation is not obstruction. PUJ (pelviureteric junction) obstruction is impaired drainage that damages the kidney. **Diuretic renography** is the functional standard and best follow-up test.

### Tracers
| Tracer | Handling | Use |
|---|---|---|
| ⁹⁹ᵐTc-**MAG3** (technetium-99m mercaptoacetyltriglycine) | Tubular secretion | **Agent of choice**: split function and drainage; good images even with poor function |
| ⁹⁹ᵐTc-**DTPA** (diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid) | Glomerular filtration only | GFR (glomerular filtration rate); poor images in renal impairment |
| ⁹⁹ᵐTc-**DMSA** (dimercaptosuccinic acid) | Cortical retention | Static split function; no drainage data |

### "Well-tempered" diuretic renogram (standard protocol)
- **Hydration:** oral or IV (intravenous) fluids; dehydration gives false obstruction.
- **Bladder catheter:** removes the effect of a full bladder and reflux.
- **Furosemide:** 0.5 mg/kg (maximum 40 mg) in adults; 1 mg/kg in children.
- **Timing:** **F+20** (20 min after tracer, when the pelvis is full), **F0** (with tracer) or **F−15** (15 min before, so the system is already in diuresis).
- **Regions of interest** around cortex (function) and pelvis (drainage); then upright (gravity) images.

### Outputs
- **DRF (differential renal function):** area under each curve at 1–3 min; normal 45–55%.
- **Drainage half-time (T½)** from maximal filling:

| T½ after furosemide | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| <10 min | Not obstructed |
| 10–20 min | Equivocal |
| >20 min | Obstructed |

- **Curve patterns (O'Reilly):** I normal; II obstructed (rising, no response); IIIa dilated non-obstructed (prompt fall after furosemide); IIIb equivocal.
- **Also know:** P40 (activity remaining at 40 min); output efficiency; NORA (normalised residual activity).

### Indications for pyeloplasty from renography
- **DRF <40%**, **fall in DRF >10%** on serial scans, **T½ >20 min**, upright retention; also symptoms, infection, increasing dilatation.

### Pitfalls
- Dehydration, early diuretic, full bladder, wrong ROI (region of interest), very poor function.
- One Campbell chapter calls T½ >40 min obstructed; children have no agreed cut-off, so serial trends and curve shape matter.

### Follow-up and alternatives
- After pyeloplasty: renogram about 1 month after stent removal; spontaneous drainage confirms success.
- Equivocal → **Whitaker test** (>15–20 cm H₂O obstructed) or magnetic resonance urography.

**Diagram to draw:** renogram curves: normal, obstructed (rising) and dilated non-obstructed (falls after furosemide).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - MAG3 is the tracer of choice; catheter and hydration.
> - DRF normal 45–55%; T½ <10 normal, >20 min obstructed.
> - Operate for DRF <40%, fall >10%, or poor drainage with symptoms.

## Q3f. Immunosuppressants.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q1)

*Taken as immunosuppressant drugs in renal transplantation.*

### Principle
- T-cell activation needs three signals: **signal 1** (TCR [T-cell receptor] binding donor antigen → calcineurin → IL-2 [interleukin-2] transcription), **signal 2** (costimulation, CD28–CD80/86) and **signal 3** (IL-2 receptor → mTOR [mammalian target of rapamycin] → proliferation). Each drug blocks a step.
- Phases: **induction** (peri-transplant), **maintenance** (lifelong), **rescue** (rejection).

### Classification
| Class | Drug | Mechanism | Main toxicity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CNI** (calcineurin inhibitor) | **Tacrolimus**, ciclosporin | Block calcineurin (signal 1) | **Nephrotoxicity**, diabetes, tremor, hypertension; ciclosporin: gum hypertrophy, hirsutism |
| **Antimetabolites** | **Mycophenolate**, azathioprine | Block purine synthesis (IMPDH [inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase]) | Diarrhoea, leucopenia |
| **mTOR inhibitors** | Sirolimus, everolimus | Block signal 3 | **Lymphocele, poor wound healing**, proteinuria, dyslipidaemia; not nephrotoxic, antitumour |
| **Corticosteroids** | Prednisolone, methylprednisolone | Block cytokine transcription | Diabetes, bone loss, cataract |
| **Costimulation blocker** | **Belatacept** | CTLA4-Ig binds CD80/86 (signal 2) | **PTLD** (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder); avoid if EBV (Epstein–Barr virus)-negative |
| **Depleting antibodies** | rATG (rabbit antithymocyte globulin), alemtuzumab | T-cell depletion | Infection, PTLD |
| **Non-depleting** | **Basiliximab** | Anti-CD25 (IL-2 receptor) | Few |
| **B-cell, antibody** | Rituximab, IVIG (intravenous immunoglobulin), bortezomib, eculizumab, imlifidase | Antibody-mediated rejection, desensitisation | — |

### Standard regimen
- **Over 90%** receive induction and **tacrolimus plus mycophenolate**; steroids in more than two-thirds. Acute rejection has fallen from ≥60% to <10%.
- **Tacrolimus trough monitoring**; CYP3A4 (cytochrome P450 3A4) interactions (azoles, macrolides raise levels; rifampicin lowers).
- **Rejection:** T-cell-mediated → IV (intravenous) steroids, then rATG; antibody-mediated → plasmapheresis, IVIG, rituximab.

### Urological relevance
- **Infection:** UTI (urinary tract infection), **BK virus** (ureteric stricture, nephropathy), CMV (cytomegalovirus).
- **Malignancy:** skin cancer, PTLD, RCC (renal cell carcinoma, mostly native kidneys), **bladder cancer about 3-fold** and more aggressive.
- **Surgery:** lymphocele and wound problems (mTOR); stones (CNI, steroids).
- **Also know:** belatacept (BENEFIT trial: better glomerular filtration rate), extended-release tacrolimus, TRANSFORM (everolimus with reduced CNI non-inferior).

**Diagram to draw:** antigen-presenting cell and T cell with signals 1–3 and the drug acting at each.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Tacrolimus + mycophenolate ± steroids after induction (basiliximab or ATG).
> - CNI nephrotoxic; mTOR inhibitors cause lymphocele and poor healing.
> - Belatacept blocks signal 2; avoid if EBV-negative.
