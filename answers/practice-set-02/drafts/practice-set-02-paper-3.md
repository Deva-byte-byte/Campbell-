## Q1. Iatrogenic ureteric injury
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(upper ureteric injury also answered in 2023-Winter Paper 3, Q2)

*Assumed scope: surgical (iatrogenic) injury at any level of the ureter, in gynaecological, colorectal, vascular and urological surgery.*

### Introduction
- Iatrogenic injury is commoner than external trauma. **The distal ureter is the commonest site** in non-urological surgery.
- It is mainly a problem of **recognition**: thermal, ligation and devascularisation injuries are invisible at operation and declare themselves days to weeks later.

### Aetiology and incidence (EAU (European Association of Urology) trauma data)

| Source | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Gynaecological** | Historically about half. Hysterectomy: vaginal 0.02–0.5%, abdominal 0.03–2%, minimally invasive 0.13–6%; prolapse surgery highest (1.7–4.3%). Endometriosis and placenta accreta raise risk |
| Colorectal | 0.15–10%; laparoscopic equals open |
| **Urological** | Ureteroscopy is the commonest cause in some series: perforation 0.2–2%, avulsion under 0.5% (proximal third most at risk); stricture about 3%. Also radical prostatectomy, partial nephrectomy or ablation (late proximal stricture) |
| Vascular | Aorto-iliac bypass: transient hydronephrosis 12–20%; up to 85% unrecognised. Rare ureteroarterial fistula (chronic stent plus pelvic surgery or radiation) |

- **Danger sites in the pelvis:** pelvic brim near the infundibulopelvic ligament; under the uterine artery; beside the uterosacral ligament; vaginal fornix near the UVJ (ureterovesical junction).
- **Mechanisms:** ligation, crush, laceration, transection, avulsion, thermal injury, devascularisation. About 10% of women get a large share of distal ureteric blood from the uterine artery, so some injuries are unavoidable.
- **Risk factors:** previous surgery, adhesions, malignancy, radiotherapy, heavy bleeding, long operation.
- **URS (ureteroscopy) risks:** long operation, 12/14-Fr sheath, impacted or proximal stone, inexperience.

### Classification: AAST (American Association for the Surgery of Trauma)

| Grade | Injury |
|---|---|
| I | Haematoma or contusion, no devascularisation |
| II | Laceration <50% of circumference |
| III | Laceration ≥50% |
| IV | Complete transection, <2 cm devascularised |
| V | Avulsion, >2 cm devascularised |

### Prevention
- See the ureter at the **pelvic brim** (crossing the common iliac artery) and trace it before ligating pedicles. Good haemostasis and exposure.
- **Prophylactic stents** help recognition in high-risk cases, but do not clearly reduce injury. **ICG (indocyanine green)** instilled into the ureter makes it fluoresce for hours.
- URS: safety wire; ≤14-Fr sheath in an unstented ureter; fragment before basketing; "stop, stent, stage".

### Recognition
**Intraoperative (best outcome)**
- Inspect the ureter; IV (intravenous) indigo carmine or methylene blue; cystoscopy for efflux, with retrograde pyelogram or stent. Check the other ureter and bladder.
- Methylene blue: avoid with SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors; serotonin toxicity) and G6PD (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) deficiency.

**Delayed (most cases)**
- Fever, ileus, flank pain, leucocytosis, rising drain output, urinoma, anuria (bilateral), or **ureterovaginal fistula**: normal voiding plus constant leak, usually within 4 weeks.
- **CT urography with 10-minute delays** (both ureters). **Retrograde pyelogram** is most sensitive and allows a stent. Drain-fluid creatinine above serum confirms urine. Double dye test separates VVF (vesicovaginal fistula) from ureterovaginal fistula.

### Management
**Principles:** debride to bleeding tissue; spatulated, tension-free, watertight, stented repair with fine absorbable suture; drain; preserve adventitia; omentum for thermal or radiation injury.

**By timing**
```
Recognised at surgery -> repair at once (stable patient)
Unstable -> ligate + later nephrostomy, or exteriorised stent
Recognised late -> try retrograde stent (or nephrostomy + antegrade stent)
   |-- succeeds -> stent 4-8 weeks, then image
   |-- fails -> nephrostomy; early repair if <=7 days,
               otherwise delayed repair after about 6 weeks
Uncontrolled sepsis / collections -> drain; repair regardless of timing
```

**At surgery, by injury**
- Suture through the wall or minor thermal injury: remove suture, stent.
- Partial tear: close transversely over a stent (4–8 weeks).
- Ligation: remove ligature; stent if viable, excise and repair if not.

**By level (complete injury)**

| Level | Repair |
|---|---|
| Upper | Ureteroureterostomy; ureteropyelostomy (PUJ (pelviureteric junction) avulsion); ureterocalicostomy |
| Mid | Ureteroureterostomy ± downward nephropexy; TUU (transureteroureterostomy) when delayed |
| Lower (commonest iatrogenic) | **Ureteroneocystostomy, non-tunnelled ± psoas hitch** (95–100% success); **Boari flap** for gaps up to 12 cm |
| Long loss (delayed only) | Ileal ureter or Yang–Monti; buccal graft ureteroplasty; autotransplantation (last resort) |

- **URS perforation:** stop, stent about 4 weeks, image later. **Avulsion:** nephrostomy, delayed reconstruction; avoid nephrectomy.
- **Ureterovaginal fistula:** stent first (best within 2 weeks); reimplant if it fails (success ≥90%).
- **Ureteroarterial fistula:** endovascular covered stent.

### Follow-up
- Ultrasound or diuretic renogram at 4–12 weeks and 1 year; strictures can be silent.
- **Also know:** robotic reconstruction for delayed repair; malpractice claims follow gynaecological surgery most often.

**Diagram to draw:** psoas hitch with non-tunnelled reimplant; ureter's relation to the uterine artery.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Distal ureter commonest iatrogenic site; most injuries present late.
> - CT with 10-minute delays; retrograde pyelogram is most sensitive and allows stenting.
> - Late: stent first; early repair ≤7 days, otherwise delayed after 6 weeks.
> - Lower ureter: psoas hitch 95–100%; Boari flap up to 12 cm.

## Q2. Discuss mgt. of b/l VUR.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2015-Summer Paper 3, Q2 and 2020-Summer Paper 1, Q2)

### Introduction
- **VUR (vesicoureteral reflux):** retrograde flow of urine from bladder to upper tract. **Primary:** short intravesical tunnel; tunnel length to diameter should be **5:1** (Paquin). **Secondary:** high bladder pressure (PUV (posterior urethral valves), neurogenic bladder, BBD (bladder and bowel dysfunction)).
- **Bilateral VUR is a high-risk feature:** it resolves less and scars more. **Infection, not sterile reflux, damages the kidney.** So management treats infection risk, not the reflux itself.

### International grading (VCUG, voiding cystourethrogram)

| Grade | Findings |
|---|---|
| I | Non-dilated ureter only |
| II | Pelvis and calyces reached, no dilatation |
| III | Mild–moderate dilatation, minimal forniceal blunting |
| IV | Moderate dilatation and tortuosity |
| V | Gross dilatation; papillary impressions lost |

### Evaluation
- Febrile UTI (urinary tract infection) history, BBD, BP (blood pressure), growth, creatinine, proteinuria.
- **RBUS (renal and bladder ultrasound)**; **VCUG** (grade, urethra: excludes PUV); **DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid)** for scars and split function. Nuclear cystogram for follow-up.
- **UDR (ureteral diameter ratio)** predicts resolution and breakthrough UTI better than grade.

### Natural history
- Grade II 63–85% resolve; grade III about 50%; **grades IV–V and bilateral III rarely**.
- **Bilateral grade IV persists most** (AUA (American Urological Association) 1997). Infants with high grade still resolve fairly well.

### Risk stratification (Hidas)

| Low risk | High risk |
|---|---|
| Male, >5 years | Female, <1 year |
| Unilateral, grade I–II | **Bilateral**, grade III–V |
| No BBD, no scars | BBD, scars, multiple febrile UTIs |

### Medical management
- **Treat BBD first:** timed voiding, hydration, constipation treatment, anticholinergics if needed.
- **CAP (continuous antibiotic prophylaxis):** one low dose at night.

| Agent | Age | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Trimethoprim or amoxicillin | <2 months | Avoid sulfonamide (jaundice) |
| TMP-SMX (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) | >2 months | Used in RIVUR |
| Nitrofurantoin | >2 months | Rare lung, liver toxicity |

- **RIVUR (2014):** recurrent UTI **23.6% → 12.9%**; **scarring unchanged**; benefit mainly in high risk.
- **Swedish Reflux Trial** (grade III–IV, 1–2 years): girls had febrile UTI 19% on CAP, 23% after endoscopy, 57% on surveillance; boys no difference.
- **IRSC (International Reflux Study in Children) and Birmingham:** surgery equalled medical care for new scars.
- **Circumcision** or topical steroid for phimosis (NNT (number needed to treat) about 4).
- **Guidelines:** AUA 2010 and EAU/ESPU (European Association of Urology/European Society for Paediatric Urology): CAP for high risk; correct after breakthrough infection.

### Indications for correction
- Breakthrough febrile UTI despite good bladder and bowel habits.
- New scars or falling function.
- Persistent high-grade (IV–V) bilateral VUR (girls traditionally corrected before puberty; benefit unproven).
- Non-adherence or intolerance to CAP; parental choice.
- **Controversy:** asymptomatic bilateral high-grade VUR in an older boy with normal kidneys can be observed.

### Surgical techniques
**Principles:** exclude secondary VUR; gentle handling; tension-free; tunnel 5:1; straight course.

**1. Endoscopic injection (Dx/HA, dextranomer/hyaluronic acid)**
- **STING** (subureteric Teflon injection): 2–3 mm below the orifice. **HIT** (hydrodistension implantation): inside the orifice, better. **Double HIT** about 90%.
- Aim for a "volcano" mound; day case. Repeat once or twice, then reimplant.
- Recurrence about 20% at 2 years; late obstruction reported.

**2. Open reimplantation** (success **95.9%**, Elder 1997)

| Technique | Method | Strength / weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Cohen** | Intravesical, cross-trigonal | Commonest, over 98%; later ureteroscopy hard |
| Politano–Leadbetter | Intravesical, new superomedial hiatus | Long tunnel; blind hiatus risks bowel |
| Glenn–Anderson | Intravesical, advanced to bladder neck | Least kinking; short tunnel |
| **Lich–Gregoir** | Extravesical detrusorotomy | No haematuria or spasm; **retention up to 10% after bilateral** |
| **RALUR-EV** (robot-assisted extravesical) | Y-shaped detrusorotomy | Less pain; approaching open results |

### Criteria for selection

| Situation | Preferred option |
|---|---|
| Grades II–IV, parents want least invasive | Dx/HA injection (lower success at higher grade) |
| **Bilateral VUR, small or trabeculated bladder** | **Cohen** (both sides through one cystotomy) |
| Grade V, dilated ureter | Intravesical reimplant ± tapering (Politano–Leadbetter or Cohen) |
| Unilateral, older child | Lich–Gregoir or RALUR-EV |
| Bilateral extravesical | Nerve-sparing dissection; warn about retention |
| Duplex system | Common sheath reimplant |
| Failed injection | Reimplant (not harder after Dx/HA) |
| Neurogenic or PUV bladder | Treat bladder first (anticholinergics, CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation)) |

### Complications and follow-up
- Ultrasound at 6–12 weeks. Persistent reflux usually resolves by 1 year. Obstruction early (oedema): stent or nephrostomy; late (ischaemia, kink): redo.
- Lifelong BP checks; scarred women risk pre-eclampsia.

```
Treat BBD -> low risk: observe | high risk (bilateral): CAP
Breakthrough UTI / new scar / persistent IV-V -> Dx/HA or Cohen
```

**Diagram to draw:** grades I–V; Cohen cross-trigonal reimplant.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Bilateral grade IV persists most; bilateral VUR is high risk.
> - RIVUR: CAP halves recurrent UTI, not scarring.
> - Open reimplant 95.9%; Cohen over 98% and suits bilateral VUR.
> - Bilateral extravesical repair: retention up to 10%.

## Q3a. Uroflowmetry
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2018-Summer Paper 2, Q3f)

### Definition
- Non-invasive urodynamic test recording **voided volume per unit time** (mL/s). It shows detrusor contraction and outlet resistance together.

### Method
- Comfortably full bladder; private void in the preferred position. Ask whether the void was typical; compare with the frequency–volume chart.
- **Voided volume ≥150 mL** (Qmax unreliable below 125–150 mL). Repeat; then measure **PVR (postvoid residual)** by ultrasound.
- Flowmeters: weight transducer (load cell), rotating disc, capacitance dipstick.

### Parameters
| Parameter | Note |
|---|---|
| **Qmax** (maximum flow) | Key value; read from smoothed curve, ignoring spikes |
| Qave (average flow) | Less accurate for obstruction |
| Voided volume | ≥150 mL |
| Flow time vs voiding time | Voiding time includes interruptions |
| Time to Qmax | Normally early in the void |
| Voiding efficiency | VV ÷ (VV + PVR) × 100 |

### Curve patterns (ICCS (International Children's Continence Society))
| Pattern | Suggests |
|---|---|
| **Bell** | Normal |
| Tower (high, short) | Overactive bladder |
| **Plateau** (flat, prolonged) | Fixed obstruction: stricture, PUV (posterior urethral valves) |
| Staccato (never reaches zero) | Sphincter overactivity, dysfunctional voiding |
| **Interrupted** (reaches zero) | Detrusor underactivity with straining, or DSD (detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia) |

### Interpretation
- **Qmax <10 mL/s:** obstruction likely (more specific). **<15 mL/s:** more sensitive, less specific.
- **Volume correction:** corrected Qmax = Qmax ÷ √VV; **Siroky** (men) and **Liverpool** (men and women) nomograms.
- **Key limitation:** cannot separate BOO (bladder outlet obstruction) from DUA (detrusor underactivity); only a pressure–flow study can.
- Children: valid if voided volume ≥50% of expected capacity; Qmax only if the peak lasts ≥2 s.

### Uses
- BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia): before active treatment and to monitor outcome.
- After urethroplasty or urethrotomy (stricture recurrence).
- Children: BBD (bladder and bowel dysfunction), after valve ablation; with patch EMG (electromyography).
- Women with voiding dysfunction.
- **AUA (American Urological Association):** pressure–flow study may be done before BPH surgery if Qmax >10 mL/s.

**Diagram to draw:** bell, plateau, tower and interrupted curves.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Voided volume ≥150 mL; Qmax is the key value.
> - Plateau = fixed obstruction; staccato = sphincter; interrupted = weak detrusor.
> - Cannot distinguish BOO from DUA.

## Q3b. Ejacultory duct obstruction
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Assumed meaning: ejaculatory duct obstruction (EDO).*

### Anatomy
- Intraprostatic continuations of the seminal vesicles, joined by the vasal ampullae; they lack muscle and open at the **verumontanum**. Seminal vesicles give about 60% of the ejaculate and its fructose.

### Aetiology
- **Congenital (commonest):** midline (Müllerian/utricular) cyst, Wolffian duct cyst, atresia. Idiopathic EDO carries CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) mutations in about half.
- **Acquired:** infection, instrumentation, duct stones, compression.
- **Zinner syndrome:** seminal vesicle cyst, ipsilateral renal agenesis, EDO.
- Complete bilateral EDO causes under 5% of azoospermia. Partial EDO gives oligoasthenospermia.

### Clinical features
- Infertility, painful ejaculation, haematospermia, perineal pain.

### Diagnosis
**Semen (complete bilateral EDO)**
- Volume **<1.0 mL**, **acidic pH**, low or absent **fructose**, azoospermia.
- Palpable vas, normal FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) and testes.
- Exclude retrograde ejaculation and CBAVD (congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens).

**Investigations**
| Test | Method / finding |
|---|---|
| **TRUS (transrectal ultrasound)** | Seminal vesicle AP diameter **>1.5 cm**, duct >2.3 mm, midline cyst. Low specificity |
| **Seminal vesicle aspiration** | **≥3 sperm per high-power field** confirms obstruction |
| Vesiculography | Contrast into vesicles; invasive |
| Chromotubation | Indigo carmine into vesicles; dye at the veru on cystoscopy |

### Management
**TURED (transurethral resection of the ejaculatory ducts)**
- For azoospermic or severely oligoasthenospermic men, after the couple declines IVF (in vitro fertilisation).
- **Steps:** TRUS-guided aspiration (cryopreserve sperm) → instil indigo carmine with contrast → 24-Fr resectoscope, finger in rectum → resect the veru until dye effluxes. Spare bladder neck, sphincter and rectum. Catheter overnight.
- **Results:** volume rises in most; sperm appear in about half. Cold-knife incision alone re-obstructs.

**Complications**
- **Urine reflux** into the ducts, **epididymitis**, retrograde ejaculation, rectal injury, re-obstruction.

**Alternatives**
- **Seminal vesiculoscopy** (4–6 Fr via utricle): avoids reflux; restenosis unknown. Sperm retrieval with ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection).

**Diagram to draw:** sagittal prostate with midline cyst compressing the ducts at the veru.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Low volume (<1 mL), acidic, fructose-negative azoospermia with palpable vasa.
> - TRUS: vesicle >1.5 cm; aspiration ≥3 sperm per high-power field confirms.
> - TURED: sperm return in about half; reflux and epididymitis are its price.

## Q3c. Ureterocele
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(ectopic ureter also answered in 2018-Summer Paper 3, Q1)

### Definition
- Cystic dilatation of the distal ureter, in the bladder or spanning bladder neck and urethra; **never wholly urethral**, never joined to Wolffian structures.
- Commoner in girls (about 4:1); about 80% drain the **upper pole of a duplex kidney** (Weigert–Meyer rule: upper pole orifice caudal-medial); about 10% bilateral.
- **Cause:** persistent **Chwalla membrane**; upper pole often dysplastic.

### Classification
| System | Basis | Types |
|---|---|---|
| **Glassberg (most useful)** | Position | **Intravesical** vs **ectopic (extravesical)**: part at bladder neck or urethra |
| Stephens | Orifice morphology | Stenotic, sphincteric, sphincterostenotic, **cecoureterocele** (cavity extends under urethra), blind, non-obstructed |
| Churchill | Function | Upper pole only / whole kidney / contralateral at risk |

### Clinical features
- Antenatal hydronephrosis (most); febrile UTI (urinary tract infection) or urosepsis; adult single-system type with stone.
- **Prolapse:** smooth interlabial mass in girls; BOO (bladder outlet obstruction).
- **No incontinence when untreated** (unlike female ectopic ureter).

### Evaluation
- **Ultrasound:** thin-walled cyst in bladder, dilated upper pole and ureter.
- **VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram):** lower pole reflux ("drooping lily"), eversion (weak floor), prolapse.
- **DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid):** upper pole function; cystoscopy before incision.

### Management
- **Goals:** preserve function; remove infection, obstruction, reflux; keep continence.
- **CAP (continuous antibiotic prophylaxis)** from birth if hydronephrotic.
- **Observation:** small, non-obstructing, low-grade reflux, non-functioning upper pole.

| Option | Indication / result |
|---|---|
| **TUI (transurethral incision)** | Sepsis, neonates, intravesical type. Low transverse full-thickness incision; relieves obstruction in 78–97%; new reflux 0–50%. Intravesical without reflux: 70–80% cured; ectopic: secondary surgery up to 70% |
| Upper pole heminephrectomy | Non-functioning upper pole, no reflux; secondary surgery 40–50% |
| Ureteroureterostomy / pyeloureterostomy | Functioning upper pole |
| Excision + common sheath reimplant ± bladder neck repair | Persistent reflux, ectopic or cecoureterocele; persisting reflux 5–10% |
| Total reconstruction | Older child, non-functioning upper pole, lower pole reflux |

- **Also know:** "watering-can" laser punctures (less reflux); pseudoureterocele; ureterocele disproportion.

**Diagram to draw:** duplex kidney with upper pole ureterocele and lower pole reflux.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Upper pole of duplex; never wholly urethral; Chwalla membrane.
> - Intravesical vs ectopic predicts TUI success.
> - TUI relieves obstruction in 78–97%; new reflux 0–50%.

## Q3d. Discuss treatment of b/l VUR. Describe surgical techniques and criteria for their selection
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

**See Practice Set 2 Paper 3, Q2 (full answer).**

> **Key points to remember (SAQ angle):**
> - Treat BBD (bladder and bowel dysfunction) and give CAP (continuous antibiotic prophylaxis) first; correct for breakthrough febrile UTI (urinary tract infection), new scars or persistent grade IV–V.
> - Endoscopic Dx/HA (dextranomer/hyaluronic acid) by HIT (hydrodistension implantation): least invasive; lower success at higher grades.
> - Bilateral VUR (vesicoureteral reflux): Cohen cross-trigonal reimplant (over 98%), as both sides are done through one cystotomy.
> - Bilateral extravesical (Lich–Gregoir) repair: retention up to 10%, so use nerve-sparing dissection.

## Q3e. Darifenacin vs. solifenacin vs. tolterodine
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in Practice Set 1 Paper 1, Q3e)

### Introduction
- Antimuscarinics treat OAB (overactive bladder) after behavioural therapy. Acetylcholine on **M3** receptors contracts the detrusor; M2 (commoner) adds to it. Blockade reduces involuntary contractions and afferent urgency signals.
- Side effects: **M1** (brain) cognition; **M2** heart rate; **M3** (glands, gut) dry mouth, constipation.
- Selectivity is by **receptor subtype** (M3) or **tissue** (bladder over salivary gland); tissue selectivity matters more.

### Comparison
| Feature | Darifenacin | Solifenacin | Tolterodine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Selectivity | **Most M3-selective (about 30-fold)**; not tissue-selective | M3 about 10-fold over M2; bladder-selective | Non-subtype-selective; **bladder-selective** |
| Dose | 7.5–15 mg daily (ER (extended-release)) | 5–10 mg daily | 2 mg twice daily (IR (immediate-release)); 4 mg daily (ER) |
| Half-life | Moderate (ER once daily) | **Long** (about 2–3 days): once daily | Short; active metabolite 5-HMT (5-hydroxymethyl tolterodine) |
| Metabolism | CYP3A4 (a liver enzyme) and CYP2D6 | CYP3A4 | CYP2D6 (and 3A4) |
| Dry mouth | Dose-related | Dose-related | Lower with ER |
| Constipation | **Highest** (gut M3) | Moderate–high | Lower |
| CNS (central nervous system) | Spares M1: **preferred in elderly** | Better persistence | High dose: QT prolongation |
| Dose limit | Lower dose with CYP3A4 inhibitors or liver disease | 5 mg in severe renal or moderate liver impairment | Halve with renal or liver impairment |

### Common points
- **Efficacy is similar**; response is individual, so switch agent or dose.
- **STAR trial:** flexible solifenacin beat tolterodine ER for urgency and incontinence.
- **Contraindications:** urinary or gastric retention, uncontrolled narrow-angle glaucoma.
- Men: add to α-blocker; retention up to 3%; check PVR (postvoid residual).
- **Dementia:** use >3 months raises risk 10–65% (SUFU (Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction)). Prefer **β3 agonist**, or ER trospium, darifenacin or fesoterodine, in the elderly. Beers criteria: avoid in dementia.
- **Combination:** solifenacin plus mirabegron beats monotherapy (SYNERGY).

**Diagram to draw:** muscarinic subtypes M1 (brain), M2 (heart), M3 (bladder, gland, gut) with each drug's selectivity.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Darifenacin most M3-selective; tolterodine bladder-selective; solifenacin longest half-life.
> - Similar efficacy; STAR: solifenacin beat tolterodine ER.
> - Elderly: darifenacin, trospium or β3 agonist; dementia risk 10–65% with >3 months' use.

## Q3f. Primary obst. megaureter.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Assumed meaning: primary obstructive megaureter (POM).*

### Definition
- **Megaureter:** ureter diameter **≥7 mm** (normal up to 16 years is 5.0–6.5 mm). It is a descriptive term.
- **Classification:** primary (intrinsic) or secondary (neurogenic bladder, outlet obstruction); and **obstructed, refluxing, non-refluxing non-obstructed, or refluxing and obstructed**.
- **POM:** an **adynamic juxtavesical segment** at the UVJ (ureterovesical junction) blocks peristalsis and causes functional obstruction above.

### Pathogenesis
- Ureteric muscle matures cranially to caudally; the juxtavesical segment matures last, even after birth. This explains spontaneous resolution.
- Excess collagen, thick muscle sleeve, abnormal interstitial cells of Cajal.

### Clinical features
- 10–23% of antenatal upper-tract dilatation. Boys, **left side**; **bilateral 25%**; contralateral dysplasia or obstruction 10–15%.
- Mostly asymptomatic; or UTI (urinary tract infection), pain, haematuria, stones.

### Evaluation
- **Ultrasound:** hydroureteronephrosis.
- **VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram):** excludes reflux and PUV (posterior urethral valves).
- **MAG3 (mercaptoacetyltriglycine) renogram:** split function; washout T½ unreliable (false positive up to 44%).
- MRU (magnetic resonance urography) for anatomy.

### Management
**Conservative (most)**
- CAP (continuous antibiotic prophylaxis) in infants; serial ultrasound and renograms; most resolve.
- Diameter <10 mm: virtually never needs surgery; ≥10 mm: a substantial minority do.
- Follow to early adulthood.

**Indications for surgery**
- Recurrent febrile UTI; progressive dilatation; **DRF (differential renal function) <40%**; **fall in DRF ≥5%**.

**Infant (small bladder, huge ureter): temporise**
- Cutaneous ureterostomy; refluxing reimplant; nephrostomy; **endoscopic balloon dilatation with double-J** for 2–6 months (70–80% success; complications up to a third).

**Definitive repair (child)**
- Excise stenotic segment → straighten → **taper** → **non-refluxing reimplant at 5:1** (Cohen or Politano–Leadbetter; extravesical for huge ureters). Stent afterwards.

| Tailoring | Method | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Starr plication | Imbricate lateral wall | Moderate (<1.75 cm) |
| Kalicinski folding | Exclude and fold lumen | Moderate |
| **Hendren excision** | Excise lateral wall | Massive; risk of ischaemia |

- Success over 90%.
- **Complications:** obstruction (stent first), VUR (vesicoureteral reflux; observe or inject), persistent dilatation.

**Diagram to draw:** dilated ureter above narrow adynamic segment; tapered reimplant.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Megaureter ≥7 mm; POM = adynamic juxtavesical segment.
> - Surgery: UTIs, progression, DRF <40% or fall ≥5%.
> - Excise, taper, reimplant 5:1; success >90%.
