## Q1. Discuss evaluation of & management of reno-vascular hypertension
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2022-Summer Paper 2, Q2)

### Definitions
- **RVH (renovascular hypertension):** hypertension caused by RAS (renal artery stenosis) that improves after revascularisation or nephrectomy. RAS is common in older adults; RVH is proved only by cure.
- **Ischaemic nephropathy:** loss of GFR (glomerular filtration rate) from RAS affecting the whole renal mass (bilateral RAS or stenosis to a solitary kidney).

### Causes
| | Atherosclerotic RAS (about 90%) | FMD (fibromuscular dysplasia) |
|---|---|---|
| Patient | Older, smoker, diabetic, vascular disease | Young women |
| Site | Ostium and proximal third | Mid and distal artery; "string of beads" (medial fibroplasia) |
| Course | Progresses; renal function lost | Rarely occludes |

**Also know:** Takayasu arteritis, neurofibromatosis, midaortic syndrome (children), aneurysm, dissection, embolism, Page kidney, transplant RAS.

### Pathophysiology (basis of the tests)
- Low perfusion pressure releases **juxtaglomerular renin**; angiotensin II causes vasoconstriction and aldosterone causes sodium retention.
- **Two-kidney, one-clip** (unilateral RAS): renin-dependent; the normal kidney excretes the sodium, so ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors lower BP (blood pressure) and the captopril test is positive.
- **One-kidney, one-clip** (bilateral or solitary): volume-dependent; ACE inhibitors may cause acute renal failure.
- The stenotic kidney keeps its GFR by angiotensin II **efferent constriction**. Later, hypertension damages the contralateral kidney, so cure after revascularisation falls with duration.

### Evaluation
**1. Clinical clues (who to test)**
- Onset below 30 years (FMD) or severe hypertension after 55 years; **resistant** hypertension (3 or more drugs) or sudden worsening.
- **Creatinine rise above 30% after ACE inhibitor or ARB (angiotensin receptor blocker)**; **flash pulmonary oedema**; kidneys differing by more than 1.5 cm; abdominal bruit; hypokalaemia.

**2. Basic tests:** creatinine and eGFR (estimated GFR), potassium, urinalysis and proteinuria, ECG; exclude primary aldosteronism and phaeochromocytoma.

**3. Imaging and functional tests**
| Test | Method and finding | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Duplex Doppler US** | PSV (peak systolic velocity) above about 180–200 cm/s; renal/aortic velocity ratio >3.5; **RI (resistive index) = (PSV − EDV)/PSV** | First line, no contrast; operator dependent. RI ≥0.8 predicts poor response |
| **CT angiography** | Stenosis, ostial plaque, beads | Contrast nephropathy |
| **MR angiography** | Gadolinium-enhanced | Caution if eGFR <30 mL/min |
| **Captopril renography (DTPA, diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid)** | Day 1 after oral captopril: slow uptake, low peak; repeat off ACE inhibitor and calcium channel blocker. Improvement of 10% or more = high probability RVH | DTPA is cleared purely by filtration; use declining |
| **Catheter angiography** | Gold standard; **translesional gradient** (systolic ≥20 or mean ≥10 mmHg = significant) | Done when intervention is planned |
| Renal vein renin | Ratio >1.5 lateralises | Before nephrectomy |

**Algorithm**
```
Clinical clue -> Duplex US (or CTA/MRA if eGFR allows)
  -> no RAS: treat as essential hypertension
  -> RAS: FMD -> angioplasty
          Atherosclerotic -> optimal medical therapy
             -> high-risk features + viable kidney -> angiography + gradient -> stent
```

### Management
**1. Medical therapy (first line for atherosclerotic RAS)**
- **ACE inhibitor or ARB**, with creatinine and potassium checked at 1–2 weeks; calcium channel blockers, diuretics.
- **Statin, antiplatelet, stop smoking, control diabetes**: most deaths are cardiovascular.

**2. Landmark trials**
- **ASTRAL (2009):** stenting plus medical therapy gave **no benefit** in renal function, BP, events or death, with procedural complications.
- **CORAL (2014):** stenting did **not reduce** cardiovascular or renal events; small BP fall only.
- Hence no routine revascularisation for atherosclerotic RAS (ESC [European Society of Cardiology] 2017).

**3. Indications for revascularisation**
- **FMD with hypertension.**
- Atherosclerotic RAS with **flash pulmonary oedema** or recurrent heart failure, **rapidly declining function** in bilateral or solitary-kidney RAS, or truly resistant hypertension.
- Kidney must be viable (not small and atrophic; RI <0.8).

**4. Endovascular**
- **PTRA (percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty) alone for FMD**; stent only for dissection or failure.
- **Ostial atherosclerotic lesion: angioplasty plus stent**, because balloon alone fails from elastic recoil of aortic plaque. Restenosis is the main late problem.
- Complications: contrast nephropathy, **atheroembolism**, dissection, rupture, thrombosis, access haematoma.

**5. Surgical revascularisation** (failed endovascular, branch disease, aneurysm, children, aortic surgery)
| Technique | Use |
|---|---|
| **Aortorenal bypass** (saphenous vein; internal iliac artery in children; PTFE [polytetrafluoroethylene]) | Standard |
| **Transaortic endarterectomy** | Bilateral ostial plaque |
| **Splenorenal (left) or hepatorenal (right) bypass** | Diseased aorta |
| **Ex vivo bench repair and autotransplantation** | Branch FMD, aneurysm |
| Nephrectomy (partial or total) | Small non-functioning kidney with lateralising renin |

**6. Transplant RAS:** raised velocity on Doppler, contrast imaging confirms; angioplasty or stent first, surgery if this fails.

### Follow-up
- BP, creatinine and duplex for restenosis; cure is commoner in FMD than atherosclerosis.

**Diagram to draw:** Goldblatt two-kidney, one-clip loop (low pressure, renin, angiotensin II, aldosterone, hypertension).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - RVH is proved only by cure; duplex first, angiography with gradient before intervention.
> - ASTRAL 2009 and CORAL 2014: no routine stenting of atherosclerotic RAS.
> - FMD: balloon angioplasty without stent; ostial atheroma: stent.
> - Revascularise for flash pulmonary oedema or declining function in a viable kidney (RI <0.8).

## Q2. Management of upper ureteric stones
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(SWL also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 2, Q2 and 2021-Summer Paper 1, Q1; flexible URS in 2019-Summer Paper 2, Q3b)

### Introduction
- **Upper (proximal) ureter:** UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) to the upper border of the sacroiliac joint.
- Proximal stones pass least often, so many need intervention. Dangers: renal damage and sepsis.

### Evaluation
- **NCCT (non-contrast CT)**: gold standard; gives size, density (HU, Hounsfield units), SSD (skin-to-stone distance) and impaction. KUB (kidney–ureter–bladder radiograph) for SWL follow-up; creatinine, urine culture, coagulation.

### 1. Emergency: drain first
- **AUA (American Urological Association) indications for urgent intervention:** infection with obstruction, bilateral obstruction, solitary kidney, immunosuppression, renal deterioration, refractory pain or vomiting.
- **Obstructed infected kidney: drain only** (double-J stent or PCN, percutaneous nephrostomy; equal for sepsis); definitive treatment later. PCN suits large stones (stent fails if transverse diameter ≥9.5 mm).

### 2. Observation and MET (medical expulsive therapy): limits
| Spontaneous passage | Rate |
|---|---|
| By size | 1–4 mm 78%; 5–7 mm 60%; 8–10 mm 39% |
| By site | UVJ (ureterovesical junction) 92%; distal 75%; mid 60%; **proximal 48%** |

- **Observation** suits small (<5–6 mm) uncomplicated stones: NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) first.
- **MET (alpha-blockers, tamsulosin or silodosin):** reduce spasm so a larger urine bolus pushes the stone. Benefit is clear only for **distal stones 5–10 mm**; meta-analysis shows **no benefit for proximal stones**. SUSPEND (2015) showed no overall benefit.
- Trial of passage is limited to **4–6 weeks**, with imaging.

### 3. Choice of active treatment
- Factors: stone (size, density, impaction), patient (habitus, anticoagulation, preference), surgeon and equipment.

**EAU (European Association of Urology) recommendation for proximal ureteric stones**
| Size | First choice | Alternative |
|---|---|---|
| <10 mm | SWL or URS (ureteroscopy) | – |
| >10 mm | URS (retrograde or antegrade) | SWL |
| Large (>15–20 mm), impacted | PCNL with antegrade URS | Laparoscopic ureterolithotomy |

**SWL versus URS**
| | SWL (shock wave lithotripsy) | URS |
|---|---|---|
| Stone-free rate (AUA/EAU meta-analysis, approximate) | <10 mm about 90%; >10 mm about 70% | <10 mm about 80%; >10 mm about 80% |
| Sessions | More retreatment | Single session; higher SFR at 2 weeks, equal at 3 months |
| Anaesthesia | Sedation or none | Usually general |
| Complications | Fewer | Perforation, stent pain |
| Anticoagulation | Must stop | Laser URS can continue |

- **SWL fails** with size >10–15 mm, impaction (CT "ring" sign), HU >1000, SSD >10 cm, cystine or COM (calcium oxalate monohydrate) stones.
- **SWL technique:** supine; 60–90 shocks/min; ramp energy; no routine pre-stent. Contraindicated in pregnancy, bleeding disorder, untreated UTI (urinary tract infection), distal obstruction, uncontrolled hypertension.

### 4. Ureteroscopy for proximal stones
- **Flexible URS** preferred: it follows a stone that migrates into the kidney.
- Safety wire; access sheath (12/14 Fr); tight ureter: pre-stent and return after 2–4 weeks.
- **Laser:** holmium:YAG (2120 nm) or thulium fibre laser; dusting or fragmenting; keep **≤10 W in the ureter** to avoid thermal injury.
- **Retropulsion** into the kidney: reduced by low-energy settings and antiretropulsion devices (Stone Cone, NTrap); pneumatic lithotripsy works poorly above the brim.
- **Push-back:** stone deliberately pushed into the renal pelvis with a catheter, then treated by SWL, RIRS (retrograde intrarenal surgery) or PCNL; now largely replaced by in-situ flexible URS.
- Stent only for perforation, impaction, infection or a planned second stage.

### 5. Antegrade approach (PCNL with antegrade URS)
- Indications: large (>15–20 mm), hard or **impacted proximal stones**, failed retrograde access, urinary diversion, transplant, concomitant renal stones.
- **Upper or mid-pole posterior calyx** gives a straight line to the UPJ; flexible nephroscope or antegrade URS down the dilated ureter; high SFR.

### 6. Laparoscopic (or robotic) ureterolithotomy
- Indications: large impacted stones, failed SWL/URS, where endourology is unavailable, or with concomitant UPJ obstruction (pyeloplasty).
- Steps: retroperitoneal or transperitoneal access; identify the bulge; **clamp the ureter above the stone** to prevent migration; longitudinal ureterotomy; remove; double-J stent; close with 4-0 absorbable sutures; drain.
- High single-stage SFR; risk of urine leak. **Open ureterolithotomy** is now rare.

### Complications of treatment
| Complication | Points |
|---|---|
| Perforation | 0–4%; stop, stent about 4 weeks |
| **Avulsion** | 0.06–0.5%; **proximal third most at risk**; nephrostomy and delayed reconstruction |
| Stricture | 3–6%; image all after URS |
| Sepsis | About 5% after URS |
| SWL | Steinstrasse, haematoma, colic |

**Also know:** pregnancy: conservative, then stent or URS (no SWL).

### Recent advances
- Single-use digital scopes; pressure sensors; thulium fibre laser; robotic URS; **BWL (burst wave lithotripsy)**.

**Diagram to draw:** algorithm for proximal stone: drain if infected; <10 mm SWL or URS; >10 mm URS; large impacted antegrade or laparoscopic.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Proximal stones pass in only 48%; MET does not help proximal stones.
> - <10 mm: SWL equals URS; >10 mm: URS first (EAU).
> - Flexible URS chases migrated fragments; laser ≤10 W in the ureter.
> - Large impacted: antegrade PCNL or laparoscopic ureterolithotomy; avulsion risk highest in proximal third.

## Q3a. Bladder dysfunction non genitourinary systemic disease
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Taken as lower urinary tract dysfunction caused by systemic (non-genitourinary) diseases.*

### Principle
- The **site of the lesion**, not the disease name, predicts the bladder:
  - Brain above the pons: **DO (detrusor overactivity), no DSD (detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia)**.
  - Spinal cord: DO **with DSD**.
  - Peripheral nerves or roots: **areflexia, reduced sensation, high PVR (post-void residual)**.
- **Urodynamics** before treatment, because symptoms do not predict findings.

### Diabetes mellitus (most important)
- **Diabetic cystopathy:** sensory then motor autonomic neuropathy. Impaired sensation leads to large capacity, poor contractility, low flow and later high PVR and UTI (urinary tract infection).
- **DO is in fact the commonest urodynamic finding** (early disease or coexisting cerebrovascular disease); hypocontractility comes late.
- Mechanisms: glycation end products, sorbitol pathway, oxidative stress, polyuria.
- Differentiate from BOO (bladder outlet obstruction) by pressure–flow study. Treatment: **timed voiding**, CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation), cautious antimuscarinics.

### Other systemic diseases
| Group | Disease | Usual finding |
|---|---|---|
| Neurological | Stroke | Acute retention, then DO; no true DSD |
| | Parkinson disease | DO; pseudodyssynergia; TURP (transurethral resection of prostate) possible after excluding MSA (multiple system atrophy) |
| | Multiple sclerosis | DO about 38%, DO with DSD 29%, areflexia 26%; changes over time |
| Infective | HIV/AIDS | DO, DSD; areflexia with CMV (cytomegalovirus) |
| | Herpes zoster/simplex, Guillain–Barré | Retention, areflexia; reversible |
| | Spinal tuberculosis, Lyme, HTLV-1 | DO with DSD or areflexia |
| Autoimmune and connective tissue | SLE (systemic lupus erythematosus) | DO; lupus cystitis |
| | Systemic sclerosis | Areflexia, low compliance |
| Infiltrative | Amyloidosis (transthyretin) | Low sensation, underactivity, SUI (stress urinary incontinence) |
| Nutritional | B12 deficiency, alcohol (thiamine) | Mixed; reversible with replacement |
| Endocrine | Hyperthyroidism | Poor emptying; resolves with treatment |

**Also know:** myasthenia gravis; schizophrenia; gastroparesis.

### Management principles
- Treat the disease; reversible therapy.
- Storage: antimuscarinics (trospium, or mirabegron in elderly brains), botulinum toxin, neuromodulation.
- Emptying: CIC; avoid Credé with DSD.

**Diagram to draw:** neuraxis with lesion levels and the bladder pattern at each.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Lesion level predicts pattern: brain DO; cord DO + DSD; peripheral areflexia.
> - Diabetic cystopathy: low sensation, large capacity, high PVR; DO common early.
> - Urodynamics before treating; reversible treatment first.

## Q3b. Mycophenolate Mofetil.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(immunosuppressants also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 1, Q3f and 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q1)

### Introduction
- **MMF (mycophenolate mofetil):** an antimetabolite immunosuppressant; morpholinoethyl ester **prodrug** of MPA (mycophenolic acid). With tacrolimus it forms maintenance therapy in **over 90%** of kidney recipients.

### Mechanism
- MPA is a reversible, non-competitive inhibitor of **IMPDH (inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase)**, the key enzyme of **de novo guanosine nucleotide synthesis**.
- **Why lymphocyte-selective:** T and B lymphocytes depend on the de novo pathway; other cells use the salvage pathway. MPA also inhibits the type II isoform found in activated lymphocytes.
- Result: arrest of T- and B-cell proliferation and reduced antibody formation.

### Pharmacology
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Dose (kidney) | **1 g twice daily** oral or IV; EC-MPS (enteric-coated mycophenolate sodium) 720 mg twice daily is equivalent |
| Metabolism | Hydrolysed to MPA; liver glucuronidation to inactive MPAG (MPA glucuronide); **enterohepatic recirculation** |
| Excretion | MPAG in urine; accumulates in renal failure |
| Interactions | **Ciclosporin lowers MPA** (blocks enterohepatic recirculation), tacrolimus does not; antacids, cholestyramine reduce absorption; never with azathioprine |

### Uses
- Kidney transplant maintenance (tacrolimus + MMF ± steroids); other solid organs.
- Lupus nephritis, vasculitis; steroid-refractory immune-related adverse events of checkpoint inhibitors.

### Adverse effects
- **GI (gastrointestinal):** diarrhoea, nausea, colitis (commonest; dose split or EC-MPS).
- **Marrow:** leucopenia, anaemia, thrombocytopenia: blood count weekly in month 1.
- **Infection:** CMV, BK virus (ureteric stricture), rarely PML (progressive multifocal leucoencephalopathy).
- **Malignancy:** skin cancer, PTLD (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder).
- **Teratogenic:** miscarriage, ear and facial malformations; contraception mandatory; switch to azathioprine before conception.
- Advantage: **not nephrotoxic**; no hypertension, diabetes or dyslipidaemia.

### Evidence
- Three mid-1990s randomised trials (US, European, Tricontinental) showed fewer early acute rejections than azathioprine or placebo, so MMF replaced azathioprine.
- **ELITE-Symphony (2007):** low-dose tacrolimus + MMF + daclizumab + steroids gave the lowest rejection and best GFR.

**Diagram to draw:** purine synthesis pathway showing IMPDH block (de novo) and the salvage pathway spared.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Prodrug of MPA; IMPDH inhibitor; lymphocytes need de novo purine synthesis.
> - 1 g twice daily; ciclosporin lowers MPA levels.
> - Toxicity: diarrhoea, leucopenia, CMV/BK, teratogenic; not nephrotoxic.

## Q3c. Cushing Syndrome
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definitions
- **Cushing syndrome:** symptomatic hypercortisolism. **Cushing disease:** due to a pituitary ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone)-secreting adenoma.
- **MACS (mild autonomous cortisol secretion):** autonomous cortisol without clinical signs (replaces "subclinical Cushing"); raises cardiovascular, bone and diabetes risk.

### Causes
| Group | Cause | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Exogenous | Glucocorticoids (oral, inhaled, topical) | Commonest overall |
| ACTH-dependent (80–85% of endogenous) | Pituitary adenoma (Cushing disease) | About 70% |
| | Ectopic ACTH (bronchial carcinoid, small cell lung cancer, phaeochromocytoma) | 10–15% |
| ACTH-independent | Adrenal adenoma, ACC (adrenocortical carcinoma), AIMAH (macronodular hyperplasia), PPNAD (pigmented nodular disease; Carney complex) | About 10% |

### Clinical features
- Central obesity, moon face, plethora (90–100%); purple striae, hypertension, hirsutism, ED (70–90%).
- **Discriminating:** proximal weakness, bruising.
- **Urological:** nephrolithiasis in up to 50%; hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (low libido, ED).

### Diagnosis
**Step 1: exclude exogenous steroids. Step 2: confirm cortisol excess (two tests)**
| Test | Method | Abnormal |
|---|---|---|
| **Overnight 1-mg DST (dexamethasone suppression test)** | 1 mg at 11 pm; 8 am cortisol | >1.8 µg/dL (50 nmol/L) |
| Late-night salivary cortisol | 11 pm, twice | Raised |
| 24-h UFC (urinary free cortisol) | Two collections | Raised; used in pregnancy |

**Step 3: locate the source**
- **Low ACTH:** adrenal; adrenal CT or MRI.
- **High ACTH:** pituitary MRI; **IPSS (inferior petrosal sinus sampling) with CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone)**: central gradient = pituitary; none = ectopic (chest CT).
- Pitfalls: pseudo-Cushing (depression, alcohol), oestrogens, enzyme inducers.

### Management
| Cause | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Cushing disease | Trans-sphenoidal surgery (cure 60–80%); radiotherapy |
| Adrenal adenoma | **Laparoscopic adrenalectomy** |
| ACC | Open radical adrenalectomy, mitotane |
| Ectopic | Resect source; otherwise bilateral adrenalectomy |
| AIMAH, PPNAD | Bilateral adrenalectomy |

- **Bilateral adrenalectomy:** for failed pituitary surgery or life-threatening hypercortisolism. Cost: lifelong replacement, **Nelson syndrome** (8–29%).
- **Medical (bridge):** metyrapone, ketoconazole, etomidate, mitotane, mifepristone.

### Perioperative care
- **Contralateral adrenal is suppressed:** adrenal insufficiency in about 99.7% after surgery for overt Cushing.
- **Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV** on the day of surgery, then slow oral taper; recovery takes a median 11 months.

**Diagram to draw:** hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis with ACTH-dependent and ACTH-independent sites.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Exclude exogenous steroids; 1-mg DST cortisol >1.8 µg/dL abnormal.
> - ACTH low = adrenal; high = pituitary/ectopic (IPSS).
> - Steroid cover after adrenalectomy; Nelson syndrome after bilateral.

## Q3d. Maximum androgen blockade
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered as Combined Androgen Blockade in 2018-Summer Paper 4, Q3f)

### Definition and rationale
- **MAB (maximum androgen blockade)**, also CAB (combined) or total androgen blockade: castration (orchidectomy or LHRH [luteinising hormone-releasing hormone] agonist) **plus a non-steroidal antiandrogen**.
- **Rationale (Labrie):** castration removes about 90–95% of testosterone, but **adrenal androgens** (DHEA, dehydroepiandrosterone; androstenedione) are converted to DHT (dihydrotestosterone) in the prostate. The antiandrogen blocks these at the AR (androgen receptor).

### Antiandrogens
| Drug | Dose | Signature toxicity |
|---|---|---|
| Flutamide | 250 mg three times daily | Diarrhoea, hepatotoxicity |
| Nilutamide | 300 mg, then 150 mg daily | Delayed dark adaptation, pneumonitis |
| **Bicalutamide** | **50 mg daily** (with LHRH agent) | Best tolerated; gynaecomastia |
| Cyproterone (steroidal) | 100 mg two to three times daily | Cardiovascular, hepatic; no CAB benefit |

- Class effects: gynaecomastia, hepatotoxicity, pneumonitis.

### Evidence
- **Crawford (1989):** leuprolide + flutamide improved survival (partly flare control); **Eisenberger (1998):** orchidectomy + flutamide, **no** benefit.
- **PCTCG (Prostate Cancer Trialists' Collaborative Group) meta-analysis, 2000** (27 trials, about 8,000 men): with non-steroidal antiandrogens, 5-year survival improved by only **about 2–3%**; cyproterone slightly worse.
- Small gain, more cost and toxicity: **AUA (American Urological Association) advises against long-term CAB**.

### Current uses
1. **Flare prevention:** an LHRH agonist raises LH (luteinising hormone) up to 10-fold for 10–20 days, risking cord compression, pain or obstruction with bone metastases. Start antiandrogen **with or before** the agonist for **21–28 days**. Antagonists (degarelix, relugolix) or orchidectomy cause no flare.
2. **Antiandrogen withdrawal:** on progression, stopping bicalutamide gives a PSA (prostate-specific antigen) fall >50% in 15–30% (mutant AR turns the drug into an agonist).

### Modern replacement in mHSPC (metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer)
- ADT (androgen deprivation therapy) plus abiraterone, enzalutamide, apalutamide, docetaxel, or triplets (PEACE-1, ARASENS).
- **ENZAMET:** enzalutamide beat a CAB control arm for overall survival.

**Diagram to draw:** testicular and adrenal androgen sources with LHRH agonist and antiandrogen sites of action.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - MAB = castration + non-steroidal antiandrogen (blocks adrenal androgens).
> - PCTCG 2000: only 2–3% 5-year survival gain.
> - Main use now: flare cover for 21–28 days.
> - Newer AR pathway inhibitors beat CAB (ENZAMET).

## Q3e. Complications of PCNL
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(mini-PCNL complications in 2022-Summer Paper 1, Q1)

### Introduction
- PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) is the standard for renal stones >2 cm. Most complications arise from **access and tract dilatation**.
- Graded by the **Clavien–Dindo** system (I: no intervention; II: drugs or transfusion; III: intervention; IV: ICU [intensive care unit]; V: death).

### Incidence (systematic review, Seitz 2012, quoted by EAU [European Association of Urology])
| Complication | Rate |
|---|---|
| Fever | 10.8% |
| Transfusion | 7% (nearer 1% with modern and mini tracts) |
| Thoracic | 1.5% |
| Sepsis | 0.5% |
| Organ injury | 0.4% |
| Angioembolisation | 0.4% |
| Death | 0.05% |

### Main complications
| Complication | Cause | Prevention and management |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute bleeding** | Infundibular or segmental vessel injury | Fornix puncture; place sheath, tamponade with balloon or **Kaye catheter**, clamp nephrostomy; transfuse |
| **Delayed bleeding** | Pseudoaneurysm, AV (arteriovenous) fistula | **Selective angioembolisation** |
| **Infection, SIRS (systemic inflammatory response syndrome), sepsis** | Infected stone, high intrarenal pressure | Sterile urine, culture-specific antibiotics, low-pressure irrigation; stop and drain if pus |
| **Pleural** (pneumothorax, hydrothorax) | Supracostal access, highest above 11th rib | Subcostal where possible, puncture in expiration; chest drain |
| **Colon** | Retrorenal colon, left lower pole | CT review; extraperitoneal: double-J stent and withdraw nephrostomy into colon (colostomy tube) |
| **Spleen, liver** | Upper-pole access, organomegaly | Ultrasound guidance; conservative or surgery |
| **Collecting system** | Perforation, extravasation | Stop, drain; later infundibular stenosis |
| **Fluid absorption** | Long operation, perforation | **Normal saline**; hyponatraemia, overload |

**Also know:** venous air embolism, DVT (deep vein thrombosis) and pulmonary embolism, neuropraxia and eye injury (prone), residual fragments, loss of renal function (multiple tracts), urinoma, nephrocutaneous fistula, retained hardware.

### Risk factors
- Staghorn, multiple tracts, supracostal access, large tract, long operation, CKD (chronic kidney disease), diabetes, positive urine culture, inexperience.

### Reducing risk
- **Tranexamic acid** reduces transfusion (Cochrane).
- Ultrasound-guided access, mini-PCNL, ECIRS (endoscopic combined intrarenal surgery).

**Diagram to draw:** kidney cross-section with posterior calyx, Brödel line, pleura and retrorenal colon.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Fever 10.8%, transfusion 7%, thoracic 1.5%, death 0.05%.
> - Fornix puncture avoids arterial injury; delayed bleeding = pseudoaneurysm, embolise.
> - Supracostal access increases pleural injury; colon injury at left lower pole.

## Q3f. VHL gene.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- **VHL (von Hippel–Lindau)** is a **tumour suppressor gene on chromosome 3p25**, identified in 1993; three exons, 213-amino-acid protein.
- Germline mutation causes **VHL disease** (autosomal dominant, about 1 in 36,000). Somatic VHL loss occurs in most sporadic clear cell RCC (renal cell carcinoma); chromosome 3p is altered in over 90%.

### Function: the oxygen-sensing pathway
- VHL protein binds elongin B, elongin C, cullin-2 and RBX1 to form an **E3 ubiquitin ligase**.
- **Normoxia:** hydroxylated **HIF (hypoxia-inducible factor) -1α and -2α** are tagged for proteasomal destruction.
- **VHL loss (or hypoxia):** HIF, chiefly HIF-2α, accumulates, joins ARNT (aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator) and switches on **VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor)**, PDGF (platelet-derived growth factor), TGF-α (transforming growth factor-α), GLUT1, erythropoietin and CA-IX (carbonic anhydrase IX).
- Hence the **hypervascularity** of clear cell RCC.
- **Knudson two-hit:** one germline plus one somatic hit, so VHL tumours are early, bilateral and multifocal.

### VHL disease manifestations
- **Clear cell RCC** 25–75% (mean age 39; commonest cause of death); renal and pancreatic cysts.
- **Craniospinal haemangioblastoma** 60–80%; retinal angioma 25–60%.
- **Phaeochromocytoma** 10–25%; pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours; endolymphatic sac tumour; epididymal cystadenoma.

### Genotype–phenotype
| Type | Mutation | Phaeochromocytoma | RCC |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Deletions, truncations | Low | High |
| 2A | Missense | High | Low |
| 2B | Missense | High | High |
| 2C | Missense | Phaeochromocytoma only | Absent |

### Clinical implications
- **Surveillance (VHL Alliance):** eye check yearly from infancy; metanephrines from 5 years; CNS MRI from 11; abdominal MRI every 2 years from 15.
- **3 cm rule:** observe solid renal tumours <3 cm; then nephron-sparing surgery (enucleation) or ablation, after excluding phaeochromocytoma.
- **Targeted therapy:** VEGF TKIs (tyrosine kinase inhibitors), bevacizumab, mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin) inhibitors.
- **Belzutifan** (HIF-2α inhibitor): FDA (Food and Drug Administration) approved 2021 for VHL disease; renal response 30–50%; anaemia.
- **Also know:** PBRM1, BAP1, SETD2 on 3p.

#### Q1. Discuss evaluation of & management of reno-vascular hypertension (LAQ)

- RVH is proved only by cure; duplex first, angiography with gradient before intervention.
- ASTRAL 2009 and CORAL 2014: no routine stenting of atherosclerotic RAS.
- FMD: balloon angioplasty without stent; ostial atheroma: stent.
- Revascularise for flash pulmonary oedema or declining function in a viable kidney (RI <0.8).

#### Q2. Management of upper ureteric stones (LAQ)

- Proximal stones pass in only 48%; MET does not help proximal stones.
- <10 mm: SWL equals URS; >10 mm: URS first (EAU).
- Flexible URS chases migrated fragments; laser ≤10 W in the ureter.
- Large impacted: antegrade PCNL or laparoscopic ureterolithotomy; avulsion risk highest in proximal third.

#### Q3a. Bladder dysfunction non genitourinary systemic disease (SAQ)

- Lesion level predicts pattern: brain DO; cord DO + DSD; peripheral areflexia.
- Diabetic cystopathy: low sensation, large capacity, high PVR; DO common early.
- Urodynamics before treating; reversible treatment first.

#### Q3b. Mycophenolate Mofetil. (SAQ)

- Prodrug of MPA; IMPDH inhibitor; lymphocytes need de novo purine synthesis.
- 1 g twice daily; ciclosporin lowers MPA levels.
- Toxicity: diarrhoea, leucopenia, CMV/BK, teratogenic; not nephrotoxic.

#### Q3c. Cushing Syndrome (SAQ)

- Exclude exogenous steroids; 1-mg DST cortisol >1.8 µg/dL abnormal.
- ACTH low = adrenal; high = pituitary/ectopic (IPSS).
- Steroid cover after adrenalectomy; Nelson syndrome after bilateral.

#### Q3d. Maximum androgen blockade (SAQ)

- MAB = castration + non-steroidal antiandrogen (blocks adrenal androgens).
- PCTCG 2000: only 2–3% 5-year survival gain.
- Main use now: flare cover for 21–28 days.
- Newer AR pathway inhibitors beat CAB (ENZAMET).

#### Q3e. Complications of PCNL (SAQ)

- Fever 10.8%, transfusion 7%, thoracic 1.5%, death 0.05%.
- Fornix puncture avoids arterial injury; delayed bleeding = pseudoaneurysm, embolise.
- Supracostal access increases pleural injury; colon injury at left lower pole.

- VHL loss: HIF-2α drives VEGF, giving vascular clear cell RCC.
- Type 2 = phaeochromocytoma; 3 cm rule for nephron sparing.
