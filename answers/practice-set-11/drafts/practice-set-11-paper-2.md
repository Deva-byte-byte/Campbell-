## Q1. Management of metastatic RCC
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 2, Q1)

### Introduction
- About one-third of RCC (renal cell carcinoma) patients have metastases at diagnosis, and 20–40% of localised tumours metastasise later.
- Treatment moved from cytokines to VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor)-targeted TKIs (tyrosine kinase inhibitors), and now to ICI (immune checkpoint inhibitor) combinations. Surgery has become selective.

### Assessment before treatment
- **Clinical:** symptoms of metastases (cough, bone pain, neurological deficit), paraneoplastic syndromes (hypercalcaemia, polycythaemia, Stauffer syndrome), performance status (Karnofsky or ECOG [Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group]).
- **Laboratory:** haemoglobin, corrected calcium, neutrophils, platelets, LDH (lactate dehydrogenase), creatinine, liver tests.
- **Imaging:** contrast CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis. Bone scan or brain MRI only when symptoms or tests suggest spread.
- **Histology:** nephrectomy or **percutaneous biopsy** before systemic therapy. It separates clear cell from non-clear cell RCC and shows sarcomatoid change.
- **Prognostic risk group** (decides first-line therapy):

| Adverse factor | MSKCC (Memorial Sloan-Kettering), cytokine era | IMDC (International mRCC Database Consortium), TKI era |
|---|---|---|
| Karnofsky <80% | Yes | Yes |
| Haemoglobin < lower limit of normal | Yes | Yes |
| Corrected calcium raised | Yes | Yes |
| LDH >1.5 × upper limit of normal | Yes | No |
| Time factor | No prior nephrectomy | <1 year from diagnosis to systemic therapy |
| Neutrophils, platelets raised | No | Yes (two factors) |

- Favourable 0 factors, intermediate 1–2, poor ≥3. IMDC median survival: 43.2, 22.5 and 7.8 months.

### 1. First-line systemic therapy, clear cell RCC

| IMDC risk | Preferred | Alternatives |
|---|---|---|
| Favourable | Pembrolizumab + axitinib; nivolumab + cabozantinib; pembrolizumab + lenvatinib | Sunitinib or pazopanib; active surveillance for slow, low-volume disease |
| Intermediate/poor | The same three, **or nivolumab + ipilimumab** | Cabozantinib; sunitinib; pazopanib |

- **Rationale:** VHL (von Hippel–Lindau) loss lets HIF (hypoxia-inducible factor) accumulate and drive VEGF, the TKI target. ICIs block PD-1 (programmed death-1) or CTLA-4 (cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen-4), releasing the brake on T cells and giving durable responses.
- **Trials (all versus sunitinib):** CheckMate 214 (nivolumab + ipilimumab; overall survival benefit in intermediate/poor risk); KEYNOTE-426 (pembrolizumab + axitinib); CheckMate 9ER (nivolumab + cabozantinib); CLEAR (pembrolizumab + lenvatinib). Benefit is independent of PD-L1 (programmed death ligand-1) status.

### 2. Later lines: switch the mechanism
- After an IO (immuno-oncology) combination: a VEGF TKI not used before (cabozantinib, axitinib, tivozanib).
- After both IO and TKI: **belzutifan** (HIF-2α inhibitor); LITESPARK-005 showed better PFS (progression-free survival) than everolimus.
- After TKI alone: nivolumab (CheckMate 025) or cabozantinib (METEOR), both with survival gain.

### 3. Cytoreductive nephrectomy (CN): from routine to selective
- Cytokine era: SWOG 8949 and EORTC (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer) trials showed longer survival with CN + interferon-α.
- **CARMENA (2018):** sunitinib alone was non-inferior to CN + sunitinib in MSKCC intermediate/poor risk (median survival 18.4 vs 13.9 months).
- **SURTIME:** deferred CN after sunitinib was safe; survival favoured deferral.
- **Now:** immediate CN for fit patients with low metastatic burden who do not need immediate systemic therapy; **deferred CN** in responders. Avoid in poor risk or poor performance status.
- Palliative nephrectomy or angioembolisation for haematuria or pain.

### 4. Metastasectomy and local therapy
- Complete resection of solitary or few metastases gives 5-year survival of 35–50%. Better with complete resection, solitary lung lesion, disease-free interval >1 year and metachronous disease.
- **SBRT (stereotactic body radiotherapy)**, radiosurgery for brain metastases, or ablation for oligometastatic disease.
- Palliative radiotherapy or surgery for cord compression and weight-bearing bones.

### 5. Adjuvant therapy after complete resection
- **KEYNOTE-564:** 1 year of pembrolizumab after surgery in high-risk clear cell RCC, including **M1 NED** (no evidence of disease after metastasectomy). Disease-free survival improved; later report showed overall survival benefit.

### 6. Non-clear cell RCC and supportive care
- Non-clear cell: clinical trial preferred; cabozantinib or sunitinib; gemcitabine + platinum for collecting duct carcinoma.
- Bone metastases: zoledronic acid or denosumab.
- Immune-related adverse events (colitis, hepatitis, hypophysitis, thyroiditis) need steroids and drug interruption.
- **Also know:** high-dose IL-2 (interleukin-2) gives durable complete response in a few percent; temsirolimus for poor risk; chemotherapy and hormones are ineffective.

### Follow-up
- CT every 8–12 weeks on therapy (response by RECIST [Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours]); monitor thyroid, liver, blood pressure and cortisol.

### Recent advances
- Belzutifan, also approved for VHL-associated tumours; biomarkers to choose IO/IO or IO/TKI; neoadjuvant ICI trials.

```
Metastatic RCC -> biopsy + IMDC risk
   -> IO/TKI (all risks) or nivo + ipi (intermediate/poor)
   -> deferred CN / metastasectomy / SBRT in responders
   -> progression: switch class (TKI, belzutifan)
```

**Diagram to draw:** the flowchart above.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - IMDC: Karnofsky, haemoglobin, calcium, neutrophils, platelets, <1 year to therapy; poor = ≥3.
> - IO/TKI for all risk groups; nivolumab + ipilimumab for intermediate/poor only.
> - CARMENA and SURTIME: no routine upfront CN.
> - Adjuvant pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-564), including M1 NED.

## Q2. Urinary crystals
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- Crystals are precipitated urinary solutes seen on microscopy of the sediment. They occur in both normal and diseased states.
- Their value lies in suggesting stone type, metabolic disease, infection or drug toxicity. **Cystine is the one crystal that is never normal.**

### How crystals form (physicochemistry)
- Urine is normally **supersaturated** with calcium oxalate and phosphate. Crystals form only when the concentration product passes a critical level.

| Saturation zone | What happens |
|---|---|
| Undersaturated (below Ksp, solubility product) | No crystals; stones may dissolve |
| Metastable (Ksp to Kf, formation product) | No spontaneous nucleation; growth on existing crystals; inhibitors effective |
| Unstable (above Kf) | Spontaneous nucleation, growth and aggregation |

- **Nucleation** in vivo is mostly heterogeneous (on cell debris or other crystals). Crystals then grow, aggregate and are retained, for example on **Randall plaque**.
- **Inhibitors:** citrate, magnesium, pyrophosphate, Tamm–Horsfall protein, nephrocalcin, osteopontin. No inhibitor exists for uric acid or cystine, so these depend on pH and concentration.
- **Determinants of crystalluria:** urine volume, solute excretion, pH, temperature and inhibitors.

### Examination of urine for crystals
- Fresh midstream specimen, examined within 2 hours at body or room temperature. **Refrigeration precipitates crystals** that may not redissolve.
- Dipstick pH (physiological range 4.5–8.0); centrifuged sediment; polarised light helps identify crystals.
- Phosphaturia (cloudy alkaline urine) **clears with acetic acid**; pyuria does not.
- Candida mimics calcium oxalate and red cells; budding and hyphae separate it.

### Classification by urine pH and shape

| Urine pH | Crystal | Shape | Clinical significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acidic | **Uric acid** | Rhomboid or lemon-shaped plates, rosettes; yellow-brown | Uric acid stones (pH <5.5; pKa 5.35), gout, tumour lysis |
| Acidic | Amorphous urates | Pink "brick-dust" granules | Usually normal (concentrated, cooled urine) |
| Acidic | **Cystine** | **Hexagonal** colourless plates | Always abnormal: cystinuria |
| Acidic or alkaline | **Calcium oxalate dihydrate** | **Envelope** (octahedral) | Common, often normal; hypercalciuria |
| Acidic or alkaline | Calcium oxalate monohydrate | Dumbbell or oval | Hyperoxaluria; ethylene glycol poisoning |
| Alkaline | **Triple phosphate (struvite)** | **Coffin-lid** prisms | Urease-producing infection (Proteus); staghorn stones |
| Alkaline | Amorphous phosphate | White granules | Benign phosphaturia |
| Alkaline | Calcium phosphate (brushite, apatite) | Needles or rosettes; amorphous | RTA (renal tubular acidosis), hyperparathyroidism |
| Alkaline | Ammonium biurate | "Thorny apple" spheres | Old specimens; ammonium urate stones (diarrhoea, laxative abuse, ileostomy) |
| Any | Cholesterol | Notched flat plates | Nephrotic syndrome, lipiduria |

- **Rare metabolic crystals:** 2,8-dihydroxyadenine, brown spiculated spheres (APRT [adenine phosphoribosyltransferase] deficiency, radiolucent stones); xanthine (hereditary xanthinuria, high-dose allopurinol); tyrosine needles and leucine spheres (severe liver disease).

### Drug crystals
- **Two routes:** the drug itself precipitates (direct), or it changes urine chemistry (indirect).

| Drug | Crystal and features | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| **Indinavir** (protease inhibitor) | Needles or fan-shaped plates; crystalluria in about two-thirds of new users; stones 4–13%; radiolucent, often invisible on CT | Hydration; more soluble below pH 5.5 |
| Atazanavir | Highest stone risk of newer antiretrovirals | Hydration |
| Sulfonamides (sulfadiazine) | "Sheaves of wheat" | Hydration, alkalinise |
| Acyclovir (high-dose intravenous) | Birefringent needles; acute kidney injury | Slow infusion, hydration |
| Amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin (high dose) | Needles; worse in alkaline urine | Hydration |
| Methotrexate (high dose) | Crystal nephropathy | Alkalinise, hydration |
| Triamterene, ephedrine, guaifenesin, silicate antacids | Stones of drug or metabolite | Stop the drug |

- **Indirect:** loop diuretics and steroids (hypercalciuria, calcium stones); acetazolamide and topiramate (alkaline urine, hypocitraturia, calcium phosphate); cytotoxics (uric acid); laxative abuse (ammonium urate).

### Clinical significance
1. **Diagnosis of stone type** before stone analysis is available; crystals in a stone former help direct metabolic work-up.
2. **Recurrence risk:** persistent crystalluria in repeated early-morning samples, and large or aggregated crystals, suggest active stone disease.
3. **Infection:** struvite crystals with urine pH >7.2 signal a urease-producing organism.
4. **Acute kidney injury:** uric acid (tumour lysis), calcium oxalate monohydrate (ethylene glycol) and drug crystals cause intratubular obstruction.
5. **Monitoring therapy:** absence of cystine or uric acid crystals confirms adequate alkalinisation.
- Most crystalluria in healthy people is transient and harmless.

### Management principles
- Repeat on a fresh, warm specimen; confirm pH; 24-hour urine metabolic evaluation and stone analysis.
- **Fluids** for urine output above 2–2.5 L/day.
- **pH manipulation:** alkalinise (potassium citrate) for uric acid, cystine, methotrexate and sulfonamides; avoid alkali in calcium phosphate and struvite.
- Treat the cause: antibiotics and stone clearance for struvite; stop or change the drug; specific therapy (thiols for cystine, allopurinol for uric acid).

**Diagram to draw:** sketches of hexagon (cystine), envelope (calcium oxalate), coffin lid (struvite), rhomboid (uric acid), sheaf of wheat (sulfonamide).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Cystine hexagons are always pathological.
> - Acidic: uric acid, cystine; alkaline: struvite, phosphates; calcium oxalate at any pH.
> - Indinavir: crystalluria in two-thirds, stones 4–13%, radiolucent.
> - Refrigerated urine precipitates crystals: examine fresh within 2 hours.

## Q3a. Biological effects of cryosurgery
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(related: cryotherapy for carcinoma prostate, 2015-Summer Paper 4, Q3f)

### Principle
- Tissue destruction by extreme cold. **Argon** expanding in the probe tip cools by the **Joule–Thomson effect**; **helium** expanding warms (active thaw).
- Used for small renal masses and prostate cancer.

### Mechanisms of cell death
Tissue dies during **both freezing and thawing**, by four mechanisms:

| Mechanism | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Direct cell injury** | *Freeze:* extracellular water freezes first and becomes hyperosmolar; cells dehydrate and membranes distort. Rapid cooling forms **intracellular ice**, which ruptures organelles. *Thaw:* water rushes back, cells swell and burst; ice recrystallises into larger crystals |
| **2. Vascular injury** | Endothelial damage, platelet activation and **thrombosis** cause ischaemic necrosis; the main delayed killing effect |
| **3. Apoptosis** | Sublethal cold at the periphery triggers programmed cell death |
| **4. Immunomodulation** | Necrotic cells release antigens and DAMPs (damage-associated molecular patterns); dendritic cells activate T cells against tumour |

- **End result:** coagulative necrosis, then fibrosis and scar.

### Factors deciding lethality
- **Temperature:** normal renal tissue dies at about −19.4 °C; tumour may need −40 to −50 °C. **Target ≤−40 °C.**
- **Fast freeze, slow thaw** maximise injury.
- **Double freeze–thaw cycle** enlarges the necrotic zone (standard of care); each freeze about 8–10 minutes.
- **Heat sink:** large vessels nearby warm tissue, so more probes or longer freezing are needed.

### Zones of the ice ball
- Centre: complete necrosis. Edge: 0 °C; below −20 °C only about 3 mm inside the edge. The lethal −40 °C isotherm lies well inside the visible ice ball.
- So the ice ball must extend **5–10 mm beyond the tumour**.

### Clinical relevance
- Real-time imaging shows the ice ball; thermocouples and a **urethral warmer** protect sphincter and urethra in prostate cryotherapy.
- **Also know:** passive versus active thaw (both used); insulated probe shafts reduce skin injury.

**Diagram to draw:** ice ball with probe, −40 °C isotherm inside the 0 °C edge and tumour margin.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Argon freezes, helium thaws (Joule–Thomson).
> - Four mechanisms: direct injury, vascular thrombosis, apoptosis, immune effect.
> - ≤−40 °C, two freeze–thaw cycles, margin 5–10 mm.

## Q3b. Mitomycin
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Pharmacology
- MMC (mitomycin C) is an antitumour antibiotic from *Streptomyces caespitosus*.
- **Mechanism:** after reductive activation in the cell it acts as an **alkylating agent**, cross-linking DNA and stopping DNA synthesis; cell-cycle non-specific.
- Molecular weight 334 Da: **little systemic absorption** from the bladder.

### Uses in NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer)
1. **Single immediate post-TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour) instillation:** 40 mg. Given **within 6 hours** it reduces recurrence; at 24 hours it does not, because it works by preventing implantation of floating cells. Best for a solitary low-grade papillary tumour. Avoid if perforation or extensive resection is suspected.
2. **Adjuvant induction:** 40 mg weekly for 6 weeks (± maintenance) in low- and intermediate-risk disease. Reduces recurrence but **not progression**; BCG (bacille Calmette–Guérin) with maintenance is superior in high risk.
3. **Optimised delivery (Au, 2001):** empty bladder, overnight fluid restriction, sodium bicarbonate (less drug breakdown), 40 mg in 20 mL; retain 1–2 hours.
4. **Device-assisted:** EMDA (electromotive drug administration) increases tissue uptake; chemohyperthermia at 42 °C (RITE [radiofrequency-induced thermochemotherapeutic effect], HIVEC [hyperthermic intravesical chemotherapy]).
5. **Sequential gemcitabine then MMC** for BCG-exposed disease.

### Other urological uses
- **Upper tract:** single bladder dose after nephroureterectomy (ODMIT-C: bladder recurrence 28% to 16% at 1 year). **UGN-101 (mitomycin gel)**, FDA (Food and Drug Administration) approved 2020 for low-grade UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma): complete response 59%, ureteric stricture about 44%. UGN-102 for low-grade intermediate-risk bladder tumours.
- **Bladder-sparing chemoradiation:** 5-FU (5-fluorouracil) + MMC (BC2001: better locoregional control than radiation alone).
- **Antifibrotic:** injected after incision of recurrent bladder neck contracture or urethral stricture.

### Side effects
- Chemical cystitis (30–40%); **skin rash and palmar or genital dermatitis** (signature toxicity; wash hands and genitals after voiding); contracted bladder (about 5%); encrustation cystitis; myelosuppression rare.
- Extravasation through a perforation can cause pelvic necrosis.

**Diagram to draw:** timeline: TURBT, single MMC within 6 hours, then weekly induction ×6.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Alkylating antibiotic; 40 mg; single dose within 6 hours of TURBT.
> - Reduces recurrence, not progression.
> - Signature toxicity: skin rash; contracted bladder about 5%.

## Q3c. HOTA and its modification
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Assumed meaning: the Transplantation of Human Organs Act (THOA) 1994 of India, its 2011 amendment and the 2014 Rules.*

### THOA 1994: aims
- Regulates removal, storage and transplantation of organs for therapeutic purposes and **prevents commercial dealing**.
- **Recognised brain-stem death as legal death**, allowing deceased donation. States adopt the Act (health is a state subject).

### Main provisions (1994)
- **Living donors:** **near relatives** (spouse, parents, children, siblings) after verification. Others, donating "by reason of affection or attachment", need **Authorisation Committee** approval.
- **Appropriate Authority** registers and inspects hospitals; only registered centres may transplant.
- **Brain-stem death certification by a Board of four:** doctor in charge of the hospital; independent specialist from an approved panel; neurologist or neurosurgeon; treating doctor. **None from the transplant team.** Two sets of tests ≥6 hours apart.
- **Payment for organs is an offence**, with imprisonment and fine.

### 2011 amendment (THOTA, Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act)

| Change | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Tissues** included | Regulates tissue banks |
| **Grandparents and grandchildren** become near relatives | Wider related donor pool |
| **Swap (paired) donation** between near-relative pairs | Overcomes ABO or crossmatch incompatibility |
| Board: if no neurologist, a nominated surgeon, physician, anaesthetist or intensivist | Smaller hospitals can certify |
| **Mandatory transplant coordinator** | Organises donation |
| **Required request:** ICU (intensive care unit) staff ask families of brain-dead patients about donation | More deceased donors |
| National network and registry (NOTTO [National Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation]) | Fair allocation |
| **Higher penalties** for organ trade (up to 10 years' imprisonment; fine up to ₹1 crore) | Deterrence |
| Safeguards for minors, mentally ill and foreign donors | Prevents exploitation |

### Rules 2014 (THOT Rules)
- Standard forms for consent and certification (**Form 10** for brain-stem death); brainstem reflexes and apnoea test set out.
- Authorisation Committee procedures; donor and recipient interviewed to exclude coercion or payment.
- **Also know:** NOTTO with regional (ROTTO) and state (SOTTO) networks; 2023 guidelines removed the domicile rule and upper age limit for deceased-donor listing.

**Diagram to draw:** flowchart: brain-dead patient, Board of four, family consent, NOTTO allocation.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - THOA 1994: brain-stem death legal; anti-commercial; Authorisation Committee for unrelated donors.
> - 2011: tissues, swap, grandparents, coordinator, required request, higher penalties.
> - Rules 2014: Form 10; Board of four; tests ≥6 hours apart.

## Q3d. Marginal kidney donor
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2024-Winter Paper 4, Q3e and 2016-Summer Paper 3, Q3f)

### Definition
- A **marginal (extended) donor** is one whose kidney carries **more DGF (delayed graft function) and shorter graft survival** than a standard donor. Such kidneys are used because of organ shortage.
- They still give better survival than staying on dialysis (Ojo, 2001).

### Deceased marginal donors
- **ECD (expanded criteria donor), UNOS (United Network for Organ Sharing):** age **60 or over**, or age **50–59 with 2 or more of:** hypertension, **creatinine >1.5 mg/dL**, death from **CVA (cerebrovascular accident)**. Graft-loss risk about 1.7 times a standard donor.
- **KDPI (Kidney Donor Profile Index) >85%** replaced ECD in the US (2014); it uses 10 donor factors. Longevity matching pairs low-KDPI kidneys with recipients with the best EPTS (Estimated Post-Transplant Survival).
- **DCD (donation after circulatory death):** warm ischaemia raises DGF; long-term survival similar to DBD (donation after brain death).
- **Others:** donor AKI (acute kidney injury); HCV (hepatitis C virus)-positive donors with antiviral treatment; paediatric donors under 15 kg (en bloc).

### Living marginal donors
- Older donors, controlled hypertension, obesity, small stones, multiple renal arteries. Acceptable after careful evaluation and consent.

### Evaluation: Remuzzi biopsy score
- Pre-implantation biopsy; four compartments, each 0–3 (total 0–12):

| Component | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glomerular sclerosis | None | <20% | 20–50% | >50% |
| Tubular atrophy | None | <20% | 20–50% | >50% |
| Interstitial fibrosis | None | <20% | 20–50% | >50% |
| Arterial narrowing | None | Wall < lumen | Wall = or slightly > lumen | Severe narrowing |

- **0–3: single** transplant; **4–6: dual** kidney transplant; **7–12: discard**.

### Optimising use
- Short cold ischaemia; **HMP (hypothermic machine perfusion)** reduces DGF without changing long-term survival.
- **Old-for-old** allocation (Eurotransplant Senior Program, 65 or over).
- Informed consent; induction; delayed calcineurin inhibitor if DGF.
- **Urological point:** older kidneys have more urine leak and ureteric stricture.

**Diagram to draw:** Remuzzi grid with four compartments scored 0–3.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - ECD: ≥60, or 50–59 with 2 of hypertension, creatinine >1.5, CVA death.
> - KDPI >85% replaced ECD (2014).
> - Remuzzi 0–3 single, 4–6 dual, ≥7 discard.

## Q3e. Medical expulsive Rx for urinary calculi.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 2, Q3d)

### Definition
- MET (medical expulsive therapy): drugs that help a ureteric stone pass into the bladder. **α-blockers** are the main agents.

### Rationale: natural history

| Width | Spontaneous passage | Location | Spontaneous passage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 mm | 98% | Vesicoureteric junction (small stones) | 92% |
| 5 mm | 65% | Distal ureter | 75% |
| >6.5 mm | 9% | Proximal ureter | 48% |

### Mechanism
- **α1 receptors** (mainly α1A and α1D) are abundant in the distal ureter. Blockade reduces spasm and the force and frequency of peristalsis, so a larger urine bolus builds higher pressure above the stone.
- Others: calcium-channel blockers (nifedipine); PDE5 (phosphodiesterase-5) inhibitors under study. **NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) relieve pain but do not aid passage.**

### Drugs
- **Tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily** (most studied); **silodosin 8 mg daily** (better expulsion in meta-analyses but twice the retrograde ejaculation); alfuzosin, doxazosin.

### Evidence

| Study | Result |
|---|---|
| **SUSPEND (Pickard, 2015):** 1136 patients; tamsulosin, nifedipine or placebo | No difference in need for intervention at 4 weeks (19% vs 20%) |
| **STONE (Meltzer, 2018):** stones up to 9 mm | Tamsulosin no better than placebo |
| Meta-analyses; large distal-stone RCT (randomised controlled trial) | Benefit for **distal stones >5 mm** |

- Stratified, the trials agree: benefit lies in **distal stones of 5–10 mm**. Smaller stones pass anyway; larger ones rarely pass.

### Indications (AUA [American Urological Association] and EAU [European Association of Urology])
- Ureteric stone **<10 mm**, especially distal, when immediate intervention is not needed.
- **After SWL (shock wave lithotripsy)** to clear fragments. Children: off-label but effective.

### Contraindications (drain urgently instead)
- Obstruction with infection or sepsis, solitary or bilateral obstruction, renal impairment, refractory pain or vomiting.

### Protocol
- NSAID analgesia; counsel on off-label use, dizziness and retrograde ejaculation.
- KUB (kidney–ureter–bladder radiograph) or ultrasound follow-up; **intervene after 4–6 weeks** if not passed.

**Diagram to draw:** ureter with α1 receptors concentrated distally and a stone above the vesicoureteric junction.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Tamsulosin 0.4 mg; best for distal stones 5–10 mm.
> - SUSPEND 2015: no benefit in an unselected population.
> - Trial of passage 4–6 weeks, then intervene.

## Q3f. Prostatectomy incontinence
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 2, Q3a)

### Definition and incidence
- PPI (post-prostatectomy incontinence) is mainly **SUI (stress urinary incontinence)** after RP (radical prostatectomy), the commonest cause of male SUI.
- Rates vary with definition and reporting; continence improves for up to 2 years.

### Pathophysiology
- RP removes the internal sphincter (bladder neck and prostatic urethra), so continence depends entirely on the **rhabdosphincter**.
- Causes: sphincter or nerve injury at the apex, short membranous urethra, afferent denervation; also detrusor overactivity, low compliance and **VUAS (vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis)**.
- Nerve sparing and posterior reconstruction give **earlier, not better final** continence.

### Evaluation
- History (severity, radiotherapy), bladder diary.
- **24-hour pad weight:** mild <150 g, moderate <400 g, severe more (thresholds vary).
- **Standing cough test** grades 0–4: leak reported but not seen; delayed drops; early drops; delayed stream; early persistent stream.
- Urinalysis, creatinine, PSA (prostate-specific antigen). **Cystoscopy** excludes VUAS and shows sphincter coaptation; urodynamics if mixed symptoms.

### Management
1. **Wait 6–12 months.** Supervised **PFMT (pelvic floor muscle training)** with biofeedback, ideally started before surgery; pads, condom catheter or penile clamp.
2. **Duloxetine** (off-label). **Bulking agents are not advised.**
3. **Surgery** (AUA/SUFU [American Urological Association/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction] 2019; EAU [European Association of Urology]):

| Situation | Choice |
|---|---|
| Mild–moderate, no radiotherapy | **Male sling**: AdVance XP (repositions the bulbar urethra; needs a coapting sphincter) or adjustable ATOMS (adjustable transobturator male system) |
| Severe, or any radiotherapy | **AUS (artificial urinary sphincter), AMS 800**: gold standard; cuff pressure 61–70 cm H₂O |
| ProACT balloons | Expert centres; avoid after radiotherapy |

- **MASTER trial:** male sling non-inferior to AUS at 12 months.
- AUS: social continence 55–77%; revision 10–29% at 3 years; lifespan 8–10 years. Erosion risk higher after radiotherapy; **transcorporal cuff** for fragile urethra.
- **Also know:** climacturia; treat VUAS first.

**Diagram to draw:** AMS 800 with cuff, scrotal pump and pressure-regulating balloon.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Wait 6–12 months with PFMT before surgery.
> - Sling for mild–moderate; AUS for severe or irradiated.
> - MASTER: sling non-inferior to AUS at 12 months.
