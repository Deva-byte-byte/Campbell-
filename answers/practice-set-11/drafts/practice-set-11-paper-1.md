## Q1. Describe pzthophysiology of urosepsis and its management.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2017-Summer Paper 1, Q2)

*"Pzthophysiology" is read as pathophysiology.*

### Definitions
- **Urosepsis:** sepsis whose source is the urinary tract or male genital organs. It is usually Gram-negative and usually follows obstruction or instrumentation.

| Term (Sepsis-3, 2016) | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Sepsis** | Life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated host response to infection: acute rise in **SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) score ≥2** |
| **qSOFA (quick SOFA)** | Respiratory rate ≥22/min, altered mentation, systolic blood pressure ≤100 mmHg; ≥2 = high risk of death |
| **Septic shock** | Vasopressor needed for MAP (mean arterial pressure) ≥65 mmHg **and** lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluids |

- SIRS (systemic inflammatory response syndrome) criteria were dropped from the definition as non-specific.

### Causes and risk factors
- **Obstruction with infection:** stone, PUJ (pelviureteric junction) obstruction, tumour, pregnancy, giving infected hydronephrosis or pyonephrosis.
- **Parenchymal infection:** emphysematous pyelonephritis, renal or perinephric abscess, prostatic abscess, Fournier gangrene.
- **Instrumentation:** PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy), ureteroscopy at high irrigation pressure, TURP (transurethral resection of prostate), prostate biopsy (sepsis 0.3–3.1%), catheters.
- **Host:** diabetes, immunosuppression, old age, neurogenic bladder.
- **Organisms:** *E. coli* (commonest), *Proteus*, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas*, *Enterococcus*; ESBL (extended-spectrum β-lactamase) producers rising; *Candida* in catheterised diabetics.

### Pathophysiology
**1. Bacteria and endotoxin enter the blood**
- Obstruction causes stasis and raises pelvic pressure. Bacteria and endotoxin then enter the circulation by **pyelovenous and pyelolymphatic backflow**. High irrigation pressure at endoscopy does the same.
- Fragmenting infected stones releases endotoxin. Stone culture predicts postoperative sepsis better than bladder urine culture.
- An obstructed infected kidney behaves like an **abscess**: antibiotics barely reach it, and bladder urine may be sterile.

**2. Host response**
```
Endotoxin (LPS, lipopolysaccharide) or Gram-positive wall products
  → TLR4 (Toll-like receptor 4) on macrophages → NF-κB (nuclear factor κB)
  → TNF-α (tumour necrosis factor α), IL-1, IL-6 (interleukins)
  → complement, coagulation and endothelial activation
```
- **Vasodilatation:** inducible nitric oxide lowers systemic vascular resistance ("warm" shock).
- **Capillary leak:** endothelial glycocalyx damage causes hypovolaemia and oedema.
- **Coagulopathy:** tissue factor release causes microthrombi and DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation).
- **Myocardial depression and mitochondrial failure:** cells cannot use oxygen, so lactate rises.
- **Immunoparalysis:** a later anti-inflammatory phase invites secondary infection.

**3. Organ dysfunction:** AKI (acute kidney injury), ARDS (acute respiratory distress syndrome), encephalopathy, liver failure, DIC, multi-organ failure.

### Clinical features
- Fever or hypothermia, rigors, flank pain, tachycardia, tachypnoea, confusion, oliguria, hypotension. Often begins within hours of endourology.

### Investigations
- **Lactate**, two blood cultures, urine culture (and pelvic urine at drainage), blood count, creatinine, bilirubin, platelets, coagulation, blood gas, glucose. Procalcitonin helps decide when to stop antibiotics.
- **Imaging:** bedside ultrasound (hydronephrosis, debris); **CT (computed tomography)** for stone, gas or abscess.

### Management
**A. Resuscitation and antibiotics (SSC, Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021)**
```
Recognise (SSC: do not use qSOFA alone for screening)
 → lactate + cultures → IV (intravenous) antibiotics within 1 hour
 → 30 mL/kg balanced crystalloid within 3 h if hypotensive or hypoperfused
 → noradrenaline first line, target MAP 65; add vasopressin
 → persistent vasopressor need: hydrocortisone 200 mg/day
 → ICU (intensive care unit); repeat lactate, capillary refill
```
- **Empirical antibiotics** by local resistance: third-generation cephalosporin or piperacillin–tazobactam ± aminoglycoside; carbapenem if ESBL risk. Adjust for renal function.

**B. Source control: the key urological step**
- **Drain the obstructed infected kidney urgently** by retrograde **double-J stent** or **PCN (percutaneous nephrostomy)**. Equally effective in a randomised trial (Pearle, 1998).
  - Stent: suits coagulopathy; avoid high-pressure contrast injection.
  - PCN: suits pyonephrosis or large impacted stones; gives pelvic urine for culture.
- **No definitive stone surgery during sepsis**; treat the stone once infection clears.
- Other sources: drain abscesses; emphysematous pyelonephritis (percutaneous drainage; nephrectomy for diffuse gas without fluid); prostatic abscess drainage; change the catheter; debride Fournier gangrene.

**C. Supportive care**
- Oxygen or ventilation; insulin if glucose ≥180 mg/dL; transfuse below haemoglobin 7 g/dL; thromboprophylaxis; renal replacement if needed.

**D. De-escalation**
- Narrow antibiotics to culture at 48–72 h; switch to oral when stable. Usual course 7–14 days; shorter courses suffice after good source control.

### Special situations and prevention
- **Also know:** pregnancy (stent or nephrostomy, avoid fluoroquinolones); fungal urosepsis (remove catheter, antifungal, drain fungal balls).
- **Prevention:** sterile urine before elective endourology; prophylaxis; low irrigation pressure and access sheath; short operating time; stage the procedure if pus is found.
- **Prognosis:** better than sepsis from other sites when drained early; delayed drainage is the main avoidable cause of death.

**Diagram to draw:** obstructed kidney → pyelovenous backflow → cytokine cascade → shock and organ failure.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Sepsis = SOFA rise ≥2; septic shock = vasopressor for MAP ≥65 plus lactate >2 mmol/L.
> - Obstruction plus infection is the emergency; bladder urine may be sterile.
> - Hour-1: lactate, cultures, antibiotics, 30 mL/kg, noradrenaline (SSC 2021).
> - Stent = nephrostomy for drainage; delay definitive stone surgery.

## Q2. Describe anatomy of pelvic floor in females and its importance in surgeries described for SUI
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(pelvic floor anatomy also answered in 2017-Winter Paper 3, Q2 and Practice Set 2 Paper 1, Q1)

### Introduction
- **SUI (stress urinary incontinence):** involuntary leakage on effort, coughing or sneezing.
- Pelvic organs are supported by the **levator ani**, the **endopelvic fascia and its ligaments** and the **perineal membrane and perineal body**, anchored to the bony pelvis. Every SUI operation uses one of these anchors.

### A. Anatomy of the female pelvic floor
**1. Pelvic diaphragm (levator ani + coccygeus)**

| Muscle | Attachment | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Puborectalis | Back of pubis, loops behind anorectal junction | Anorectal sling |
| Pubococcygeus (pubovaginal part) | Posterior pubis and arcus tendineus → viscera, anococcygeal raphe | Slings urethra and vagina |
| Iliococcygeus | ATLA (arcus tendineus levator ani), ischial spine → raphe, coccyx | Horizontal **levator plate** |
| Coccygeus (not levator) | Ischial spine → sacrum, coccyx | Lies on sacrospinous ligament |

- Nerve to levator ani **(S3–S5)** runs on the pelvic surface; **pudendal nerve (S2–S4)** supplies the sphincters.
- Tonic contraction closes the **urogenital hiatus**. Muscle fails first (birth injury, avulsion); the fascia then carries the whole load and stretches.

**2. Endopelvic fascia and ligaments**
- **Pubocervical (pubovesical) fascia** attaches laterally to the **ATFP (arcus tendineus fasciae pelvis)**, the "white line" from the lower pubis to the ischial spine.
- **Pubourethral ligaments** suspend the urethra from the pubis; **urethropelvic ligaments** anchor it to the ATFP.
- **Cardinal–uterosacral complex** suspends the apex; **rectovaginal fascia** posteriorly.

**3. Perineal membrane, perineal body and urethra**
- Perineal membrane spans the pubic arch; urethra and vagina pierce it.
- **Striated urogenital sphincter**: sphincter urethrae (mid-urethra), **compressor urethrae** and **urethrovaginal sphincter**.
- Urethra 4–5 cm; continence zone is the **proximal and mid-urethra**. The **oestrogen-dependent vascular submucosa** gives up to 30% of closure force.

**4. DeLancey levels (1992)**

| Level | Structures | Defect |
|---|---|---|
| I suspension | Cardinal–uterosacral | Uterine or vault prolapse |
| II attachment | Paravaginal attachment to ATFP | Cystocele (central or paravaginal), rectocele |
| III fusion | Urethra, perineal membrane, perineal body | **Urethral hypermobility**, perineal descent |

### B. Theories of continence and the operations they produced

| Theory | Mechanism | Surgical answer |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure transmission (Enhorning 1961) | Hypermobile urethra drops out of the abdominal pressure zone | Restore position: Burch, MMK (Marshall–Marchetti–Krantz) |
| **Hammock** (DeLancey 1994) | Urethra is compressed against a firm backing of anterior vaginal wall attached to levator and ATFP; repositioning not essential | Restore backing |
| **Integral theory** (Petros and Ulmsten 1990) | Laxity of pubourethral ligaments at the **mid-urethra** | Tension-free mid-urethral sling |
| ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency; McGuire 1978) | Urethra fails to close; low VLPP (Valsalva leak point pressure) <60 cmH₂O | Sling, bulking, AUS (artificial urinary sphincter) |

- Most SUI includes some ISD. So slings work in hypermobility, but Burch fails in ISD with a fixed urethra.

### C. Importance in SUI surgery

| Operation | Anatomy used | Anatomical hazards |
|---|---|---|
| **Burch colposuspension** | Space of Retzius (avascular: symphysis in front, bladder behind, endopelvic fascia below). Paravaginal fascia at mid-urethra and bladder neck sutured to **Cooper's (iliopectineal) ligament** | Bleeding from Cooper's ligament and perivesical veins; ureteric kinking (cystoscopy); needs a mobile vagina |
| Paravaginal repair / VOS (vagino-obturator shelf) | Lateral vaginal sulcus to **ATFP** or obturator fascia (level II) | Obturator vessels and nerve |
| **Retropubic MUS (mid-urethral sling), TVT** | Tape under mid-urethra (integral theory), through **endopelvic fascia** and Retzius; suprapubic exits about 2 cm from midline | Bladder perforation (4.5%), vessels, bowel |
| **TOT (transobturator tape)** | Through obturator membrane, **obturator internus and externus**; exit at adductor longus tendon notch | Groin pain (6.4%), obturator neurovascular bundle; bladder injury rare (0.6%) |
| **SIMS (single-incision mini-sling)** | Anchors in obturator internus or endopelvic fascia | Works by restricting mobility |
| **Autologous PVS (pubovaginal sling)** | Rectus fascia at the **bladder neck** (proximal urethra), arms to rectus | Voiding dysfunction; best for ISD |
| Bulking agents | **Submucosa** coaptation | Repeated injections |

- **Why mid-urethra:** it is the zone of maximal closure pressure and pubourethral support. A loose tape lets the posterior urethral wall be compressed (and kinked) during stress.
- **TOMUS (2010):** retropubic 80.8% vs transobturator 77.7% objective success, not different.
- **SISTEr:** autologous sling more successful than Burch at 2 years, with more voiding dysfunction.
- **Also know:** needle suspensions abandoned (sutures pulled through); anterior colporrhaphy is not an SUI operation; levator palpable through the lateral vaginal walls for PFMT (pelvic floor muscle training) assessment; uncorrected level I loss causes recurrent prolapse after SUI repair.

**Diagram to draw:** cross-section of urethra on the vaginal hammock (DeLancey) with Burch, bladder-neck sling and mid-urethral sling sites.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Levator ani = puborectalis, pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus; S3–S5.
> - DeLancey I suspension, II attachment (ATFP), III fusion (urethral hypermobility).
> - Integral theory → mid-urethral sling; hammock → backing, not repositioning.
> - Burch → Cooper's ligament; TOT → obturator foramen; PVS → bladder neck.

## Q3a. Retrocaval ureter
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2017-Winter Paper 3, Q3d)

### Definition
- The **right ureter** passes **behind the IVC (inferior vena cava)**, then crosses in front of it from medial to lateral before resuming a normal course.
- Better named **"preureteral vena cava"**: the anomaly is venous; the ureter is trapped.

### Embryology
- Fetal veins form a collar around each ureter: posterior cardinal and supracardinal (dorsal), subcardinal (ventral).
- Normally the right **supracardinal** vein, **dorsal** to the ureter, forms the infrarenal IVC.
- If the **lumbar subcardinal vein** (ventral) persists as the IVC, the ureter is trapped behind it.
- Variant: double right IVC, with the ureter between the limbs.

### Incidence and presentation
- About **1 in 1500** autopsies; **male 3:1**; presents in the **third–fourth decade** with right flank pain, UTI (urinary tract infection), stones or incidental hydronephrosis.

### Types (Bateson and Atkinson)
| | **Type I (low loop), commoner** | **Type II (high loop)** |
|---|---|---|
| Crossing | Lower, L3–L4 | Higher, at renal pelvis level |
| Shape | **J or "fishhook"** | Smooth horizontal curve |
| Obstruction | Yes, at iliopsoas edge | Mild or none |

### Investigations
- **CT urography (computed tomography urography): investigation of choice**; MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) alternative.
- IVU (intravenous urography): ureter not seen beyond the J hook. Retrograde: **S-curve** at L3–L4. **Diuretic renogram**: degree of obstruction, split function.
- Pitfall: mistaken for **PUJ (pelviureteric junction) obstruction**, so pyeloplasty fails.

### Management
- **Non-obstructed (usually type II):** observe.
- **Obstructed:** divide the ureter, transpose it **anterior to the IVC**, and do a spatulated **ureteroureterostomy** (or ureteropyelostomy) over a **double-J stent**. Excise or bypass the **aperistaltic retrocaval segment**; respect the ureteric blood supply.
- **Laparoscopic or robotic** (transperitoneal or retroperitoneal) now preferred. Nephrectomy if non-functioning.
- **Also know:** retroiliac ureter (L5–S1); bilateral cases in situs inversus.

**Diagram to draw:** right ureter hooking behind the IVC with a fishhook pelvis.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Venous anomaly (persistent subcardinal vein); right side; 1 in 1500; male 3:1.
> - Type I fishhook obstructs; type II does not.
> - CT urography; anterior transposition and ureteroureterostomy.

## Q3b. Glandular hypospadias
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*"Glandular" is read as glanular hypospadias: meatus on the glans.*

### Definition
- The mildest **distal (anterior)** hypospadias: the meatus opens on the ventral glans, proximal to the normal tip.
- Hypospadias occurs in 1 in 150–300 male births; distal forms are 70–85%.
- Usual features: **dorsal hooded foreskin**, ventral glans tilt, little or no curvature. **MIP (megameatus intact prepuce)** variant (up to 5%): wide meatus with normal foreskin.

### Embryology
- Urethral folds close from proximal to distal during weeks 8–16 under **DHT (dihydrotestosterone)**.
- Glanular urethra: ectodermal ingrowth (classic) or endodermal plate ("two-zipper", current view). Arrest at the last step gives a glanular meatus.

### Evaluation
- Examine meatus, plate, glans size, curvature, torsion; retract the foreskin before any **newborn circumcision**.
- **GMS (glans–meatus–shaft) score** (each 1–4, total 3–12) predicts complications.
- No workup needed for isolated distal hypospadias; DSD (difference of sexual development) workup if a testis is undescended.

### Indications and timing
- Deflected or spraying stream, meatal stenosis, curvature, cosmesis. A straight penis that voids standing may need no surgery; counsel parents.
- **Age 6–18 months** (AAP, American Academy of Pediatrics, 6–12 months).

### Operations
| Procedure | Suitable for | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Urethromeatoplasty | Stenotic meatus, blind pit | Heineke–Mikulicz: vertical incision, horizontal closure |
| **MAGPI** (meatal advancement and glanuloplasty, Duckett 1981) | Glanular meatus, mobile skin | Advance meatus, two-layer glanuloplasty; retracts if used more proximally |
| MIV (M inverted V) glansplasty | Glanular or coronal | Avoids MAGPI retraction |
| **GAP** (glans approximation) | Wide deep groove, fish-mouth meatus (MIP) | Tubularise and close glans wings |
| **TIP** (tubularised incised plate, Snodgrass 1994) | Narrow plate | Most used; slit-like meatus |
| Mathieu flip-flap; Thiersch–Duplay | Selected | Stenosis, fistula |

- Preputioplasty or circumcision; dartos barrier layer.

### Complications
- Meatal stenosis or retraction, fistula, **glans dehiscence**, residual curvature, poor cosmesis. Distal repairs succeed in about 80%.

**Diagram to draw:** MAGPI steps (meatal advancement, glans wings closed beneath).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Glanular = most distal hypospadias; dorsal hood, minimal curvature.
> - MAGPI only for a truly glanular meatus; GAP for MIP; TIP is the workhorse.
> - Repair at 6–18 months; may be left alone if function is normal.

## Q3c. Treatment of submeatal stricture urethra in males
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(meatal stenosis also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 2, Q3a)

*"Submeatal stricture" is read as a stricture of the meatus and fossa navicularis (glanular urethra).*

### Causes
- **LS (lichen sclerosus, formerly BXO [balanitis xerotica obliterans]):** main inflammatory cause today; begins at the glans and meatus and progresses proximally.
- **Iatrogenic:** catheter or instrument trauma, TURP (transurethral resection of prostate).
- **Failed hypospadias repair**; post-circumcision ammoniacal meatitis (children).

### Evaluation
- Spraying stream, straining; uroflowmetry.
- **RUG (retrograde urethrogram) before any meatal surgery**: a proximal stricture often coexists, especially in LS.
- Biopsy suspicious lesions: LS turns malignant (squamous cell carcinoma) in up to 8%.

### Treatment
| Option | Method | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Topical steroid** | Clobetasol 0.05% once or twice daily for 1–2 months, then alternate days | Early LS |
| **Dilation or self-calibration** | Meatal dilator lubricated with clobetasol | Elderly, unfit; seldom durable |
| **Ventral meatotomy** | Crush and incise ventrally; **three sutures** (apex and each side), mucosa to glans skin; dilate afterwards | Simple stenosis; in LS extend it with an inverted-V relaxing incision |
| **Dorsal meatotomy** (Y-V plasty) | Excise the scar ridge, advance a flap | Effective; cosmesis poorer |
| **Fossa navicularis reconstruction (flaps)** | **Jordan flap:** ventral urethrotomy through the stenosis; **transverse ventral penile skin island on dartos** transposed and inverted into the defect. **De Sy:** ventral longitudinal island advanced | Non-LS; excellent function and cosmesis |
| **Graft** | **BMG (buccal mucosal graft)** inlay or onlay, one or two stages; glans wings developed | **LS: oral mucosa, never genital skin** |
| **Perineal urethrostomy** | Bypass | Pan-anterior LS, failed repairs, elderly |

- LS affects genital skin, so skin flaps recur; it spares the proximal urethra, so perineal urethrostomy works.
- Children: ventral meatotomy plus parental dilation for 7–10 days.

### Complications
- Restenosis (commoner in LS), bleeding, spraying, poor cosmesis.

**Diagram to draw:** Jordan flap: transverse ventral skin island on dartos inverted into the ventral meatotomy.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - RUG first; adult meatal stenosis is usually the tip of a fossa stricture.
> - Non-LS: meatotomy or Jordan/De Sy skin island flap.
> - LS: clobetasol, buccal mucosa (never genital skin), perineal urethrostomy if extensive.

## Q3d. TOT in SUI
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 2, Q3f)

### Introduction
- **TOT (transobturator tape):** a **MUS (mid-urethral sling)** of macroporous monofilament polypropylene passed through the obturator foramina instead of the retropubic space. Delorme (2001) designed it to avoid bladder, bowel and vessel injury.

### Mechanism
- **Integral theory:** pubourethral ligaments and the suburethral hammock maintain continence at the mid-urethra.
- A loose tape supports the posterior urethral wall, which is compressed against it on straining. It anchors in **obturator internus and externus**.

### Indications and evaluation
- **SUI (stress urinary incontinence)** or stress-predominant mixed incontinence after failed PFMT (pelvic floor muscle training).
- History, examination, **objective stress test**, urinalysis, PVR (post-void residual); urodynamics only if complicated. Counsel about permanent mesh and alternatives.

### Technique (outside-in)
1. Lithotomy, hips hyperflexed; 1.5 cm midline vaginal incision 1.5 cm from the meatus; dissect to the ischiopubic ramus.
2. Groin stab incision at the notch between inferior pubic ramus and **adductor longus tendon**.
3. Trocar perforates the obturator membrane (felt as a give), turns medially onto the finger and exits the vaginal incision.
4. Cystourethroscopy; check the vaginal sulci.
5. Tension-free: scissors pass easily between tape and urethra.
- **Inside-out** (de Leval): fewer vaginal perforations, more voiding dysfunction.

### Outcomes
- **TOMUS (2010):** objective success 77.7% vs 80.8% retropubic, not significantly different.
- Poorer with **ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency)** or a **fixed urethra**: retropubic sling preferred.

| | TOT | Retropubic |
|---|---|---|
| Bladder perforation | 0.6% | 4.5% |
| Groin pain | 6.4% | 1.3% |
| Voiding dysfunction | Less | More |
| Long-term repeat surgery | Possibly more | Less |

### Complications
- Groin or thigh pain, vaginal mesh exposure, voiding dysfunction (sling incision), de novo urgency, UTI (urinary tract infection), rarely thigh abscess.
- **Also know:** SIMS (single-incision mini-sling); alternatives: Burch colposuspension, autologous fascial sling, bulking agents.

**Diagram to draw:** pelvis with tape under the mid-urethra through both obturator foramina.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Delorme 2001; anchors in obturator muscles.
> - TOMUS: equivalent to retropubic (77.7% vs 80.8%).
> - Fewer bladder injuries, more groin pain; weaker in ISD.

## Q3e. Testicular biopsy
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 4, Q2)

### Indications
- **Azoospermia** with **normal testicular size, palpable vasa and normal FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone)**: separates **OA (obstructive azoospermia)** from **NOA (non-obstructive azoospermia)** (AUA/ASRM, American Urological Association/American Society for Reproductive Medicine).
- Clues to obstruction: FSH ≤7.6 IU/L and testis long axis >4.6 cm.
- Because ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection) needs one sperm, biopsy is **diagnostic and therapeutic**: plan it as **TESE (testicular sperm extraction)** and **cryopreserve** sperm.
- **Not needed:** CBAVD (congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens) with normal FSH; high antisperm antibodies after iatrogenic vasal injury. Do not operate with complete AZFa or AZFb (azoospermia factor) deletion.

### Types
| Type | Method | Use | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open biopsy** | Small albugineal incision, pea-sized sample | **Gold standard** for histology and retrieval | Open procedure |
| Percutaneous core | 14 G biopsy gun, cord block | Fresh retrieval in OA | Blind; epididymis or testicular artery injury |
| Aspiration (TESA, testicular sperm aspiration) | 23 G needle, suction, 3–4 passes | OA; backup to epididymal retrieval | Poor architecture; low yield in NOA |
| **Micro-TESE** (microdissection) | Operating microscope, plump tubules | NOA | Long operation |

### Open biopsy technique
1. Local anaesthesia with **spermatic cord block** (or general/spinal); choose the larger testis.
2. 2 cm transverse scrotal incision; open tunica vaginalis.
3. Stay sutures; 3–4 mm albugineal incision avoiding surface vessels (loupes).
4. Excise extruding tubules with iris scissors, **no forceps**.
5. Fix in **Bouin's, Zenker's or glutaraldehyde, never formalin** (distorts histology).
6. **Touch prep**, then wet squash prep for sperm; negative → contralateral biopsy.
7. Close tunica vaginalis separately; no antibiotics.

### Histology
| Pattern | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Normal spermatogenesis | Obstruction |
| Hypospermatogenesis | All stages, fewer cells |
| Maturation arrest | Stops at a germ-cell stage |
| SCO (Sertoli cell–only) | No germ cells |
| Tubular sclerosis | End-stage (e.g. Klinefelter) |

### Complications
- **Haematoma** (commonest); **inadvertent epididymal biopsy** (most serious, causes obstruction); infection rare.

**Diagram to draw:** open biopsy: small albugineal window with extruding tubules snipped off.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Indication: azoospermia, normal testes, vasa and FSH.
> - Bouin's, never formalin; no forceps; touch prep.
> - Haematoma commonest; epididymal biopsy most serious.

## Q3f. Premature Ejaculation
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2023-Winter Paper 3, Q3a)

### Definition
- **ISSM (International Society for Sexual Medicine) unified definition (2014):** all three needed:
  1. Ejaculation always or nearly always within **about 1 minute** of vaginal penetration (lifelong), or a bothersome fall in latency, often to **≤3 minutes** (acquired).
  2. Inability to delay ejaculation on all or nearly all penetrations.
  3. Negative personal consequences: distress, frustration, avoidance.
- **Types:** lifelong, acquired, variable and subjective.

### Physiology and aetiology
- Ejaculation = **emission** (sympathetic T10–L2) then **expulsion** (somatic S2–S4, pudendal). **Serotonin brakes and dopamine accelerates**.
- **Lifelong PE (premature ejaculation):** neurobiological and genetic; 5-HT2C hyposensitivity and/or 5-HT1A hypersensitivity.
- **Acquired:** performance anxiety, relationship problems, **ED (erectile dysfunction)**, chronic prostatitis, hyperthyroidism, drug withdrawal.

### Evaluation
- Diagnosis is by **history**: latency, control, bother.
- **IELT (intravaginal ejaculatory latency time):** self-estimate in clinic; stopwatch in trials (population median 5.4 minutes).
- **PEDT (Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool)**; IIEF-5 (International Index of Erectile Function) for ED; examination; TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) only if suggested.

### Management
- **Psychosexual education** for all; behavioural therapy (stop–start, squeeze).
- Acquired PE: treat the cause first (ED, prostatitis, thyroid).

| Drug | Dose and timing |
|---|---|
| **Dapoxetine** (short-acting SSRI [selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor]) | 30–60 mg, 1–3 h before |
| Paroxetine (strongest delay) 10–40 mg, sertraline, fluoxetine, citalopram | Daily, off-label; full effect 2–3 weeks; do not stop abruptly |
| Clomipramine | 12.5–50 mg daily or 3–4 h before |
| **Topical lidocaine–prilocaine** spray | At least 5 min before; condom avoids partner numbness |
| PDE5Is (phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors) | With ED, alone or with SSRI |

- SSRIs raise synaptic serotonin and delay ejaculation. Avoid in bipolar disorder.
- **Also know:** tramadol (addiction risk); high dropout from drug therapy.
