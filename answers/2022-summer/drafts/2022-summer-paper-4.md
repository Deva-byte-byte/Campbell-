## Q1. Natural orifice endoscopic surgery.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

*Taken as NOTES (natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery) and its relatives (hybrid NOTES, LESS [laparoendoscopic single-site surgery]).*

### Definition and concept
- **NOTES:** entry into the peritoneal or retroperitoneal cavity through a deliberate incision in a hollow viscus reached through a natural orifice (mouth, vagina, bladder, rectum). The aim is **"scarless" surgery**: no abdominal wall wound, so no wound pain, infection or hernia.
- It differs from **endoluminal** surgery (e.g. ureteroscopy), which stays inside the lumen.
- **Pure NOTES:** all instruments through the orifice. **Hybrid NOTES:** natural orifice plus one or more small abdominal ports. Hybrid is what is actually used in humans.
- Chapter 142 places NOTES at the end of a spectrum: transperitoneal → retroperitoneal → hand-assisted → LESS → SP (single-port) robotic → NOTES. **Each step to fewer incisions buys cosmesis and less pain at the cost of working space and triangulation.**

### History (milestones)
| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 2002 | Transvaginal laparoscopic nephrectomy in pigs (Gettman, Cadeddu) |
| 2004 | Transgastric peritoneoscopy in pigs (Kalloo) |
| 2006 | NOSCAR (Natural Orifice Surgery Consortium for Assessment and Research) white paper listed barriers |
| 2008–2010 | Hybrid, then pure, transvaginal nephrectomy in humans |

### Routes of access
| Route | Features | Urological use |
|---|---|---|
| **Transvaginal** (posterior colpotomy) | Commonest; easy closure under vision; large specimens pass; women only | Nephrectomy, donor nephrectomy, specimen extraction |
| **Transgastric** | Upper abdomen reachable; gastrotomy closure and leak are the main risk | Experimental (peritoneoscopy, renal surgery in animals) |
| **Transvesical** | Sterile urine; bladder dome port; small calibre; closure difficult | Peritoneoscopy (animal, Lima), transvesical access to pelvis |
| **Transurethral** | Urologist's familiar route | Experimental transurethral prostatectomy in cadavers |
| **Transcolonic/transanal** | Faecal contamination risk | Rarely urological |

### Hybrid NOTES and natural orifice extraction
- Standard laparoscopy does the dissection; **the vagina is the access for a camera or instrument and the extraction route** (Chapter 142). This is the only NOTES form with real clinical urological use.
- **NOSE (natural orifice specimen extraction):** kidney removed through the vagina after laparoscopic nephrectomy, avoiding the extraction incision, which is the largest and most painful wound.
- **NOTES-assisted donor nephrectomy:** laparoscopic or robotic donor nephrectomy with **transvaginal kidney extraction**. Reported benefits: less pain, faster recovery, no extraction scar; warm ischaemia acceptable.

### Steps of transvaginal (hybrid) nephrectomy
1. Lithotomy then modified flank; bowel and vaginal preparation; antibiotics.
2. Umbilical port (camera or instrument) plus one or two small ports.
3. **Posterior colpotomy** under laparoscopic vision; long port or flexible scope through the vagina.
4. Colon reflection, hilar dissection, ureter and vessels stapled.
5. Kidney in a bag, delivered through the colpotomy.
6. Colpotomy closed with absorbable sutures under vision.

### LESS: the close relative
- All ports through **one skin incision** (umbilicus or below the waistline) using several low-profile trocars or a multichannel port (TriPort; da Vinci SP 25–27-mm entry guide).
- Problems: **no triangulation**, instrument clashing, in-line view. Solutions: articulating or bent instruments, flexible-tip scopes, instruments of different lengths.
- Evidence (Chapter 142): randomised trial versus laparoscopy showed the same time, blood loss and complications, with less analgesia. Donor meta-analysis: lower pain scores. Oncological outcome unchanged.
- Drawback (Chapter 20): **more incisional and umbilical hernias** because of the larger single incision.
- **SP robot (FDA [Food and Drug Administration] approval 2018)** has revived single-site surgery.

### vNOTES (vaginal NOTES)
- A gynaecological development: a single-port device in the vagina gives access to the peritoneum.
- **HALON RCT (randomised controlled trial):** vNOTES hysterectomy was non-inferior to laparoscopic hysterectomy, with more same-day discharge and less pain.
- Urogynaecological uses: vNOTES apical prolapse repair (uterosacral suspension), adnexal surgery.

### Challenges (NOSCAR list)
- **Safe access** and **secure closure** of the viscerotomy (leak, fistula); peritoneal **infection**.
- **Spatial orientation**: retroflexed, off-axis view.
- **Platform**: flexible scopes lack stiffness, traction and triangulation.
- Control of **haemorrhage** and other complications.
- Training and cost; dyspareunia and fertility concerns after colpotomy; transvaginal route unavailable in men.
- Chapter 142: adoption is limited by **no clear therapeutic benefit**, safety concerns and very selective patients; **pure NOTES has very few urological cases.**

### Recent advances
- **Flexible robotic platforms** (Chapter 39): MASTER (NOTES, haptics), ViaCath.
- **Transvesical single-port** (LESS or da Vinci SP) surgery for diverticulectomy and foreign bodies (Chapter 124).
- Magnetic anchoring and guidance systems to restore triangulation.

**Also know:** LESSCAR consortium standardised the term LESS; "embryonic NOTES" (umbilicus as the natural scar); hidden-incision endoscopic surgery (HIdES) in children.

**Diagram to draw:** sagittal female pelvis showing posterior colpotomy with a transvaginal port and kidney extraction, plus umbilical port.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - NOTES = transluminal via natural orifice; hybrid transvaginal is the only routinely used urological form.
> - Transvaginal extraction removes the most painful wound.
> - LESS: one incision, lost triangulation, more hernias.
> - Barriers: closure, infection, orientation, platform; no proven benefit yet.

## Q2. Tissue engineering in urology.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Definition and rationale
- **Tissue engineering:** applying engineering and life sciences to build biological substitutes that restore, maintain or improve tissue function.
- **Why in urology:** bowel used for bladder and diversion causes mucus, stones, metabolic acidosis, infection and malignancy; grafts for urethra are limited in supply. The aim is **to replace "wrong tissue" with "right tissue"**.
- **Regenerative medicine** is the wider term: cells, scaffolds, growth factors and gene therapy.

### The tissue-engineering triad
```
Cells  +  Scaffold  +  Signals (growth factors, mechanical cycling in a bioreactor)
           -> construct -> implanted -> vascularised and remodelled by the host
```

### Cell sources
| Source | Features |
|---|---|
| **Autologous differentiated cells** (urothelial and smooth muscle cells from a bladder biopsy) | No rejection; expanded in culture; unusable if the bladder is malignant or diseased |
| **Adult stem cells:** bone marrow or adipose mesenchymal, muscle-derived, urine-derived | Easy harvest; paracrine effect |
| **Embryonic stem cells** | Pluripotent; ethics, teratoma risk |
| **iPSCs** (induced pluripotent stem cells) | Adult cells reprogrammed; patient-specific; tumour risk |

### Scaffolds (biomaterials)
- **Requirements:** biocompatible; biodegradable at the rate of tissue ingrowth; porous for cell attachment and vessel ingrowth; mechanically suitable (compliance for bladder).

| Type | Examples | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural** | Collagen, alginate, silk fibroin | Good cell recognition, weaker |
| **Acellular tissue matrices** | Bladder acellular matrix, SIS (small intestinal submucosa), decellularised dermis | Retain native architecture and growth factors |
| **Synthetic polymers** | PGA (polyglycolic acid), PLA (polylactic acid), PLGA (their copolymer) | Controlled degradation and shape; no bioactivity |
| **Newer** | Electrospun nanofibres, hydrogels | Mimic extracellular matrix |

- **Unseeded (acellular)** grafts depend on host cell migration and work only for short or onlay defects (about 0.5 cm for tubular urethra in animal work). Longer gaps need **seeded** scaffolds, or they fibrose and contract.

### Clinical applications
**1. Bladder**
- **Atala et al. (2006):** first seeded biodegradable bladder construct (autologous urothelial and muscle cells on collagen–PGA, wrapped in omentum) for augmentation in **7 myelomeningocele patients**; compliance improved.
- **Industry (Tengion) multi-institution phase II trial** in spina bifida: **no clinically or statistically significant improvement in compliance or capacity** (Chapter 48).
- Chapter 48 conclusion: a bladder with specialised urothelium and contractile muscle has **not** been achieved; it remains experimental.
- Chapter 111: an engineered bladder gives **storage only**; innervation for voluntary emptying cannot be recreated.

**2. Urinary diversion: Neo-Urinary Conduit (Tengion, NCT01087687)** (Chapter 135)
- Seeded biodegradable conduit to avoid bowel. Small enrolment; conduits survived about 250 days on average; **half developed stenosis or stricture.**
- Lessons: stiff, non-collapsible material; no cells formed around the connective-tissue sleeve; **no blood supply**.

**3. Urethra**
- Acellular bladder matrix onlay for hypospadias and stricture: success in short onlay repairs.
- **Raya-Rivera et al. (2011, Lancet):** tubular PGA–PLGA scaffolds seeded with autologous cells in **5 boys** with posterior urethral defects; functional urethras at up to 6 years.
- Tissue-engineered oral mucosa (MukoCell) for urethroplasty, avoiding donor-site morbidity.

**4. Other organs**
- **Sphincter:** autologous muscle-derived cells for SUI (stress urinary incontinence); also for underactive detrusor (Chapter 111: open-label improvement). Biodegradable scaffold slings (Chapter 48).
- **Vagina:** engineered vaginas in MRKH (Mayer–Rokitansky–Küster–Hauser) syndrome (Raya-Rivera, 2014).
- **Penis:** engineered corporal tissue, animal stage. ED (erectile dysfunction) stem cell therapy is investigational (Chapter 72).
- **Kidney:** decellularised–recellularised scaffolds (animal); renal assist devices; kidney organoids.
- **Testis:** spermatogonial stem cell banking for prepubertal cancer patients (future, Chapter 4).

### 3D bioprinting
- Layer-by-layer deposition of **"bio-ink"** (cells in hydrogel) with or without polymer, driven by patient CT (computed tomography) or MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) data.
- Advantages: exact patient-specific shape, multiple cell types placed precisely, built-in channels for vessels.
- Current urological use: 3D-printed (non-living) kidney and prostate models for planning and teaching; bioprinted urethra and bladder constructs are still at the animal stage.

### Organoids
- 3D self-organising mini-organs grown from stem cells or tumour tissue.
- **Kidney organoids** from iPSCs: models of development and disease, drug toxicity testing.
- **Tumour organoids** (bladder, prostate): personalised drug testing and biology (WNT/β-catenin work, Chapter 129).

### Challenges
- **Vascularisation** of thick constructs (diffusion limit), **innervation** and contractility.
- Fibrosis and contracture; scale-up, cost, culture time; regulatory approval.
- Safety of stem cells (tumour formation). Disappointing controlled trials after promising pilots.

### Recent advances
- Robotic bladder auto-transplantation and vascularised composite bladder allograft (Chapter 135): a transplant, not engineered, route to "right tissue".
- Smart scaffolds releasing growth factors; urine-derived stem cells; gene-edited cells.

**Also know:** bioreactors apply cyclic stretch to train smooth muscle.

**Diagram to draw:** biopsy → cell culture → seeding on scaffold → bioreactor → implant with omental wrap.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Triad: cells, scaffold, signals; vascularity is the main barrier.
> - Atala 2006 (7 patients) promising; phase II trial: no gain in compliance or capacity.
> - Neo-urinary conduit: half stenosed; no blood supply.
> - Raya-Rivera 2011: seeded tubular urethras in 5 boys.

## Q3a. Urological applications in nanotechnology.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Taken as "applications of nanotechnology in urology".*

### Definition
- **Nanotechnology:** design and use of materials of about **1–100 nm**. At this scale, surface area and optical, magnetic and thermal properties change.
- **Why useful:** particles pass through leaky tumour vessels (**enhanced permeability and retention**), carry drugs or tracers, and can be targeted with ligands such as PSMA (prostate-specific membrane antigen) antibodies.

### Applications
| Area | Application |
|---|---|
| **Imaging and staging** | **USPIO (ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide) lymphotropic MRI (magnetic resonance imaging):** normal nodes take up particles and lose T2 signal; metastatic nodes do not (Chapter 6; investigational in penile cancer, Chapter 84). Gold-nanoparticle CT (computed tomography) contrast, quantum dots |
| **Sentinel node** | Technetium-99m **nanocolloid** for dynamic sentinel node biopsy in penile cancer (Chapter 84) |
| **Diagnosis** | Nanowire and nanoparticle biosensors detecting PSA (prostate-specific antigen) and urinary markers at very low levels |
| **Drug delivery** | Nanoparticle (albumin-bound) paclitaxel in urothelial and prostate cancer (Chapter 164: tolerated, no pathological complete response); **NanoPac** intratumoral paclitaxel (Chapter 163); liposomal drugs; mucoadhesive intravesical carriers; siRNA (small interfering RNA) delivery |
| **Focal ablation** | **Gold nanoshells (AuroLase):** collect in tumour vessels and are heated by near-infrared laser; about three-quarters success on 12-month biopsy (Chapter 163). Magnetic nanoparticle hyperthermia |
| **Irreversible electroporation** | Electric pulses create **nanoscale membrane pores** ("NanoKnife"); spares matrix, vessels and ducts; investigational (Chapter 143) |
| **Devices** | Silver and other nanocoatings on catheters and stents against biofilm and encrustation |
| **Tissue engineering** | Electrospun nanofibre scaffolds that mimic extracellular matrix |
| **Stone disease** | "Calcifying nanoparticles" (nanobacteria) linked to Randall plaque, not proven causal (Chapter 95); nano-CT of stones and tissue |

### Limitations
- Toxicity and long-term tissue accumulation; uptake by liver and spleen; manufacturing cost; regulatory hurdles. Most uses remain experimental.

**Diagram to draw:** nanoparticle with drug core, polymer shell and surface PSMA ligand entering a leaky tumour vessel.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 1–100 nm; enhanced permeability and retention allows tumour targeting.
> - USPIO MRI: benign nodes lose signal; metastatic nodes do not.
> - AuroLase gold nanoshells: photothermal focal therapy.

## Q3b. Guidelines for declaration of brain stem death.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2018-Summer Paper 4, Q3c)

### Definition and law
- **Brain-stem death:** irreversible loss of consciousness and of the capacity to breathe; equivalent to death of the person.
- **India:** THOA (Transplantation of Human Organs Act) 1994; amended 2011; **Rules 2014**. Internationally: Harvard criteria (1968), AAN (American Academy of Neurology) criteria (updated 2023).

### Board of Medical Experts (India)
| Member |
|---|
| 1. Registered medical practitioner **in charge of the hospital** |
| 2. **Independent specialist** nominated from a panel approved by the Appropriate Authority |
| 3. **Neurologist or neurosurgeon** (if unavailable: surgeon, physician, anaesthetist or intensivist) |
| 4. Medical practitioner **treating the patient** |
- **No member may belong to the transplant team.**

### Preconditions
- Apnoeic coma on a ventilator with a **known irreversible cause**.
- Exclude **sedatives, relaxants**, hypothermia, metabolic or endocrine causes, shock.

### Tests: all reflexes absent
| Brainstem reflex | Method | Cranial nerves |
|---|---|---|
| Pupillary | Fixed pupils, no light reaction | II, III |
| Corneal | Touch cornea: no blink | V, VII |
| Oculocephalic | Turn head: eyes move with head | VIII, III, VI |
| Oculovestibular | Ice-cold water in each ear: no eye movement | VIII, III, VI |
| Motor in cranial nerve area | Supraorbital pressure: no grimace | V, VII |
| Gag and cough | Pharynx touch and tracheal suction: no response | IX, X |

- **Apnoea test (last):** pre-oxygenate with 100% oxygen; disconnect ventilator with oxygen insufflation; **no breathing effort at PaCO₂ (arterial carbon dioxide tension) ≥60 mmHg** (or ≥20 mmHg above baseline). Abort for hypotension, desaturation or arrhythmia.
- **Two sets of tests at least 6 hours apart**, recorded on **Form 10**.
- **Ancillary tests** if examination is impossible (facial trauma): angiography, perfusion scan, EEG (electroencephalogram).

### After declaration
- **Mandatory request** for donation by ICU (intensive care unit) staff; next-of-kin consent; **NOTTO** (National Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation) allocates organs.

**Diagram to draw:** flowchart: coma → preconditions → test 1 → 6 h → test 2 → Form 10 → consent → NOTTO.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Four-member Board; no transplant-team member.
> - Two tests ≥6 h apart; Form 10.
> - Apnoea test: no breathing at PaCO₂ ≥60 mmHg.

## Q3c. Different types of trocars in laparoscopy.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- A **trocar** is a **cannula (port) plus an obturator**; 3–20 mm diameter, 5–20 cm long (Chapter 18).
- Parts: obturator tip, cannula, **valve** (keeps gas in), insufflation side-arm, retention mechanism.

### Classification
| Basis | Types |
|---|---|
| **Obturator tip** | Bladed (cutting) with spring safety shield; **bladeless (blunt dilating)**; **optical (visual)**; blunt (Hasson) |
| **Material** | Reusable metal; disposable plastic |
| **Valve** | Flap or trumpet valve (needs reducer); **multiseal** (5–12 mm, no reducer); **valveless (AirSeal)** |
| **Retention** | Smooth (suture-anchored); threaded sleeve; expandable arms; **balloon (Blunt Tip)** |
| **Special** | Hasson open cannula; radially expanding; hand ports; single-site multichannel ports; robotic 8-mm metal ports |

### Main types explained
- **Bladed trocars:** cut through tissue; more epigastric injury and port-site hernia. Now of historical interest.
- **Bladeless (dilating) trocars:** spread muscle fibres. About **fivefold less inferior epigastric injury**; trocar-site bleeding 3% vs 9%. Switching cut port hernia from 1.83% to 0.19%. **Fascial closure only for midline ports >10 mm** or stretched sites.
- **Optical trocars:** clear tip with a 0° scope inside; each layer is seen during entry.
- **Hasson cannula:** blunt obturator and cone, fixed with fascial stay sutures after open entry; safer with adhesions; leaks more gas.
- **Radially expanding trocar:** a sleeve over the Veress needle is dilated to 12 mm; less port-site bleeding and pain (Chapter 39).
- **Balloon (Blunt Tip) trocar:** internal balloon and external foam cuff seal the wall; prevents leak and **subcutaneous emphysema** in retroperitoneoscopy.
- **AirSeal valveless port:** stable pneumoperitoneum at 8–12 mmHg despite suction.
- **Single-site ports:** TriPort; da Vinci SP (single-port) 25–27-mm guide. **Hand ports:** GelPort.

### Safety points
- **Never use a metal trocar with a plastic retaining ring**: capacitive coupling burns viscera out of view.
- Insert under vision; ports 8–10 cm apart.

**Also know:** Veress needle (14-gauge, spring-loaded blunt stylet) is an entry needle, not a trocar.

**Diagram to draw:** trocar with obturator tip types (bladed, bladeless, optical) and valve.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Trocar = cannula + obturator.
> - Bladeless: fewer epigastric injuries and hernias.
> - Balloon trocar prevents retroperitoneal emphysema.

## Q3d. Current ureter scopes.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Taken as "current ureteroscopes"; see also 2019-Summer Paper 4, Q3c.*

### Classification
| Type | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Rigid rod-lens** | Larger calibre; largely historical |
| **Semirigid** | **Fibreoptic** image bundle; tip **≤7 Fr**; channel >3 Fr; often **two channels**; in-line or offset eyepiece |
| **Flexible fibreoptic** | Tip 4.5–7.5 Fr (smallest); image degrades as fibres break (black dots) |
| **Flexible digital** | "Chip-on-tip" CMOS (complementary metal-oxide semiconductor) or CCD (charge-coupled device) sensor plus LED (light-emitting diode); no camera head |
| **Single-use flexible** | Digital; LithoVue (2016) first; now several makers |
| **Ureteral resectoscope** | 11.5 Fr; largely replaced by laser |

### Semirigid ureteroscope
- Uses **coherent fibre bundles** for the image (random fibres carry only light).
- **Two channels** are preferred: irrigation through one while an instrument is in the other, or a laser through one to break a stone held in a basket.
- **Offset eyepiece** gives a straight channel for rigid probes (pneumatic, biopsy).
- **Rule:** semirigid below the iliac vessels, flexible above.

### Flexible ureteroscope
- Parts: optics, deflection mechanism (lever pulls wires), working channel.
- **Working channel 3.6 Fr**; active deflection **>270° both ways** in one plane; enhanced primary deflection reaches the lower pole (ureteroinfundibular angle up to about 175°).

### Digital versus fibreoptic
- Digital: sharper image, **shorter operating time, same stone-free rate**. But larger tip, so **more access sheaths and sheath complications**.

### Single-use scopes
- **LithoVue:** CMOS, LED, tip 7.7 Fr, shaft 9.5 Fr, 3.6 Fr channel, 270°.
- Reason: reusable scopes may need repair after 3–5 cases.
- Best value in low-volume centres and for **large lower-pole stones**.

### Care
- **Fire laser only when the fibre tip is seen touching the stone**; pass fibre with the scope straight. Sterilise with ethylene oxide, glutaraldehyde or peracetic acid.

### Recent advances
- Intrarenal **pressure sensing**; AI (artificial intelligence); robotic flexible ureteroscopy; suction access sheaths.

**Diagram to draw:** flexible scope tip showing CMOS chip, LEDs, 3.6 Fr channel and 270° deflection.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Semirigid: two channels; below the iliac vessels.
> - Flexible: 3.6 Fr channel, >270° deflection.
> - Digital: shorter operation, larger tip; LithoVue first single-use (2016).

## Q3e. General complications related to laparoscopy.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(adapted from 2019-Summer Paper 1, Q2)

### Incidence
- Historically about 4%, mortality 0.03–0.08%. Contemporary series 13–22% because procedures are complex. **Vascular, then adjacent-organ injuries are commonest.**
- Risk factors: complex procedures, operating time >4 h, creatinine >1.5 mg/dL, learning curve.

### Complications by phase
| Phase | Complication | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Access (Veress, first trocar)** | Preperitoneal insufflation; vessel or bowel puncture; **major vascular injury (0.11–2%)** | Confirm Veress tests; open access with adhesions. Major vessel: leave trocar in, laparotomy |
| **Insufflation** | **Gas embolism**; subcutaneous emphysema; pneumothorax (1.6–4%); pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium; hypercarbia | Embolism: end-tidal CO₂ (carbon dioxide) rises then falls, mill-wheel murmur → stop gas, left lateral head-down, 100% oxygen, aspirate central line |
| **Secondary trocars** | Epigastric bleeding; crossing swords, rollover | Transilluminate; bladeless trocars; insert under vision |
| **Dissection** | Thermal or mechanical bowel injury; vascular injury; bladder or ureter injury | Four electrosurgical mechanisms below |
| **Positioning** | Nerve injury (about 2.8%: brachial plexus, femoral, sciatic); **rhabdomyolysis** (about 1% retroperitoneal) | Padding, kidney rest <1 h, cases <5 h |
| **Exit** | Bowel entrapment, late port bleeding | Inspect each site at 5 mmHg |
| **Postoperative** | Port-site hernia (0.3–5.4%), wound infection, shoulder-tip pain, DVT (deep vein thrombosis) | Close fascia of large ports |

### Electrosurgical bowel injury: four mechanisms
1. **Inappropriate activation** out of view.
2. **Direct coupling** with another metal instrument.
3. **Capacitive coupling** (metal cannula in a plastic retainer).
4. **Insulation failure.**
- Monopolar injury presents **late (up to 18 days)**; resect with margin.

### Missed bowel injury (Bishoff)
- Not classic peritonitis: **pain at one trocar site, leucopenia, low fever, distension, diarrhoea** at 24–48 h.
- **CT (computed tomography) with oral contrast**; free gas is normal for up to 7–9 days.

### Physiological complications
- Hypercarbia and acidosis; oliguria from renal vein compression; reduced venous return; raised intracranial pressure in head-down tilt.

**Diagram to draw:** abdominal wall showing capacitive coupling through a metal cannula in a plastic anchor.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Vascular injury is commonest; leave the trocar in place.
> - Gas embolism: left lateral, head down.
> - Missed bowel injury: trocar-site pain, leucopenia.

## Q3f. Approach to evaluation of adrenal mass/s.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- **Adrenal incidentaloma:** mass ≥1 cm found on imaging done for another reason.
- Two questions: **Is it malignant? (imaging)** and **Is it functioning? (biochemistry).**

### Differential diagnosis
- Non-functioning adenoma (commonest); cortisol-secreting adenoma (5.3%); phaeochromocytoma (5.1%); aldosteronoma (1%); ACC (adrenocortical carcinoma, 4.7%); metastasis (2.5%); myelolipoma, cyst, haemorrhage.
- **Bilateral:** metastases, lymphoma, **tuberculosis** and histoplasmosis, haemorrhage, CAH (congenital adrenal hyperplasia), macronodular hyperplasia, bilateral phaeochromocytoma (MEN-2 [multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2], VHL [von Hippel–Lindau]).

### Imaging
- CT (computed tomography), HU (Hounsfield units), MRI (chemical-shift magnetic resonance imaging), MDT (multidisciplinary team), FDG-PET (fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography).
```
Unenhanced CT -> <=10 HU or MRI signal drop-out -> lipid-rich adenoma
              -> >10 HU -> washout CT (15 min): APW >60% or RPW >40% -> adenoma
                         -> indeterminate -> MDT: repeat imaging 6-12 months, FDG-PET or surgery
```
- **APW** (absolute percentage washout) = (enhanced − delayed) ÷ (enhanced − unenhanced) × 100; **RPW** (relative) = (enhanced − delayed) ÷ enhanced × 100.
- Malignant features: heterogeneous, irregular, invasion, >4 cm. Macroscopic fat = myelolipoma.
- **Size and malignancy:** <4 cm 0.1%; 4–6 cm 2.4%; >6 cm 19.5%.

### Hormonal work-up (all lesions)
| Hormone | Test |
|---|---|
| Cortisol | **1-mg overnight DST (dexamethasone suppression test):** ≤1.8 µg/dL (50 nmol/L) excludes; >1.8 = **MACS** (mild autonomous cortisol secretion); >5 µg/dL autonomous |
| Catecholamines | **Plasma free or 24-h urinary fractionated metanephrines** |
| Aldosterone | **ARR** (aldosterone-to-renin ratio) if hypertension or hypokalaemia |
| Sex steroids | DHEA-S (dehydroepiandrosterone sulphate) etc. only if ACC suspected |
| Bilateral | 17-hydroxyprogesterone (CAH); exclude adrenal insufficiency |

- **Exclude phaeochromocytoma before biopsy or surgery.** Biopsy only for suspected metastasis with known cancer.

### ESE–ENSAT 2023 (European Society of Endocrinology–European Network for the Study of Adrenal Tumors)
- Homogeneous **≤10 HU: benign, no further imaging whatever the size**; metanephrines may be omitted.
- MACS: screen for hypertension, diabetes and osteoporosis; surgery individualised.

### Management
- Surgery: functioning tumour, suspicious imaging, >4 cm in fit patients, growth ≥5 mm and >20%.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - ≤10 HU = lipid-rich adenoma.
> - APW >60%, RPW >40%.
> - 1-mg DST: >1.8 µg/dL = MACS.
> - Rule out phaeochromocytoma before biopsy.
