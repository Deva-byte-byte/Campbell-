## Q1. Describe various methods of Nephron sparing surgery in relation to Renal masses?
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2017-Summer Paper 1, Q1 and 2017-Winter Paper 4, Q3f)

### Definition and rationale
- NSS (nephron-sparing surgery): complete removal of the tumour with maximal preservation of functioning parenchyma.
- RN (radical nephrectomy) causes CKD (chronic kidney disease), which raises cardiovascular events and death. PN (partial nephrectomy) gives equal cancer control with better function.
- Function after PN depends mainly on the **parenchyma preserved**, less on ischaemia time.

### Indications

| Category | Situations |
|---|---|
| Imperative (absolute) | Solitary kidney; bilateral tumours; RN would mean dialysis |
| Relative | Contralateral kidney threatened (stones, renal artery stenosis, diabetes, hypertension); hereditary RCC (renal cell carcinoma) |
| Elective | cT1a with a normal contralateral kidney: **standard of care** |

- Now extended to cT1b–T2 when feasible (better function).
- **AUA (American Urological Association) 2021:** RN only if all three: high complexity; no CKD or proteinuria; normal contralateral kidney with eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) likely >45 after RN.
- **Relative contraindications:** <20% of nephron mass left; pedicle encasement; venous tumour thrombus; cT4; cN1.

### Preoperative planning
- Multiphase CT (computed tomography) or MRI (magnetic resonance imaging); chest imaging; eGFR, proteinuria.
- **R.E.N.A.L. score** (Radius, Exophytic, Nearness to collecting system, Anterior/posterior, Location to polar lines): 4–6 low, 7–9 moderate, 10–12 high. It predicts complications, ischaemia time and conversion. PADUA is an alternative.
- RMB (renal mass biopsy) when it changes management, and before (or at) ablation. 3D models shorten ischaemia (randomised trial).

### Methods

| Group | Methods |
|---|---|
| Surgical PN | Open, laparoscopic, robot-assisted (RAPN) |
| Excision technique | Standard (wedge) resection with a rim; enucleation; polar resection or heminephrectomy (≥30% of parenchyma) |
| Thermal ablation | Cryoablation, RFA (radiofrequency ablation), MWA (microwave ablation) |
| Developmental | SBRT (stereotactic body radiotherapy), HIFU (high-intensity focused ultrasound), irreversible electroporation |

### 1. Open PN: steps
1. Flank extraperitoneal or transperitoneal approach.
2. **Early hilar control** of artery, vein and accessory vessels, even if off-clamp is planned.
3. Defat kidney; **intraoperative ultrasound** maps borders and depth.
4. Clamp; **ice slush** if ischaemia is expected ≥25–30 minutes (solitary kidney, CKD).
5. Excise with a thin rim; a negative margin is the priority, its width is not.
6. **Two-layer renorrhaphy:** inner layer closes vessels and collecting system; outer capsular sutures over a haemostatic bolster.
7. Unclamp, check haemostasis, close Gerota fascia, drain.
- Suits complex tumours in a solitary kidney.

### 2. Laparoscopic and robotic PN
- First laparoscopic PN 1993 (Winfield). **Transperitoneal** for anterior tumours (more space); **retroperitoneal** for posterior tumours (direct hilum, no bowel mobilisation).
- **Robotic vs laparoscopic:** shorter warm ischaemia, fewer conversions. **Robotic vs open:** less blood loss, shorter stay, fewer complications; same margins and eGFR.
- Haemostasis: sliding-clip renorrhaphy, barbed sutures, sealants.
- **Trifecta:** negative margin + no major complication + warm ischaemia ≤25 minutes.

### 3. Enucleation vs standard resection

| | Standard PN | Enucleation |
|---|---|---|
| Plane | Through normal parenchyma (thin rim) | Along tumour pseudocapsule |
| Pros | Fewer positive margins | Less bleeding, maximal parenchyma, eases zero ischaemia |
| Use | Default | Hereditary RCC (e.g. VHL [von Hippel–Lindau]), multifocal, solitary kidney, severe CKD |

### 4. Vascular control strategies

| Strategy | Verdict |
|---|---|
| En bloc or artery-only clamp | Equivalent; traditional warm ischaemia <25–30 minutes |
| Early unclamping (after inner layer) | May reduce pseudoaneurysm |
| Selective or super-selective segmental clamping | Avoids global ischaemia; no functional gain in a randomised trial |
| Off-clamp (zero ischaemia) | Small exophytic tumours; more blood loss; no long-term eGFR benefit in randomised trials |
| Parenchymal compression | Peripheral tumours |
| Hypothermia, mannitol | No benefit with a normal contralateral kidney |

### 5. Thermal ablation
- Mostly **percutaneous** (CT or ultrasound guided). AUA 2021: alternative for **cT1a <3 cm**, especially elderly, comorbid, solitary kidney or VHL.
- **Cryoablation:** double freeze–thaw; cell death at ≤−40 °C; iceball 5–10 mm beyond the tumour. **RFA/MWA:** heat causes coagulative necrosis.
- Success = loss of enhancement on CT/MRI. Local recurrence is higher than PN but equal after repeat ablation.

### Complications of PN
- Urine leak (drain, stent); haemorrhage, **pseudoaneurysm** or AV (arteriovenous) fistula (angioembolisation); acute kidney injury.
- Positive margin 0–7%: surveillance. Local recurrence 1–2% (elective), 3–5% (imperative).
- Hyperfiltration proteinuria when >50% of a solitary kidney is lost: ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors.

### Evidence
- **EORTC (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer) 30904 (2011):** RN vs PN, ≤5 cm: PN better function, similar oncology; 10-year overall survival 81% RN vs 76% PN; trial flawed.
- **Also know:** hereditary RCC treated at 3 cm; surveillance for frail patients.

**Diagram to draw:** clamped renal artery, tumour excised with rim, two-layer renorrhaphy.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Imperative, relative, elective; elective PN is standard for cT1a.
> - Preserved parenchyma, not clamp time, governs function; trifecta warm ischaemia ≤25 min.
> - Enucleation for hereditary/multifocal; ablation for cT1a <3 cm after biopsy.
> - EORTC 30904: PN better function, similar cancer control.

## Q2. Describe etiopathogenisis, clinical presentation, diagnosis and management of Renal TB?
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2018-Summer Paper 2, Q1)

### Introduction
- Caused by the MTBC (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis* complex); *M. bovis* (and BCG [bacille Calmette–Guérin]) is innately resistant to pyrazinamide.
- UG (urogenital) TB (tuberculosis) is 15–20% of pulmonary TB in developing countries; second extrapulmonary site after lymph nodes. **Kidney is involved in 80%.**

### Etiopathogenesis
- **Primary infection:** inhaled bacilli spread to hilar nodes, then by blood to the well-perfused kidney and epididymis, before immunity develops (up to 12 weeks).
- **Granuloma:** epithelioid cells and Langhans giant cells in a lymphocyte cuff, maintained by IFN-γ (interferon-γ). In 90–95% infection stays latent.
- **Reactivation** (lifetime 5–15%), even decades later: old age, diabetes, renal failure, HIV (human immunodeficiency virus), malnutrition, immunosuppression.
- **Routes:** haematogenous (principal); ascending (intravesical BCG, about 1%); contiguous (psoas, bowel).
- **Renal sequence:** bacilli lodge in **periglomerular capillaries** → cortical granulomas coalesce → **caseation** and cavities open into calyces → papillary necrosis, abscess, calcification → bacilluria seeds ureter and bladder.
- **Autonephrectomy:** caseo-cavernous type, or fibrotic calcified "putty" kidney.
- **Ureter and bladder (descending spread):** mural granulomas give strictures and a beaded or pipestem ureter. Cystitis starts around the ureteric orifices; after about a year of fibrosis the bladder contracts (<100 mL; thimble bladder <20 mL).
- **Key concept:** healing occurs by fibrosis, so infundibular and ureteric strictures (commonest at the **VUJ (vesicoureteric junction)**) and bladder contraction can worsen during treatment.
- **Also know:** granulomatous interstitial nephritis; miliary renal TB; amyloidosis; squamous metaplasia of the pelvis (squamous cell carcinoma risk); ESRD (end-stage renal disease) in about 7%.

### Clinical presentation
- Renal TB is silent until it reaches the bladder; asymptomatic in about 8%.
- **Storage LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms)** and dysuria (commonest), haematuria and flank pain (about one-third), colic <10% (sloughed papillae, clot, debris).
- Constitutional symptoms <20%. Recurrent "UTI (urinary tract infection)" not responding to antibiotics.
- Signs: renal angle tenderness, flank sinus, beaded vas, epididymal nodule; hypertension; renal failure if bilateral.

### Diagnosis
- **Sterile pyuria** ± microscopic haematuria (>90%).
- **Urine AFB (acid-fast bacilli) culture: gold standard.** 3–5 first-void morning samples on consecutive days. LJ (Löwenstein–Jensen) solid medium 4–6 weeks; liquid MGIT (mycobacteria growth indicator tube) as little as 10 days. Gives drug susceptibility. Ziehl–Neelsen smear sensitivity <50%.
- **GeneXpert MTB/RIF:** result in 1–2 days; detects rifampicin resistance (MDR [multidrug-resistant] TB surrogate). Cannot monitor treatment.
- **LF-LAM (lateral-flow lipoarabinomannan):** point-of-care urine test in people with HIV (WHO [World Health Organization] 2019).
- **TST (tuberculin skin test)/IGRA (interferon-γ release assay):** show infection only, not active disease.
- **Chest radiograph in all** (pulmonary TB coexists in up to one-fifth).

| Imaging | Findings |
|---|---|
| KUB (kidney–ureter–bladder) film | Calcification (24–44%); lobar "putty" kidney |
| IVU (intravenous urography) / CT (computed tomography) urography | **Moth-eaten calyx**, **phantom calyx** (infundibular stenosis), cavities, hydrocalycosis, **Kerr's kink**, pipestem or beaded corkscrew ureter, small bladder |
| Ultrasound | Follows hydronephrosis during treatment |
| MRU (magnetic resonance urography) | Children, pregnancy |
| Cystoscopy | **"Golf-hole" ureteric orifice**, ulcers; biopsy if culture negative |

### Management
**Medical (all patients)**
- **Multidrug therapy** because bacilli exist in different metabolic states and single drugs select resistance.
- Baseline liver and renal function, HIV, hepatitis, diabetes; DOT (directly observed therapy).
- **2HRZE/4HR:** isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol for 2 months, then isoniazid and rifampicin for 4 months; **pyridoxine** against isoniazid neuropathy. India's NTEP (National TB Elimination Programme) uses 2HRZE/4HRE.
- Complicated disease: some treat 12–24 months, or ≥6 months after surgery. The new 4-month rifapentine–moxifloxacin regimen is not used for UG TB.
- Toxicity: hepatitis (all except ethambutol); optic neuritis (ethambutol).
- **Monitor ultrasound** for worsening obstruction; steroids (anecdotal) for strictures.
- MDR-TB: second-line drugs (bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid).

**Surgical (about half need it), after 4–6 weeks of ATT (antituberculous therapy)**

| Problem | Procedure |
|---|---|
| Obstruction | Early **DJ (double-J) stent** (succeeds in 41%) or **PCN (percutaneous nephrostomy)** |
| Localised polar lesion not responding | Partial nephrectomy |
| Non-functioning kidney + persistent disease or hypertension | **Nephrectomy** (laparoscopic preferred; ligate artery and vein separately) |
| PUJ (pelviureteric junction) stricture | Pyeloplasty; ureterocalicostomy |
| Lower ureteric stricture | Reimplantation, psoas hitch, Boari flap; ileal ureter if long |
| Contracted bladder | Augmentation; substitution if <20 mL |

- Stricture not improving after 6 weeks of drugs → open repair.
- Contested: nephrectomy of a non-functioning kidney without active disease.

### Follow-up
- Relapse 6.3–22% (mean about 5 years): urine culture and imaging for up to 10 years.

**Diagram to draw:** kidney showing caseous cavity, phantom calyx and VUJ stricture.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Kidney 80%; periglomerular seeding; heals by fibrosis, so obstruction worsens on treatment.
> - Sterile pyuria; 3–5 early-morning urine AFB cultures; GeneXpert for rifampicin resistance.
> - 2HRZE/4HR with pyridoxine; surgery after 4–6 weeks of ATT.
> - Relapse up to 22%; follow up 10 years.

## Q3a. Assessment criteria for kidney donor.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2017-Summer Paper 2, Q3b)

*Assumed meaning: evaluation and acceptance criteria for a living kidney donor.*

### Principles
- **Donor safety first.** KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) 2017 is risk-based: lifetime ESKD (end-stage kidney disease) risk vs the centre's threshold.
- Multidisciplinary team with an **independent donor advocate**.
- India: **THOA (Transplantation of Human Organs Act) 1994, amended 2011:** near relatives; others need authorisation committee approval; swap donation allowed.

### Assessment sequence

**1. Compatibility:** ABO group, HLA (human leukocyte antigen) typing, crossmatch, DSA (donor-specific antibody). Incompatible pair → paired exchange.

**2. Kidney function** (two methods, one measured, e.g. DTPA [diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid] GFR)

| Test | Accept | Individualise | Decline |
|---|---|---|---|
| GFR (glomerular filtration rate), mL/min/1.73 m² | ≥90 | 60–89 | <60 |
| Albuminuria, ACR (albumin–creatinine ratio), mg/g | <30 | 30–100 | >100 |

- Urinalysis; persistent microscopic haematuria → cystoscopy, imaging, ± biopsy.

**3. Medical exclusions and limits**
- **Diabetes:** type 1 excludes; HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) and OGTT (oral glucose tolerance test) if at risk.
- **Hypertension:** controlled on 1–2 drugs without organ damage may be acceptable.
- **Obesity:** individualised risk.
- **Stones:** metabolic work-up; bilateral stones generally exclude.
- Active cancer, chronic infection, cardiovascular disease exclude.

**4. Infection screen:** HIV (human immunodeficiency virus), hepatitis B and C, CMV (cytomegalovirus), syphilis, tuberculosis.

**5. Cardiopulmonary:** ECG (electrocardiogram), chest X-ray, echo.

**6. Age-appropriate cancer screening.**

**7. Anatomy:** **CT (computed tomography) angiography** for vessel number, early branching, stones, masses. Split function if asymmetric: **the better kidney stays with the donor**; left preferred (longer vein).

**8. Psychosocial:** voluntary, no coercion or payment.

### Counselling figures
- Function settles at 60–80% of baseline; mortality 3 per 10,000.
- **Muzaale (2014):** lifetime ESKD 90 per 10,000 donors vs 14 in matched healthy non-donors.

**Also know:** APOL1 (apolipoprotein L1) testing in African ancestry; more pre-eclampsia in later pregnancy.

**Diagram to draw:** donor work-up flowchart.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - KDIGO 2017: GFR ≥90 accept, <60 decline; ACR >100 decline.
> - Type 1 diabetes and bilateral stones exclude.
> - CT angiography; better kidney stays with the donor.
> - THOA: near relatives, else authorisation committee.

## Q3b. Management of Haemorrhagic cystitis.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- Diffuse inflammation and bleeding of the bladder mucosa, after excluding other sources (cystoscopy, cytology, upper tract imaging).
- **Exclude bladder cancer first:** cyclophosphamide and radiation are also risk factors for it.

### Causes and mechanism

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide** | Liver makes **acrolein**, excreted in urine, which sloughs urothelium; onset within 48 hours |
| **Pelvic radiation** (5%, 6 months to 10 years later) | **Obliterative endarteritis** → ischaemia, fibrosis, fragile neovessels |
| **Viral** | **BK polyomavirus**, adenovirus 11 and 35; after bone marrow or renal transplant |
| Others | Bacterial infection; busulfan, thiotepa; penicillins; amyloidosis |

### Prevention
- **Mesna** binds and inactivates acrolein; hyperhydration with forced diuresis, CBI (continuous bladder irrigation).

### Management (escalate by severity; stability decides the branch)
```
Resuscitate, transfuse; correct coagulopathy; treat infection
→ large-bore catheter, CBI
→ cystoscopy under anaesthesia: clot evacuation, fulguration, biopsy
→ intravesical alum
→ bleeding after 48 h → hyperbaric oxygen (+ nephrostomies if bleeding continues)
→ formalin 1% → 4% (cystogram first)
→ unstable: internal iliac embolisation
→ cystectomy + conduit
```

| Intravesical agent | Mechanism and method | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Alum 1%** (first line) | Protein precipitation, vasoconstriction; 200–300 mL/h; no anaesthesia | Aluminium toxicity in renal failure |
| Aminocaproic acid | Antifibrinolytic CBI, 200 mg/L | Only in a clot-free bladder; thromboembolism |
| Prostaglandins (carboprost), silver nitrate, hyaluronate | Vasoconstriction, coagulation, GAG (glycosaminoglycan) repair | Silver nitrate: cystogram |
| **Formalin 1–4%** | Protein fixation; **80–90% success** | Anaesthesia; **cystogram to exclude reflux**; fibrosis, small bladder, ureteric stricture |

- **HBOT (hyperbaric oxygen therapy):** 100% oxygen at 2–3 atmospheres, 30–40 sessions; neovascularisation; 80–90% response, but many relapse. **RICH-ART** trial: better urinary symptom score in radiation cystitis.
- **Angioembolisation:** anterior division of internal iliac; spare the posterior division (gluteal pain).
- **Nephrostomies:** divert urokinase-rich urine.
- **Cystectomy with diversion:** last resort; remove the bladder (pyocystis risk).
- **Viral:** supportive; cidofovir (intravenous or intravesical).

**Also know:** tranexamic acid and laser ablation are unproven; oral pentosan polysulfate in children.

**Diagram to draw:** stepwise escalation ladder.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Acrolein (prevent with mesna); radiation endarteritis; BK virus.
> - Alum first; formalin only after cystogram.
> - Hyperbaric oxygen for radiation cystitis (RICH-ART); embolise if unstable.

## Q3c. Mini Perc.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

**See 2022-Summer Paper 1, Q1 (full answer).**

> **Key points to remember (SAQ angle):**
> - Definition by sheath size: mini-PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) 15–22 Fr; ultra-mini 11–14 Fr; micro-perc <10 Fr (standard 24–34 Fr).
> - Main advantage: less bleeding and fewer transfusions, shorter stay, with stone-free rates non-inferior to standard PCNL.
> - Main limitation: longer operating time and higher intrarenal pressure (fever, sepsis); the advantage disappears against a 24 Fr tract.

## Q3d. Methods of preoperative bowel preparation.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Rationale
- Bowel used in urinary diversion is opened into a sterile urinary tract. Faecal spillage causes wound infection, abscess, leak and sepsis.
- Flora: aerobes (*E. coli*, *Enterococcus faecalis*); anaerobes (*Bacteroides*, *Clostridium*).

| Segment | Organisms per gram |
|---|---|
| Jejunum | 10–10⁵ |
| Distal ileum | 10⁵–10⁷ |
| Ascending colon | 10⁶–10⁸ |
| Descending colon | 10¹⁰–10¹² |

### Methods

**1. Dietary:** clear liquids the day before; clear drinks until 2 hours before surgery.

**2. Mechanical bowel preparation (MBP)** reduces faecal **amount**, so total bacteria, not concentration.
- **PEG (polyethylene glycol)–electrolyte** lavage: non-absorbable; preferred in elderly, renal or heart failure, cirrhosis.
- Sodium phosphate, magnesium citrate; enemas.
- Historical: 3-day purgation, whole-gut irrigation by nasogastric tube (9–12 L saline), mannitol (feeds bacteria).
- Harms: dehydration, electrolyte loss; liquid stool may spill more.

**3. Oral antibiotic preparation** reduces bacterial **concentration**.
- **Neomycin + erythromycin base** (Nichols–Condon, 1977: septic complications 43% with MBP alone vs 9% with added antibiotics); neomycin + metronidazole; kanamycin.
- Harms: diarrhoea, *C. difficile* colitis, candidiasis; useless in obstruction.

**4. Intravenous prophylaxis (most important):** anaerobic plus aerobic cover (e.g. cephalosporin + metronidazole) within 1–2 hours before incision; it must be in tissue before contamination.

**5. ERAS (enhanced recovery after surgery):** **no routine MBP**; prehabilitation, multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia, chewing gum, **alvimopan** (peripheral µ-opioid antagonist, 12 mg before surgery), early feeding and mobilisation.

### Evidence
- Colorectal meta-analyses and Cochrane: MBP does not reduce leak or abscess; may raise SSI (surgical site infection).
- Oral antibiotics added to MBP reduce SSI and leaks in large colectomy series.
- Small cystectomy trials: no difference without preparation.

### Who still needs full preparation
- Salvage cystectomy after radiation, or cystectomy for benign disease (radiation cystitis, fistula, neurogenic bladder); immunosuppression.

**Also know:** wound protectors reduce SSI; enema before transrectal biopsy.

**Diagram to draw:** bacterial load along the gut.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Mechanical lowers amount; antibiotics lower concentration.
> - Nichols–Condon: neomycin + erythromycin.
> - Intravenous antibiotics before incision are essential; ERAS omits routine MBP.

## Q3e. IPSS score
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score):** validated, self-administered questionnaire grading male LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) over the past month.
- Based on the **AUA-SI (American Urological Association Symptom Index, 1992)**; adopted by WHO (World Health Organization) in 1993 by adding one quality-of-life question.

### Items

| No. | Symptom | Phase |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Incomplete emptying | Voiding |
| 2 | Frequency (again within 2 hours) | Storage |
| 3 | Intermittency | Voiding |
| 4 | Urgency | Storage |
| 5 | Weak stream | Voiding |
| 6 | Straining | Voiding |
| 7 | Nocturia | Storage |

- **Scoring:** items 0–5. 0 not at all; 1 less than 1 time in 5; 2 less than half the time; 3 about half; 4 more than half; 5 almost always. **Nocturia scored by number of times** (0 to ≥5).
- **Total 0–35:** voiding subscore 4 items, storage 3 (not validated).

| Severity | Score |
|---|---|
| Mild | 0–7 |
| Moderate | 8–19 |
| Severe | 20–35 |

- **QoL (quality of life)/bother question:** "If you were to spend the rest of your life with your urinary condition as it is now, how would you feel?" Scored 0 (delighted) to 6 (terrible); reported separately, outside the 35.

### Uses
- First step in LUTS work-up (AUA and EAU [European Association of Urology]); grades severity and bother.
- Guides treatment: mild, non-bothersome → watchful waiting; bothersome → medical therapy or surgery.
- Monitors response: **a 3-point fall is the minimum perceptible change**.
- Standard end point in trials.

### Limitations
- **Not disease-specific:** women score similarly; does not diagnose BOO (bladder outlet obstruction) or BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia); correlates poorly with urodynamics, prostate size and flow.
- No incontinence, pain or post-micturition items.
- Needs literacy.

### Alternatives
- **VPSS (Visual Prostate Symptom Score):** pictures, for low literacy.
- BPH Impact Index; ICIQ-MLUTS (International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Male LUTS); DAN-PSS (Danish Prostate Symptom Score); OAB-q (overactive bladder questionnaire); LURN (Lower Urinary Tract Research Network) SI-29 adds incontinence and pain.

**Diagram to draw:** IPSS chart with scoring columns.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 7 items × 0–5 = 0–35; bands 0–7, 8–19, 20–35.
> - QoL 0–6 is separate; IPSS = AUA-SI + QoL.
> - 3-point change is noticeable; IPSS does not diagnose BOO.

## Q3f. Isolated penopubic Epispadias.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- Part of the **exstrophy–epispadias complex**: a defect of the dorsal urethral wall, replaced by a broad dorsal mucosal strip (urethral plate).
- Male classification by meatus: glanular, penile, **penopubic** (subsymphyseal). About **70% of male cases are complete (penopubic)**, with incontinence.
- Incidence of isolated male epispadias about 1 in 117,000.

### Embryology
- Absent mesodermal reinforcement of the cloacal membrane; genital tubercles fuse **caudal** to it, giving a dorsal urethral defect.

### Clinical features
- Penopubic meatus; outlet may admit a finger → **total incontinence**.
- **Short, broad, upturned penis with dorsal chordee**, like exstrophy.
- **Pubic diastasis**, less than in exstrophy.
- Small bladder (no outlet resistance); **VUR (vesicoureteric reflux) 30–40%** in males.
- **Female (Davis retrosymphyseal type):** bifid clitoris, incontinence.

### Evaluation
- Renal ultrasound; VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram); cystoscopy and capacity under anaesthesia.
- **Key decision:** epispadias or exstrophy pathway. Treat as exstrophy (osteotomy, closure) if pubic diastasis >3 cm, poor intrasymphyseal bar, absent sphincter or tiny bladder.

### Management
- **Aims:** continence, safe upper tracts, a straight functional penis.
- **Preoperative testosterone** (2 mg/kg intramuscular, 5 and 2 weeks before).

**Stage 1: urethroplasty and penile reconstruction (6–12 months)**
- Release chordee, divide suspensory ligaments, free corpora from inferior rami.
- **Modified Cantwell–Ransley:** plate tubularised and moved ventral to the corpora, which are rotated medially (± cavernocavernostomy).
- **Mitchell–Bägli complete penile disassembly:** reliable chordee correction, but often leaves hypospadias.
- Outlet resistance rises, so capacity grows.

**Stage 2: bladder neck reconstruction (4–5 years, capacity about 100 mL)**
- **Young–Dees–Leadbetter** bladder neck repair + Marshall–Marchetti–Krantz suspension + **ureteric reimplantation**. Osteotomy usually unnecessary.
- **Good capacity:** single-stage repair possible.

### Outcomes and complications
- Continence after bladder neck reconstruction: **males 47–82%**, females 70–87%; capacity is the main predictor.
- Fistula (up to about one-fifth early), stricture rare; erections and paternity usually preserved.
- Better than exstrophy: bladder never exposed or scarred.
- Failure: injectables, augmentation.

**Diagram to draw:** dorsal penopubic meatus and plate.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - About 70% of male epispadias are penopubic and incontinent; VUR 30–40%.
> - Diastasis >3 cm → exstrophy pathway.
> - Cantwell–Ransley at 6–12 months; YDL (Young–Dees–Leadbetter) at about 100 mL.
