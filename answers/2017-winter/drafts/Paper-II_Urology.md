## II-1. Discuss principles of hemodialysis and vasular access in details.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

*Assumed meaning: "vasular" is a typo for "vascular" access for haemodialysis.*

### Introduction
- Haemodialysis (HD) removes solutes and water from blood across a semipermeable membrane in an extracorporeal circuit. It is the commonest KRT (kidney replacement therapy) for ESKD (end-stage kidney disease) and severe AKI (acute kidney injury).
- It needs a reliable vascular access that gives a blood flow of about 250–350 mL/min.

### Principles of haemodialysis
| Process | Driving force | What it removes |
|---|---|---|
| **Diffusion** | Concentration gradient across the membrane | Small solutes (urea, creatinine, potassium); bicarbonate enters blood |
| **Convection** | Transmembrane pressure; solute is dragged with water | Middle molecules (e.g. β2-microglobulin) |
| **Ultrafiltration** | Transmembrane (hydrostatic) pressure | Excess water, down to "dry weight" |

- **Countercurrent flow:** dialysate runs opposite to blood, so a gradient is kept along the whole dialyser.
- Clearance rises with blood flow, dialysate flow (about 500 mL/min), membrane area and permeability, and session time.
- **Dialyser:** hollow fibres of synthetic, biocompatible membrane (e.g. polysulfone). High-flux membranes have larger pores and clear more middle molecules.
- **Dialysate:** ultrapure water (reverse osmosis) with sodium about 140 mmol/L, potassium about 2 mmol/L, bicarbonate buffer and calcium, adjusted to the patient.
- **Anticoagulation:** unfractionated heparin. Use heparin-free dialysis (saline flushes) or regional citrate when bleeding risk is high, e.g. after surgery.
- **Schedule and variants:** usually about 4 h three times a week (3–5 h, 3–5 times). Haemofiltration is pure convection with replacement fluid; haemodiafiltration (HDF) combines both. CRRT (continuous renal replacement therapy) suits unstable ICU (intensive care unit) patients.

### Adequacy
- **Kt/V:** K is dialyser urea clearance, t is time and V is urea distribution volume (total body water). Target single-pool Kt/V 1.4, minimum 1.2 per session for thrice-weekly HD (KDOQI [Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative] 2015).
- **URR (urea reduction ratio) ≥65%.**
- **HEMO study (2002):** a higher dose or high-flux membranes did not improve survival.

### Indications
- **Acute ("AEIOU"), each refractory to medical treatment:** Acidosis; Electrolytes (mainly hyperkalaemia); Ingestions (lithium, salicylates, alcohols); Overload with respiratory distress; Uraemia (encephalopathy, pericarditis, bleeding).
- **Chronic:** symptomatic ESKD, usually at eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) 5–10 mL/min/1.73 m². **IDEAL trial (2010):** starting early (eGFR 10–14) gave no benefit over starting later (5–7).

### Complications of haemodialysis
- **During dialysis:** hypotension (commonest), cramps, nausea, headache. **Disequilibrium syndrome:** in early sessions, rapid urea removal causes an osmotic shift into the brain and cerebral oedema; prevent it with short, gentle first sessions. Also arrhythmia, dialyser reactions, air embolism, haemolysis, bleeding.
- **Long term:** cardiovascular disease (main cause of death), CKD-MBD (chronic kidney disease–mineral bone disorder), vascular calcification, calciphylaxis, β2-microglobulin amyloidosis (carpal tunnel), anaemia, infection, hepatitis B and C.
- **Urological:** acquired cystic kidney disease in about 80% of patients with ESKD; 1–2% of these develop RCC (renal cell carcinoma).

### Types of vascular access
| Access | Construction | Ready to use | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Radiocephalic AVF** (arteriovenous fistula; Brescia–Cimino, 1966) | Radial artery to cephalic vein at the wrist (now usually vein end to artery side) | 6–8 weeks or more | First choice. Best long-term patency and fewest infections, but the highest failure to mature |
| Brachiocephalic AVF | Brachial artery to cephalic vein at the elbow | 6–8 weeks or more | Higher flow; more steal |
| Brachiobasilic AVF | The deep basilic vein is transposed to lie superficial (one or two stages) | Longer | When the cephalic vein is unusable |
| **AVG** (arteriovenous graft) | PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) loop in the forearm or straight in the upper arm | 2–3 weeks (early-cannulation grafts within days) | Matures quickly; more stenosis, thrombosis and infection |
| **Tunnelled cuffed CVC** (central venous catheter) | Right internal jugular preferred. A cuff in the tunnel blocks bacterial migration | At once | Bridge, or when no AV access is possible. Highest infection and central stenosis |
| Non-tunnelled CVC | 10–12 Fr dual-lumen, jugular or femoral | At once | Acute dialysis, short term only |

### Planning the access
- **Hierarchy:** AVF before AVG before CVC; distal before proximal; non-dominant arm first. The US **Fistula First** initiative (2003) promoted this. **KDOQI 2019** now asks for an individual "ESKD Life-Plan": the right access for the right patient at the right time.
- **Timing:** refer when eGFR falls below 30. Create an AVF at least 6 months before dialysis is expected, to allow maturation and revision.
- **Vein preservation:** no venepuncture, cannula or PICC (peripherally inserted central catheter) in the non-dominant arm. Avoid subclavian catheters, which cause central stenosis.
- **Assessment:** history (dominant arm, pacemaker, previous lines); pulses and arm blood pressure difference; **Allen test** (ulnar collateral flow); **Doppler vein mapping** (artery and vein about ≥2 mm); venography if central stenosis is suspected.
- **Maturity, "rule of 6s":** flow >600 mL/min, diameter >6 mm, depth <6 mm, by 6 weeks.
- **Cannulation:** rotate sites ("rope-ladder"); the buttonhole technique has more infection.

### Access complications
| Complication | Key point | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Failure to mature | Juxta-anastomotic stenosis; competing side veins | Angioplasty; ligate side branches; superficialise |
| Stenosis | Neointimal hyperplasia; in AVG at the venous anastomosis | PTA (percutaneous transluminal angioplasty), stent graft, surgical revision |
| Thrombosis | Usually on an underlying stenosis | Surgical or endovascular thrombectomy, then treat the stenosis |
| **Steal syndrome** | Hand ischaemia (pain, pallor, ulcers); diabetes, brachial-based access | Banding, DRIL (distal revascularisation–interval ligation), ligation |
| Aneurysm, pseudoaneurysm | Repeated needling at one site | Repair if skin is thin or bleeding |
| Infection | AVG more than AVF; CVC highest | Antibiotics; excise infected graft; remove catheter |
| Venous hypertension | Central vein stenosis | Venoplasty |
| High-output cardiac failure | Very high-flow access | Banding or ligation |

- **Also know:** ischaemic monomelic neuropathy (rare acute nerve ischaemia after brachial access, needs urgent ligation); seroma around grafts; catheter fibrin sheath.

### Monitoring and surveillance
- **Each session:** thrill and bruit, pulse augmentation, arm-elevation test (a healthy fistula collapses), prolonged bleeding after needle removal, rising venous pressure, falling Kt/V, recirculation.
- Duplex scan or access-flow measurement when these are abnormal. Treat a stenosis that causes clinical or functional change.

### Recent advances
- **Endovascular AVF:** percutaneous anastomosis by radiofrequency or thermal energy (WavelinQ, Ellipsys).
- Drug-coated balloons for stenosis; bioengineered vessels (trials).
- **CONVINCE (2023):** high-dose HDF lowered all-cause mortality compared with high-flux HD.
- Home and nocturnal HD.

**Diagram to draw:** dialyser with countercurrent blood and dialysate, and a radiocephalic end-to-side fistula at the wrist.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Diffusion clears small solutes, convection clears middle molecules, ultrafiltration removes water.
> - Kt/V minimum 1.2 (target 1.4); URR ≥65%.
> - AVF first, distal, non-dominant arm, ≥6 months ahead; rule of 6s.
> - Thrombosis usually sits on a stenosis; steal is treated by banding or DRIL.

## II-2. Discuss evaluation for urolithiasis and management of bilateral renal staghorn calculi
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Evaluation for urolithiasis
**1. Clinical**
- History: colic, haematuria, UTI (urinary tract infection), previous stones, fluid and diet (sodium, protein, oxalate). Ask about drugs (calcium, vitamins C and D, acetazolamide, steroids), GI (gastrointestinal) disease, gout, sarcoidosis and family history.
- Examination: fever, loin tenderness, signs of sepsis.

**2. Laboratory**
- Urinalysis (pH, crystals, pyuria, nitrites) and urine culture.
- Serum creatinine (eGFR, estimated glomerular filtration rate), sodium, potassium, calcium, uric acid. PTH (parathyroid hormone) if calcium is high; bicarbonate if RTA (renal tubular acidosis) is suspected.

**3. Imaging**
| Test | Role |
|---|---|
| **NCCT** (non-contrast computed tomography) | Gold standard: sensitivity 97%, specificity 95%. Gives size, volume, site, density (uric acid 200–400 HU [Hounsfield units]; calcium oxalate 600–1200 HU) and skin-to-stone distance. Misses indinavir and matrix stones |
| Low-dose NCCT (≤3 mSv) | Equal accuracy for stones ≥3 mm |
| Ultrasound | First line in pregnancy and children; follow-up |
| KUB (plain X-ray) | Follow-up of radio-opaque stones; not for acute diagnosis |
| CT urography | Collecting-system anatomy before PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) |
| MAG3 renogram or DMSA scan | Split function of a poorly functioning kidney |
| Dual-energy CT | Predicts composition (uric acid) |

**4. Stone analysis:** infrared spectroscopy or X-ray diffraction. Uric acid suggests a metabolic cause, cystine a genetic cause, struvite infection.

**5. Metabolic evaluation**
- **Screening for every first-time former:** history, serum tests, urinalysis, stone analysis.
- **High-risk formers** (recurrent, children, GI disease, obesity, diabetes, hyperparathyroidism, RTA, gout, family history, solitary kidney, large bilateral stones) need one or two **24-h urines** on a usual diet: volume, pH, calcium, oxalate, uric acid, citrate, sodium, potassium, creatinine (cystine if suspected).
- A patient with bilateral staghorns is high risk by definition.

### Staghorn calculus
- **Definition:** a branched stone filling the renal pelvis and extending into the calyces. **Partial:** pelvis and some calyces. **Complete:** pelvis and all major calyces.
- **Composition:** classically struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate) with carbonate apatite. Bacterial **urease** (Proteus, Klebsiella, Ureaplasma) splits urea into ammonia, so urine pH rises above 7.2 and struvite crystallises. Bacteria live in biofilm inside the stone. In modern series fewer than half are infection stones; calcium phosphate, cystine and uric acid also form staghorns.
- **Why treat:** untreated staghorns destroy the kidney and cause recurrent sepsis, XGP (xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis) and death. Only unfit patients are observed.

### Work-up before surgery
- NCCT with coronal and 3D views: burden, calyceal anatomy, position of colon and spleen.
- **Renal function:** eGFR plus split function (DMSA or MAG3), because both kidneys are at risk.
- **Infection:** urine culture and culture-specific antibiotics before surgery. Drain an obstructed or infected kidney first, by nephrostomy through a calyx that can later serve PCNL, or by stent. Send stone fragments for culture.
- Correct coagulopathy; group and save.
- **Guy's stone score (2011)** predicts stone-free rate:

| Grade | Stone |
|---|---|
| I | Single stone in mid or lower pole or pelvis, simple anatomy |
| II | Single upper-pole stone; multiple stones in simple anatomy; or single stone in abnormal anatomy |
| III | Multiple stones in abnormal anatomy; calyceal diverticulum; partial staghorn |
| IV | Staghorn; or any stone in spina bifida or spinal injury |

- **Also know:** STONE score, CROES nomogram, S-ReSC score.

### Principles
- **Complete clearance**, because each fragment is an infected nidus. Also eradicate infection, preserve renal function and prevent recurrence.

### Treatment options (AUA [American Urological Association] and EAU [European Association of Urology])
| Option | Role |
|---|---|
| **PCNL** | **First line.** Multiple tracts if needed. Upper-pole access reaches the pelvis, lower pole and ureteropelvic junction. Flexible nephroscopy raises clearance; second-look for residue |
| **ECIRS** (endoscopic combined intrarenal surgery) | Supine PCNL plus retrograde flexible ureteroscopy: fewer tracts, less bleeding, higher clearance |
| Staged RIRS (retrograde intrarenal surgery) | Selected partial staghorns; patients who cannot stop anticoagulants |
| SWL (shock wave lithotripsy) | Not as monotherapy; adjunct for residual fragments |
| **Anatrophic nephrolithotomy** (Boyce–Smith) | Incision along Brödel's avascular line, renal artery clamped, cold ischaemia, infundibula reconstructed. Now rare (open, laparoscopic or robotic); used when endoscopy cannot clear the stone in a reasonable number of sessions |
| **Nephrectomy** | Poorly functioning kidney (split function below about 10–20%) that is infected or painful; XGP |

### Bilateral staghorns: sequence
```
Sepsis or obstruction on either side?
  Yes -> drain (nephrostomy/stent) + antibiotics -> treat that side once sterile
  No  -> treat the better-functioning (or symptomatic) kidney first
              |
  NCCT: stone-free? -> second look if residue
              |
  Treat the other kidney after recovery
  (selected fit patients: same-session bilateral PCNL, or PCNL + contralateral RIRS)
```
- **Why better kidney first:** it secures renal function early. Some surgeons start with the simpler side to make one kidney stone-free quickly. No trial defines the order.
- **Same-session bilateral surgery:** feasible at experienced centres if the first side was short and uncomplicated. It saves an anaesthetic and hospital days but risks bilateral bleeding or obstruction. Tubeless PCNL is safe in bilateral procedures.
- Check creatinine after each side and avoid nephrotoxins. Remove a non-functioning kidney only after the other side is cleared.

### Complications of PCNL
- Bleeding (transfusion now about 1%; angioembolisation if severe), SIRS (systemic inflammatory response syndrome) and sepsis, pleural injury with supracostal access, colon, spleen or liver injury, residual stones.

### Follow-up and prevention
- NCCT for stone-free status; treat residual fragments >4 mm.
- Culture-directed antibiotics and surveillance cultures.
- 24-h urine even in struvite formers (hypercalciuria and hypocitraturia are common), then targeted therapy; urine ≥2.5 L/day.
- **AHA (acetohydroxamic acid)** 250 mg three times daily, a urease inhibitor, only when surgery is exhausted. Avoid if creatinine >2.5 mg/dL or in pregnancy; causes DVT (deep vein thrombosis) and haemolytic anaemia.

**Diagram to draw:** staghorn in the pelvicalyceal system with upper-pole PCNL tract and a retrograde flexible scope (ECIRS).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - NCCT is the gold standard (97%/95%); 24-h urine for all high-risk formers.
> - Staghorn: PCNL first line; complete clearance is the goal.
> - Bilateral: drain the infected side first, then the better kidney; same-session only in selected cases.
> - AHA 250 mg three times daily only when surgery is not possible.

## II-3a. LASERS in BPH.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Principle
- LASER (light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation) light is monochromatic and coherent, so it passes down thin fibres.
- The tissue effect depends on wavelength and **chromophore** (water or haemoglobin), which set **penetration depth**.
- About 60 °C coagulates tissue (delayed sloughing); ≥100 °C vaporises it (immediate removal).

### Lasers used in BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia)
| Laser | Wavelength | Chromophore | Penetration | Procedure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ho:YAG (holmium) | ~2120 nm | Water | 0.4 mm | **HoLEP** (enucleation) |
| Thulium (Tm:YAG, TFL [thulium fibre laser]) | 2013 / 1940 nm | Water (TFL at peak) | 0.2 mm | ThuLEP, vaporesection, vaporisation |
| KTP/LBO ("GreenLight") | 532 nm | Haemoglobin | 0.8 mm | **PVP** (photoselective vaporisation), 120–180 W |
| Diode | 940–1470 nm | Water and haemoglobin | 0.5–5 mm | Vaporisation, enucleation |
| Nd:YAG | 1064 nm | Weak | About 1 cm | Coagulation; abandoned (sloughing) |

### Enucleation versus vaporisation
| Feature | Enucleation (HoLEP, ThuLEP) | Vaporisation (PVP) |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue | Whole transition zone to the capsule, then morcellated | Removed layer by layer |
| Histology | Yes | No |
| Gland size | **Any (size-independent)** | Up to 80 cc |
| Retreatment | Rare | Higher, especially >80 cc |
| Learning curve | Steep | Shorter |

### Evidence
- **HoLEP** (about 2 J × 40–50 Hz): the only endoscopic procedure **superior to TURP** (transurethral resection of prostate) for IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score) and Qmax. Less bleeding, shorter catheter time and stay.
- **GOLIATH RCT** (randomised controlled trial; 180 W PVP vs TURP, about 270 men, ≤100 cc): PVP non-inferior for IPSS, Qmax and complications at 2 years; shorter catheter time and stay.
- **ThuLEP:** outcomes equal to HoLEP. TFL energy is absorbed about four times more by water than holmium.
- **AUA (American Urological Association):** HoLEP or ThuLEP for any size; PVP for <80 cc; HoLEP, ThuLEP or PVP for men at **higher bleeding risk**.
- Saline irrigation, so no TUR syndrome.

### Complications
- Storage symptoms and dysuria; transient stress incontinence; retrograde ejaculation (about three-quarters after HoLEP); bladder neck contracture (0–3%); stricture; capsular perforation; morcellator bladder injury.
- **Safety:** wavelength-specific eyewear for all, door signage, laser safety officer.

**Diagram to draw:** HoLEP planes: enucleated adenoma pushed into the bladder, then morcellated.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Chromophore sets penetration: thulium 0.2, holmium 0.4, KTP 0.8 mm, Nd:YAG 1 cm.
> - HoLEP is size-independent and superior to TURP.
> - GOLIATH: PVP non-inferior to TURP; best for anticoagulated men.

## II-3b. Interstitial cystitis (Bladder pain syndrome).
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definitions
- **AUA (American Urological Association):** an unpleasant sensation (pain, pressure, discomfort) perceived to be related to the bladder, with LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) for **more than 6 weeks**, without infection or another identifiable cause.
- **ESSIC (European Society for the Study of Interstitial Cystitis, 2008):** chronic (>6 months) bladder-related pain with at least one other urinary symptom (persistent urge or frequency), after confusable diseases are excluded.
- NIDDK (National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases, 1987) criteria were for research and missed about 60% of patients.

### Phenotypes
- **Hunner lesion (HL) disease, "IC":** about 20%, mainly over 50. True inflammation (lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, mast cells). Never shown to arise from non-HL disease.
- **Non-Hunner BPS (bladder pain syndrome):** bladder normal or shows glomerulations.
- **ESSIC type:** cystoscopy 1 normal, 2 glomerulations, 3 Hunner lesion; biopsy A normal, B inconclusive, C positive.
- 75–80% have pain beyond the pelvis, often with IBS (irritable bowel syndrome) or fibromyalgia.

### Pathogenesis (theories)
- Leaky GAG (glycosaminoglycan) layer; mast-cell activation; neurogenic inflammation and central sensitisation; pelvic organ cross-sensitisation; stress-axis dysfunction; small-fibre neuropathy. No infection proven.

### Evaluation (diagnosis of exclusion)
- History (**ask about ketamine**), pelvic-floor palpation, PVR (post-void residual), urinalysis, culture; cytology if risk factors.
- Voiding chart; symptom scores (O'Leary–Sant ICSI/ICPI); body map.
- **Office cystoscopy if over 50 or HL suspected;** biopsy to exclude CIS (carcinoma in situ).
- **Local anaesthetic challenge** localises pain to the bladder. Potassium sensitivity test is not recommended.
- **Also know:** urodynamics optional (low capacity, hypersensitivity); hydrodistension glomerulations are non-specific.

### Treatment (AUA 2022: phenotype-based, no fixed tiers)
| Group | Options |
|---|---|
| Behavioural | Education, avoid dietary triggers, stress management; **pelvic-floor myofascial therapy** if tender |
| Oral | **Amitriptyline** 10 mg rising to about 50 mg; hydroxyzine; cimetidine; **PPS** (pentosan polysulfate) 100 mg three times daily (**pigmentary maculopathy**: eye checks) |
| Intravesical | **DMSO** (dimethyl sulfoxide) 50%, 50 mL, 15–20 min dwell; heparin; alkalinised lidocaine |
| Procedures | **HL: fulguration and/or triamcinolone injection** (75–90% improve; repeat when needed); short low-pressure hydrodistension; onabotulinumtoxinA; sacral neuromodulation |
| Refractory HL | Ciclosporin A |
| Last resort (<10%) | Diversion with or without cystectomy; augmentation |

- **Do not offer:** long-term antibiotics, intravesical BCG (bacille Calmette–Guérin), long-term systemic steroids.

**Diagram to draw:** stellate Hunner lesion with radiating vessels.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - AUA definition: bladder pain with LUTS >6 weeks, no other cause.
> - Hunner lesion: cystoscopy over 50; fulguration or triamcinolone.
> - Amitriptyline, PPS, DMSO; potassium test obsolete.

## II-3c. Silodosin.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Pharmacology
- The **most α1A-selective** blocker (well over 100-fold vs α1B; about 50-fold vs α1D), used for LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) due to BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia).
- α1A receptors are in the prostate, bladder neck, vas and seminal vesicles; α1B in vessels; α1D in bladder and spinal cord.
- Sparing α1B gives **minimal hypotension**. Blocking α1A in the vas and seminal vesicles causes **failure of emission**.
- Onset: flow improves within 2–6 hours, symptoms within 3–4 days. Metabolised by CYP3A4.

### Dose and contraindications
| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Dose | **8 mg once daily with a meal** |
| CrCl (creatinine clearance) 30–50 mL/min | **4 mg daily** |
| Contraindicated | CrCl <30; severe liver impairment; strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g. ketoconazole) |
| Caution | Other α-blockers, PDE5 (phosphodiesterase 5) inhibitors, planned cataract surgery |

### Efficacy
- Registration trials: better than placebo for IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score) and flow; also effective in severe LUTS.
- **Non-inferior to tamsulosin 0.4 mg**, not better (European trial, 2011).
- **Greatest fall in BOOI (bladder outlet obstruction index)** of all α1-blockers.
- Does not shrink the prostate; add a 5-ARI (5α-reductase inhibitor) for large glands.

### Adverse effects
- **Anejaculation:** over a quarter of men in trials (about 14% vs 2% with tamsulosin head-to-head), yet few stop. Men with anejaculation had better responses.
- No excess hypotension in the elderly or with antihypertensives; no QT prolongation. Falls and fractures rise in men ≥66 on any α-blocker.
- **IFIS (intraoperative floppy iris syndrome):** not specifically studied but reported; warn before cataract surgery.

### Other uses
- **MET (medical expulsive therapy)** for distal ureteric stones 5–10 mm. α-blockers reduce ureteric spasm. Silodosin gave faster expulsion than tamsulosin in meta-analyses; **SUSPEND (2015)** found no overall tamsulosin benefit.
- **Also know:** before ureteroscopy (easier access); recent-onset CP/CPPS (chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome).

| Drug | Selectivity | Dose | Hypotension | Ejaculatory dysfunction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Silodosin** | α1A (highest) | 8 mg | Lowest | **Highest** |
| Tamsulosin | α1A, α1D > α1B | 0.4 mg | Low | Moderate |
| Alfuzosin | Clinically uroselective | 10 mg | Low | Lowest |
| Doxazosin, terazosin | Non-selective; titrate | 1–8 mg, 1–10 mg | Highest | Low |

**Diagram to draw:** α1A in prostate and vas, α1B in vessels, α1D in bladder.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 8 mg with a meal; 4 mg if CrCl 30–50; avoid if <30.
> - Equal to tamsulosin 0.4 mg; greatest BOOI fall.
> - Anejaculation is the signature effect; least hypotension.

## II-3d. ADPKD (Adult polycystic kidney disease)
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Genetics
- ADPKD (autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease) is the commonest inherited renal cystic disease (about 1 in 400–1000). It causes 7–15% of patients on haemodialysis. About 10% have no family history.

| Gene | Chromosome | Protein | Share | ESRD (end-stage renal disease) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **PKD1** | 16 | Polycystin-1 | ~85% | 50s |
| **PKD2** | 4 | Polycystin-2 | ~15% | 70s |

- Polycystins in the primary cilium restrain cell proliferation. PKD1 lies next to TSC2 (tuberous sclerosis).

### Clinical features (usually 30–50 years)
- **Pain** (commonest): mass effect, cyst haemorrhage, infection, stones.
- **Haematuria** in about 50%. **Stones** in 20–30% (uric acid and calcium oxalate).
- **Hypertension:** about 50% at 20–35 years with normal function; nearly all in ESRD. Stretched vessels cause ischaemia and renin release.
- **Infection:** infected cysts, mainly in women.
- **Renal failure:** function falls with kidney volume. Risk factors: PKD1, male sex, early haematuria or hypertension.
- **Extrarenal:** liver cysts (commonest; up to 90% by 50); **intracranial aneurysms 10–30%**; seminal vesicle cysts (40%); pancreatic and arachnoid cysts; mitral valve prolapse; diverticulosis.
- RCC (renal cell carcinoma) is not more common, but when it occurs it is more often bilateral and sarcomatoid.

### Diagnosis
| Age | Ravine criteria (PKD1 family) | Pei unified criteria (2009) |
|---|---|---|
| 15–29 | ≥2 cysts, one or both kidneys | ≥3 cysts in total (15–39) |
| 30–59 | ≥2 cysts in each kidney | ≥2 cysts in each kidney (40–59) |
| ≥60 | ≥4 cysts in each kidney | ≥4 cysts in each kidney |
- A young person with PKD2 can have a negative scan. Fewer than 2 cysts at ≥40 years excludes ADPKD (Pei).
- CT or MRI for extrarenal cysts and TKV (total kidney volume), which predicts progression. Genetic testing if imaging is equivocal or for a living donor.

### Management
- **Blood pressure <130/80 mmHg** with ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitor or ARB (angiotensin receptor blocker). HALT-PKD: tighter control slowed TKV growth; dual blockade added nothing.
- **Tolvaptan** (V2-receptor antagonist; lowers cyclic AMP): **TEMPO 3:4 (2012)** slowed TKV growth and function loss; **REPRISE (2017)** slowed eGFR decline in later-stage disease. Side effects: thirst, polyuria, liver toxicity (monitor liver tests).
- **Pain:** exclude infection, stone, tumour; avoid NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs). Cyst aspiration, then laparoscopic unroofing (relieves pain, does not change function).
- **Infection:** lipid-soluble antibiotics (trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, fluoroquinolones); drain if no response.
- **Haemorrhage:** usually settles with rest and hydration; embolisation or nephrectomy rarely.
- **Aneurysm screening** (MR angiography) if family history of aneurysm or bleed.
- **ESRD:** dialysis or transplant (no recurrence in the graft). **Native nephrectomy** before or at transplant for recurrent infection, bleeding, pain, suspected tumour, or no room for the graft.

**Diagram to draw:** enlarged kidneys with multiple cysts, liver cysts and berry aneurysm.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - PKD1 (chromosome 16, 85%, earlier ESRD); PKD2 (chromosome 4).
> - Ravine: <30 years ≥2 cysts; 30–59 ≥2 each kidney; ≥60 ≥4 each kidney.
> - Tolvaptan (TEMPO 3:4, REPRISE); ACE inhibitor or ARB, BP <130/80.

## II-3e. RIRS (Retrograde intrarenal surgery)
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- RIRS (retrograde intrarenal surgery) is flexible ureterorenoscopy (fURS) used to treat disease inside the kidney through the natural route. Active deflection, a working channel and the holmium laser made it practical.

### Instrument
| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Tip | 4.5–7.5 Fr, tapered (digital tips up to about 8.5 Fr) |
| Working channel | 3.6 Fr (laser fibre, basket) |
| Deflection | ≥270° both ways; lower pole needs about 140° (up to 175°) |
| Types | **Fibreoptic** (smallest, image degrades); **digital** (chip on tip, better image, not more durable); **single-use** (7.7 Fr; cheaper below about 99 cases a year) |

### Indications
- **Stones:** renal stones <2 cm; lower pole <1 cm (or >1 cm as an alternative to PCNL [percutaneous nephrolithotomy]); patients who cannot stop anticoagulants; failed SWL (shock wave lithotripsy); obesity; staged treatment of larger stones.
- **Others:** diagnosis and laser ablation of low-grade UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma); filling defects, positive cytology; endopyelotomy; calyceal diverticulum; essential haematuria; foreign bodies.

### Technique
1. Sterile urine; single-dose antibiotic.
2. Cystoscopy, **safety guidewire**, retrograde pyelogram.
3. **Access sheath** (usually 12/14 Fr) over a stiff wire: lowers intrarenal pressure and allows repeated passes, but severe ureteric injury occurs in about 1 in 8.
4. Low-pressure irrigation. **Ho:YAG (holmium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet) or thulium fibre laser**: dusting (low energy, high frequency) or fragment-and-basket. Keep power ≤10 W in the ureter and ≤25 W in the kidney (tissue damage above 43 °C).
5. Inspect the ureter on withdrawal; stent unless uncomplicated.
- **Difficult lower pole:** steep infundibulopelvic angle, long (>10 mm) or narrow (<5 mm) infundibulum. Relocate the stone to an upper calyx with a basket.

### Complications
- Sepsis about 5%; stricture about 3%; bleeding or subcapsular haematoma <1%; perforation; avulsion <1%; stent symptoms; scope damage.

### Recent advances
- Single-use digital scopes; **thulium fibre laser** and pulse-modulated (Moses) holmium (less retropulsion, finer dust); **suction and flexible navigable sheaths** (FANS); intrarenal pressure sensing; robotic flexible ureteroscopy.

**Diagram to draw:** flexible scope deflected into the lower pole calyx through an access sheath.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 3.6 Fr channel; ≥270° deflection.
> - Access sheath lowers pressure but injures the ureter in about 1 in 8.
> - Laser power ≤10 W ureter, ≤25 W kidney.

## II-3f. Renal isotope scans
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Principle
- A radiotracer, given in a tiny dose, follows a physiological process. A **gamma camera** (sodium iodide crystal, photomultiplier tubes, collimator) images the emitted photons. SPECT (single-photon emission computed tomography) gives 3D images; PET (positron emission tomography) is used for cancer.
- **⁹⁹ᵐTc (technetium-99m):** 6-hour half-life, single 140 keV photon, so it is the workhorse.

### Renal tracers
| Tracer | Handling | Study | Main uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DTPA** (diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid) | Glomerular filtration only | Dynamic | GFR (glomerular filtration rate); captopril renography; poor images in renal impairment |
| **MAG3** (mercaptoacetyltriglycine) | Tubular secretion (protein-bound), high extraction | Dynamic | **Split function and obstruction (first choice)**; transplant |
| **DMSA** (dimercaptosuccinic acid) | Filtered, then retained by proximal tubules | Static at 2 h | **Pyelonephritis and scars** |

### Diuretic renography
- **Preparation:** good hydration; catheter if outlet obstruction or neurogenic bladder; cap a nephrostomy.
- **Dose:** 5–10 mCi (185–370 MBq) intravenously.
- **Phases:** perfusion (first minute); function: **split function** from the area under each curve at 1–3 min (normal 45:55 to 55:45); drainage.
- **Furosemide** 0.5 mg/kg (adult maximum 40 mg). Timing: F+20 (classic), F+15, F0 or F−15. Always note when it was given.

| Post-furosemide T½ (half-clearance time) | Meaning |
|---|---|
| <10 min | Not obstructed |
| 10–20 min | Indeterminate |
| >20 min | Obstructed |
- **Curve types (O'Reilly):** I normal; II obstructed (rising); IIIa dilated, not obstructed (falls after furosemide); IIIb equivocal.
- **Pitfalls:** poor function, dehydration, full bladder, a very capacious pelvis.

### Other uses
- **Captopril renography (DTPA):** captopril removes angiotensin II support of GFR in the stenotic kidney. Improvement of ≥10% off ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors means renovascular hypertension is likely.
- **Transplant:** acute rejection (perfusion falls, worsens) versus delayed graft function (perfusion kept, improves); urine leak.
- **DMSA:** acute pyelonephritis defects resolve; scars persist at about 6 months.
- **Nuclear cystography** for follow-up of reflux (more sensitive, lower radiation than VCUG [voiding cystourethrogram]).
- **Also know:** bone scan (MDP, methylene diphosphonate); labelled leucocyte scans for infection; PSMA (prostate-specific membrane antigen) PET in prostate cancer.
- **Radiation:** low dose (ALARA, as low as reasonably achievable).

**Diagram to draw:** renogram curves: normal, obstructed (rising) and dilated non-obstructed (falls after furosemide).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - DTPA = filtration; MAG3 = secretion (best for obstruction); DMSA = cortex.
> - T½ <10 min not obstructed; >20 min obstructed.
> - Normal split 45–55%; furosemide 0.5 mg/kg.
