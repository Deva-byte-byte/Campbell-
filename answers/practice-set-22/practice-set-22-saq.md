## Q3a. Noonan syndrome
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and genetics
- Noonan syndrome is an **autosomal dominant RASopathy** (disorder of the RAS–MAPK [mitogen-activated protein kinase] signalling pathway) with variable expression; many cases are de novo. Incidence about 1 in 1,000–2,500.
- **Karyotype is normal** (46,XY or 46,XX). The phenotype resembles Turner syndrome, hence the old name "male Turner".
- **Mechanism:** gain-of-function variants increase RAS–MAPK signalling, which disturbs growth and organ development.

| Gene | Approximate share | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **PTPN11** (encodes SHP2 phosphatase) | About 50% | Pulmonary stenosis common |
| SOS1 | About 10% | Milder growth and learning effects |
| RAF1 | About 5–10% | Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy |
| RIT1, KRAS, others | Smaller | KRAS: more learning difficulty |

- Related RASopathies: Costello, cardiofaciocutaneous, NF-1 (neurofibromatosis type 1).

### Clinical features
- Short stature; webbed neck; hypertelorism, ptosis, low-set posteriorly rotated ears; pectus deformity.
- **Heart:** pulmonary valve stenosis (commonest), HCM (hypertrophic cardiomyopathy), septal defects.
- **Bleeding tendency:** factor XI deficiency, platelet dysfunction.
- Lymphatic dysplasia: genital (penoscrotal) lymphoedema; mild learning difficulty.

### Urological aspects
- **Cryptorchidism:** the main urological feature, in most affected boys (often bilateral).
- **Testicular dysfunction:** primary Sertoli cell dysfunction (low inhibin B, high FSH [follicle-stimulating hormone]) with poor spermatogenesis, even after orchidopexy. Leydig function is usually adequate; puberty is often delayed. Female fertility is usually normal.
- **Renal anomalies (about 10%):** mostly mild renal pelvic dilatation; also duplex system, solitary kidney, hypoplasia.
- Small excess of childhood cancers (JMML [juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia], embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma).

### Evaluation
- Clinical diagnosis (van der Burgt criteria), confirmed by gene panel; karyotype excludes Turner in girls.
- Before surgery: **echocardiography, coagulation screen and factor XI level**, anaesthetic review.
- Renal and bladder ultrasound at diagnosis.

### Management
- **Orchidopexy by 18 months** (EAU [European Association of Urology] and AUA [American Urological Association]); laparoscopic for non-palpable testes.
- Follow-up: pubertal hormones, semen analysis, fertility counselling.
- **Also know:** growth hormone for short stature; multidisciplinary cardiac and haematology care.

**Diagram to draw:** RAS–MAPK cascade with SHP2 (PTPN11) acting upstream of RAS.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Autosomal dominant, normal karyotype; PTPN11 in about half.
> - Cryptorchidism plus Sertoli dysfunction: male subfertility.
> - Check heart and clotting (factor XI) before orchidopexy.

## Q3b. Neurogenic bladder in myelodysplasia
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(prenatal and neonatal assessment also in 2016-Summer Paper 3, Q3e)

### Introduction
- Myelodysplasia (NTD [neural tube defect], mainly **myelomeningocele**) is the commonest cause of neurogenic LUT (lower urinary tract) dysfunction in children. Lesions: lumbosacral 47%, lumbar 26%, sacral 20%.
- **Bony or motor level does not predict bladder function**; the lesion can progress (re-tethering), so urodynamic testing is essential.
- About 10% of deaths in SB (spina bifida) are urological. Folic acid 400 µg daily before conception prevents 50–70% of NTDs.

### Patterns
- Detrusor: overactive (NDO, neurogenic detrusor overactivity) or underactive. Outlet: competent, incompetent or dyssynergic.
- Newborns: most have detrusor contractions; over a third are acontractile. Needle EMG (electromyography): intact sacral arc about 40%, partial denervation 25%, complete 35%.

### Evaluation (EAU [European Association of Urology] 2022)
- Renal and bladder ultrasound after birth, then serially; **UDS (urodynamics) within 3 months**; baseline DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid); creatinine (cystatin C better with low muscle mass); VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) or video-UDS for VUR (vesicoureteric reflux), which is dilating in 7%.

| Risk (McGuire 1981) | Finding |
|---|---|
| Hostile | DLPP (detrusor leak point pressure) or end-filling pressure ≥40 cm H₂O |
| Intermediate | NDO, poor compliance, 25–39 cm H₂O |
| Safe | Below 25 cm H₂O |

### Management (kidneys first, then continence)
- **Early CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation; Lapides 1971) plus oxybutynin** for hostile bladders. Hydronephrosis 0–15% versus 18–80% expectant; ESRD (end-stage renal disease) 0–1.6% versus about 30%.
- Others: solifenacin, mirabegron; **botulinum toxin A** about 10 U/kg (maximum 360 U), continence 65–87%.
- Surgery: vesicostomy (bridge); **augmentation cystoplasty**; Mitrofanoff channel; bladder-neck sling, reconstruction or artificial sphincter.
- VUR: CIC and antimuscarinic first; avoid Credé; reimplant during augmentation if grade ≥III.
- **Also know:** latex allergy; bowel programme; sexual dysfunction (about 75% of men have ED [erectile dysfunction]; sildenafil helps); precocious puberty.

**Diagram to draw:** UDS trace with DLPP above 40 cm H₂O.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Level does not predict bladder: UDS within 3 months.
> - DLPP ≥40 cm H₂O = hostile (McGuire 1981).
> - Early CIC plus antimuscarinic protects kidneys.

## Q3c. Eponymous techniques of VVF repair
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(management of VVF also in Practice Set 3 Paper 3, Q2)

### Introduction
- VVF (vesicovaginal fistula): an epithelialised bladder–vagina tract. **Principles:** exclude ureteric involvement and cancer; tension-free, multilayer, non-overlapping, watertight closure; interposition when needed; drainage ≥10–14 days. **The first repair has the best chance.**

### Transvaginal techniques
- **Sims:** first successful systematic vaginal repair (silver wire).
- **Flap-splitting (Raz)**:
  - Stent ureters if close; Foley through the tract for traction.
  - J or U incision with a 1–3 mm cuff; raise anterior and posterior vaginal flaps.
  - Layer 1: bladder (3-0 or 4-0), with the tract edge. Layer 2: perpendicular perivesical layer. Fill test 200–300 mL.
  - Optional Martius or peritoneal flap; advance the opposite vaginal flap ≥2 cm beyond. Closure ≥90%.
- **Latzko (1942), high partial colpocleisis**:
  - Denude vaginal epithelium 1–2 cm around the tract; the bladder is **not opened or dissected**.
  - Close vaginal walls over the tract in layers.
  - ≥90% success in non-irradiated post-hysterectomy apical VVF; favoured near ureters or after radiation. Drawback: **vaginal shortening and dyspareunia**.
- **Also know:** vaginal cuff excision (all apical scar excised as an inverted cone).

### Abdominal technique
- **O'Conor and Sokol (1951)**, suprapubic extraperitoneal:
  - Midline cystotomy at the dome, **bivalved down** to the fistula; ureteric catheters.
  - Excise the tract; separate bladder from vagina 2–3 cm beyond.
  - Close vagina; omentum or peritoneum between; close bladder in layers; suprapubic catheter.
- Modifications: limited-cystotomy transvesical; extravesical. Laparoscopic (1994) and robotic (2005) versions give equal closure with less morbidity.

### Interposition flaps
| Flap | Blood supply | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Martius (1928)**, labial fibrofatty pad | Internal pudendal (inferior), external pudendal (superior) | Mid–distal VVF; does not reach the apex |
| Peritoneal | Local | High apical VVF |
| Omental J-flap | Gastroepiploic | Abdominal, radiation |
| Gracilis, Singapore | Myocutaneous | Refractory cases |
- RCT (randomised controlled trial): Martius adds nothing in simple VVF.

**Diagram to draw:** Latzko denuded area versus flap-splitting layers.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Latzko: colpocleisis, bladder not opened, vagina shortened.
> - O'Conor: bivalve bladder to the fistula, interpose omentum.
> - Martius for distal, omentum or peritoneum for apical VVF.

## Q3d. Endometriosis in urology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and epidemiology
- Endometriosis is endometrial glands and stroma outside the uterus. Urinary tract involvement occurs in about 1–5% of women with endometriosis, mostly with deep disease.
- Sites: **bladder** (commonest, about 80%) > ureter (about 15%) > kidney, urethra (rare).

### Pathogenesis
- Retrograde menstruation (Sampson), coelomic metaplasia, lymphovascular spread, and **iatrogenic implantation** at pelvic surgery (e.g. caesarean section).
- Bladder lesions start on the **serosa** (vesicouterine pouch) and invade the detrusor.
- Ureteric disease: **extrinsic** (periureteric fibrosis, commoner) or intrinsic (muscularis or mucosa); usually distal, left more often.

### Clinical features
- **Bladder:** cyclical haematuria or frequency, urgency and dysuria with menses; suprapubic pain. Differential: BPS (bladder pain syndrome), vesicouterine fistula (menouria).
- **Ureter:** often **silent**; hydronephrosis and loss of renal function may go unnoticed.

### Evaluation
- Transvaginal and transabdominal ultrasound gauge size and depth; **renal ultrasound in every woman with deep endometriosis**.
- **MRI (magnetic resonance imaging):** extent; high T1 signal from blood products.
- **Cystoscopy:** limited value (disease is extravesical); may show a bluish submucosal lesion. Biopsy excludes carcinoma.
- CTU (computed tomography urography) and MAG3 (mercaptoacetyltriglycine) renogram for obstruction and split function.
- Staging: rASRM (revised American Society for Reproductive Medicine) I–IV; #Enzian records bladder and ureter.

### Management (with gynaecologist)
- **Medical:** progestins (dienogest), combined pill, GnRH (gonadotrophin-releasing hormone) analogues. Symptomatic relief; not for obstructive ureteric disease.
- **Bladder:** **partial cystectomy** (full-thickness excision with margin), laparoscopic or robotic; stent ureters if near. TUR (transurethral resection) is incomplete, with recurrence and perforation risk.
- **Ureter:**
  - Extrinsic, mild: ureterolysis.
  - Intrinsic or stricture: segmental resection with ureteroureterostomy, or **reimplantation with psoas hitch or Boari flap**.
  - Non-functioning kidney: nephrectomy.
- Treat all pelvic disease at the same operation to lower recurrence.
- **Also know:** risk factor for VVF (vesicovaginal fistula) and ureteric injury at hysterectomy; rare malignant change (clear-cell, endometrioid).

**Diagram to draw:** endometriotic nodule in the vesicouterine pouch invading the detrusor.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Bladder commonest; cyclical haematuria.
> - Ureteric disease silent: screen kidneys.
> - Partial cystectomy, not TUR.

## Q3e. Hypospadias repairs – single-stage versus staged techniques
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(present concepts also in Practice Set 6 Paper 4, Q1)

### Introduction
- Choice depends on **curvature after degloving**, plate quality and glans size, not on meatal position alone.
- Algorithm: deglove, artificial erection. Straight or <30°: dorsal plication, single-stage. >30°: divide plate, ventral corporotomy ± graft, then staged.

### Single-stage techniques
| Group | Examples | Indication |
|---|---|---|
| Advancement | **MAGPI** (meatal advancement and glanuloplasty, Duckett 1981); MIV (M inverted V) | Glanular meatus, no curvature |
| Tubularisation | **TIP** (tubularised incised plate, Snodgrass 1994); Thiersch–Duplay; GAP (glans approximation procedure) | Distal–midshaft; TIP in >90% of distal |
| Flap | Mathieu (flip-flap); **OIF** (onlay island flap) | Thin plate |
| Proximal one-stage | TPIF (transverse preputial island flap, Duckett tube); proximal OIF | Curvature corrected; good tissue |
| Graft | Dorsal buccal inlay | Narrow plate |
- Pros: one anaesthetic. Proximal TIP is abandoned: devascularised plate, stricture, recurrent curvature.

### Staged techniques
- Now the approach of choice for most proximal hypospadias. **Rationale:** a urethroplasty over a fresh corporal graft fails; stage 1 builds a supple, vascular bed.

| Type | Stage 1 | Stage 2 (≥6 months) |
|---|---|---|
| **Bracka / STAG** (staged tubularised autograft) | Correct curvature, divide plate, quilt inner preputial or buccal graft | Tubularise, glansplasty, tunica vaginalis cover |
| **Byars flaps** | Split dorsal prepuce, transpose ventrally | Tubularise |
| Distal-first (Ulaanbaatar) | Curvature and distal urethra | Join proximal urethra |
- Repeat artificial erection before stage 2.

### Comparison of outcomes
| Measure | Single-stage | Staged |
|---|---|---|
| Distal success | 83–95% | – |
| Proximal complications | About 60% (long TIP, mixed) | STAG about 23%; Byars 43–81% |
| Recurrent curvature | Higher after plication | 0–10% |
| Penile length, perception | Lower | Better |
| Extra procedures | Fewer planned | 25–75% need more |
- Fistula is the commonest complication. Long, rigid neourethra without spongiosum raises resistance (Poiseuille's law), causing fistula and diverticulum.

**Diagram to draw:** STAG stage 1 graft and stage 2 tubularisation.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Curvature >30° after degloving: stage.
> - TIP for distal; STAG has lowest proximal complications.
> - Stage 2 after ≥6 months.

## Q3f. Instestinal segments in urology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2022-Summer Paper 1, Q3a)

*Assumed meaning: intestinal segments used in urinary diversion and bladder reconstruction.*

### Introduction
- Bowel is used for conduits, continent pouches, neobladders, augmentation and ureteric replacement. Choice depends on renal function, irradiation, previous surgery, mesenteric length and the reconstruction needed.

### Segments compared

| Segment | Electrolyte disturbance | Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stomach | Hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis | Secretes acid, less mucus; for renal failure, short gut | Haematuria–dysuria syndrome, ulcers |
| Jejunum | Hyponatraemic, hypochloraemic, **hyperkalaemic** acidosis | Outside radiation field | Severe salt loss; avoid |
| Ileum | **Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis** | Mobile, constant supply; default conduit | B12 and bile-salt loss; obstruction ~10% |
| Colon | Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis | Easy antireflux tunnel; transverse colon spared by pelvic radiation | Diarrhoea if ileocaecal valve used |

- **Mechanism (ileum, colon):** NH₄⁺ replaces Na⁺ on the Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger, coupled with Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchange, so ammonium chloride is absorbed. Treat with sodium bicarbonate or citrate (potassium citrate if hypokalaemic); chlorpromazine or nicotinic acid limit chloride transport.

### Other complications
- **Hypokalaemia** (worse with colon); correct potassium before acidosis.
- **Altered sensorium:** hyperammonaemia (drain diversion, lactulose), hypomagnesaemia, drug reabsorption (phenytoin, methotrexate).
- **Osteomalacia:** acidosis, vitamin D resistance, calcium loss; growth retardation in children.
- **Infection, stones** (ileal conduit 10–12%, continent caecal reservoir ~20%), short-gut effects.
- **Cancer:** ureterosigmoidostomy 6–29% after 10–20 years; endoscopy from 5 years.

### Renal function needed for continent diversion
- Creatinine <2 mg/dL; GFR (glomerular filtration rate) >35 mL/min; urine pH ≤5.8 after ammonium chloride load; osmolality ≥600 mOsm/kg.

### Technical principles
- Clear ≤8 cm of mesentery from a segment end; avoid watershed areas (Griffiths, Sudeck points).
- **Also know:** ERAS (enhanced recovery after surgery) with no routine mechanical bowel preparation; Wallace (stricture ~3%) and Bricker anastomoses; nipple stoma.

**Diagram to draw:** ileal loop with mesenteric arcade and Wallace anastomosis.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Ileum and colon: hyperchloraemic acidosis (NH₄Cl absorption).
> - Jejunum: hyperkalaemia; stomach: hypochloraemic alkalosis.
> - Continent diversion: GFR >35, pH ≤5.8, osmolality ≥600.
