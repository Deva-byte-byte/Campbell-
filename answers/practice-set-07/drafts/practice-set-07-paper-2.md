## Q1. How will you evaluate and manage 38 yr male with uncontrolled hypertension
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(renovascular hypertension also answered in Practice Set 2 Paper 2, Q1; phaeochromocytoma in Practice Set 6 Paper 1, Q1)

*Assumed focus: secondary (urological, renal and adrenal) causes of hypertension in a young adult.*

### Introduction
- **Resistant hypertension:** BP (blood pressure) above goal despite 3 drugs of different classes, including a diuretic, at optimal doses; or needing 4 or more drugs.
- Onset or poor control below 40 years is a classic trigger to search for a **secondary cause**. Many are curable (adenoma, phaeochromocytoma, renal artery stenosis, obstruction).

### Causes
| Group | Causes | Clues |
|---|---|---|
| Renovascular | FMD (fibromuscular dysplasia), atherosclerotic RAS (renal artery stenosis), Takayasu arteritis, reninoma | Bruit; creatinine rise >30% on ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitor; flash pulmonary oedema; kidneys differing >1.5 cm |
| Renal parenchymal | CKD (chronic kidney disease), ADPKD (autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease), reflux nephropathy, glomerulonephritis, Page kidney | Raised creatinine, proteinuria, haematuria |
| Obstructive uropathy | Bilateral obstruction (about 77% hypertensive), unilateral (about 20%), chronic retention | LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms), palpable bladder, hydronephrosis |
| Primary aldosteronism (PA) | Adenoma (Conn), bilateral hyperplasia | Hypokalaemia (present in <40% only) |
| Phaeochromocytoma, paraganglioma | Sporadic or hereditary | Paroxysmal headache, sweating, palpitations |
| Cushing syndrome | Exogenous steroid, adrenal adenoma, pituitary | Central obesity, striae, diabetes |
- **Also know:** sleep apnoea; drugs (NSAIDs [non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs], steroids, decongestants, liquorice); alcohol; thyroid and parathyroid disease; coarctation.

### Evaluation
**Step 1: Exclude pseudo-resistance**
- Correct cuff technique; home or ambulatory BP monitoring to exclude white-coat effect; check adherence, doses and interfering drugs; salt and alcohol intake.

**Step 2: History and examination**
- Paroxysms, weakness or polyuria (hypokalaemia), stones, UTI (urinary tract infection) or childhood reflux, snoring, family history.
- BP both arms and radio-femoral delay; abdominal bruit; palpable kidneys or bladder; cushingoid habitus; café-au-lait spots (NF1 [neurofibromatosis type 1]); fundus.

**Step 3: Baseline tests and target-organ damage**
- Creatinine, eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate), electrolytes, urinalysis with albumin–creatinine ratio, glucose, lipids, ECG (electrocardiogram), echocardiography.
- **Ultrasound KUB (kidneys, ureters, bladder):** renal size and asymmetry, scars, cysts, masses, hydronephrosis, residual urine.

**Step 4: Targeted screening (method and interpretation)**
| Cause | Screen | Confirm or localise |
|---|---|---|
| PA | Morning **ARR (aldosterone-to-renin ratio)**: >20–30 with aldosterone >15 ng/dL; stop spironolactone first; correct potassium | **IV saline 2 L over 4 h** (aldosterone stays >5 ng/dL) or oral salt loading → adrenal CT → **AVS (adrenal vein sampling)**; skip AVS if age <40 with a clear unilateral adenoma >1 cm |
| Phaeochromocytoma | **Plasma free or 24-h urinary fractionated metanephrines** | CT or MRI (magnetic resonance imaging); ⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE PET (positron emission tomography) or MIBG (metaiodobenzylguanidine); genetic testing |
| Cushing | **1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression** (cortisol ≤1.8 µg/dL excludes); late-night salivary cortisol; 24-h urinary free cortisol | ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone): low = adrenal, high = pituitary or ectopic |
| Renovascular | **Duplex Doppler**: peak systolic velocity >180–200 cm/s, renal/aortic ratio >3.5 | CT or MR angiography; catheter angiography with pressure gradient if intervention planned |
| Parenchymal, obstructive | Ultrasound, urine tests | Renogram (DTPA [diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid] or MAG3 [mercaptoacetyltriglycine]) for split function and drainage; CT urography |

```
Uncontrolled BP, age 38 → exclude pseudo-resistance
 → baseline tests + US KUB
 → ARR, metanephrines, dexamethasone test, renal Doppler
    ├─ positive → confirm → localise → treat cause
    └─ all negative → essential: optimise drugs
```

### Management
**1. General (all patients)**
- Lifestyle: salt restriction, weight loss, exercise, limit alcohol.
- Optimise **ACE inhibitor or ARB (angiotensin receptor blocker) + calcium channel blocker + thiazide-like diuretic**. Fourth drug: **spironolactone** (PATHWAY-2, 2015: best add-on in resistant hypertension).

**2. Cause-specific**
| Cause | Treatment |
|---|---|
| PA, lateralised | **Laparoscopic adrenalectomy**: biochemical cure about 94%, but only 37–52% need no antihypertensives |
| PA, bilateral or unfit | Spironolactone 25–50 mg/day (up to 400 mg) or eplerenone |
| Phaeochromocytoma | **α-blockade 7–14 days**, β-blocker only after α, volume expansion → laparoscopic adrenalectomy; metanephrines yearly for life |
| Adrenal Cushing | Adrenalectomy with steroid cover; pituitary disease → trans-sphenoidal surgery |
| FMD | **Angioplasty alone** (cure common in the young) |
| Atherosclerotic RAS | Medical therapy (**ASTRAL 2009, CORAL 2014**: stenting no benefit); stent for flash pulmonary oedema or falling function |
| Branch disease, aneurysm | Surgical bypass or ex vivo repair with autotransplantation |
| Reninoma | Partial nephrectomy (cures most) |
| Small non-functioning kidney (reflux, segmental hypoplasia) | **Nephrectomy** if renal vein renin lateralises (ratio >1.5) |
| Page kidney | Drain or decapsulate |
| Obstruction | **Relieve it** (catheter, nephrostomy, pyeloplasty, prostate surgery); watch post-obstructive diuresis |
| CKD | ACE inhibitor or ARB for proteinuria; scheduled diuretics |

**3. Follow-up:** home BP, creatinine and potassium; duplex for restenosis after angioplasty.

### Recent advances
- **Catheter renal denervation** (radiofrequency or ultrasound) approved in 2023 for selected resistant hypertension.
- SPARTACUS trial questioned routine AVS (no difference in drug needs at 1 year).

**Diagram to draw:** stepwise algorithm from pseudo-resistance to cause-specific treatment.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Hypertension below 40 years: screen for secondary causes.
> - ARR → saline suppression → CT → AVS (skip if <40 with adenoma >1 cm).
> - Metanephrines for phaeochromocytoma; α before β.
> - FMD: angioplasty; atherosclerotic RAS: medical (ASTRAL, CORAL).

## Q2. Describe etiopathogenesis of stone formation and medical management of urolithiasis
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q2)

### Introduction
- Urolithiasis is a **chronic metabolic disease** punctuated by stone episodes; recurrence is up to 50% within 5 years. Removal is surgical; recurrence is metabolic.
- **Composition**: calcium oxalate about 60%, mixed with apatite about 20%, uric acid and struvite about 7% each, cystine 1–3%.

### Aetiology: risk factors
- **Epidemiological**: male sex (gap narrowing), hot climate and occupation (low urine volume), diet (animal protein, salt), family history.
- **Systemic**: **metabolic syndrome**, obesity, diabetes (insulin resistance lowers urine pH), gout, primary hyperparathyroidism, RTA (renal tubular acidosis), bowel disease or bypass, sarcoidosis.
- **Anatomical**: PUJ (pelviureteric junction) obstruction, horseshoe kidney, calyceal diverticulum, medullary sponge kidney (stasis plus metabolic defects).
- **Drugs**: direct (indinavir, triamterene); indirect (acetazolamide, topiramate, vitamin C and D excess).

### Physicochemistry: state of saturation
| State | Concentration product (CP) | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Undersaturated | < Ksp (solubility product) | No crystals; stones may dissolve |
| **Metastable** | Between Ksp and Kf (formation product) | No spontaneous nucleation; growth on existing crystals; inhibitors work. **Zone of therapy** |
| Unstable | > Kf | Spontaneous nucleation, growth, aggregation |

- Crystals form in metastable urine by local high CP, stasis, or **heterogeneous nucleation** on cells, debris or other crystals (needs less energy).

### Steps of stone formation
1. **Supersaturation** (low volume, high calcium, oxalate, uric acid; low citrate).
2. **Nucleation**, then **growth** and **aggregation** (aggregation can occlude a tubule within the 5–7 minute nephron transit).
3. **Retention**: fixed-particle theory. **Randall plaque** (calcium apatite) starts in the basement membrane of the thin loop of Henle, spreads through the interstitium, erodes the papillary urothelium and anchors calcium oxalate growth (idiopathic formers). **Ductal plugs** (crystals in collecting ducts) suggest a secondary cause (enteric hyperoxaluria, distal RTA, hyperparathyroidism, cystinuria).

### Inhibitors, promoters, matrix
- **Inhibitors**: **citrate** (complexes calcium), **magnesium** (complexes oxalate), pyrophosphate, glycosaminoglycans, nephrocalcin, **Tamm-Horsfall protein**, osteopontin, bikunin. There is **no inhibitor of uric acid**.
- **Matrix** (about 2.5% of stone weight; matrix substance A): role uncertain.

### Pathogenesis by abnormality
| Abnormality | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Hypercalciuria** (commonest) | Absorptive (gut), renal leak (high PTH (parathyroid hormone)), resorptive (primary hyperparathyroidism) |
| Hyperoxaluria | Primary (hepatic enzyme defect, PH1 AGXT), **enteric** (fat binds calcium, freeing oxalate), dietary |
| Hyperuricosuria | Urate promotes calcium oxalate nucleation |
| **Hypocitraturia** | Distal RTA, diarrhoea, hypokalaemia, animal protein (intracellular acidosis) |
| **Uric acid** | **Low urine pH** (<5.5; pKa 5.5) more than excess uric acid |
| **Cystine** | Autosomal recessive defect in renal cystine reabsorption |
| **Struvite** | **Urease** (Proteus) splits urea → ammonia, pH >7.2 → magnesium ammonium phosphate + carbonate apatite; biofilm |

### Medical management
**1. Acute colic and MET (medical expulsive therapy)**
- **NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs)** first line; opioids only if refractory.
- **α-blocker** (tamsulosin; silodosin faster) for **distal ureteric stones 5–10 mm**: relaxes ureteric muscle. **SUSPEND (2015)** was negative because it included all sizes and sites. Trial of passage 4–6 weeks.

**2. Dissolution**: uric acid stones (radiolucent) dissolve with oral potassium citrate (EAU (European Association of Urology): pH 7.0–7.2 for dissolution).

**3. Prevention: general (every former)**
- **Fluid** for urine **≥2.5 L/day** (Borghi 1996: 12.1% vs 27% recurrence at 5 years).
- **Normal calcium**, low sodium, less animal protein, more fruit and vegetables (Borghi 2002: halved recurrence versus low-calcium diet).

**4. Metabolic evaluation** (high-risk and recurrent formers): stone analysis, serum chemistry, one or two **24-h urines**; repeat within 6 months of therapy, then yearly.

**5. Selective drug therapy**
| Abnormality | Drug and dose | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Hypercalciuria | **Thiazide**: hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg twice daily, chlorthalidone 25–50 mg, indapamide 2.5 mg | Distal calcium reabsorption; add potassium citrate (hypokalaemia → hypocitraturia) |
| Hypocitraturia, distal RTA | **Potassium citrate 30–60 mEq/day** | Alkali load reduces tubular citrate reabsorption |
| Hyperuricosuria | Allopurinol 300 mg/day | Watch hypersensitivity |
| Uric acid stones | Potassium citrate to **pH 6.0–6.5** | pH disease |
| Enteric hyperoxaluria | **Calcium with meals**, low oxalate and fat, magnesium | Binds gut oxalate |
| Primary hyperoxaluria type 1 | Pyridoxine; **lumasiran** (RNA (ribonucleic acid) interference) | ILLUMINATE-A |
| Cystinuria | Urine ≥3 L, pH **7.0–7.5**; **tiopronin** | Cystine <250 mg/L; mixed disulfide |
| Struvite | Complete clearance; AHA (acetohydroxamic acid) 250 mg three times daily | Urease inhibitor; thromboembolism |

- **Also know:** recurrent calcium stones with normal work-up: thiazide or citrate (AUA (American Urological Association)); NOSTONE (2023): hydrochlorothiazide did not reduce recurrence; citrus juices are adjuncts.

**Diagram to draw:** saturation zones (Ksp, Kf) and Randall plaque on a papilla.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Metastable zone between Ksp and Kf is where inhibitors and therapy act.
> - Randall plaque = interstitial apatite anchor; ductal plugs suggest secondary cause.
> - Urine ≥2.5 L; MET for distal 5–10 mm stones.
> - Uric acid pH 6.0–6.5; cystine pH 7.0–7.5, <250 mg/L.

## Q3a. Discuss management of T1G3 bladder tumor
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 2, Q1)

### Introduction
- T1G3: high-grade urothelial carcinoma invading the lamina propria, not muscle. Every high-grade T1 is **high risk** of NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer): recurrence >80%, progression 30–50%.
- **Adverse factors:** concurrent CIS (carcinoma in situ), LVI (lymphovascular invasion), variant histology, size >3 cm, multifocality, prostatic urethral involvement, residual T1 at re-resection.

### Step 1: Complete TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour)
- Resect all tumour **with detrusor muscle**; en bloc resection where possible; blue-light or NBI (narrow-band imaging) for CIS; prostatic urethral biopsy if indicated. CT urography for the upper tracts.

### Step 2: Re-TURBT at 2–6 weeks (all T1)
- Residual tumour in ≥40%; upstaging to muscle invasion in 25–30%. Improves recurrence- and progression-free survival.

```
HG T1 → re-TURBT
   ├─ ≥T2                → neoadjuvant chemotherapy + cystectomy
   ├─ very high risk     → offer early radical cystectomy
   └─ T0, Ta, CIS, T1    → BCG induction + 3-year maintenance
```

### Step 3: BCG (bacille Calmette-Guérin) – standard
- Triggers a **Th1 (T-helper 1)** immune response; the **only intravesical agent that reduces progression**.
- Induction weekly ×6, 2–4 weeks after TURBT. **Maintenance (SWOG [Southwest Oncology Group], Lamm 2000)**: 3 weekly doses at 3 and 6 months, then 6-monthly to **3 years**.
- Avoid with gross haematuria, traumatic catheterisation or UTI (urinary tract infection). BCG sepsis: antituberculous drugs plus steroids.

### Step 4: Early radical cystectomy
- Clinical T1 is understaged in one-third to one-half; those who progress do worse than primary muscle-invasive disease.
- Offer for **very high risk** (EAU [European Association of Urology]): T1 HG with CIS plus risk factors (age >70, multiple tumours, ≥3 cm), LVI, variant histology, prostatic urethral CIS, persistent T1 at re-TURBT.

### Step 5: BCG-unresponsive disease
- HG T1 at 3 months, or recurrence within 6 months (T1) or 12 months (CIS) of adequate BCG.
- **Radical cystectomy**; if unfit: pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-057), nadofaragene firadenovec, gemcitabine–docetaxel, trials.

### Follow-up
- Cystoscopy and cytology 3-monthly for 2 years, 6-monthly to 5 years, then yearly; upper tracts yearly.

**Diagram to draw:** bladder wall layers with Ta, T1 and T2.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Re-TURBT in all T1 at 2–6 weeks.
> - BCG with 3-year maintenance reduces progression.
> - Very high risk or BCG-unresponsive: radical cystectomy.

## Q3b. Organ preservation solution
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Assumed context: preservation of the kidney for transplantation.*

### Principles
- **Hypothermia (4 °C)** slows metabolism to roughly a tenth. It does not stop it, so injury still accumulates with time.
- Ischaemia causes ATP (adenosine triphosphate) depletion, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase failure, **cell swelling**, anaerobic glycolysis with **acidosis**, and calcium influx. At reperfusion, hypoxanthine is converted by xanthine oxidase to **free radicals** (ischaemia–reperfusion injury). Clinically this appears as **DGF (delayed graft function)**.
- A solution therefore needs:
  - **Impermeants** (lactobionate, raffinose, mannitol, glucose) to prevent cell swelling.
  - **Buffers** (phosphate, histidine) against acidosis.
  - **Colloid** (hydroxyethyl starch, polyethylene glycol) against interstitial oedema.
  - **Antioxidants and energy precursors** (glutathione, allopurinol, adenosine).
  - Electrolytes: "intracellular" (high K⁺, low Na⁺) or "extracellular" (low K⁺, high Na⁺) type.

### Solutions
| Solution | Type and key components | Points |
|---|---|---|
| **UW (University of Wisconsin, Belzer)** | Intracellular; lactobionate, raffinose, hydroxyethyl starch, glutathione, adenosine, allopurinol | Gold standard for static storage; viscous; high K⁺ needs flushing before reperfusion |
| **HTK (histidine–tryptophan–ketoglutarate, Custodiol)** | Low K⁺; histidine buffer, tryptophan (membrane stabiliser), ketoglutarate (substrate), mannitol | Low viscosity, fast cooling; needs a large volume; similar outcomes to UW |
| Euro-Collins | Intracellular; glucose impermeant, phosphate | First-generation; inferior for long storage; largely abandoned |
| Celsior | Extracellular; lactobionate, mannitol, glutathione, histidine | Designed for heart; used for abdominal organs |
| IGL-1 (Institut Georges Lopez) | UW-like, but polyethylene glycol and reversed Na⁺/K⁺ | Extracellular; less viscous |
- **Also know:** Marshall hypertonic citrate (UK); cold lactated Ringer for living donation when cold ischaemia is very short.

### Methods
- **Static cold storage:** in situ aortic flush with cold solution, IVC (inferior vena cava) venting, ice slush; kidney stored at 4 °C. Aim cold ischaemia <24 hours (thrombosis and DGF rise beyond).
- **Hypothermic machine perfusion:** pulsatile cold perfusion; flow and resistance help assess viability. Reduces DGF; **Moers (2009)**: DGF 20.8% vs 26.5%, 1-year graft survival 94% vs 90%. The chapter notes no difference in long-term survival. Used especially for DCD (donation after circulatory death) kidneys.
- **Normothermic perfusion:** warm oxygenated perfusate allows assessment and repair; limited clinical experience.

**Diagram to draw:** ischaemia–reperfusion cascade with the solution component that blocks each step.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Hypothermia slows, not stops, metabolism.
> - UW: lactobionate, raffinose, starch; HTK: histidine, tryptophan, ketoglutarate.
> - Machine perfusion reduces DGF.

## Q3c. Intra op mgt of pheo.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(preoperative management also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 2, Q3c; perioperative in Practice Set 6 Paper 1, Q1)

### Why it is difficult
- Phaeochromocytoma surgery is a **two-phase haemodynamic problem**: catecholamine **surge** while the tumour is handled, then catecholamine **withdrawal** once the vein is tied (hypotension, hypoglycaemia).
- Surges occur at intubation, positioning, pneumoperitoneum and tumour manipulation.

### Prerequisites
- **α-blockade 7–14 days** (phenoxybenzamine or doxazosin), β-blocker only after α, volume expansion; ECG (electrocardiogram) and echocardiography; joint plan with the anaesthetist.

### Monitoring
- **Arterial line before induction**, central venous line, ECG, urine output, frequent glucose; cardiac output or transoesophageal echo if cardiomyopathy.

### Anaesthesia
- Deep, smooth induction (propofol, opioid such as fentanyl or remifentanil); attenuate intubation response.
- **Avoid triggers:** histamine-releasing drugs (morphine, atracurium), ketamine, ephedrine, metoclopramide, droperidol, desflurane.
- Sevoflurane or isoflurane; vecuronium or rocuronium; magnesium sulphate as an adjunct (reduces catecholamine release and arrhythmia).

### Haemodynamic control
| Event | Action |
|---|---|
| **Hypertensive surge** | **Pause manipulation**; nitroprusside, nicardipine or clevidipine, nitroglycerin, phentolamine |
| **Tachyarrhythmia** | **Esmolol**, lidocaine; amiodarone if needed |
| **Hypotension after vein ligation** | **Aggressive volume**, then α-agonist (phenylephrine, noradrenaline); vasopressin if refractory |
| **Hypoglycaemia** (insulin rebound) | Dextrose infusion, glucose checks |

### Surgical principles
- **Laparoscopic adrenalectomy** (transperitoneal or posterior retroperitoneoscopic) is standard; open for large or invasive tumours.
- **Minimal handling**; "no-touch" dissection away from the tumour.
- **Early adrenal vein ligation** where possible, after warning the anaesthetist.
- Remove intact in a bag (avoid capsule rupture and seeding).
- **Cortical-sparing** (partial) adrenalectomy in MEN2 (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2), VHL (von Hippel–Lindau) and bilateral tumours.
- Steroid cover if bilateral adrenalectomy.

### Postoperative
- HDU (high dependency unit): BP, heart rate, glucose; metanephrines after surgery, then yearly for life (recurrence up to 16%).

**Diagram to draw:** intraoperative BP curve: surges during handling, fall after vein ligation, with drugs marked.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Arterial line before induction; avoid histamine releasers and ephedrine.
> - Surge: pause handling, nitroprusside or phentolamine; esmolol for tachycardia.
> - After vein ligation: fluids, vasopressors, glucose.

## Q3d. Saturation biopsy in CA prostate
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2016-Summer Paper 4, Q3a)

### Definition
- Systematic prostate biopsy with **more cores than the extended 12-core scheme**, usually **18–21 or more** (often 20–30), sampling the peripheral zone, transition zone, anterior gland and apex.
- Route: transrectal, or **transperineal template mapping biopsy** through a brachytherapy grid with a core every **5 mm**.

### Rationale
- Sextant biopsy (Hodge, 1989) under-sampled the lateral peripheral zone; the 12-core scheme is today's standard.
- Saturation reduces sampling error, mainly for **anterior and apical cancers** missed transrectally.

### Indications
- **Repeat biopsy** after a negative biopsy with persistent suspicion: rising PSA (prostate-specific antigen), ASAP (atypical small acinar proliferation), negative or unavailable MRI (magnetic resonance imaging).
- **Active surveillance** confirmation; **focal therapy** mapping (shows multifocality; TRUS [transrectal ultrasound] biopsy misses almost half of disease relevant to hemiablation).

### Templates
| Template | Division | Cores |
|---|---|---|
| Original Barzell | Octants (anterior/posterior × apex/base, each side) | 4–8 per octant |
| Modified Barzell | Apex and base of each side × 5 sectors | 20 |
| Ginsburg | Anterior, mid, posterior sectors per side | 4 per sector |
- Lithotomy, stepper and grid; general or spinal anaesthesia, or periprostatic block (up to 22 mL of 1% lidocaine).

### Results
- **Initial biopsy**: 12 to 18–21 cores did not raise detection.
- **Repeat setting**: early series gained; a later large prospective study did not. Extra cancers are often insignificant (overdiagnosis).
- **PROMIS (2017)** used 5-mm template mapping as the reference: mpMRI (multiparametric MRI) sensitivity 93% vs TRUS biopsy 48%.

### Complications
- More haematuria, haematospermia, pain and **urinary retention**; anaesthesia and theatre time. Transperineal route has less infection.

### Current position
- Largely replaced by **mpMRI with targeted biopsy**: **PRECISION (2018)** detected more significant cancer (38% vs 26%) and 28% avoided biopsy.
- Still used for MRI-negative high suspicion and focal therapy planning.

**Diagram to draw:** transperineal grid over the prostate with sampling sectors.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Saturation = >12 cores (18–21 or more); 5-mm template mapping.
> - No gain at initial biopsy; used after negatives and for focal therapy.
> - PROMIS: template mapping as reference; mpMRI sensitivity 93%.

## Q3e. Steinstresse.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Assumed meaning: steinstrasse.*

### Definition
- **Steinstrasse** (German, "stone street"): a column of stone fragments in the ureter that fails to pass and may obstruct. Typically after **SWL (shock wave lithotripsy)**; occasionally after PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) or URS (ureteroscopy).
- Incidence is low overall (about 4–7% after SWL, EAU [European Association of Urology]) but rises several-fold with stone burden **>2 cm²**.

### Risk factors
- Large burden (>2 cm), renal pelvic stones, hard stones that break into large fragments, high energy, impaired ureteric drainage.

### Classification (Coptcoat)
| Type | Composition |
|---|---|
| I | Small fragments only |
| II | Larger lead fragment (4–5 mm) followed by small fragments |
| III | Large fragments throughout |

### Clinical features
- Asymptomatic (silent obstruction), colic, nausea; fever and sepsis if infected; anuria and AKI (acute kidney injury) in a solitary kidney. Most lie in the distal ureter.

### Evaluation
- KUB X-ray, ultrasound for hydronephrosis, or non-contrast CT; creatinine, urine culture, blood counts.

### Management
```
Steinstrasse
 ├─ infection / sepsis  → urgent PCN (percutaneous nephrostomy) + antibiotics
 ├─ asymptomatic, no obstruction → MET + analgesia, imaging every 1–2 weeks
 └─ failure, pain, obstruction:
      ├─ lead fragment (type II/III) → SWL to lead fragment
      └─ URS with laser lithotripsy (most definitive)
```
- **MET (medical expulsive therapy):** α-blockers (tamsulosin) improve fragment clearance and reduce pain.
- **PCN** is preferred in infected obstruction: drains without manipulating the ureter, and fragments often pass once the kidney is decompressed. A stent is an alternative.
- Retained fragments with poor progress: URS clears the column.

### Prevention
- Choose PCNL or RIRS (retrograde intrarenal surgery) for large burdens.
- Energy ramping, slower shock rate; MET after SWL.
- **Stent before SWL:** may reduce steinstrasse with large stones but does not improve stone-free rate and adds stent symptoms. The chapters disagree: Chapter 97 says it may reduce steinstrasse; Chapter 98 advises against stenting at SWL.

**Diagram to draw:** ureter with lead fragment and trailing column (Coptcoat types I–III).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Risk rises several-fold with burden >2 cm².
> - Coptcoat I–III by fragment size.
> - Infected: PCN; uncomplicated: MET; failed: SWL to lead fragment or URS.

## Q3f. Renal aangiomyolipoma
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 3, Q3d)

*Assumed meaning: renal angiomyolipoma (AML).*

### Introduction
- Benign **PEComa (perivascular epithelioid cell tumour)** of dysmorphic **vessels, smooth muscle and fat**. Female predominance; fourth–fifth decades.
- **Sporadic** (solitary, small) or with **TSC (tuberous sclerosis complex**; younger, bilateral, multifocal, larger) or LAM (lymphangioleiomyomatosis).
- **Mechanism:** loss of **TSC2** (tuberin) or TSC1 (hamartin) → unchecked **mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin)** → growth and angiogenesis.
- **Epithelioid AML**: little fat; aggressive (venous extension, metastasis); treat as RCC (renal cell carcinoma).

### Clinical features
- Mostly incidental. Large tumours bleed: **Wunderlich syndrome** (spontaneous retroperitoneal haemorrhage; Lenk triad: flank pain, mass, shock). CKD (chronic kidney disease) in TSC.

### Diagnosis
- **CT (computed tomography): macroscopic fat (below about −15 to −20 HU) on non-contrast images is diagnostic.**
- US (ultrasound) hyperechoic (so are some RCCs); MRI (magnetic resonance imaging): signal loss on fat suppression, India-ink artefact.
- Fat-poor lesion → MRI ± biopsy (HMB-45 [human melanoma black-45], Melan-A, smooth muscle actin positive).
- **Aneurysm ≥5 mm** on CT angiography predicts bleeding.

### Management
| Situation | Management |
|---|---|
| Acute haemorrhage | Resuscitate → **SAE (selective arterial embolisation)** → nephrectomy if SAE fails |
| Symptomatic | SAE, NSS (nephron-sparing surgery), ablation in selected cases |
| Asymptomatic with **>6 cm, aneurysm >5 mm, growth >0.25 cm/year, childbearing age or pregnancy** | Active treatment |
| None of these | **Surveillance**: imaging at 6 and 12 months, then yearly |
| TSC, asymptomatic, **>3 cm** | **Everolimus**; MRI every 1–3 years |
- The old **4 cm** cut-off overtreats; it should not be a general trigger.

### Treatments
- **SAE:** first line; **post-embolisation syndrome** (fever, pain, leucocytosis), abscess, infarction.
- **Partial nephrectomy:** preferred surgery, essential in TSC (multifocal, recurrent).
- Thermal ablation: limited data.
- **Everolimus (EXIST-2):** ≥50% shrinkage in **42% vs 0%**; stomatitis, acne, hyperlipidaemia.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Macroscopic fat on non-contrast CT is diagnostic.
> - Treat if >6 cm, aneurysm >5 mm, growth, pregnancy or symptoms.
> - SAE for bleeding; partial nephrectomy preferred; everolimus for TSC >3 cm.
