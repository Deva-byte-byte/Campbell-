## Q1. Discuss anatomy of urethra. Classify its congenital anomalies and discuss management of epispadias.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(male urethral anatomy also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 1, Q1; isolated epispadias in 2022-Summer Paper 3, Q3f)

### Anatomy of the male urethra
- About 18–20 cm long, 8–9 mm wide. **Posterior urethra:** bladder neck to **perineal membrane**; **anterior urethra:** perineal membrane to meatus, inside the corpus spongiosum.

| Segment | Features | Lining |
|---|---|---|
| Prostatic | Urethral crest; **verumontanum** with utricle (Müllerian remnant) and ejaculatory ducts; angle divides preprostatic and prostatic parts | Urothelium |
| Membranous | 2–2.5 cm; shortest and narrowest; striated sphincter | Pseudostratified columnar |
| Bulbar | Widens as the bulb; **Cowper ducts at 3 and 9 o'clock**; lies eccentric, nearer the dorsum | Pseudostratified columnar |
| Penile | About 15 cm; uniform calibre; glands of Littré | Pseudostratified columnar |
| Fossa navicularis | Glanular; lacuna magna on the roof | Stratified squamous |

- **Sphincters:** (1) **internal (preprostatic) smooth-muscle sphincter**, autonomic, gives continence at rest; (2) **external striated rhabdosphincter**, horseshoe-shaped, supplied by the pudendal nerve (mainly S3, some S2; Onuf nucleus).
- **Arteries:** internal pudendal → common penile artery → bulbourethral branches; retrograde flow through the glans from the dorsal arteries. This **dual supply** allows bulbar transection in urethroplasty.
- **Veins** to the pudendal and prostatic plexuses; **lymph** to internal and external iliac nodes.
- **Female urethra:** about 4 cm; smooth muscle plus striated sphincter over the distal two-thirds; **Skene glands** (prostate homologue) near the meatus; oestrogen-dependent mucosal seal.

### Classification of congenital urethral anomalies
| Group | Anomalies |
|---|---|
| Abnormal meatal position | **Hypospadias** (ventral); **epispadias** (dorsal) |
| Obstructive | **PUV (posterior urethral valves)**, Young types I–III; **anterior urethral valves** (with diverticulum); urethral atresia or hypoplasia (congenital stricture); congenital meatal stenosis |
| Dilatation | **Megalourethra**: scaphoid (spongiosum deficient) or fusiform (all erectile tissue deficient, more severe); anterior urethral diverticulum; Cowper duct cyst (syringocele) |
| Duplication | **Effmann classification**: type I blind incomplete; type II complete (IIA2 "Y-type"); type III with caudal duplication. The ventral channel is the functional one |
| Accessory openings | Congenital urethrocutaneous fistula; parameatal urethral cyst |
| Posterior urethra | Enlarged prostatic utricle (utricular cyst) |
| Female | Epispadias; urethral prolapse; persistent urogenital sinus |

### Epispadias: definition and classification
- A **dorsal defect of the urethral wall**, replaced by a broad dorsal mucosal strip (urethral plate). It is part of the **exstrophy–epispadias complex**; most cases occur with exstrophy.
- Embryology: an overdeveloped cloacal membrane blocks mesenchymal ingrowth; genital tubercles fuse caudal to it.

| | Male | Female |
|---|---|---|
| Incidence | About 1 in 117,000 | About 1 in 484,000 |
| Types | Glanular, penile, **penopubic** (about 70%, incontinent) | **Davis:** vesicular (patulous meatus), subsymphyseal, retrosymphyseal (incontinent) |
| Genitalia | Short, upturned penis with **dorsal chordee** | **Bifid clitoris**, flat glabrous mons |
| VUR (vesicoureteric reflux) | 30–40% | 30–75% |

- The more proximal the meatus, the worse the continence (glanular never incontinent). Pubic diastasis is less than in exstrophy.

### Evaluation
- Renal ultrasound; VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) for reflux; cystoscopy and **bladder capacity** under anaesthesia.
- **Key decision:** isolated epispadias pathway, or exstrophy pathway (osteotomy and closure) if pubic diastasis >3 cm, poor intrasymphyseal bar, absent sphincter tissue or tiny bladder.

### Management
**Aims:** continence with safe upper tracts, and a straight penis of adequate length that works for intercourse.
- **Preoperative testosterone** (2 mg/kg intramuscular, 5 and 2 weeks before) enlarges the penis.

**Stage 1: urethroplasty and penile reconstruction (6–12 months)**
1. Release dorsal chordee: divide suspensory ligaments; free corpora from the inferior pubic rami.
2. **Modified Cantwell–Ransley:** an 18-mm plate strip is outlined; reverse MAGPI (meatal advancement and glanuloplasty) brings the meatus to the tip; the plate is tubularised over an 8-Fr stent and moved **ventral to the corpora**, which are rotated medially over it (diamond corporotomies if curvature persists).
3. Glans wings closed; skin flaps resurface the dorsum; base Z-plasty; stent 10–12 days.
- **Mitchell–Bägli complete penile disassembly:** reliable chordee correction, but often leaves hypospadias.
- Urethroplasty adds outlet resistance, so the bladder grows before stage 2.

**Stage 2: bladder neck reconstruction (4–5 years, capacity about 100 mL)**
- **Young–Dees–Leadbetter** repair with Marshall–Marchetti–Krantz suspension and **ureteric reimplantation**. Osteotomy is rarely needed.
- **Good capacity at presentation:** single-stage repair is possible.
- **Female:** urethroplasty and genitoplasty (clitoral halves and labia joined, mons reconstructed) at 6–12 months; bladder neck repair at 4–5 years.

### Outcomes and complications
- Continence after bladder neck reconstruction: **males 47–82%, females 70–87%**; capacity is the main predictor.
- Early fistula about 1 in 6, mostly at the penile base; stricture rare; neourethra always catheterisable. Erections and paternity usually preserved.
- Better than exstrophy: the bladder was never exposed or scarred. Failures: injectables, augmentation.

**Diagram to draw:** sagittal male urethra with segments; Cantwell–Ransley with plate transposed under the corpora.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Bulbar urethra eccentric (dorsal); Cowper ducts 3 and 9 o'clock.
> - Megalourethra: fusiform worse than scaphoid; duplication: ventral channel functional.
> - About 70% of male epispadias are penopubic; diastasis >3 cm → exstrophy pathway.
> - Cantwell–Ransley at 6–12 months; YDL (Young–Dees–Leadbetter) at about 100 mL.

## Q2. Write in detail about physiology of erection. How will you evaluate a diabetic with ED?
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Functional anatomy
- **Corpora cavernosa:** sinusoids lined by endothelium, separated by **trabecular smooth muscle**. Flaccid tone depends on smooth-muscle contraction.
- **Tunica albuginea:** inner circular and outer longitudinal layers. **Emissary veins** pass obliquely between them and are compressed when the corpora expand: the **veno-occlusive mechanism**.
- **Arteries:** internal pudendal → common penile → **cavernous (helicine branches)**, dorsal and bulbourethral arteries. Accessory pudendal arteries in about one-third.
- Spongiosum and glans have a thin or absent tunica, so no veno-occlusion; they stay low-pressure.

### Haemodynamic phases
| Phase | Events | Intracavernous pressure |
|---|---|---|
| Flaccid | Smooth muscle contracted; low inflow | Low |
| Latent (filling) | Arterial dilation; **inflow peaks** | Barely rises |
| Tumescence | Sinusoids fill; venous outflow compressed | Rising |
| Full erection | Maximal veno-occlusion | About 100 mmHg |
| Rigid | **Ischiocavernosus** contraction (pudendal nerve) | Several hundred mmHg |
| Detumescence | Initial, slow, fast phases | Falls to baseline |

### Molecular mechanism
- **Relaxation (erection):** NO (nitric oxide) from **nNOS (neuronal nitric oxide synthase)** in cavernous nerves initiates erection; endothelial eNOS sustains it.
  - NO → soluble guanylyl cyclase → **cGMP (cyclic guanosine monophosphate)** → protein kinase G → K⁺ channels open, Ca²⁺ channels close, Ca²⁺ sequestered → **low cytosolic Ca²⁺** → relaxation.
  - Second route: PGE1 (prostaglandin E1), VIP (vasoactive intestinal peptide) → **cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate)** → protein kinase A.
  - **PDE5 (phosphodiesterase type 5)** degrades cGMP and ends relaxation; PDE5 inhibitors block it.
- **Contraction (flaccidity):** **noradrenaline (α1)**, endothelin-1, angiotensin II → raised Ca²⁺ → calmodulin → myosin light-chain kinase → contraction. **Rho-kinase** inhibits myosin phosphatase and maintains tone.
- Gap junctions (connexin-43) synchronise the smooth muscle.

### Neural control
| Pathway | Origin | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Parasympathetic | **S2–S4** → pelvic plexus → cavernous nerves | Erection |
| Sympathetic | **T11–L2** → hypogastric nerves | Detumescence, emission |
| Somatic | Pudendal nerve (Onuf nucleus) | Penile sensation; ischiocavernosus rigidity |

- **Three types:** **psychogenic** (brain, chiefly by reducing sympathetic tone), **reflexogenic** (genital touch, sacral arc; preserved in upper cord injury), **nocturnal** (REM [rapid eye movement] sleep).
- **Central:** MPOA (medial preoptic area) and **PVN (paraventricular nucleus)**. Dopamine and oxytocin promote; serotonin, prolactin, GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid) and opioids inhibit.

### Evaluation of a diabetic with ED (erectile dysfunction)
**Why diabetics:** ED is commoner, earlier and more severe in DM (diabetes mellitus). Mechanisms: endothelial NO loss, autonomic neuropathy, arteriopathy, smooth-muscle loss and fibrosis (veno-occlusive failure), AGEs (advanced glycation end products) and low testosterone. ED is a **sentinel of cardiovascular disease**.

**1. History**
- Onset, duration (≥3 months), severity, morning erections, libido, ejaculation; partner.
- **IIEF-5 (International Index of Erectile Function, SHIM):** 5–7 severe, 8–11 moderate, 12–16 mild-to-moderate, 17–21 mild, 22–25 no ED.
- Diabetes: type, duration, **glycaemic control**, complications (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, foot disease), autonomic symptoms (postural giddiness, gastroparesis, bladder dysfunction).
- Cardiovascular: angina, exercise tolerance, smoking, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, nitrates.
- Drugs: antihypertensives, antidepressants. Depression screen.

**2. Examination**
- BMI, waist, blood pressure, peripheral pulses.
- Genitalia: Peyronie plaques, testicular size, gynaecomastia.
- Neurological: genital sensation, **bulbocavernosus reflex**, foot sensation. Penile neuropathy can exist without foot neuropathy.

**3. Laboratory**
- **HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin)** and fasting glucose, **lipid profile**, creatinine/eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate), urine albumin.
- **Morning total testosterone**; if low, repeat with free testosterone, LH (luteinising hormone), prolactin. TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) if indicated.

**4. Cardiovascular risk stratification (Princeton Consensus)**
- **High risk** (unstable angina, recent infarction, uncontrolled hypertension, some arrhythmias): defer sexual activity and treatment; cardiology referral. **Intermediate:** stress testing. **Low:** start treatment.

**5. Specialised tests (selected cases)**
- **CIS (combined intracavernous injection and stimulation):** alprostadil 10–20 µg; a rigid erection excludes major vascular disease.
- **Penile duplex Doppler:** PSV (peak systolic velocity) **<25 cm/s = arterial insufficiency** (>35 normal); EDV (end-diastolic velocity) **>5 cm/s** and RI (resistive index) **<0.75 = veno-occlusive dysfunction**.
- **NPTR (nocturnal penile tumescence and rigidity), RigiScan:** separates psychogenic from organic ED.
- **Neurophysiological:** bulbocavernosus reflex latency, dorsal nerve conduction velocity, biothesiometry.
- DICC (dynamic infusion cavernosometry and cavernosography) and angiography: rarely, as diffuse diabetic vascular disease is not suited to revascularisation.

```
Diabetic with ED → history + IIEF-5 + examination
   → HbA1c, lipids, testosterone, renal function
   → Princeton cardiac risk → treat risk factors
   → PDE5 inhibitor trial → failure → duplex/CIS → ICI or prosthesis
```

**Diagram to draw:** NO–cGMP pathway in the cavernous smooth-muscle cell.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - nNOS initiates, eNOS maintains; PDE5 ends erection.
> - Rigid phase: ischiocavernosus, suprasystolic pressure.
> - Duplex: PSV <25 cm/s arterial; EDV >5 cm/s, RI <0.75 venous leak.
> - Diabetic ED: check HbA1c, testosterone and cardiac risk.

## Q3a. Tear drop bladder
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- A **tear-drop (pear-shaped or inverted-pear) bladder** is a bladder that is symmetrically **compressed from both sides** and elongated upwards on cystogram, IVU (intravenous urogram) or CT (computed tomography).
- It is a radiological sign of a **bilateral perivesical mass or collection**, not a disease itself.

### Causes
| Group | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Collections** | **Pelvic haematoma** (pelvic fracture: commonest in trauma); urinoma (extraperitoneal leak); bilateral lymphocele (after lymphadenectomy or transplant); pelvic abscess |
| **Fat** | **Pelvic lipomatosis** |
| **Muscle** | **Iliopsoas hypertrophy** (muscular young men) |
| **Vascular** | Dilated pelvic venous collaterals (IVC [inferior vena cava] occlusion); bilateral iliac artery aneurysms |
| **Nodes and tumour** | Pelvic lymphadenopathy (lymphoma, metastases); pelvic retroperitoneal fibrosis; pelvic tumours |

### Pelvic lipomatosis (the classic cause)
- Overgrowth of benign, non-encapsulated fat in the perivesical and perirectal spaces; mainly in men, more in Black men.
- **Presentation:** LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms), suprapubic pain, constipation; often incidental.
- **Imaging:**
  - Plain film: pelvic lucency. IVU: elevated, elongated pear-shaped bladder; **medial deviation of both ureters**; hydronephrosis.
  - Barium enema: straightened, elongated rectosigmoid.
  - **CT or MRI (magnetic resonance imaging):** fat density is diagnostic.
- **Cystoscopy:** high bladder neck, difficult passage; often **cystitis glandularis**, which can progress to adenocarcinoma. Biopsy and surveillance are needed.
- **Management:** observe if no obstruction. Upper-tract obstruction → stent or nephrostomy, ureteric reimplantation or urinary diversion. Weight loss rarely helps; surgical excision of fat is difficult.

### Evaluation of a tear-drop bladder
- History: trauma, pelvic surgery or transplant, malignancy.
- **CT** shows the cause (blood, fat, fluid, muscle, nodes, vessels). Ultrasound for collections; aspiration (creatinine for urinoma, lymph for lymphocele).

### Management
- Treat the cause: pelvic haematoma usually observed; lymphocele drained or marsupialised (laparoscopic fenestration); urinoma drained and leak repaired.

**Diagram to draw:** cystogram outline of a bladder squeezed into a tear drop by bilateral pelvic haematoma.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Tear-drop bladder = bilateral extrinsic compression.
> - Trauma: pelvic haematoma; non-trauma: pelvic lipomatosis.
> - Lipomatosis: fat on CT, medially deviated ureters, cystitis glandularis.

## Q3b. TESA
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 2, Q3c)

### Definition
- **TESA (testicular sperm aspiration):** percutaneous needle aspiration of seminiferous tubules under local anaesthesia to retrieve sperm for **ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection)**, which needs one sperm per oocyte.

### Indications
- **OA (obstructive azoospermia) with normal spermatogenesis:** CBAVD (congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens), unreconstructable obstruction, failed vasectomy reversal, or failed epididymal aspiration.
- Anejaculation (spinal cord injury) after failed vibratory stimulation or electroejaculation.
- **NOA (non-obstructive azoospermia):** poor yield; **micro-TESE (microdissection testicular sperm extraction)** preferred.

### Technique
1. Spermatic cord block with local anaesthetic.
2. Testis fixed; **23-G needle** (or angiocatheter) on a high-suction syringe.
3. Multiple passes in different directions; tubules drawn into the needle (often 10–20 passes needed).
4. Immediate wet examination for sperm; repeat until sperm are found. **Cryopreserve** spare sperm.

### Comparison of retrieval methods
| Technique | Site | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| PESA (percutaneous epididymal sperm aspiration) | Caput epididymis | OA | Motile but few sperm; may obstruct epididymis |
| MESA (microsurgical epididymal sperm aspiration) | Single tubule at 16–25× | OA | Most motile sperm; many frozen vials |
| **TESA** | Testis, needle | OA | Least invasive; few, immotile sperm |
| Percutaneous biopsy | 14-G biopsy gun | OA | Core of tissue; blind |
| TESE / micro-TESE | Open, larger tubules under microscope | **NOA** | Most sperm, least tissue |

### Advantages and limitations
- **Advantages:** office procedure, no microsurgery, cheap, repeatable.
- **Limitations:** few tubules with poor architecture (not diagnostic histology); small numbers of immature, immotile sperm; often too few to freeze; blind.

### Complications
- Haematoma (commonest), pain, testicular vascular injury, infection, epididymal injury.

### Outcome
- OA: retrieval almost always succeeds; ICSI is essential for testicular sperm.
- NOA: fine-needle aspiration yields far fewer sperm than micro-TESE.
- Before ICSI: karyotype, Y-microdeletion and CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) testing as indicated.

**Diagram to draw:** testis with 23-G needle on a suction syringe and multiple passes.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - TESA: 23-G needle, local anaesthesia, OA with normal spermatogenesis.
> - NOA: micro-TESE best; TESA yield poor.
> - Testicular sperm always need ICSI; cryopreserve extras.

## Q3c. ESBL producing enterobacterial UTI.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2019-Summer Paper 1, Q3f)

### Definition
- **ESBLs (extended-spectrum β-lactamases):** **plasmid-encoded** enzymes that hydrolyse penicillins, **third-generation cephalosporins** and aztreonam. They are **inhibited by clavulanate** and spare **carbapenems** and cephamycins.
- Mainly *E. coli* and *Klebsiella* (Enterobacterales); first reported 1983.

### Mechanism and spread
- Older types **TEM** and **SHV**; **CTX-M** now dominates. Community spread is linked to *E. coli* **ST131**, whose hallmark is fluoroquinolone resistance.
- Plasmids carry co-resistance to **fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides and TMP-SMX (trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole)**, so oral options are few.
- India has one of the highest prevalence rates.

### Risk factors
- Prior antibiotics (cephalosporins, quinolones); recent hospital stay; **indwelling catheter** and instrumentation; recurrent UTI (urinary tract infection); diabetes; nursing-home residence; travel; transrectal prostate biopsy.

### Detection
- Screening: reduced susceptibility to ceftriaxone, cefotaxime or ceftazidime.
- **Phenotypic confirmation:** clavulanate synergy (combination disc or double-disc test); automated systems.
- Genotypic PCR (polymerase chain reaction) for CTX-M.

### Treatment
| Infection | Options |
|---|---|
| **Cystitis** | **Nitrofurantoin** 5 days; **fosfomycin** 3 g single dose; **pivmecillinam**; TMP-SMX or quinolone only if susceptible |
| **Pyelonephritis, complicated UTI** | Quinolone or TMP-SMX if susceptible; otherwise **aminoglycoside** |
| **Urosepsis, bacteraemia** | **Carbapenem** (meropenem, ertapenem), then oral step-down by culture |

- **MERINO trial (2018):** in ceftriaxone-resistant *E. coli*/*Klebsiella* bacteraemia, **piperacillin–tazobactam** had higher 30-day mortality than meropenem (12.3% vs 3.7%).
- Ceftazidime–avibactam and ceftolozane–tazobactam are **reserved** for carbapenem-resistant organisms.
- **Source control:** change or remove catheters; drain obstruction.
- Treat **asymptomatic bacteriuria** only before urological procedures or in pregnancy.
- **Febrile prostatitis after transrectal biopsy:** carbapenem is favoured.

### Prevention
- **Antibiotic stewardship:** culture before treatment; narrowest spectrum; shortest course.
- Hand hygiene, contact precautions, catheter care; rectal swab culture before biopsy, or transperineal biopsy.

**Also know:** AmpC (not inhibited by clavulanate); carbapenemases KPC (*Klebsiella pneumoniae* carbapenemase) and NDM (New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase).

**Diagram to draw:** flowchart from culture result to oral or intravenous choice.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Plasmid CTX-M; resists third-generation cephalosporins; clavulanate inhibits.
> - Oral: nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, pivmecillinam.
> - Serious infection: carbapenem; MERINO: piperacillin–tazobactam inferior.

## Q3d. Flow cytometry
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **Flow cytometry** measures physical and fluorescent properties of **single cells in suspension** as they flow one at a time through a laser beam. Thousands of cells are analysed per second.

### Principle
1. **Sample:** fresh cells, body fluid (urine, bladder washings) or nuclei extracted from paraffin blocks.
2. **Staining:** fluorochrome-labelled monoclonal antibodies (surface markers, e.g. CD [cluster of differentiation] antigens) or **DNA-binding dyes** (propidium iodide, DAPI [4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole]).
3. **Hydrodynamic focusing** lines the cells up in single file.
4. **Laser interrogation:**
   - **Forward scatter** = cell size.
   - **Side scatter** = internal complexity (granularity).
   - **Fluorescence** = amount of bound antibody or DNA.
5. Photomultiplier detectors → histograms and dot plots; **gating** selects populations.
- **FACS (fluorescence-activated cell sorting)** physically separates selected cells.

### DNA content analysis
- **DNA index** = tumour G0/G1 peak ÷ normal diploid peak. DI 1.0 = diploid; about 2.0 = tetraploid; any other = **aneuploid**.
- **S-phase fraction** estimates proliferation.
- Aneuploidy and high S-phase generally mean higher grade and worse prognosis.

### Applications in urology
| Area | Use |
|---|---|
| **Bladder cancer** | DNA ploidy of bladder washings: aneuploidy with high grade, CIS (carcinoma in situ) and recurrence; largely replaced by **UroVysion FISH (fluorescence in situ hybridisation)**, which also detects aneuploidy |
| **Upper-tract urothelial cancer** | Aneuploidy → poor long-term survival |
| **Prostate cancer** | Aneuploid tumours progress more after treatment than diploid |
| **Neuroblastoma** | **Hyperdiploid favourable**; diploid or tetraploid adverse; part of COG (Children's Oncology Group) risk |
| **Urinalysis** | Automated urine analysers count RBC (red blood cells), WBC (white blood cells) and bacteria |
| **Transplantation** | **Flow cytometric crossmatch**: more sensitive than CDC (complement-dependent cytotoxicity) crossmatch for donor-specific antibody; Luminex single-antigen bead assays (virtual crossmatch); CD3 count to guide antithymocyte globulin |
| **Male infertility** | Sperm DNA fragmentation (SCSA [sperm chromatin structure assay]); haploid cells in testicular aspirates; leucocytospermia |
| **Lymphoma** | Immunophenotyping of testicular or renal lymphoma |

### Advantages and limitations
- **Advantages:** rapid, objective, quantitative, many parameters per cell.
- **Limitations:** needs a single-cell suspension, so architecture is lost; near-diploid aneuploidy missed; normal cell contamination dilutes tumour peaks; ploidy adds little independent value in most urological tumours today.

**Diagram to draw:** DNA histogram with diploid G0/G1 peak, S-phase and aneuploid peak.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Forward scatter = size; side scatter = granularity; fluorescence = marker or DNA.
> - DNA index 1.0 diploid; aneuploidy = worse prognosis.
> - Flow crossmatch is more sensitive than CDC crossmatch.

## Q3e. Vascular grafts in urology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 3, Q2 and 2015-Summer Paper 2, Q3e)

### Introduction
- A vascular graft **replaces, bypasses, extends or widens** a vessel when primary repair would leave tension or narrowing. Patch widens a wall; interposition graft replaces a segment; bypass routes flow around a lesion.

### Types
| Type | Examples | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Autologous vein** | Saphenous, gonadal, donor external iliac vein | Resists infection; small vessels | Limited length |
| **Autologous artery** | Internal iliac, **Carrel aortic patch** | Best for renal artery | Limited supply |
| **Xenograft** | Bovine pericardium | Pliable patch | Patch only |
| **Synthetic** | **PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene)**, Dacron | Large calibre, any length | Infection; venous grafts thrombose |

### Applications
1. **Renal transplantation:** multiple arteries (18–43%) joined by Carrel patch or bench reconstruction; **preserve the lower-pole artery** (supplies the ureter). Short right renal vein extended with donor IVC (inferior vena cava), external iliac or spiral gonadal vein.
2. **RCC (renal cell carcinoma) with IVC thrombus:** residual lumen <50% → **patch cavoplasty**; circumferential wall invasion → **PTFE replacement (16–20 mm)**, then lifelong aspirin.
3. **PC-RPLND (post-chemotherapy retroperitoneal lymph node dissection):** IVC narrowing >25% → patch or graft; IVC ligation tolerated. **Aorta never ligated**: PTFE or Dacron replacement (about 1%).
4. **Retroperitoneal sarcoma:** caval resection and graft; thrombosis 10–40%.
5. **Renovascular repair and autotransplantation:** ex vivo bench repair with internal iliac artery or saphenous vein.
6. **Dialysis access:** PTFE AV (arteriovenous) graft when no native fistula is possible.
7. **Penile:** Grayhack saphenous–cavernosal shunt for priapism.

### Principles
- Autologous in contaminated fields and small vessels; synthetic for large, high-flow vessels.
- Venous grafts: use the widest graft. Heparin before clamping; tension-free spatulated anastomosis with monofilament polypropylene; **omental wrap** keeps synthetic grafts away from bowel and urine.

### Complications
- Thrombosis, stenosis, infection (synthetic usually removed), graft–enteric fistula, **ureteroarterial fistula**.

**Diagram to draw:** IVC patch cavoplasty and PTFE caval graft.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Autologous for small or contaminated fields; PTFE or Dacron for large vessels.
> - IVC: lumen <50% → patch; invaded wall → PTFE 16–20 mm.
> - PC-RPLND: IVC may be ligated; aorta must be grafted.

## Q3f. Hypokalemia
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and physiology
- **Hypokalaemia:** serum potassium **<3.5 mEq/L**; severe <2.5 mEq/L.
- About **98% of body potassium is intracellular**; the ratio sets the resting membrane potential.
- Two defences: **shift into cells** (insulin, β2-adrenergic stimulation, alkalosis) and **renal excretion** (distal nephron). Distal secretion rises with high urine flow, distal sodium delivery, **aldosterone** and alkalosis.

### Causes
| Mechanism | Causes |
|---|---|
| Low intake | Malnutrition |
| **Shift into cells** | Insulin, catecholamines (stress, β-agonists), alkalosis, refeeding, periodic paralysis |
| **GI (gastrointestinal) loss** | Diarrhoea, villous adenoma, **ureterosigmoidostomy and colonic diversions** (colon secretes potassium, ileum absorbs it) |
| **Renal loss, high BP (blood pressure)** | **Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn)**, renin-secreting juxtaglomerular tumour, Cushing, Liddle, **abiraterone** (mineralocorticoid excess) |
| **Renal loss, normal BP** | Loop and thiazide diuretics, **postobstructive diuresis**, osmotic diuresis, **RTA (renal tubular acidosis) types 1 and 2**, Bartter, Gitelman, hypomagnesaemia, amphotericin, cisplatin |

- **Vomiting:** potassium is lost mainly in urine (alkalosis → distal sodium delivery → kaliuresis), not in gastric juice.

### Clinical features
- Muscle weakness, cramps, ileus, **flaccid paralysis**, rhabdomyolysis.
- Arrhythmias; potentiates digoxin toxicity.
- Polyuria (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus); **hypocitraturia** → calcium stones.
- **ECG (electrocardiogram):** flat T wave, **prominent U wave**, ST depression, prolonged QU; ventricular arrhythmias.

### Evaluation
- Repeat potassium; magnesium, bicarbonate, glucose.
- **24-h urine potassium:** <20 mmol/day suggests extrarenal loss; higher suggests renal loss.
- Renal loss: check blood pressure and acid–base; **aldosterone–renin ratio** if hypertensive (only a minority of Conn patients are hypokalaemic).

### Management
- Treat the cause; stop offending drugs.
- **Potassium chloride** preferred (potassium citrate if acidotic, phosphate if hypophosphataemic). **Oral** 20–40 mEq repeated when possible.
- **Intravenous** for severe or symptomatic cases: usually ≤10 mEq/h peripherally; faster only centrally with ECG monitoring. Never as a bolus.
- **Correct magnesium**, or potassium will not correct.
- **Urinary diversion:** replace potassium **before or with bicarbonate**; correcting acidosis alone drives potassium into cells → paralysis.
- Correct before abiraterone and adrenalectomy for Conn syndrome (spironolactone preoperatively).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Shift or loss; urine potassium separates renal from extrarenal.
> - ECG: flat T, U wave.
> - Diversion acidosis: give potassium before bicarbonate.
> - Correct magnesium first.
