## Q1. Describe early and late management of PFUD injuries
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

*PFUD is taken as pelvic fracture urethral distraction defect, i.e. PFUI (pelvic fracture urethral injury).*

### Introduction and mechanism
- PFUI occurs in about 10% of male and up to 6% of female pelvic fractures. Highest risk: straddle fracture of all four pubic rami, unstable fractures, symphyseal diastasis.
- The posterior urethra is fixed to the pubis (puboprostatic ligaments, perineal membrane), so displacement shears it, usually at the **bulbomembranous junction**. In prepubertal boys the tear may extend into the prostate and bladder neck.
- The sphincter is avulsed but usually functional, so most men stay continent after repair.
- The gap fills with scar: an obliterative **distraction defect**, not a true stricture.

### Classification
| System | Types |
|---|---|
| Campbell-Walsh | **Partial**; **minor distraction** (a cystoscope passes retrogradely into the bladder); **major distraction** (it cannot). Need for urethroplasty rises from partial to major |
| Goldman | I stretched, intact; II tear above the perineal membrane; III tear of membranous urethra extending below the membrane into the bulb (commonest); IV bladder neck tear into the urethra; IVa bladder base injury mimicking IV; V pure anterior injury |

### Diagnosis
- Triad: **blood at the meatus, inability to void, palpable bladder**. A "high-riding" prostate is unreliable.
- **Blood at the meatus mandates RUG (retrograde urethrogram)** before catheterisation (oblique, 20 mL contrast). Extravasation with bladder filling means partial injury; without filling, complete.
- Females: vaginal examination and urethroscopy. Cystogram or CT for bladder injury.

### Early management
1. ATLS (Advanced Trauma Life Support) resuscitation; associated injuries take priority.
2. **SPC (suprapubic cystostomy): the standard of care.** Percutaneous (peel-away sheath, 16 Fr Foley; avoid small "punch" tubes) or open through a lower midline incision, which allows bladder inspection.
   - SPC is safe with pelvic plating (AUA [American Urological Association] Urotrauma guideline); tunnel it high, away from hardware.
3. **Primary realignment (PR):** a brief attempt in a stable patient, within days, by antegrade plus retrograde flexible cystoscopy over a guidewire. Catheter out at 4–6 weeks; keep the SPC until voiding is good.
4. Partial tear: one gentle catheter attempt is acceptable.
5. **Immediate open exploration** only for bladder neck or rectal injury, or laparotomy for other reasons.
- **Also know:** female urethral injury is usually repaired early, with closure of any vaginal laceration.

### SPC versus primary realignment
| | SPC + delayed urethroplasty | Primary realignment |
|---|---|---|
| Advantage | Simple; haematoma untouched | Partial tears may heal; aligned ends ease repair |
| Drawback | Complete injuries need urethroplasty | Most still form a 1–2 cm stenosis |
| Evidence | Prospective data: **no significant difference** in need for urethroplasty | Modern PR does not add ED (erectile dysfunction) or incontinence |

### Late management
**Timing:** after **3 months** (scar stable), once the patient is ambulant.

**Evaluation**
- **"Up-and-down-o-gram":** cystogram plus RUG while the patient tries to void, to measure the gap; antegrade flexible cystoscopy via the SPC tract if the bladder neck stays closed. Bladder neck appearance does **not** predict its later function.
- **MRI (magnetic resonance imaging):** gap length, prostatic displacement (cephalad displacement predicts pubectomy).

**Endoscopic:** DVIU (direct vision internal urethrotomy) only for short, incomplete stenosis. "Cut-to-the-light" for gaps ≥1 cm fails (false passages).

**Perineal anastomotic urethroplasty (treatment of choice)**
1. Exaggerated lithotomy (under 5 hours); sounds via meatus and SPC tract.
2. Midline or lambda incision; mobilise the bulbar urethra up to the penoscrotal junction (not beyond: chordee).
3. Divide the urethra at the scar; excise **all** fibrosis until healthy prostatic mucosa appears over the sound.
4. Spatulate both ends to 30 Fr; 8–10 interrupted 4-0 absorbable sutures; tension-free mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis over a silicone catheter.

**Progressive perineal approach** (each step shortens the path to bridge a longer gap):
| Step | Manoeuvre |
|---|---|
| 1 | Bulbar urethral mobilisation (enough for most) |
| 2 | Separation of the corporal bodies (intracrural space) |
| 3 | Inferior wedge pubectomy (Kerrison rongeur) |
| 4 | Supracrural rerouting of the urethra around one corpus |

- **Abdominoperineal or transpubic approach:** urethrorectal fistula, failed previous repair, bladder neck injury, children.
- **Postoperative:** contrast voiding study at 21–28 days; flexible cystoscopy at about 6 months.

### Outcomes and complications
- Success above 95%. Restenosis 5–15%; often DVIU-treatable because the scar is already excised.
- **ED** up to 82%, mostly from the injury (cavernous nerves, pudendal arteries). **Incontinence <4%** (bladder neck). Follow ≥1 year.
- **Also know:** chordee, penile shortening, ejaculatory dysfunction, lithotomy neuropathy, penile Doppler before repair in impotent men.

**Diagram to draw:** sagittal pelvis showing the gap at the bulbomembranous junction, with the four steps of the progressive perineal approach.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Blood at meatus → RUG; SPC is the standard initial step.
> - Primary realignment: brief, endoscopic; no difference in final urethroplasty rates.
> - Delayed perineal anastomotic urethroplasty after 3 months; bulbar mobilisation → crural separation → pubectomy → rerouting.
> - ED mostly from the injury; incontinence <4%.

## Q2. Describe in detail TCC of ureter.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered for the whole upper tract in 2023-Winter Paper 2, Q1 and 2024-Winter Paper 3, Q1)

*TCC (transitional cell carcinoma) of the ureter is taken as ureteric UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma).*

### Introduction and epidemiology
- UTUC is 5–10% of urothelial cancers; pelvicalyceal tumours are about twice as common as ureteric ones. Incidence is rising, **driven by ureteric tumours**.
- Peak age 70–90; male:female 2:1. About 60% are invasive at diagnosis, because the upper tract wall is thin.
- **Site: distal ureter 70%, mid 25%, proximal 5%**, explained by downstream implantation.

### Aetiology and pathogenesis
| Factor | Key point |
|---|---|
| **Tobacco** | Commonest; risk 2.5–7-fold (aromatic amines) |
| **Aristolochic acid** | Balkan and Chinese herb nephropathy; A:T → T:A transversions in TP53; often bilateral |
| **Lynch syndrome** | Mismatch repair genes (MSH2 highest risk); screen if young or family history |
| Occupational | Aniline dyes, benzidine, β-naphthylamine |
| Phenacetin | Papillary necrosis (history in 11% of ureteric tumours) |
- **Also know:** arsenic, cyclophosphamide, chronic stones and infection (squamous carcinoma).
- **Intraluminal seeding** explains distal predominance and bladder recurrence (22–47%); **field change** explains multifocality.

### Pathology and spread
- 85% papillary, 15% sessile. Ureteric tumours are low grade and low stage in 55–75%. Grade by WHO (World Health Organization) 2004/2016: low or high grade.
- **Why ureteric tumours do worse:** the renal parenchyma is a barrier for pelvic tumours, but periureteric fat holds a rich lymphovascular plexus. At equal stage, ureteric tumours have more local and distant failure.
- Spread: direct, lymphatic (**mid and distal ureter → pelvic nodes**; upper ureter → hilar and retroperitoneal), blood (lung, liver, bone).

### Clinical features
- **Haematuria** (56–98%); **flank pain** (20–32%), dull from obstruction or colic from clots; incidental in about 15%. Weight loss or bone pain suggests metastases.

### Investigations
- **CTU (computed tomography urography):** best test (sensitivity ~92%, specificity ~95%). Filling defect, hydronephrosis, ureteric wall thickening. Tumour ~46 HU (Hounsfield units) versus uric acid stone ~100 HU.
- **Hydronephrosis predicts invasive ureteric tumour in up to 80%.**
- **Cystoscopy** always (concurrent bladder cancer 17%). **Selective ureteric cytology**, taken before contrast.
- **Retrograde pyelography:** **goblet (champagne-glass) sign**, the ureter dilated just below the tumour; **Bergman's sign**, the catheter coiling below it.
- **Ureteroscopy with biopsy:** grade correct in ~90% but **stage cannot be assessed**. Diagnostic URS before RNU raises bladder recurrence.
- MRU (magnetic resonance urography) if contrast is contraindicated; renogram for split function.

### Staging (TNM 8th edition)
| T | Ureter |
|---|---|
| Ta / Tis | Papillary non-invasive / carcinoma in situ |
| T1 | Subepithelial connective tissue |
| T2 | Muscularis |
| T3 | Periureteric fat |
| T4 | Adjacent organs |
- N1 single node ≤2 cm; N2 >2 cm or multiple; M1 distant.

### Risk stratification (EAU, European Association of Urology)
| Low risk: all needed | High risk: any one |
|---|---|
| Unifocal, <2 cm | Multifocal or ≥2 cm |
| Low-grade cytology and biopsy | High-grade cytology or biopsy |
| No invasion on CTU | Hydronephrosis; invasion on imaging |
| — | Previous cystectomy; variant histology |

### Management
**A. Kidney-sparing:** elective in low risk; imperative in solitary kidney, bilateral disease, CKD (chronic kidney disease) or unfit patients.
- **Ureteroscopic laser ablation** (holmium or thulium) after biopsy; avoid circumferential ablation (stricture).
- **Segmental ureterectomy:** first examine the whole tract above for multifocality; pelvic node dissection if high grade. Gives a staging specimen. Lower-ureteric tumours with unfavourable features need resection, not endoscopy.

| Site | Operation |
|---|---|
| **Distal ureter** | Distal ureterectomy with bladder cuff and reimplantation; psoas hitch (~5 cm extra) or Boari flap (10–15 cm) |
| Mid or proximal | Excision with 1–2 cm margins (frozen section) and ureteroureterostomy (rare) |
| Whole ureter | Ileal ureter or autotransplantation |
- Refluxing reimplant eases surveillance; non-refluxing limits upward seeding (debated).
- Adjuvant upper-tract BCG (bacillus Calmette–Guérin) or MMC (mitomycin C).

**B. RNU (radical nephroureterectomy) with bladder cuff:** standard for high risk.
- Kidney outside Gerota's fascia; clip the ureter early; **remove the intramural ureter and cuff** (stump recurrence 30–75% if left). Open, laparoscopic and robotic routes are equivalent; "pluck" and stapling techniques are abandoned.
- Lymphadenectomy: pelvic nodes for lower ureteric tumours.
- **Single postoperative intravesical MMC** (ODMIT-C): bladder recurrence 28% → 16% at 1 year.

**C. Perioperative chemotherapy:** **POUT (2020)**, adjuvant platinum for pT2–T4 or N+, improved disease-free survival. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy when post-RNU renal function would exclude cisplatin.

**D. Metastatic:** enfortumab vedotin + pembrolizumab (EV-302); gemcitabine–platinum with avelumab maintenance.

### Prognosis and follow-up
- 5-year disease-specific survival: localised ~89%, regional ~63%, distant ~17%. Stage is the strongest factor.
- Cystoscopy from 3 months and yearly CTU after RNU; closer URS and CTU after kidney-sparing treatment.

**Diagram to draw:** distal ureterectomy with bladder cuff and psoas-hitch reimplantation, beside the goblet sign.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Ureteric tumours: 70% distal; hydronephrosis predicts invasion (up to 80%).
> - Ureteric T3 does worse than pelvic T3 (no parenchymal barrier).
> - URS biopsy gives grade, not stage; distal ureterectomy for selected distal tumours.
> - RNU + cuff + single MMC (ODMIT-C); adjuvant platinum (POUT).

## Q3a. Early vs. delayed repair in VVF
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 2, Q3b)

### Introduction
- VVF (vesicovaginal fistula): an epithelialised bladder–vagina tract causing continuous leakage.
- Causes: **obstetric** (obstructed labour) in low-resource settings; **iatrogenic** (mainly hysterectomy) in high-resource settings; radiation; malignancy. Common mechanism: **ischaemic necrosis**.
- Timing of presentation: unrecognised cystotomy within days; thermal injury 7–10 days; radiation months to years.

### The debate
| | Early repair | Delayed repair |
|---|---|---|
| Definition (varies) | Days, <6 weeks or <3 months | Traditionally ≥3 months |
| Rationale | Clean iatrogenic tissue heals well; ends wetness and distress early | Inflammation settles, necrosis sloughs, margins become clear |
| Evidence | **Comparable closure** in most series; Waaldijk: immediate obstetric repair gave very high first-attempt closure | One small series: more recurrence when repaired <6 weeks |
| Drawback | Friable tissue if inflamed | Prolonged distress, no better outcome |

- Some authors avoid the **4–12 week** window (active healing).
- **EAU (European Association of Urology) consensus:** no minimum delay if there is **no active infection and no recent irradiation**.

### Timing by cause
- **Clean post-hysterectomy:** early repair.
- **Obstetric with necrotic edges:** catheter trial, then repair at about 3 months.
- **Radiation:** wait **≥12 months** after radiotherapy; biopsy to exclude recurrence; interposition flap; consider diversion if the bladder is poor.

### Conservative trial while waiting
- Catheter drainage cures about 15%. Best if small (<3 mm best; <1 cm), fresh, non-irradiated and catheterised **within 3 weeks**. Once epithelialised (≥6 weeks) closure is unlikely. EAU: if tried, at least 12 weeks.
- **Also know:** fulguration of tiny tracts; fibrin glue (not recommended by EAU).

### Principles at repair
- Treat infection; ureteric stents if within 1 cm of a ureter.
- Vaginal or abdominal route (open, laparoscopic, robotic); tension-free, multilayer, watertight closure; **interposition flap** (Martius, omentum, peritoneum) if irradiated or recurrent; catheter drainage for about 2 weeks.

**Diagram to draw:** timeline showing early window, 4–12 week zone, 3 months (obstetric) and 12 months (radiation).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Early repair for clean iatrogenic VVF; closure comparable to delayed.
> - Delay ~3 months for obstetric, ≥12 months for radiation fistula.
> - Catheter trial works only if small, fresh and started within 3 weeks.

## Q3b. Maldescended testes
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(algorithm also in 2018-Summer Paper 3, Q3a; bilateral cases in 2024-Winter Paper 1, Q2)

*Taken as any testis not in a stable scrotal position: undescended, ectopic, retractile and acquired.*

### Definitions
| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Undescended (cryptorchid)** | Arrested on the normal path: abdominal, canalicular, prescrotal |
| **Ectopic** | Off the normal path: **superficial inguinal pouch** (commonest), perineal, femoral, prepubic, penile, transverse (crossed) |
| **Retractile** | Scrotal testis pulled up by the cremaster; stays down after traction |
| **Acquired (ascending)** | Previously scrotal; presents at 8–11 years |
| Vanished / agenesis | Lost in utero (torsion) / never formed |

### Epidemiology and aetiology
- 1–4% of term boys, far more in preterm; most descend by 6 months. 75–80% palpable; 60–70% unilateral.
- Descent needs **INSL3 (insulin-like peptide 3)** and **androgen**, acting through the gubernaculum and genitofemoral nerve. Risk factors: prematurity, low birth weight; syndromes (prune belly, PUV [posterior urethral valves], cerebral palsy).

### Evaluation
- Warm room, supine and cross-legged. Sweep from the anterior superior iliac spine to the scrotum; find the lowest position; check the superficial inguinal pouch. Gentle traction fatigues the cremaster and separates retractile from undescended.
- **No ultrasound or MRI** (ultrasound sensitivity 45% for non-palpable testes).
- **Bilateral non-palpable, or with hypospadias:** karyotype, 17-OHP (17-hydroxyprogesterone), AMH (anti-Müllerian hormone) to exclude **CAH (congenital adrenal hyperplasia)** and DSD (differences of sex development).

### Management
- Observe to 6 months (corrected age); **orchidopexy from 6 months, preferably by 12, by 18 months at the latest**. No hormonal therapy (LHRH works in about 20%; hCG may harm germ cells).
```
Palpable (undescended or ectopic) → inguinal or scrotal (Bianchi) orchidopexy
Non-palpable → EUA → diagnostic laparoscopy
   testis seen        → laparoscopic orchidopexy / Fowler–Stephens
   vessels enter ring → inguinal exploration, excise nubbin
   blind-ending vessels → stop (vanished testis)
Retractile → yearly review
```
- Postpubertal intra-abdominal testis with a normal contralateral testis: orchidectomy.

### Sequelae
- **Infertility:** paternity about four-fifths after unilateral, one-third after bilateral.
- **Cancer:** 2–5-fold TGCT (testicular germ cell tumour) risk; higher with surgery after puberty. Teach self-examination.
- **Also know:** hernia (patent processus), torsion, trauma, psychological.

**Diagram to draw:** normal line of descent with ectopic sites marked.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Ectopic = off the path; commonest site superficial inguinal pouch.
> - Orchidopexy 6–12 months, by 18 months.
> - No imaging, no hormones; laparoscopy for non-palpable.
> - Bilateral non-palpable = DSD work-up first.

## Q3c. Extended indications of Shodgrass repair in hypospadias
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*"Shodgrass" is taken as Snodgrass TIP (tubularised incised plate) repair. Technique: see Practice Set 5 Paper 3, Q3d.*

### Introduction
- Snodgrass described TIP in 1994 for distal hypospadias. A deep midline incision widens a narrow urethral plate, which is then tubularised; the raw incision heals by re-epithelialisation.
- It is now used for **over 90% of distal and about 80% of midshaft repairs**, and has been extended to more difficult cases.

### Prerequisites for any extended use
- Curvature **<30° after degloving**, corrected by dorsal plication **without dividing the plate**.
- Supple, well-vascularised plate of adequate width and depth. **POST (plate objective scoring tool):** a low AB/BC ratio means a poor plate, favouring an inlay graft.
- **Glans width ≥14 mm**, or preoperative testosterone to enlarge it.

### Extended indications
| Indication | Rationale | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **Midshaft hypospadias** | Same principle, longer tube | Good results |
| **Proximal hypospadias with mild curvature** and a good plate | Single stage; avoids flaps | More fistula, stricture and recurrent curvature; plate mobilisation devascularises. Campbell-Walsh authors now prefer staged repair |
| **Redo or failed repairs** with a preserved plate | Plate often survives a failed repair | Reasonable success but worse than primary; add inlay graft if plate scarred |
| **MIP (megameatus intact prepuce)** variant | Wide, deep plate; glans tissue adequate | GAP (glans approximation procedure) is an alternative |
| **Narrow or shallow plate**: TIP + **dorsal inlay graft** ("Snodgraft") | Inner preputial (or buccal) graft fills the incision, so it cannot contract | Less meatal stenosis; also for redo |
| **Adult and circumcised** patients | No preputial tissue needed | Adult success similar to children |

- **Also know:** dorsal inlay buccal graft is preferred to onlay or tube in redo cases with poor blood supply; the same incise-and-graft idea is used in adult anterior urethral stricture (Asopa dorsal inlay) and in female urethroplasty.

### Contraindications
- Curvature >30° needing plate division; absent or scarred plate; severe proximal hypospadias with penoscrotal transposition. Use staged repair (Bracka, STAG [staged tubularised autograft], Byars).

### Outcomes
- Pfistermuller meta-analysis (2015): complications 10.6%, fistula 5.7%, meatal stenosis 3.6%; **proximal and redo cases did significantly worse**. A dartos barrier layer improves results.

**Diagram to draw:** cross-sections of plate incision, inlay graft in the incision, and tubularisation.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Extended to midshaft, selected proximal, redo, MIP and adults.
> - Key condition: curvature <30° without plate division.
> - Poor plate → dorsal inlay graft ("Snodgraft").
> - Proximal and redo TIP fail more often.

## Q3d. Snodgrass Repair for distal hypos.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2023-Winter Paper 3, Q3c)

*"Distal hypos." is taken as distal hypospadias.*

### Introduction
- **TIP (tubularised incised plate) urethroplasty**, Snodgrass 1994, based on Rich's hinging incision (1989). Used for over 90% of distal repairs.
- Distal hypospadias (glanular, coronal, subcoronal, distal shaft) forms 70–85% of cases.

### Principle
- A **deep midline relaxing incision** of the urethral plate widens a narrow plate so that it can be tubularised without tension.
- The raw area **heals by re-epithelialisation** without a graft, giving a **vertical slit-like meatus**.

### Indications and assessment
- Distal hypospadias with curvature <30° after degloving, and an adequate plate and glans.
- **Glans width <14 mm** raises complications: give preoperative testosterone.
- **POST:** low ratio means a poor plate; consider an inlay graft.
- Timing: 6–12 months of age.

### Operative steps
1. Glans stay suture; tourniquet.
2. U-incision around the plate and meatus; circumscribe and deglove; artificial erection.
3. Separate the glans wings deeply from the plate.
4. **Midline incision of the plate** from inside the meatus to the end of the plate, down to the corpora.
5. Tubularise over a **6–8 Fr stent** with subepithelial 6-0/7-0 absorbable sutures, distal to proximal; the first stitch about 3 mm from the plate end leaves an oval meatus.
6. **Dartos flap** (dorsal preputial) over the neourethra as a barrier.
7. Two-layer glansplasty; mucosal collar; skin closure (Byars flaps) or preputioplasty.
8. Stent 5–7 days; compressive dressing; oxybutynin for spasm.

### Complications and outcomes
- Pfistermuller meta-analysis (2015, 4,675 boys): complications 10.6%, reoperation 4.5%; **fistula 5.7%**, meatal stenosis 3.6%, stricture 1.3%.
- **Meatal stenosis is commoner than after Mathieu**; overall complications similar. Glans dehiscence also occurs.
- Distal repair success 83–95%. A dartos layer improves results.

### Modifications
- **Dorsal inlay graft** (inner preputial skin) for a shallow plate; lateral plate incisions to 12 mm width.
- **Also know:** alternatives: MAGPI (meatal advancement and glanuloplasty), Mathieu flip-flap, Thiersch–Duplay, onlay island flap.

**Diagram to draw:** cross-sections showing plate incision, tubularisation and dartos cover.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Snodgrass 1994; incise, tubularise, heals by epithelialisation.
> - Slit-like meatus; dartos barrier reduces fistula.
> - Fistula 5.7%, meatal stenosis 3.6% (Pfistermuller 2015).

## Q3e. Mixed gonadal dysgenesis.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(DSD management in general in 2019-Summer Paper 3, Q3f)

### Definition
- MGD (mixed gonadal dysgenesis): a **dysgenetic testis on one side and a streak gonad on the other**, with persistent Müllerian structures and variable virilisation.
- A sex chromosome DSD (difference of sex development); name unchanged in the 2006 Chicago nomenclature.
- Usually **45,X/46,XY mosaicism** (about 1 in 5,000 births). **Second commonest cause of neonatal atypical genitalia after CAH (congenital adrenal hyperplasia).**

### Pathogenesis
- The Y-bearing cell line forms a testis; the 45,X line leaves a streak.
- AMH (anti-Müllerian hormone) and testosterone act **locally**, so anatomy is asymmetric: **testis side**, Wolffian duct and Müllerian regression; **streak side**, tube and hemiuterus.
- 45,X/46,XY spectrum: female genitalia (Turner + Y), atypical genitalia (MGD), or male genitalia with normal testes (about 85%).

### Clinical features
- **Asymmetric genitalia:** clitorophallus with chordee, proximal or perineal hypospadias, urogenital sinus; one testis in a hemiscrotum, the other side impalpable with a flat labioscrotal fold.
- Inguinal hernia containing a uterus or tube.
- **Turner features:** short stature, coarctation, bicuspid aortic valve, horseshoe kidney.

### Evaluation (MDT, multidisciplinary team)
- **Karyotype** (count many cells for mosaicism); FISH (fluorescence in situ hybridisation) for X and Y.
- **17-OHP (17-hydroxyprogesterone)** to exclude CAH; LH (luteinising hormone), FSH, testosterone, AMH; hCG (human chorionic gonadotrophin) stimulation.
- **Pelvic ultrasound:** uterus, asymmetric gonads. Genitography and endoscopy.
- **Laparoscopy with gonadal biopsy.** No gonadectomy during diagnosis.
- Echocardiography and renal ultrasound.

| | MGD | Ovotesticular DSD |
|---|---|---|
| Karyotype | 45,X/46,XY | Mostly 46,XX |
| Gonads | Dysgenetic testis + streak | Ovary + testis, or ovotestis |
| Tumour risk | 15–35% | About 3% |

### Management
- **Sex of rearing:** individualised; greater androgen exposure predicts male identity, and dysphoria is commoner when raised female. Avoid irreversible infant surgery unless needed.
- **Gonads:** germ cell neoplasia 15–35% (gonadoblastoma, then dysgerminoma or seminoma) in streak and testis alike. **Remove streak gonads** (biopsy first if tissue uncertain).
  - Raised male: keep a scrotal testis under surveillance; bring down an intra-abdominal testis if possible.
  - Raised female: remove the testis, or relocate it for surveillance.
- **Surgery:** male, hypospadias repair (often staged), orchidopexy; female, feminising surgery deferred.
- **Hormones:** testosterone or oestrogen at puberty; growth hormone for short stature. Infertility usual.

**Diagram to draw:** asymmetric internal anatomy (testis with vas on one side; streak with tube and hemiuterus on the other).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - 45,X/46,XY; testis + streak + Müllerian remnants; asymmetric.
> - Second commonest cause of atypical genitalia after CAH.
> - Tumour risk 15–35%: remove streaks.
> - Screen for Turner features.

## Q3f. Hemostasis in laparoscopic surgery.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in Practice Set 2 Paper 4, Q3f; partial nephrectomy haemostasis in 2019-Summer Paper 3, Q1)

### Principles
- All energy devices seal by **denaturing protein** (current, light or vibration). Plan from imaging, know vascular anomalies, and keep a **haemorrhage tray** ready (laparoscopic Satinsky, 10-mm suction, preloaded vascular suture with Lapra-Ty, haemostatic agent).

### Effect of pneumoperitoneum
- CO₂ at 12–15 mmHg **tamponades venous bleeding**. Before exit, **lower pressure to about 5 mmHg** and inspect the operative site and each port to unmask hidden bleeders.

### Energy devices
| Device | Lateral thermal spread | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Monopolar | 5–7 mm | Capacitive coupling, insulation failure, direct coupling |
| Bipolar | 1–6 mm | Current only between the jaws |
| Vessel sealer (LigaSure) | 1–3 mm | Seals vessels **≤7 mm**; one application only |
| Ultrasonic (Harmonic) | ≈1 mm | No current; blade >200 °C, cools slowly (bowel burns) |
| Argon beam | Superficial | Raw surfaces; gas embolism risk |

### Clips
- **Titanium clips:** no diathermy near them (clip slip); ends should project beyond the vessel.
- **Hem-o-lok** (locking polymer): circumferential dissection, see the tip beyond the vessel, feel the snap, at least two clips on the patient side with a 2-mm cuff. The manufacturer **contraindicates it on the living-donor renal artery** after stump haemorrhages.
- **Lapra-Ty** absorbable clips act as knots (renorrhaphy).

### Staplers
- Endo GIA via a 12-mm port; **grey (2.0 mm) or white (2.5 mm)** loads for renal vessels; blue or green for thicker tissue.
- **Never fire across clips.**

### Sutures and haemostatic agents
- Intracorporeal or barbed sutures. Fibrin sealant, gelatin–thrombin matrix, oxidised cellulose.

### Managing bleeding
- **Venous injury:** raise pressure to 25 mmHg → suction to identify → pledget tamponade → sealant → extra 5-mm port for suturing or Satinsky → hand-assist → convert. Keep the patient well filled (gas embolism).
- **Arterial injury:** minor, tamponade; renal artery, staple; aorta, convert.
- **Port-site bleeding:** found by cantilevering the trocar; diathermy, transfascial suture over a bolster, or closure device. Blunt trocars reduce it.

**Diagram to draw:** Hem-o-lok placement with two patient-side clips and a 2-mm cuff.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Pneumoperitoneum hides venous bleeding: inspect at low pressure before exit.
> - LigaSure seals ≤7 mm; spread monopolar 5–7 mm, ultrasonic ≈1 mm.
> - Hem-o-lok contraindicated on the donor renal artery.
> - Never staple across clips; grey or white loads for vessels.
