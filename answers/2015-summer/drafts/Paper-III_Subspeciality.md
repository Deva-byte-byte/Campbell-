## III-1. Discuss various ureteric stents. Discuss ureteroscopy with or without stenting.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction / History
- A ureteric stent is a hollow tube that keeps the upper tract draining past obstruction, injury or an anastomosis.
- **Zimskind 1967**: first indwelling silicone splint; being straight, it migrated.
- **Finney 1978**: the **double-J (double-pigtail)** stent. Self-retaining coils solved migration; it is still the commonest design.

### Stent design
| Parameter | Details |
|---|---|
| Diameter | 3–8.5 Fr (adults usually 6 Fr). Diameter does not affect symptoms |
| Length | Adults 22–28 cm by height (usually 24–26 cm); children about age + 10 cm |

**Diagram to draw:** double-J stent in situ with renal coil, side holes and bladder coil.

### Stent materials
| Material | Features |
|---|---|
| Polymers (polyurethane, silicone) | Silicone is most biocompatible and least encrusting but soft; hydrocoated silicone causes less pain |
| **Metallic (Resonance)** | Tightly coiled alloy that resists extrinsic compression; malignant obstruction and retroperitoneal fibrosis; changed about yearly |
| **Memokath 051** | Thermo-expandable nitinol for long-term strictures |
| Biodegradable | No removal, so no forgotten stent; still experimental |

### Indications
- Relief of obstruction: obstructed infected kidney (stent and PCN (percutaneous nephrostomy) are equally effective), malignant obstruction, stricture, pregnancy.
- After intervention: selected URS (ureteroscopy), pyeloplasty, reimplantation, transplant.
- Healing of injury, leak or fistula; pre-stenting to dilate a tight ureter before URS.

### Stent-related symptoms
- About four in five patients have frequency, urgency, haematuria or flank pain on voiding, measured by the validated **USSQ (Ureteral Stent Symptom Questionnaire)**.
- Mechanism: **intravesical stent irritating the bladder** and **reflux of urine up the stent** during voiding.
- Relief: **alpha-blockers and antimuscarinics**, less intravesical material, shortest dwell time.

### Complications
- **Misplacement** (submucosal, below the block) and **migration** (stent too short).
- **Stent failure** from compression, debris or encrustation; highest with malignant or irradiated ureters. New obstructive symptoms need imaging.
- **Encrustation** rises with dwell time, infection and stone-forming urine; it risks fracture or avulsion on removal.
- **Forgotten stent**: obstruction, sepsis, renal loss. A stent register prevents this.
- **FECal (Forgotten, Encrusted, Calcified) grading**: I minimal linear; II circular; III partial encrustation of one or both coils; IV complete encrustation of one coil; V both coils.

### Management of an encrusted stent
- Never pull it out by force. Assess the burden with NCCT (non-contrast computed tomography) and treat infection first.
- Clear the **bladder coil** first (cystolithotripsy), then the **ureter** (URS and laser), then the **renal coil** (flexible URS, or PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) for a large burden).

### Ureteroscopy
| | Semirigid | Flexible |
|---|---|---|
| Tip | 7 Fr or less | 4.5–7.5 Fr; 3.6 Fr working channel |
| Best for | Distal and mid ureter | Upper ureter and kidney |
| Deflection | None | More than 270°, both directions |

- **Safety guidewire first**: it holds access and allows a stent after perforation.
- **UAS (ureteral access sheath)**, usually 12/14 Fr, lowers intrarenal pressure and eases repeated passes, but caused **severe ureteric injury in about one in eight**; pre-stenting lowers this.
- **Ho:YAG (holmium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet) laser** is standard; **TFL (thulium fibre laser)** is the alternative. Dusting uses low energy at high frequency; basketing gives stone for analysis.
- A **tight ureter** is not forced: stent and return after **2–4 weeks**.
- Complications: sepsis about 5%, stricture about 3%, avulsion under 1%.

### Stenting after URS
- **Denstedt (2001)** randomised trial: URS is safe without a stent; a Cochrane review agrees.
- **AUA (American Urological Association)**: omit the stent when **all four** hold: normal anatomy, normal renal function, no ureteric injury, no planned second stage. **EAU (European Association of Urology)** also advises against routine stenting.
- **Stent required**: perforation, stricture or tight ureter, infection, planned second stage; also solitary kidney, impacted stone, pregnancy.
- Removal at 3 days gave more unplanned returns than at 7 days. A string allows home removal.

```
End of URS -> uncomplicated? (normal anatomy and function, no injury, no 2nd stage)
  Yes -> no stent (or short string stent)
  No  -> double-J: injury 2-6 wk; residual/impacted or 2nd stage until re-look
```

### Recent advances
- Suction access sheaths, single-use digital ureteroscopes, intrarenal pressure sensors, biodegradable stents.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Finney 1978: double-J stent.
> - Symptoms come from intravesical stent and reflux; alpha-blocker plus antimuscarinic helps.
> - Encrusted stent: clear bladder coil, then ureter, then kidney.
> - Denstedt 2001: no routine stent after uncomplicated URS (AUA four conditions).

## III-2. Describe medical management of VUR. Describe treatment of an 8-yr male child with b/l grade IV VUR.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Definition
- **VUR (vesicoureteral reflux)** is retrograde flow of urine from the bladder into the upper tract.
- **Primary** VUR: congenital deficiency of the UVJ (ureterovesical junction) flap-valve; tunnel length to ureteric diameter should be about **5:1** (Paquin).
- **Secondary** VUR: high bladder pressure or outlet obstruction, e.g. PUV (posterior urethral valves), neurogenic bladder, BBD (bladder and bowel dysfunction).
- Found in about **30% of children with UTI (urinary tract infection)** and about 30% of siblings.

### Pathophysiology
- **Infection, not reflux, is the injury.** Reflux carries infected urine to the kidney; compound papillae allow intrarenal reflux and **scarring**.
- Scarring causes hypertension, proteinuria and CKD (chronic kidney disease): **reflux nephropathy**.

### International (IRS, International Reflux Study) grading on VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram)
| Grade | Findings |
|---|---|
| I | Non-dilated ureter only |
| II | Ureter, pelvis and calyces, no dilatation |
| III | Mild–moderate dilatation, minimal forniceal blunting |
| IV | Moderate dilatation and tortuosity; **papillary impressions maintained** |
| V | Gross dilatation and tortuosity; **papillary impressions lost** |

**Diagram to draw:** grades I–V sketches and a Cohen cross-trigonal reimplant.

### Natural history
- Resolution falls with grade and age: grade II 63–85%, grade III about 50%, grade IV–V rarely.
- **Bilateral grade IV persists most** (AUA (American Urological Association) 1997: about 10% resolve in 5 years).

### Evaluation
- History of febrile UTI and BBD; BP (blood pressure), spine, urine culture, creatinine, urine protein.
- **RBUS (renal-bladder ultrasound)**: renal size, hydronephrosis, residual. A normal scan does not exclude VUR.
- **VCUG**: grade, and the urethra in boys to exclude PUV.
- **DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid) scan**: scars and differential function.

### Medical management
**1. Principle:** prevent UTI and scars while awaiting resolution. High risk: female, bilateral, grade III–V, BBD, scars, multiple febrile UTIs.

**2. CAP (continuous antibiotic prophylaxis):** single nightly dose, about one-quarter of the treatment dose.
| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| TMP-SMX (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) | **2 mg/kg TMP** | Avoid under 2 months (kernicterus) |
| Nitrofurantoin | **1–2 mg/kg** | Avoid in G6PD deficiency |
| Trimethoprim, amoxicillin or a first-generation cephalosporin | — | Young infants |

**3. Evidence**
- **RIVUR (2014)**: 607 children, grade I–IV, TMP-SMX vs placebo. Recurrent UTI fell from **23.6% to 12.9%**, but **scarring was unchanged**. Benefit was greatest with BBD; only about 8% had grade IV.
- **Swedish Reflux Trial**: age 1–2 years, grade III–IV. In **girls**, febrile UTI recurred in 19% on CAP, 23% after endoscopy and 57% on surveillance. **Boys showed no difference.**

**4. Treat BBD first**: timed voiding, treat constipation, anticholinergics. BBD increases breakthrough UTI and lowers resolution and endoscopic success.

**5. Circumcision**: in boys with VUR, about 4 circumcisions prevent one UTI.

**6. Follow-up**: RBUS every 6–12 months, cystogram every 12–24 months, BP.

**Indications for intervention**: breakthrough febrile UTI on CAP, new scars, persistent high-grade VUR, non-compliance, parental preference.

### Case: 8-year-old boy with bilateral grade IV VUR
**Assessment**
- Exclude PUV, neurogenic bladder and BBD. DMSA, creatinine, BP, UTI history.
- At 8 years, bilateral grade IV is unlikely to resolve. Older boys have a low UTI risk, so treating the asymptomatic boy is **controversial**.

**Options**
1. **CAP with BBD treatment**: if no febrile UTI, no scars, normal bladder.
2. **Endoscopic injection (Dx/HA, dextranomer/hyaluronic acid, Deflux)** by STING (subureteric injection) or HIT (hydrodistension implantation). Lower success in grade IV (about 60% per injection); 20% recurred by 2 years in the Swedish trial.
3. **Ureteral reimplantation**: open success **95.9%** (Elder meta-analysis 1997).
   - **Cohen cross-trigonal** (intravesical): commonest, over 98% success; one bladder opening corrects both sides; later ureteroscopy is difficult.
   - **Lich-Gregoir** (extravesical): no haematuria or spasm, but **transient retention in up to 10% after bilateral repair** (pelvic plexus injury).
   - **RALUR (robot-assisted laparoscopic ureteral reimplantation)**: less pain; results approaching open.

**Recommended plan**
- Breakthrough febrile UTI, scars or falling function: **bilateral Cohen reimplantation** (Deflux is less invasive but less durable).
- Asymptomatic, normal kidneys: CAP or observation with BBD therapy; correct if febrile UTI or new scar.
- After surgery: ultrasound at 6–12 weeks; VCUG after high-grade repair; long-term BP checks.

```
Exclude PUV / neurogenic bladder, treat BBD
 -> febrile UTI, scars or renal loss? Yes -> bilateral Cohen (or Deflux)
                                     No  -> CAP + BBD therapy, correct if UTI/scar
```

### Recent advances
- ceVUS (contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography); UDR (ureteral diameter ratio) and risk calculators predict resolution better than grade.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - IRS grade IV keeps papillary impressions; grade V loses them.
> - RIVUR 2014: CAP halves recurrent UTI (23.6% to 12.9%) but does not change scarring.
> - Open reimplantation 95.9%; Cohen over 98%; Deflux lower in grade IV.
> - Bilateral extravesical repair risks transient retention (up to 10%).

## III-3a. Sacral neuromodulation
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition and mechanism
- **SNM (sacral neuromodulation)**: continuous low-amplitude **S3 root** stimulation via a tined lead and an IPG (implantable pulse generator).
- It acts through **afferents, not motor nerves**, on forebrain areas of awareness. In OAB (overactive bladder) this damps the PMC (pontine micturition centre); in retention it restores filling sensation, so the PMC switches on and the sphincter relaxes. Hence it treats **both overactivity and retention**.

### Indications
| FDA (Food and Drug Administration) approved | Not approved |
|---|---|
| Refractory UUI (urgency urinary incontinence), 1997 | Bladder pain syndrome |
| Urgency-frequency and **non-obstructive retention** (e.g. Fowler's syndrome), 1999 | Neurogenic bladder |
| Faecal incontinence, 2011 | Pelvic pain |
- Third-line OAB therapy in American and European guidelines.

### Technique
- **Test phase**: **PNE (percutaneous nerve evaluation)** for **5–7 days**, or a **staged tined lead** for up to 4 weeks (more reliable; preferred for retention).
- S3 foramen: about **9 cm above the coccyx tip, 1–2 cm lateral**.
- **S3 response**: perineal "bellows" plus **great-toe flexion**.
- **Pass**: at least **50% improvement** in one diary parameter (or 50% fewer catheterisations); then implant the IPG in the upper buttock.

### Outcomes
- **InSite (2015)**: success 61% with SNM vs 42% with medical therapy at 6 months.
- **ROSETTA (2016)**: Botox 200 U reduced UUI slightly more, but caused more UTIs (urinary tract infections) (24% vs 10%) and self-catheterisation.

### Complications
- IPG-site pain, lead migration, loss of effect, infection; **revision over 30% at 5 years**.

### Contraindications
- Failed test phase, inability to use the programmer, sacral anomalies.

### Recent advances
- Rechargeable devices (15 years or more); **full-body MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) conditional at 1.5 and 3 T**.

**Diagram to draw:** sacrum with the lead in S3 and the IPG in the buttock.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Afferent modulation; treats both overactivity and retention.
> - S3 response: bellows plus great-toe flexion.
> - Implant if the test gives at least 50% improvement.

## III-3b. Uses of buccal mucosa in urology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction / history
- **BMG (buccal mucosal graft)** is the workhorse oral mucosal graft for urethral reconstruction.
- **Humby 1941** first used it for hypospadias; **Bürger 1992** and **El-Kasaby 1993** popularised it for stricture.

### Advantages
- Thick, **non-keratinised**, hairless epithelium used to a wet environment.
- **Thin, highly vascular lamina propria** allows rapid imbibition and inosculation, so graft take is excellent.
- Minimal contraction, easy harvest, hidden scar, low donor morbidity.

### Harvest technique
- Nasotracheal intubation. Graft about 4–6 × 2–2.5 cm per cheek.
- Stay about **1 cm from the lip commissure** and **avoid Stensen's (parotid) duct** opposite the upper second molar.
- Excise mucosa without muscle, **defat** the graft, cut it about 10% larger than the defect.
- Alternatives: **lingual** and **lip** grafts (lip is thinner, better in the glans).

### Donor-site complications
- Pain, numbness, mouth tightness, duct injury; mostly transient.

### Uses
| Area | Use |
|---|---|
| **Bulbar urethroplasty** (main use) | Dorsal onlay (**Barbagli**), ventral onlay, Asopa dorsal inlay, **augmented anastomotic repair** |
| **Panurethral stricture** | **Kulkarni** one-stage dorsal onlay via a perineal incision |
| **Staged urethroplasty** (Johanson, Bracka) | Graft first; tubularise after at least 6 months. For **lichen sclerosus** and failed hypospadias |
| **Hypospadias** | Redo cases with no local skin; dorsal inlay when the plate is narrow |
| **Others** | BMG perineal urethrostomy, robotic ureteroplasty, bladder neck contracture, female urethral stricture |

### Outcomes
- Bulbar BMG urethroplasty succeeds in about 85–90%. Dorsal equals ventral onlay; graft equals flap.
- Results fall with time and in penile, lichen sclerosus and redo cases. **Avoid skin in lichen sclerosus**; the disease recurs in skin.

### Recent advances
- Tissue-engineered oral mucosa (investigational).

**Diagram to draw:** cross-section of the bulbar urethra showing dorsal onlay, ventral onlay and Asopa inlay.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Thin vascular lamina propria gives excellent take; avoid Stensen's duct.
> - Staged BMG (Johanson/Bracka) for lichen sclerosus.
> - Bulbar BMG urethroplasty success about 85–90%.

## III-3c. HSG in neonates
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*HSG (hysterosalpingography) is not done in neonates, so this is taken as "USG (ultrasonography) in neonates".*

### Introduction
- First-line, radiation-free, repeatable imaging. Main use is **postnatal follow-up of antenatal UTD (urinary tract dilation)**, seen in 1–3% of pregnancies.
- Other uses: febrile UTI (urinary tract infection), abdominal mass, haematuria, renal vein thrombosis, ambiguous genitalia.

### Timing
- **First scan after 48 hours.** Newborns are relatively dehydrated in the first 24–48 hours, so earlier scans underestimate dilatation.
- **Scan early (within 24–48 hours) if bladder outlet obstruction is suspected**: bilateral severe dilatation, thick bladder, solitary kidney. Then decompress the bladder and start prophylaxis.
- A normal early scan can miss dilatation, so many repeat it at about 4–6 weeks.

### Normal neonatal kidney
- **Prominent hypoechoic pyramids** and a relatively bright cortex are normal and should not be mistaken for hydronephrosis.
- Measure renal pelvic **APD (anteroposterior diameter)** on a transverse mid-kidney view. Normal postnatal APD is **under 10 mm**.

### Grading of dilatation
- **SFU (Society for Fetal Urology) grades 0–4**: 0 none; 1 pelvis only; 2 major calyces; 3 major and minor calyces; 4 grade 3 plus parenchymal thinning.

**UTD classification 2014, postnatal (graded by the worst feature)**
| Grade | Findings | Management |
|---|---|---|
| P1 (low risk) | APD **10 to <15 mm** or central calyceal dilatation | Repeat USG at 3–6 months; no VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) or prophylaxis |
| P2 (intermediate) | APD **15 mm or more**, peripheral calyces or **dilated ureter** | VCUG and prophylaxis at discretion; prophylaxis if ureter >7 mm |
| P3 (high risk) | **Abnormal parenchyma** (thinning, cysts, echogenicity) or **abnormal bladder**, at any APD | USG at 1 month, VCUG, prophylaxis; MAG3 (mercaptoacetyltriglycine) scan if persistent |
- **Medullary pyramid thickness under 3 mm** predicts the need for pyeloplasty.

### Findings in specific conditions
| Condition | USG findings |
|---|---|
| PUJO (pelviureteric junction obstruction) | Dilated pelvis and calyces, normal ureter |
| VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) | Fluctuating dilatation; up to 25% with VUR have a **normal first scan** |
| **PUV (posterior urethral valves)** | Bilateral hydroureteronephrosis, **thick-walled bladder**, dilated posterior urethra: the **"keyhole sign"**, highly suggestive of PUV. Confirm with VCUG |
| Prune belly | Large bladder **without** a thick wall; dilated ureters |
| Ureterocele / duplex | Cystic intravesical lesion; dilated upper pole |
| MCDK (multicystic dysplastic kidney) | **Non-communicating** cysts of varying size, scant parenchyma |
| ARPKD (autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease) | Large, bright kidneys |

### Recent advances
- ceVUS (contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography) detects reflux without radiation.

**Diagram to draw:** SFU grades 0–4.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - First scan after 48 hours, except suspected PUV or bilateral severe dilatation.
> - UTD 2014: P1 APD 10 to <15 mm; P2 15 mm or more; P3 abnormal parenchyma or bladder.
> - Keyhole sign (thick bladder plus dilated posterior urethra) suggests PUV.

## III-3d. Types of penile prosthesis. What are biofilms in penile prosthesis?
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- Introduced by **Scott (1972, inflatable)** and **Small–Carrion (1978, semirigid)**.
- Used for ED (erectile dysfunction) when less invasive treatment fails, is contraindicated or is unacceptable. Patient and partner satisfaction is **80–90%**.

### Types
| Type | Examples | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Malleable (semirigid)** | Tactra (nitinol core), Genesis (silver), Rigi 10 | Simple, cheap, no mechanical failure; good for poor dexterity | Always rigid, hard to conceal |
| **2-piece inflatable** | Ambicor (now discontinued) | No abdominal reservoir | Less flaccidity; never popular |
| **3-piece inflatable** | AMS 700 CX/LGX/CXR, Coloplast Titan, Rigicon Infla 10 | **Best rigidity with true flaccidity** | Cost, mechanical failure |

### Components of the 3-piece device
- Paired cylinders, a scrotal pump, and a reservoir in the space of Retzius. After pelvic surgery the reservoir goes to an **ectopic submuscular** site; lockout valves prevent autoinflation.
- Repairs are needed in about **15% at 5 years and 30% at 10 years**.

### Biofilm
- **Definition**: a structured community of microorganisms in a self-produced **polysaccharide (EPS, extracellular polymeric substance) matrix**, attached to the implant surface.
- **Stages**: conditioning film, attachment, microcolonies, maturation, then dispersal causing clinical infection.
- **Organisms**: coagulase-negative staphylococci (S. epidermidis) commonest; also S. aureus, Gram-negatives and Candida.
- **Why antibiotics fail**: poor penetration of the matrix and slow-growing bacteria. Organisms survive at up to **1000–1500 times** the usual killing concentration, and the matrix shields them from host defences.
- **Clinical relevance**: bacteria introduced at the first operation lie dormant in biofilm. Revision of an uninfected device carries about 10% infection; an **antiseptic revision washout** lowers this to about 3%.

### Prevention
- **Infection rate 1–3%** for first implants; higher with diabetes, revision and immunosuppression.
- **Chlorhexidine-alcohol** skin preparation (about 40% fewer wound infections than povidone-iodine).
- Intravenous vancomycin or a third-generation cephalosporin plus gentamicin within 1 hour, for up to 24 hours.
- **Coated devices halve infection**: **InhibiZone** (AMS) with **rifampin–minocycline**; Coloplast **hydrophilic** coating soaked in antibiotic.
- **No-touch technique (Eid)**: under 0.5% infection in over 1500 cases.

### Management of infection
- Remove **all** components.
- **Mulcahy salvage**: remove the device, wash with an antiseptic series (dilute povidone-iodine, then saline or antibiotic), and implant a new device at once. Success **84%**. It fails with early onset, aggressive organisms, or pus with cellulitis.
- Otherwise explant and reimplant later; this loses corporal length.

**Diagram to draw:** 3-piece device in situ and the biofilm life cycle.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Types: malleable, 2-piece and 3-piece inflatable (best rigidity and flaccidity).
> - Biofilm is a microbial community in a matrix; S. epidermidis is commonest.
> - Infection 1–3%; coatings halve it; Mulcahy salvage succeeds in 84%.

## III-3e. Uretero-vaginal fistula
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **UVF (uretero-vaginal fistula)**: an abnormal communication between the ureter and vagina, causing continuous leakage of urine.

### Aetiology
- Usually **unrecognised iatrogenic injury to the distal third of the ureter**. About **three-quarters follow abdominal hysterectomy**, often an uneventful benign one.
- The ureter is at risk where it passes **under the uterine artery** and over the vaginal fornix. Ureteric injury occurs in 0.2–2.4% of hysterectomies.
- Others: caesarean section, obstetric trauma, cancer, radiation, endometriosis.

### Clinical features
- **Continuous leakage with normal voiding**, because the bladder still fills from the other kidney.
- Usually appears **within 4 weeks** of surgery, often with fever, flank pain or ileus. Thermal or ischaemic injury takes time to necrose.

### Investigations
- Confirm urine: creatinine of vaginal fluid.
- **Double dye test**: oral phenazopyridine stains urine orange; blue dye is placed in the bladder. An **orange tampon means UVF**, blue means VVF (vesicovaginal fistula), both means both. The three-swab test is an alternative: a wet but unstained swab suggests UVF.
- **CT (computed tomography) urography with delayed phase**: leak, hydroureteronephrosis. Ultrasound alone is not enough.
- **Cystoscopy and retrograde pyelogram**: site and continuity; try a stent at the same sitting.
- **Exclude a coexisting VVF** (up to 25% of VVF have a UVF) and bilateral injury.

### Management
**1. Stent first (endourological)**
- **Retrograde double-J stent** if a guidewire crosses; otherwise PCN (percutaneous nephrostomy) and antegrade stent.
- **Stenting within 2 weeks** of detection succeeds far more often. Keep it **4–6 weeks**, then re-image. Most fistulae heal; stricture may follow.

**2. Surgical repair** (stent fails or impossible)
- **Ureteroneocystostomy**, with **psoas hitch** (about 5 cm) or **Boari flap** (up to 15 cm) for longer gaps. Open, laparoscopic or robotic.
- Timing: early repair has equal success to delayed repair (no difference before or after 6 weeks) and shortens suffering.
- Success **90% or more**.

```
Wet with normal voiding -> double dye test, CTU, cystoscopy (exclude VVF)
 -> wire passes? Yes -> double-J 4-6 wk -> re-image
                 No  -> PCN + antegrade stent
 -> fails -> ureteroneocystostomy + psoas hitch / Boari flap
```

### Follow-up and prevention
- Ultrasound after stent removal; stricture can appear up to 30 months later.
- Identify the ureter at hysterectomy; prophylactic stents only in selected high-risk cases.

**Diagram to draw:** double dye test; psoas hitch.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Continuous leak with normal voiding; orange tampon on double dye test = UVF.
> - Exclude coexisting VVF (up to 25% of VVF have UVF).
> - Early stent (within 2 weeks); reimplant with psoas hitch if it fails.

## III-3f. Prune belly syndrome
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **PBS (prune belly syndrome)**, also called Eagle–Barrett or triad syndrome:
  1. Deficient abdominal wall muscles (wrinkled "prune" abdomen).
  2. Dilated, poorly contracting urinary tract.
  3. Bilateral intra-abdominal testes.
- Incidence about **1 in 29,000–44,000** live births; **95% male**.
- **Prognosis depends on the kidneys (dysplasia), not the abdominal wall.**

### Pathogenesis (none proven)
- **Urethral obstruction theory**: transient fetal outlet obstruction distends the bladder and abdomen. Most patients, however, have no obstruction after birth.
- **Mesodermal theory**: a lateral plate mesoderm defect affects the abdominal wall, urinary tract and gubernaculum.
- Mostly sporadic; smooth-muscle gene variants (MYOCD, FLNA) in familial cases.

### Genitourinary features
- Kidneys: dysplasia in about 50%; decides outcome.
- Ureters: dilated and tortuous, **worse distally**; collagen replaces muscle, so peristalsis fails. **VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) in about 75%.**
- Bladder: large, smooth, thick-walled, urachal diverticulum; poor emptying.
- Prostatic hypoplasia with a dilated prostatic urethra; megalourethra; infertility.

### Extra-genitourinary features (about 75%)
- Pulmonary hypoplasia; cardiac defects about 10%; gastrointestinal (malrotation) at least 30%; orthopaedic up to 65% (talipes, hip dysplasia, scoliosis).

### Woodard classification
| Category | Features | Outlook |
|---|---|---|
| I | Oligohydramnios, **pulmonary hypoplasia**, renal dysplasia, often urethral atresia | Stillbirth or neonatal death; supportive care only |
| II | Full triad, moderate uropathy, **no pulmonary hypoplasia** | Risk of urosepsis and CKD (chronic kidney disease); management debated |
| III | Mild or incomplete features, **stable renal function** | Good; the majority |

### Management
- **Neonatal**: cardiorespiratory care first, chest X-ray, creatinine trend, ultrasound. Prophylaxis and circumcision. VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) only for renal insufficiency or outlet obstruction, under antibiotic cover.
- **Category III**: conservative, with surveillance for infection and dilatation.
- **Drainage** for sepsis, rising creatinine or obstruction: **cutaneous vesicostomy** (Blocksom), made wide. Avoid proximal ureterostomy, which wastes the better proximal ureter.
- **Reconstruction** (after 3–6 months): ureteric tailoring and reimplantation using the healthier proximal ureter; reduction cystoplasty; CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) if emptying is poor.
- **Orchidopexy**: bilateral **transabdominal at about 6 months**; staged Fowler–Stephens if vessels are short.
- **Abdominoplasty**: Randolph, Ehrlich or **Monfort**, often with orchidopexy and urinary surgery.

### Prognosis
- About 20% die perinatally. **Up to 30% develop chronic renal failure** in childhood or adolescence. Nadir creatinine **under 0.7 mg/dL** predicts stable function. Transplant results equal other causes.
- Females (5%) have no gonadal anomaly; "pseudo-prune belly" is an incomplete form presenting late.

**Diagram to draw:** VCUG with large bladder, urachal diverticulum and dilated prostatic urethra.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Triad: deficient abdominal wall, dilated urinary tract, intra-abdominal testes; 95% male.
> - Renal dysplasia, not the abdominal wall, decides prognosis; Woodard I–III.
> - Vesicostomy if drainage needed; orchidopexy at about 6 months; nadir creatinine under 0.7 mg/dL is good.
