## Q1. Radionuclide scans and its applications in uro
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in Practice Set 2 Paper 1, Q3d–Q3f)

*"Uro" is read as urology.*

### Principles
- Nuclear medicine shows **function, not structure**. A tracer, given in a tiny (subpharmacological) dose, follows a physiological or molecular process; the patient is the radiation source.
- **Gamma camera:** photon, collimator (admits photons from one direction), sodium iodide crystal (light), photomultiplier tubes, image.
- **PET (positron emission tomography):** the positron annihilates with an electron, giving two 511 keV photons 180° apart, detected in coincidence. This rejects noise and allows true quantitation (SUV, standardised uptake value).

| | Planar | SPECT (single-photon emission computed tomography) | PET |
|---|---|---|---|
| Image | 2D | 3D, with CT (computed tomography) | 3D, with CT or MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) |
| Resolution | About 20 mm | About 10 mm | About 5 mm |
| Quantitation | Semiquantitative | Semiquantitative | SUV |
| Main use | Renal function | Problem-solving | Cancer |

- **⁹⁹ᵐTc (technetium-99m):** 6-hour half-life, single 140 keV photon: the workhorse. **¹⁸F:** about 110 minutes, lowest positron energy, so the sharpest PET image. ⁶⁸Ga comes from a generator; ¹¹C (20 minutes) needs an on-site cyclotron.

### Renal tracers
- Plasma clearance is about 20% filtration and 80% tubular secretion.

| | ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DTPA (diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid) | ⁹⁹ᵐTc-MAG3 (mercaptoacetyltriglycine) | ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handling | Filtration only | Protein-bound; tubular secretion | Filtered, then retained in proximal tubules (megalin, cubilin) |
| Study | Dynamic | Dynamic | Static cortical at 2 hours |
| Use | **GFR (glomerular filtration rate)**; captopril study | **Split function, obstruction (first choice)**; transplant | **Pyelonephritis, scars** |
| Renal failure | Poor images | Good images | Delay imaging |

### Dynamic renography (MAG3)
- **Preparation:** hydrate; catheter if outlet obstruction or neurogenic bladder; cap nephrostomy. Dose 5–10 mCi IV (intravenous).
- **Perfusion phase:** 1–2 second frames; the kidney appears within seconds of the aorta.
- **Function phase:** **split function = area under each curve at 1–3 minutes**; normal up to 45:55. Peak at 3–5 minutes; bladder by 10–15 minutes.
- **Diuretic renography:** dilatation is not obstruction. Furosemide 0.5 mg/kg (adult maximum 40 mg) separates a patulous system from obstruction. Timing (F+20, F+15, F0, F−15) must be stated.

| T½ (half-clearance time) after furosemide | Meaning |
|---|---|
| <10 minutes | Not obstructed |
| 10–20 minutes | Indeterminate |
| >20 minutes | Obstructed |

### Other renal applications
- **Captopril renography (DTPA):** the stenotic kidney keeps GFR by angiotensin II efferent constriction. Captopril removes this, so uptake falls. Improvement ≥10% off ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors means renovascular hypertension is likely. Largely replaced by CT angiography.
- **Transplant:** acute rejection: perfusion falls and worsens; delayed graft function: perfusion kept, improves. Detects urine leak without iodinated contrast.
- **DMSA:** pyelonephritis gives an ill-defined defect that resolves by about 6 months; a scar is sharp, with cortical thinning, and persists.
- **Radionuclide cystography:** direct (tracer instilled into the bladder) or indirect (after MAG3). More sensitive and lower dose than VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram), but little anatomy. So VCUG first; nuclear cystography for follow-up.

### Infection imaging
- **¹¹¹In-oxine leucocytes** (image at 24 hours): best for occult abdominal or genitourinary infection. ⁹⁹ᵐTc-HMPAO (hexamethylpropyleneamine oxime) leucocytes give better images, but urinary excretion obscures the tract. Neither in a transplant (leucocytes home to the graft).
- **Also know:** ⁶⁷Ga-citrate (non-specific, slow).

### Oncology
- **FDG (¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose):** a glucose analogue phosphorylated and trapped (Warburg effect). Urinary excretion limits it to distant disease.
  - **Seminoma residual mass >3 cm** after chemotherapy (EAU [European Association of Urology]). Not for NSGCT (non-seminomatous germ cell tumour), because teratoma is not FDG-avid.
  - Penile cancer with palpable nodes; equivocal bladder or upper-tract metastases.
  - Poor in prostate cancer and clear cell RCC (renal cell carcinoma).
- **Prostate:** **PSMA (prostate-specific membrane antigen) PET** is preferred (NCCN [National Comprehensive Cancer Network]) for high-risk staging and biochemical recurrence. Specificity >90%; nodal sensitivity only 30–40%. Calais trial (2019): detection 56% (PSMA) versus 26% (fluciclovine). Bone scan (⁹⁹ᵐTc-MDP, methylene diphosphonate) remains conventional.
- **Renal mass:** **sestamibi SPECT** "hot" in oncocytoma; **girentuximab** (anti-CAIX, carbonic anhydrase IX) PET "hot" in clear cell RCC (ZIRCON trial).
- **Also know:** ¹²³I-MIBG (metaiodobenzylguanidine) and ⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE for phaeochromocytoma; ⁹⁹ᵐTc-nanocolloid sentinel node biopsy in penile cancer; ⁹⁹ᵐTc-pertechnetate scrotal scan (torsion cold, epididymitis hot) when ultrasound is equivocal.

### Therapy (theranostics)
- **¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA-617:** beta emitter for mCRPC (metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer) after ARPI (androgen receptor pathway inhibitor) and taxane: **VISION (2021)**. PSMA PET confirms the target first.
- **Radium-223:** alpha emitter for bone-only mCRPC.

### Advantages and limitations
- Quantitative function, low radiation, safe in contrast allergy.
- Poor anatomy; PET is costly; results depend on technique.

**Diagram to draw:** MAG3 renogram: perfusion spike, peak at 3–5 minutes, washout; obstructed curve rising.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - DTPA = GFR; MAG3 = obstruction; DMSA = scars.
> - Split function at 1–3 minutes; T½ >20 minutes obstructed.
> - PSMA PET preferred; FDG for residual seminoma >3 cm.
> - Sestamibi hot = oncocytoma; ¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA-617 (VISION).

## Q2. Describe embryology and anatomy of kidney.. Describe role of nephron sparing surgery in a small renal mass.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2017-Summer Paper 1, Q1 and Practice Set 1 Paper 1, Q1; NSS techniques: see Practice Set 13 Paper 2, Q1)

### Embryology
- Three successive kidneys form from **intermediate mesoderm**, in cranial-to-caudal order:

| Kidney | Time and site | Fate |
|---|---|---|
| Pronephros | Week 3–4, cervical | Non-functional; regresses |
| Mesonephros | Week 4, thoracolumbar | Functions briefly; tubules become efferent ductules; **mesonephric (Wolffian) duct** persists |
| **Metanephros** | Week 5, sacral | **Definitive kidney** |

- **Reciprocal induction:** the **ureteric bud (UB)** grows from the caudal Wolffian duct (about week 5) into the **metanephric blastema**.
  - UB branches into ureter, pelvis, calyces and collecting ducts.
  - Blastema, induced by UB tips, forms nephrons (glomerulus to distal tubule).
- **Molecular control:** GDNF (glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor) from the blastema acts on RET on the UB; SLIT2/ROBO2 restricts GDNF to one bud. Also WT1, PAX2.
- Urine production is established by 14 weeks; nephrogenesis ends before birth.
- **Ascent and rotation (weeks 6–9):** the kidney rises from the pelvis to the lumbar region and rotates 90° so the hilum faces medially. It takes successively higher arteries; persistent lower ones become accessory arteries.
- **Clinical correlation:** no UB gives agenesis; abnormal induction gives MCDK (multicystic dysplastic kidney); failed ascent gives ectopia; fused lower poles trapped by the IMA (inferior mesenteric artery) give horseshoe kidney; also malrotation, duplication.

### Anatomy
- T12–L3; right 1–2 cm lower (liver). About 11 cm, 125–170 g. Lies on psoas: upper pole medial and posterior; angled 30–50° behind the coronal plane.
- **Relations:** right: liver, duodenum (2nd part), hepatic flexure; left: spleen, stomach, pancreatic tail, splenic flexure. Posterior: diaphragm, psoas, quadratus lumborum, subcostal nerve.
- **Coverings:** capsule, perirenal fat, **Gerota fascia** (closed above and laterally, open below), pararenal fat.
- **Structure:** cortex; 8–18 pyramids; 0.4–1.2 million nephrons.
- **Vessels (basis of partial nephrectomy):**
  - Hilum, front to back: vein, artery, pelvis.
  - **Five segmental end arteries:** posterior (first branch) plus apical, upper, middle, lower. Each can be clamped or resected separately.
  - **Brödel line:** avascular plane behind the lateral border.
  - Accessory arteries in 25–28%. **Veins anastomose freely**, so a segmental vein can be ligated.
- **Also know:** lymph drains to para-aortic (left) and paracaval or interaortocaval nodes (right).

**Diagram to draw:** transverse section with segmental arteries and Brödel line.

### Small renal mass (SRM)
- Enhancing mass **≤4 cm (cT1a)**, mostly incidental. 20–30% are benign (about 40% at ≤2 cm). Under 2% metastasise.
- **Biology:** about 20% benign, 60% indolent, 20% potentially aggressive.
- **Work-up:** multiphase CT or MRI; chest imaging; eGFR (estimated GFR) and proteinuria (CKD [chronic kidney disease] stage); **RMB (renal mass biopsy)** when it would change management (non-diagnostic about 14%). R.E.N.A.L. nephrometry (Radius, Exophytic, Nearness to collecting system, Anterior/posterior, Location): 4–6 low, 7–9 moderate, 10–12 high complexity.

### Why spare nephrons
- RN (radical nephrectomy) causes CKD, linked to cardiovascular events and death. PN (partial nephrectomy) keeps about 90% of global function versus 65–70% after RN, with equal cancer control in cT1a.

### Choosing the option (AUA [American Urological Association] 2021)

| Option | Role |
|---|---|
| **Active surveillance** | <2 cm or predominantly cystic; elderly, comorbid, life expectancy <5 years. Imaging every 3–6 months. Intervene if >3 cm or growth >5 mm a year |
| **Thermal ablation** (cryoablation, radiofrequency) | cT1a **<3 cm**, percutaneous, biopsy first. Higher local recurrence, salvaged by repeat ablation |
| **Partial nephrectomy** | **Prioritised for cT1a** needing treatment. Imperative: solitary kidney, bilateral tumours, CKD or proteinuria; also familial RCC (renal cell carcinoma), young patients |
| **Radical nephrectomy** | Only if all three: high complexity; no CKD or proteinuria; normal contralateral kidney with new GFR likely >45 |

- **PN principles:** negative margin (width does not matter); maximise preserved parenchyma; avoid long warm ischaemia; enucleation for familial or multifocal tumours. Approaches, steps and complications: see Practice Set 13 Paper 2, Q1.

### Evidence
- **EORTC (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer) 30904 (2011):** PN versus RN for ≤5 cm: better function and similar cancer control with PN; survival slightly favoured RN, but the trial is flawed.
- **DISSRM (Delayed Intervention and Surveillance for Small Renal Masses) registry:** surveillance almost never led to metastasis; about 24% had delayed intervention.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Ureteric bud gives collecting system; blastema gives nephrons (GDNF–RET).
> - Five segmental end arteries; Brödel line; veins anastomose.
> - SRM: surveillance <2 cm, ablation <3 cm, PN standard for cT1a.
> - AUA: RN only if complex + no CKD + GFR likely >45.

## Q3a. MOA of botulinum toxin. Indications and dosages in UROLOGY
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2025-Winter Paper 2, Q3d and 2017-Winter Paper 3, Q3b)

### Introduction
- *Clostridium botulinum* neurotoxin, serotypes A–G. Urology uses **onabotulinumtoxinA (BoNT-A)**, the only licensed formulation.

### Mechanism of action
1. The heavy chain binds the cholinergic nerve terminal; the toxin is internalised.
2. The light chain (a zinc protease) **cleaves SNAP-25** (synaptosomal-associated protein 25), so vesicles cannot fuse and **acetylcholine release stops**: chemical denervation.
- **Afferent action:** less ATP (adenosine triphosphate) and substance P; fewer purinergic (P2X3) and capsaicin (TRPV1) receptors, so urgency and pain fall.
- Lasts about 6–9 months, until terminals sprout; repeat injections stay effective.

### Technique
- Cystoscopic intradetrusor injection, trigone usually spared: 100 U in 10 mL at 20 sites; 200 U in 30 mL at 30 sites. Check PVR (post-void residual) at about 2 weeks.

### Indications and doses

| Indication | Dose and evidence |
|---|---|
| **Idiopathic OAB (overactive bladder)**, third line | **100 U** (EMBARK) |
| **NDO (neurogenic detrusor overactivity):** SCI (spinal cord injury), MS (multiple sclerosis) | **200 U** (DIGNITY); works with or without antimuscarinics |
| **Paediatric NDO** | About **10 U/kg (maximum 360 U)**, about 30 sites (Campbell series); FDA (Food and Drug Administration) label 6 U/kg, maximum 200 U |
| **DSD (detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia)** | Intrasphincteric injection, mainly men |
| **IC/BPS (interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome)** | Intradetrusor, often trigonal; AUA option, off-label |
| **BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia)** | 100–300 U intraprostatic: large RCTs (randomised controlled trials) equal to saline; not used |

- **Also know:** pelvic floor spasm; chronic orchialgia (RCT negative).

### Complications and contraindications
- **Retention needing CISC (clean intermittent self-catheterisation)**, more in NDO; each 10 mL of baseline PVR raises the risk about 27%. The patient must accept CISC beforehand.
- UTI (urinary tract infection), haematuria; rarely generalised weakness. Avoid in active UTI, myasthenia gravis, pregnancy.

**Diagram to draw:** cholinergic synapse with SNAP-25 cleavage.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Cleaves SNAP-25: efferent and afferent action.
> - 100 U idiopathic OAB; 200 U NDO.
> - Children 10 U/kg (maximum 360 U); FDA 6 U/kg (maximum 200 U).
> - Retention needing CISC is the main risk.

## Q3b. Vanishing testes
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- A testis that **developed normally and was then lost**, usually from **torsion or a vascular accident in fetal life**, after genital masculinisation but before scrotal fixation.
- Unilateral loss gives **monorchia**; bilateral loss gives **anorchia** (testicular regression syndrome), which is rare.
- **Evidence:** haemosiderin in the excised "nubbin".

### Incidence
- **15–40% of nonpalpable testes** at surgery (abdominal or "peeping" testis 25–50%; extra-abdominal 10–30%).

### Clinical features
- **Contralateral compensatory hypertrophy:** volume >2 mL (sensitivity 72%, specificity 100%) or length about 19–20 mm suggests a solitary testis.
- No palpable intrascrotal "appendage" tissue; sometimes a scrotal nubbin.
- These signs help counselling but **do not replace exploration**.
- **Anorchia:** micropenis in nearly half.

### Evaluation
- **Imaging** (ultrasound, MRI [magnetic resonance imaging]) is not recommended: unreliable, and it does not change the operation.
- **Bilateral nonpalpable:** exclude DSD (difference of sex development) and CAH (congenital adrenal hyperplasia) first.
  - Anorchia: high FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) and LH (luteinising hormone); low AMH (anti-Müllerian hormone) and inhibin B; no rise after hCG (human chorionic gonadotrophin). Most reliable during mini-puberty.

### Management: diagnostic laparoscopy (after examination under anaesthesia)

| Laparoscopic finding | Action |
|---|---|
| **Blind-ending vessels and vas** above a closed ring | Diagnosis confirmed; stop |
| Vessels and vas entering the internal ring | Inguinal or scrotal exploration; excise the nubbin |
| Testis seen | Orchidopexy (single stage or Fowler–Stephens) |
| Neither vas nor vessels | Search up to the kidney (agenesis very rare) |

- **Excise the remnant:** viable germ cells or tubules in 5–15%.
- **Scrotal-first exploration** is reasonable with a palpable nubbin and contralateral hypertrophy (length ≥1.8 cm); laparoscopy is then needed in about a quarter.
- **Contralateral fixation:** debated; not required by the AUA (American Urological Association) algorithm.
- **Prosthesis:** offer at least 6 months after scrotal surgery or after puberty, inguinal approach.
- **Anorchia:** testosterone from puberty.
- **Also know:** agenesis shows ipsilateral Müllerian persistence.

**Diagram to draw:** laparoscopic view of blind-ending spermatic vessels above a closed internal ring.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Prenatal torsion after masculinisation; 15–40% of nonpalpable testes.
> - Contralateral hypertrophy (>2 mL) suggests it, but explore anyway.
> - Blind-ending vessels confirm; excise remnant (germ cells 5–15%).
> - Anorchia: high FSH/LH, low AMH and inhibin B.

## Q3c. Management of failed pyeloplasty
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition of failure
- Persistent or recurrent pain, UTI (urinary tract infection) or stones; **obstructive diuretic renogram** (T½ >20 minutes); increasing hydronephrosis; or fall in split function.
- Dilatation settles slowly, so judge by renography and trend.
- About 1 in 9 children need a secondary procedure. Most failures occur within 1–2 years, but up to 30% occur later.

### Causes
- **Ischaemia** (over-stripping) and **tension** at the anastomosis.
- **Missed crossing vessel**; inadequate excision of the narrow segment; non-dependent anastomosis.
- **Urine leak** or infection causing periureteric fibrosis.
- Missed distal stricture or high-grade VUR (vesicoureteral reflux).

### Early failure (first days or weeks)
- Pain, fever, increasing dilatation: usually **oedema or clot**; temporary nephrostomy or stent, and most settle.

### Evaluation of late failure
- **MAG3 (mercaptoacetyltriglycine) diuretic renogram:** obstruction and split function.
- **CT (computed tomography) angiography:** crossing vessel, stricture length.
- Antegrade or retrograde pyelography; **Whitaker test** if equivocal. Nephrostomy first if infected, then reassess function.

### Treatment options

| Option | When | Results |
|---|---|---|
| **Endopyelotomy** (retrograde or antegrade) | Short stricture, no crossing vessel; first choice after failed open repair | About 70% |
| **Redo pyeloplasty** (open, laparoscopic, robotic) | Long or dense stricture, crossing vessel | About 86–89%; best choice in most children |
| **Ureterocalicostomy** | Scarred, small intrarenal pelvis; dilated lower calyx | About 70% (redo series) |
| **Buccal mucosa onlay** (robotic, omental wrap) | Long scarred UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) | Failure about 15–25% (CORRUS) |
| **Nephrectomy** | Split function <15%, normal other kidney; multiple failures | Definitive |

- **Also know:** ileal ureter or autotransplantation for long ureteric loss.

### Principles of redo surgery
- Transperitoneal route in children (retroperitoneum is scarred).
- Ureteric catheter; wide mobilisation of kidney and ureter; **excise all scar**; spatulate; **tension-free, watertight** anastomosis over a stent; wrap with omentum or perirenal fat.
- **Also know:** near-infrared fluorescence shows perfusion; downward nephropexy.

### Follow-up
- Stent out at 4–6 weeks; renogram at about 1 month, then 6 and 12 months; follow adults for 2–3 years.

**Diagram to draw:** ureterocalicostomy: lower-pole calyx joined to spatulated ureter.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Causes: ischaemia, tension, missed crossing vessel, leak.
> - Failed open repair: endourology first; redo pyeloplasty most reliable.
> - Ureterocalicostomy for small scarred pelvis; buccal onlay emerging.
> - Nephrectomy if split function <15%.

## Q3d. Circumcision undone
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*"Circumcision undone" is read as an incomplete or complicated circumcision needing revision. Foreskin restoration is mentioned briefly at the end.*

### Introduction
- Circumcision complications: **0.2–5%**, rising **10- to 20-fold after infancy**.
- Most late problems concern **skin quantity** (too little or too much removed) or the **exposed meatus**.

### Inadequate ("undone") circumcision
- **Too little skin removed** or asymmetrical excision: redundant prepuce covering the glans.
- Leads to **secondary (cicatricial) phimosis**, smegma retention, balanitis, adhesions and cosmetic distress.
- A large hydrocele or hernia pushes skin forward and raises this risk.

### Other complications

| Complication | Management |
|---|---|
| **Bleeding** (commonest, about 0.1%), usually frenular | Pressure, cautery, silver nitrate or suture |
| Glanular adhesions; **skin bridges** (can cause chordee, torsion) | Office lysis or excision; steroids do not help |
| **Excess skin removal**, degloving | Heals by secondary intention with ointment; severe: grafts |
| **Trapped penis** (cicatrix over glans) | Betamethasone 0.05% and retraction; else relaxing incision, flaps, Z-plasty |
| **Meatal stenosis** (almost only after circumcision) | Meatotomy or meatoplasty |
| Subcoronal fistula; glans amputation; necrosis (cautery on clamp) | Hypospadias-type repair; reattach glans within 8 hours |

### Evaluation
- Original procedure (age, device); examine skin quantity, meatus, curvature; exclude hypospadias, buried or webbed penis and BXO (balanitis xerotica obliterans).

### Circumcision revision
- **Indications:** redundant or asymmetrical skin, recurrent phimosis, adhesions, cosmetic concern.
- **Needs general anaesthesia** in children, unlike neonatal circumcision.
- **Technique (sleeve):** mark inner and outer incisions (first 5–10 mm from the corona), excise the excess collar, bipolar haemostasis, interrupted absorbable sutures (continuous sutures risk strangulation).
- Lyse adhesions and skin bridges; correct torsion or chordee; meatotomy if needed; histology if BXO suspected.

### Prevention
- Separate prepuce fully and inspect the meatus before using a device (Gomco, Mogen, Plastibell); defer with hypospadias, curvature, buried or webbed penis; trained operator.

### Also know: foreskin restoration
- Traction tissue expansion or skin grafting, for men wanting a foreskin; results variable.

**Diagram to draw:** sleeve revision with two circumferential incisions.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Complications 0.2–5%; bleeding commonest.
> - Too little skin: redundancy, phimosis, adhesions; too much: trapped penis, chordee.
> - Meatal stenosis almost only in circumcised boys.
> - Revision needs general anaesthesia.

## Q3e. Female urethral diverticula
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **UD (urethral diverticulum):** a urine-filled periurethral sac within the periurethral fascia, connected to the urethra by an **ostium**.
- Third to seventh decades; prevalence up to 1–6% in some series.

### Aetiology
- Mostly **acquired**. **Routh theory** (most accepted): infection of **periurethral glands** leads to duct obstruction, suburethral abscess, rupture into the lumen and an epithelialised sac.
- Hence the ostium is **posterolateral at mid or distal urethra** in ≥90%.
- **Also know:** childbirth trauma theory (but 20–30% are nulliparous).

### Clinical features
- "Three Ds" (dysuria, dyspareunia, post-void dribbling) in only about 5%.
- Commonest: frequency, urgency, pain, **recurrent UTI (urinary tract infection)**.
- Tender anterior vaginal wall mass; about one-third incontinent.
- **Complications:** stones (4–10%); carcinoma (**adenocarcinoma** commonest); 10% show metaplasia, dysplasia or cancer.

### Evaluation
- Urinalysis, culture; **cystourethroscopy** (ostium seen in under half).
- **Imaging sensitivity:** **MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) 97%**, double-balloon urethrography 95%, ultrasound 82%, VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) 74%.
- MRI: low T1, high T2; shows saddlebag or circumferential extension.
- **Videourodynamics** separates true SUI (stress urinary incontinence) from dribbling from the sac.
- **Differential diagnosis:** Skene cyst (distal, distorts meatus, no communication), Gartner cyst, leiomyoma, caruncle, bulking agent.

### Classification
- **L/N/S/C3 (Leach):** location, number, size, configuration, communication, continence.
- **Also know:** Leng–McGuire (absent fascia = pseudodiverticulum).

### Management
- **Asymptomatic or unfit:** suppressive antibiotics and post-void stripping; counsel about cancer risk.
- **Symptomatic: transvaginal diverticulectomy.**
- **Principles:**
  1. Sterile urine; topical oestrogen if atrophic.
  2. **Inverted-U** anterior vaginal wall flap.
  3. **Preserve periurethral fascia** as a separate layer.
  4. Excise the **entire sac** and close the ostium.
  5. **Watertight** urethral closure over a 12–14 Fr catheter.
  6. **Multilayered, non-overlapping** closure; obliterate dead space.
  7. **Martius flap** if poor tissue.
- **Bothersome SUI:** concomitant **autologous pubovaginal sling**; **never synthetic mesh**.
- **Also know:** Spence–Duckett marsupialisation for distal lesions only.
- **Postoperative:** pericatheter VCUG at 14–21 days.

### Complications
- Recurrence 10–22%; de novo SUI 7–16%; urethrovaginal fistula 1–8%; stricture; recurrent UTI.

**Diagram to draw:** sagittal section showing mid-urethral sac with posterolateral ostium.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Periurethral gland infection (Routh); posterolateral ostium.
> - MRI most sensitive (97%).
> - Preserve fascia; non-overlapping layers; Martius flap.
> - No synthetic mesh at diverticulectomy.

## Q3f. Endoscopic procedure of SUI
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Read as endoscopic (transurethral or periurethral) injection of urethral bulking agents for stress urinary incontinence.*

### Introduction
- **SUI (stress urinary incontinence)** results from urethral hypermobility and **ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency)**.
- **Bulking agents**, injected submucosally under cystoscopic vision, are the least invasive surgical option.

### Mechanism
- Bulk at the proximal urethra improves **mucosal coaptation** and urethral resistance.
- It does not correct hypermobility, but most SUI includes some ISD, so it can still help.

### Indications
- ISD with a **fixed, immobile urethra** (low VLPP [Valsalva leak point pressure] <60 cmH₂O, historically).
- Women wanting a **low-risk** office procedure; elderly or unfit; future pregnancy.
- Persistent leakage after a sling; neurogenic SUI (safer for bladder compliance than a sling).
- **Contraindications:** active UTI (urinary tract infection), untreated detrusor overactivity, small-capacity bladder.

### Agents

| Agent | Note |
|---|---|
| Polyacrylamide hydrogel (Bulkamid) | Non-particulate; widely used |
| Polydimethylsiloxane (Macroplastique) | Silicone particles |
| Calcium hydroxylapatite; carbon-coated zirconium beads | Particulate |
| Bovine collagen (skin test); PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene); autologous fat | Withdrawn or abandoned: allergy, migration, granuloma, embolism |


### Technique
1. Sterile urine; local anaesthesia; cystoscope (0–30° lens) in the urethra.
2. **Transurethral:** needle into the submucosa about 1–1.5 cm distal to the bladder neck. **Periurethral:** needle alongside the urethra through the vestibule, guided cystoscopically.
3. Inject at 2–4 sites until the mucosal cushions **coapt in the midline**.
4. Avoid an indwelling catheter (it moulds the bulk); for retention use short-term CISC (clean intermittent self-catheterisation).

### Outcomes
- Early improvement is common, but cure is lower than with slings and **declines with time**; repeat injections are often needed.
- **Cochrane review (Kirchin 2017):** limited evidence of benefit.
- **AUA/SUFU (American Urological Association/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction):** an option; counsel about lower durability and retreatment.
- **Men:** EAU (European Association of Urology): **do not offer** for post-prostatectomy incontinence.

### Complications
- Transient retention, UTI, dysuria, haematuria, urgency.
- Sterile abscess, migration, erosion; the mass may mimic a urethral diverticulum on MRI (magnetic resonance imaging).

**Diagram to draw:** cystoscopic view before and after injection, showing coapted mucosal cushions.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Bulking improves coaptation; does not correct hypermobility.
> - Best for fixed urethra with ISD, or unfit patients.
> - Less durable than slings; repeat injections common.
> - Not recommended for post-prostatectomy incontinence.
