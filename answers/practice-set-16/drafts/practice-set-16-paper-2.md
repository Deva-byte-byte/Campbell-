## Q1. Outline the management of muscle invasive TCC. Discuss the role of adjuvant & neoadjuvant chemotherapy.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(MIBC management also answered in Practice Set 12 Paper 2, Q2; neoadjuvant therapy in 2025-Winter Paper 4, Q1)

### Introduction
- MIBC (muscle-invasive bladder cancer; TCC, transitional cell carcinoma, now called urothelial carcinoma) is cT2–T4a disease.
- Nearly half of patients treated by RC (radical cystectomy) alone develop metastases from occult micrometastases. Cure therefore needs good local treatment plus systemic therapy, planned by an MDT (multidisciplinary team).

### Staging
- Complete re-TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour) including detrusor muscle; bimanual examination under anaesthesia.
- MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) with VI-RADS (Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System) for local stage; CT (computed tomography) urogram for upper tracts and nodes; chest CT; bone scan if symptoms.
- Assess fitness, renal function and cisplatin eligibility.

### Management algorithm
```
cT2–T4a N0 M0
 ├─ Cisplatin-eligible → cisplatin NAC → RC + bilateral PLND
 ├─ Cisplatin-ineligible → RC + PLND directly
 │      (pT3–4 or N+ after either → adjuvant nivolumab or chemotherapy)
 ├─ Selected, motivated → trimodality therapy (TMT)
 └─ Unfit → chemoradiation / palliative RT
cN+ → induction chemotherapy → consolidation RC in responders
M1  → systemic therapy
```

**1. Radical cystectomy (standard of care)**
- Men: cystoprostatectomy. Women: anterior exenteration; organ-sparing in selected cases.
- **Standard bilateral PLND (pelvic lymph node dissection):** obturator, external and internal iliac nodes. **SWOG S1011:** extended PLND gave no survival benefit.
- Do not delay beyond **12 weeks** from diagnosis (more extravesical disease, worse survival). Open or robotic.
- Diversion: ileal conduit, orthotopic neobladder (tumour-free urethra) or continent cutaneous pouch.

**2. Bladder preservation: TMT**
- Visibly complete TURBT + radiotherapy (64 Gy, or 55 Gy in 20 fractions) + radiosensitiser. **BC2001:** 5-FU (fluorouracil) + mitomycin C improved 2-year locoregional disease-free survival (67% vs 48%).
- Best candidates: cT2, solitary tumour, **no CIS (carcinoma in situ), no hydronephrosis**, good bladder function.
- 5-year cancer-specific survival 50–84%; salvage cystectomy 10–30%.
- **Also know:** partial cystectomy (solitary dome or urachal tumour, no CIS); chemotherapy alone or radical TURBT alone is not recommended.

**3. Metastatic disease**
- First line: **EV (enfortumab vedotin) + pembrolizumab** (EV-302: about doubled overall survival vs platinum). Alternatives: platinum chemotherapy then avelumab maintenance; erdafitinib for FGFR3 (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3) alterations.

### Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC)
**Rationale**
- Treats micrometastases early, while tumour burden is low, and is better tolerated before surgery.
- Downstages; **pT0 at cystectomy** gives about 90% 5-year recurrence-free survival.
- Drawback: delays surgery in non-responders.

**Eligibility:** cT2–T4a N0 M0 and cisplatin-fit. **Cisplatin-ineligible** if any one: GFR (glomerular filtration rate) <60 mL/min, ECOG (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group) performance status ≥2, grade ≥2 hearing loss or neuropathy, NYHA (New York Heart Association) class III–IV failure. About half of patients are ineligible.

| Trial | Regimen | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **SWOG 8710** | MVAC (methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, cisplatin) ×3 then RC | pT0 38% vs 15%; median survival 77 vs 46 months; P = 0.06 |
| **BA06 30894** (EORTC/MRC) | CMV (cisplatin, methotrexate, vinblastine) ×3 | 16% relative reduction in mortality; 10-year OS (overall survival) 36% vs 30% |
| **ABC meta-analysis** (Advanced Bladder Cancer) | Cisplatin combinations | OS HR (hazard ratio) 0.86: **5% absolute 5-year survival gain** |
| **VESPER** | ddMVAC (dose-dense MVAC) ×6 vs GC (gemcitabine–cisplatin) ×4 | Organ-confined response 77% vs 63%; 3-year PFS (progression-free survival) 66% vs 56%; more toxicity |
| **NIAGARA** | Durvalumab + GC, then adjuvant durvalumab, vs GC | Better event-free survival (HR 0.68) and OS (HR 0.75) |

- **Regimens:** ddMVAC 3–6 cycles or GC 4 cycles (EAU, AUA, NCCN: European Association of Urology, American Urological Association, National Comprehensive Cancer Network).
- **No benefit:** single-agent cisplatin, carboplatin regimens, neoadjuvant radiotherapy. Cisplatin-ineligible patients go straight to RC.
- The chapter calls neoadjuvant immunotherapy unproven; NIAGARA has since made perioperative durvalumab + GC a guideline option.

### Adjuvant therapy
| | Neoadjuvant | Adjuvant |
|---|---|---|
| Selection | Clinical stage (understaging risk) | True pathological stage, but still overtreats |
| Delivery | Better tolerated | Post-RC morbidity; a quarter never receive it |
| Evidence | Level 1 | Weaker: small, early-closed trials |

**Adjuvant chemotherapy**
- Trials (German MVAC/MVEC, Italian GC, EORTC 30994) were small or closed early. **ABC 2022 update:** cisplatin-based adjuvant chemotherapy improves 5-year OS by about 6%. Benefit greatest in node-positive and extravesical disease.
- Use: cisplatin-naive patients with **pT3–4 or N+**: ddMVAC or GC.

**Adjuvant immunotherapy**
- **CheckMate 274:** nivolumab for 1 year after RC (pT3–4a or N+, with or without prior NAC): median disease-free survival **20.8 vs 10.8 months**.
- **IMvigor010** (atezolizumab): negative overall (included pT2); benefit in ctDNA (circulating tumour DNA)-positive patients.
- Use nivolumab if cisplatin-ineligible or declining chemotherapy, or with ypT3 or worse after NAC.
- **Also know:** adjuvant pelvic RT (Cairo trials) improves pelvic control in pT3–4; ctDNA-guided therapy is emerging.

**Diagram to draw:** bladder wall T-staging (T2a, T2b, T3, T4) beside the standard PLND template.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - NAC: cisplatin combinations only; ABC 5% absolute survival gain; SWOG 8710 pT0 38% vs 15%.
> - VESPER: ddMVAC beat GC; NIAGARA: perioperative durvalumab improved event-free and overall survival.
> - CheckMate 274: adjuvant nivolumab disease-free survival 20.8 vs 10.8 months.
> - TMT: T2, no CIS, no hydronephrosis; salvage cystectomy 10–30%.

## Q2. Desc. mgt optns for locally adv. CaP. Discuss complic. of b/l orchidectomy.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

*Assumed meaning: describe the management options for locally advanced carcinoma prostate, and discuss the complications of bilateral orchidectomy.*

### Definition and staging
- **Locally advanced CaP (carcinoma prostate):** T3–T4, N0 or N+, M0. Clinically: extracapsular spread (cT3a), seminal vesicle invasion (cT3b), adjacent organs (cT4). Today most "high-risk" disease is defined by grade and PSA (prostate-specific antigen) rather than palpation.
- At least 10% of new diagnoses; these men contribute a disproportionate share of cancer deaths.
- **Staging:** mpMRI (multiparametric MRI) for local extent; bone scan + CT, or **PSMA PET (prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography)**, which is more accurate (proPSMA: 92% vs 65%).
- Untreated, Gleason 8–10 disease carries high cancer mortality, so fit men need treatment; no single modality suffices, hence **combined therapy**.

### Management options
**1. Radiotherapy + long-term ADT (androgen deprivation therapy): the commonest standard**
- Dose-escalated EBRT (external-beam radiotherapy, ≥78 Gy) with or without pelvic node RT; **brachytherapy boost** improves biochemical control (ASCENDE-RT).
- ADT works synergistically with RT: neoadjuvant, concurrent and adjuvant, **18–36 months** for high risk.

| Trial | Finding |
|---|---|
| **EORTC 22863** | 3 years of goserelin with RT: 5-year OS 79% vs 62% |
| **RTOG 85-31 / 92-02** | Long adjuvant ADT better; OS gain in Gleason 8–10 |
| **TROG 96.01** | 6 months better than 3 months |
| **SPCG-7; MRC PR07** | ADT + RT beats ADT alone (overall survival gain) |
| **STAMPEDE** (high-risk M0) | Adding 2 years of abiraterone: metastasis-free survival HR 0.53 |
| **RTOG 0521; GETUG 12** | Docetaxel added modest benefit |

**2. Radical prostatectomy + extended PLND (pelvic lymph node dissection)**
- For fit men with **completely resectable** disease (cT3a, selected cT3b, limited nodes). Clinical overstaging occurs in 7–27%, so cT3 alone is no bar.
- Disease-specific survival 85–92% at 5 years and 79–82% at 10 years, but biochemical recurrence is common, so multimodal care is planned.
- **Neoadjuvant ADT before RP:** clinically downstages, but no survival or PSA benefit.
- **Adjuvant/salvage RT:** EORTC 22911, SWOG 8794, ARO 96-02 showed benefit; RADICALS-RT and RAVES favour **early salvage RT** (PSA rise) for most men.
- **pN+:** adjuvant ADT (ECOG 7887: better survival) ± RT.

**3. ADT alone**
- For men unfit for, or declining, local therapy. Early ADT delays progression; survival gain mainly in aggressive disease.
- Options: bilateral orchidectomy, LHRH (luteinising hormone-releasing hormone) agonist or antagonist; bicalutamide 150 mg monotherapy preserves libido.

**4. Others**
- Watchful waiting with deferred ADT in elderly comorbid men.
- **Also know:** cryotherapy and HIFU (high-intensity focused ultrasound) remain investigational; TURP or stents for local obstruction; trial enrolment.

### Bilateral orchidectomy
- Removes about 95% of testosterone: **castrate level (<20 ng/dL) within 12 hours**. Cheap, no compliance issues, no flare. Simple or subcapsular (all Leydig tissue must be removed).

**A. Surgical complications**
- Scrotal haematoma (commonest), wound infection, chronic scrotal or groin pain.
- Psychological: empty scrotum and altered self-image. Prevent with subcapsular or epididymis-sparing technique, or a testicular prosthesis.
- Irreversible: intermittent ADT is impossible.

**B. Systemic effects of castration**
| Effect | Details and management |
|---|---|
| Hot flushes | Commonest (50–80%); venlafaxine, gabapentin, megestrol or cyproterone if bothersome |
| Sexual dysfunction | Loss of libido, ED (erectile dysfunction), penile shortening; ED aids help selected men |
| Osteoporosis, fractures | Fracture risk up 20–45%; baseline DEXA (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry), FRAX; calcium, vitamin D, exercise; **denosumab 60 mg 6-monthly** or zoledronic acid |
| Metabolic | Weight gain, fat gain, **sarcopenia**, insulin resistance, diabetes (HR about 1.34), metabolic syndrome |
| Cardiovascular | Coronary events, thromboembolism; fewer than with LHRH agonists |
| Anaemia | Normocytic; reversible after stopping |
| Others | Gynaecomastia (≤5%), fatigue, depression, cognitive change |

- **Also know:** risk of castration resistance over time; check testosterone with rising PSA.

**Diagram to draw:** T3a, T3b and T4 extension of a prostate tumour, and subcapsular orchidectomy.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Locally advanced = T3–4, N0/N+, M0; treat with combined therapy.
> - RT + 18–36 months ADT: EORTC 22863 OS 79% vs 62%; STAMPEDE abiraterone MFS HR 0.53.
> - Neoadjuvant ADT before RP: no survival benefit; early salvage RT favoured.
> - Orchidectomy: castrate in 12 hours; flushes, osteoporosis (denosumab), metabolic, sexual effects.

## Q3a. Vesicourodynamics
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2016-Summer Paper 4, Q3d)

*Assumed meaning: videourodynamics (VUDS), i.e. urodynamics with simultaneous fluoroscopic imaging of the bladder and urethra.*

### Definition
- **VUDS:** multichannel urodynamics (pressure–flow, with or without EMG [electromyography]) recorded together with fluoroscopy, using **contrast as the filling fluid**. It is the most precise test of lower urinary tract function.

### Set-up
- Vesical and rectal catheters (Pdet [detrusor pressure] = Pves − Pabd), flowmeter, EMG; C-arm (most flexible) or tilting table.
- SUI (stress urinary incontinence) views standing; voiding in the usual position (women sitting, men standing).
- **Screen only at key moments** (provocation, pressure rise, voiding) to limit radiation.

### What fluoroscopy adds
| Situation | VUDS finding |
|---|---|
| NLUTD (neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction) | **Gold standard**; DESD (detrusor–external sphincter dyssynergia) and **DISD** (detrusor–internal sphincter dyssynergia, seen only on VUDS); trabeculation, diverticula |
| **VUR** (vesicoureteric reflux) | Pressure and volume at onset, grade; reflux "pops off" pressure, so compliance looks falsely safe |
| **Bladder neck** | Only way to assess it; **PBNO** (primary bladder neck obstruction) |
| Female obstruction | **Nitti criteria:** radiographic narrowing between bladder neck and meatus with a sustained detrusor contraction |
| Dysfunctional voiding | "Spinning-top" urethra at the external sphincter; more accurate than surface EMG |
| SUI | Leakage with ALPP (abdominal leak point pressure); open bladder neck at rest suggests ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency) |
| Male obstruction | Localises prostatic obstruction, bladder neck contracture or stricture |

**PBNO in young men:** type I high pressure, low flow; type II normal pressure, low flow, narrow bladder neck; type III delayed bladder-neck opening.

### Indications
- Known or suspected NLUTD; unexplained retention in women; young men with LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms); complex or recurrent incontinence; prior radical pelvic surgery or radiation; urinary diversion; before or after renal transplant.
- Children: PUV (posterior urethral valves), spinal dysraphism.

### Risk factors it shows
- Poor compliance, DLPP (detrusor leak point pressure) >40 cm H₂O, high-pressure detrusor overactivity, DESD.

### Limitations
- Radiation, cost, space, contrast allergy. EAU: optimum test in NLUTD; AUA/SUFU (Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction): lowest dose that answers the question.

**Diagram to draw:** VUDS trace (Pves, Pabd, Pdet, flow, EMG) beside a voiding cystourethrogram image.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - VUDS = urodynamics + fluoroscopy with contrast.
> - Only test for bladder neck dysfunction, PBNO and DISD.
> - Reflux pop-off makes compliance look falsely good.

## Q3b. Early surgical intervention in urethral injuries
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(posterior urethral injury also answered in Practice Set 5 Paper 3, Q1)

### Principles
- Key acute decision: **immediate repair versus deliberate delay**. Immediate repair suits clean, localised injuries (penile fracture, low-velocity wounds). PFUI (pelvic fracture urethral injury) is drained and reconstructed later.
- **Blood at the meatus mandates RUG (retrograde urethrogram)** before catheterisation. Classification: partial, minor distraction, major distraction; anterior: contusion, incomplete, complete (Armenakas–McAninch).

### Posterior urethra (PFUI)
- **SPC (suprapubic cystostomy) is the standard first step:** percutaneous 16 Fr Foley via peel-away sheath, or open (allows bladder inspection). Safe even with pelvic plating; tunnel it high.
- **Primary realignment** (AUA Urotrauma guideline option): brief attempt in a stable patient, acutely or within days, by combined antegrade and retrograde flexible cystoscopy over a guidewire.
  - Catheter out at 4–6 weeks; keep the SPC; remove it 7–14 days later if voiding is good.
  - Most still form a **1–2 cm stenosis**; prospective data show **no difference** in urethroplasty rates vs SPC. Modern realignment does not add ED (erectile dysfunction) or incontinence. Avoid prolonged attempts (infection).
- Partial tear: one gentle catheter attempt, position confirmed radiologically.
- **Immediate open repair** only for associated **bladder neck or rectal injury**, or when laparotomy is needed anyway.
- **Also know:** female urethral injuries are usually repaired or realigned early; children may have injuries extending into the bladder neck.

### Anterior urethra
| Injury | Early management |
|---|---|
| Contusion, incomplete straddle tear | Urethral catheter alone |
| Major straddle injury | SPC (standard); primary realignment for lesser injuries; delayed anastomotic urethroplasty |
| **Low-velocity gunshot or stab** | **Immediate primary repair:** debride minimally, spatulate, tension-free anastomosis over a catheter |
| High-velocity or close-range shotgun | SPC, staged repair |
| **Penile fracture with urethral tear** | **Immediate repair:** partial tear oversewn; complete tear debrided and anastomosed; catheter 2–3 weeks |

- Penile fracture: about 20% have urethral injury in Western series (about 3% in Asia); **intraoperative flexible cystoscopy** before catheterisation.
- Limit spongiosal debridement: its blood supply is robust.

**Diagram to draw:** RUG showing partial vs complete posterior urethral disruption.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Blood at meatus → RUG; SPC is standard for PFUI.
> - Primary realignment: brief, endoscopic; equal urethroplasty rates.
> - Immediate repair: penile fracture urethra, low-velocity penetrating injury, bladder neck/rectal injury.

## Q3c. TVT
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(TVT versus TOT also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 2, Q3e)

### Definition and history
- **TVT (tension-free vaginal tape):** retropubic **MUS (mid-urethral sling)** of polypropylene for SUI (stress urinary incontinence). Ulmsten and Petros, 1995; renamed TVT in 1998.

### Mechanism
- **Integral theory** (Petros and Ulmsten, 1990): continence needs pubourethral ligaments, the suburethral vaginal hammock and pubococcygeus. Hypermobility is a sign of their damage, not the cause.
- The loose tape secures the **posterior urethral wall** during stress, with compression and dynamic kinking. It is not tight at rest.

### Material
- **Type 1 (Amid) macroporous monofilament polypropylene**, pores >75 µm: allows macrophage entry and fibrous ingrowth, so it resists infection.

### Indications
- Index SUI with urethral hypermobility; preferred over TOT (transobturator tape) in **ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency)** or recurrent SUI.
- Counsel about mesh risks and alternatives (Burch colposuspension, autologous sling, bulking). Avoid if urethral injury occurs intraoperatively.

### Technique
1. Local, spinal or general anaesthesia; lithotomy.
2. Two suprapubic stab incisions 2 cm lateral to midline; 1.5 cm midline vaginal incision 1.5 cm from the meatus; minimal dissection, endopelvic fascia not opened.
3. **Bottom-up** 5 mm trocar hugging the back of the pubis, through the retropubic space to the skin (catheter guide deflects the bladder).
4. **Cystoscopy with a 70° lens, full bladder**, after each pass; if perforated, re-pass more laterally.
5. Tension **loosely** with a clamp between tape and urethra; remove sheath.

### Outcomes
- Ulmsten (1998): 91% cured at 1 year. **Ward–Hilton:** equal to colposuspension at 5 years. **TOMUS:** 80.8% (retropubic) vs 77.7% (TOT) at 12 months. Long-term cure varies 48–97% by definition.

### Complications
| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Bladder perforation | 4.5% (TOT 0.6%); re-pass and continue |
| Urethral injury | Abandon mesh; staged repair |
| Bleeding/haematoma | 2–3%; rarely bowel or vascular injury |
| Voiding dysfunction | Surgery needed 2.7%; incise tape in midline within 3 months |
| Mesh exposure | About 2% |
| Others | De novo urgency, suprapubic pain (groin pain less than TOT) |

**Diagram to draw:** coronal pelvis with U-shaped retropubic tape at mid-urethra.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Integral theory; loose tape at mid-urethra.
> - Type 1 macroporous monofilament polypropylene.
> - Cystoscopy after each trocar pass; bladder perforation 4.5%.

## Q3d. Undescended testis
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2018-Summer Paper 3, Q3a and 2024-Winter Paper 1, Q2)

### Definition and classification
- Testis not in the normal scrotal position: **palpable** (canalicular, high scrotal, **ectopic**, commonest at the superficial inguinal pouch) or **non-palpable** (intra-abdominal, "peeping", vanished/absent).
- **Retractile:** can be brought down and stays; yearly review (may ascend). **Acquired:** documented scrotal before, ascends later.

### Epidemiology and aetiology
- 2–4% of term boys at birth, 1–2% at 3 months; far commoner in preterm boys. Most descend by 3–6 months.
- Descent needs Leydig hormones: **INSL3** (transabdominal phase) and **androgens** (inguinoscrotal phase), with genitofemoral nerve/CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide) guiding the gubernaculum.

### Evaluation
- Warm room, supine and frog-leg; "milk" from the anterior superior iliac spine. Note contralateral hypertrophy (suggests vanished testis).
- **No ultrasound or MRI** (magnetic resonance imaging): they do not change management. Diagnostic laparoscopy is the investigation of choice for non-palpable testes.
- **Bilateral non-palpable, or with hypospadias:** urgent karyotype and hormones to exclude **CAH** (congenital adrenal hyperplasia) or other DSD (differences of sex development).

### Management
- **Observe to 6 months corrected age; operate after 6 months, preferably by 12, and by 18 months at the latest** (AUA 2014, EAU similar). Early surgery restores testicular growth.
- **Hormones (hCG [human chorionic gonadotrophin], LHRH [luteinising hormone-releasing hormone]): not recommended**; descent in about 20% only.

| Situation | Operation |
|---|---|
| Palpable | Inguinal orchidopexy (mobilise cord, ligate processus, subdartos pouch) or scrotal (Bianchi) |
| Non-palpable | Examination under anaesthesia, then laparoscopy |
| Testis seen, vessels long | Laparoscopic orchidopexy (primary most successful) |
| Short vessels | **Fowler–Stephens** one- or two-stage (testicular vessels divided; survives on vasal collaterals) or Shehata traction |
| Vessels and vas enter ring | Inguinal or scrotal exploration; excise nubbin |
| Blind-ending vessels | Stop: vanished testis |
| Postpubertal, normal contralateral testis | Orchidectomy |

- **Complications:** atrophy (about 2%, higher after Fowler–Stephens), re-ascent, vas injury.

### Prognosis
- **Fertility:** paternity about four-fifths in unilateral and one-third in bilateral cases.
- **Cancer:** risk 2–5 times; orchidopexy after 10–11 years carries relative risk 5.8 (Walsh). Teach self-examination.

**Diagram to draw:** laparoscopic view of the internal ring: testis, vessels entering ring, blind-ending vessels.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Operate from 6 months, preferably by 12, latest 18 months.
> - No imaging, no hormones; laparoscopy for non-palpable testis.
> - Bilateral non-palpable: exclude CAH/DSD.

## Q3e. Sperm Retrival techniques
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2016-Summer Paper 3, Q3b and 2021-Summer Paper 4, Q2)

*Assumed meaning: sperm retrieval techniques.*

### Indications
- **OA (obstructive azoospermia):** CBAVD (congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens), unreconstructable obstruction, failed or declined vasectomy reversal, back-up at reconstruction.
- **NOA (non-obstructive azoospermia):** only testicular retrieval works.
- Anejaculation (spinal cord injury): penile vibratory stimulation or electroejaculation first.
- Retrieved sperm need **ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection)**; cryopreserve spares.
- **Do not operate** with AZFa or AZFb (azoospermia factor) complete deletion.

### Techniques
| Technique | Method | Best for | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PESA** (percutaneous epididymal sperm aspiration) | Fine needle into caput, local anaesthesia | OA | Few sperm; may obstruct epididymis |
| **MESA** (microsurgical epididymal sperm aspiration) | Single tubule opened at 16–25×; micropipette by **capillary action**, never suction | **OA (best)** | Microsurgical skill |
| **TESA** (testicular sperm aspiration) | 23 G needle, suction, many passes | OA | Immotile sperm, small yield; poor in NOA |
| Percutaneous core biopsy | 14 G biopsy gun | OA | Blind; vessel injury |
| **TESE** (testicular sperm extraction) | Open biopsy, one or more sites | OA, NOA | Blind sampling |
| **Micro-TESE** | Testis bivalved; larger opaque tubules picked under the microscope | **NOA (best)** | Long operation |

- **MESA:** move proximally until motile sperm appear; the most motile sperm are often in the efferent ductules. Yields 4–12 pipettes for multiple frozen vials.
- **Micro-TESE (Schlegel 1999):** spermatogenesis in NOA is **focal**. Plump tubules hold sperm; thin white ones are Sertoli cell–only. Incise through an avascular plane, map positive sites; if negative, explore the other testis.

### Results
- OA: retrieval almost always succeeds. NOA micro-TESE: sperm in **about half**.

| Condition | Retrieval |
|---|---|
| Post-orchidopexy | 74% |
| AZFc deletion | 70% |
| Klinefelter | 68% |
| Post-chemotherapy | 53% |
| Maturation arrest | 40% |
| Sertoli cell–only | 30% |
| AZFa/AZFb deletion | 0% |

- NOA pregnancies: more miscarriage (aneuploidy, DNA damage). Frozen sperm equal fresh, except in spinal cord injury.

### Complications
- Haematoma (commonest), infection, epididymal obstruction (PESA), testicular atrophy or fall in testosterone (TESE).
- **Also know:** post-mortem retrieval needs prior consent; microfluidic sperm sorting.

**Diagram to draw:** micro-TESE: bivalved testis with plump tubules beside thin Sertoli cell–only tubules.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - OA: MESA (capillary action); NOA: micro-TESE.
> - Micro-TESE finds sperm in about half of NOA.
> - AZFa/AZFb deletion: 0%, do not operate.

## Q3f. Salvage procedures for Complicated PUJO.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Assumed meaning: salvage options for recurrent PUJO (pelviureteric junction obstruction) after a failed pyeloplasty or endopyelotomy.*

### Definition and causes
- **Failure:** persistent pain or infection, unchanged or worse hydronephrosis, obstructed renogram or falling function. Most failures occur within 2 years, but up to 30% appear later.
- **Causes:** missed crossing vessel, ischaemic or tense anastomosis, urine leak with fibrosis, kinking, non-dependent anastomosis, infection.

### Evaluation
- Diuretic renogram (MAG3, mercaptoacetyltriglycine): split function and drainage.
- CT (computed tomography) angiography for a crossing vessel; retrograde or antegrade pyelogram for stricture length.
- Drain first (stent or nephrostomy) if infected or function is uncertain, then reassess.

### Salvage options
| Option | Indication | Results/notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Endopyelotomy** (antegrade or retrograde ureteroscopic, laser) | Short stricture, no significant crossing vessel, moderate hydronephrosis | About 70% after failed pyeloplasty; lateral incision; stent 4–6 weeks |
| **Redo pyeloplasty** (open, laparoscopic, robotic) | Most failures; failed endopyelotomy | About 83–89%; wide mobilisation of kidney and ureter; transperitoneal (scarred retroperitoneum) |
| **Ureterocalicostomy** | Scarred or small intrarenal pelvis, dilated lower-pole calyx | About 70% in redo series; **resect** lower-pole parenchyma (not nephrotomy) |
| **Buccal mucosa onlay graft** | Long scarred UPJ or proximal ureter, dense scar | Robotic with omental wrap; failure 15–25% (CORRUS) |
| **Appendiceal onlay flap** | Right-sided proximal stricture | Pedicled on appendiceal artery |
| Flaps (spiral, vertical), downward nephropexy | Gain length | Combine with redo repair |
| **Ileal ureter** | Long defect | Contraindicated if creatinine >2 mg/dL, bladder dysfunction, IBD (inflammatory bowel disease), radiation |
| **Autotransplantation** | Solitary or poor contralateral kidney; other repairs impossible | About 90% long term |
| **Nephrectomy** | Function <15% with normal contralateral kidney | Laparoscopic |

- Rule of thumb: failed pyeloplasty → try endourology first if anatomy suits; failed endopyelotomy → redo pyeloplasty.
- **Also know:** robotic near-infrared fluorescence (indocyanine green) to identify ureter and perfusion; Davis intubated ureterotomy (historical).

**Diagram to draw:** ureterocalicostomy: lower-pole parenchyma excised, spatulated ureter sewn to calyx.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Redo pyeloplasty is most successful (about 85–90%).
> - Ureterocalicostomy: resect lower-pole parenchyma; about 70% in redo series.
> - Function <15% with normal other kidney: nephrectomy.
