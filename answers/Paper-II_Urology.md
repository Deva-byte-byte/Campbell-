## II-1. Describe medical management of BPH and role of various surgical modalities in its management.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks) | ~1500 words

### Introduction / Definition
- **BPH** = histological diagnosis (stromal + epithelial hyperplasia of transition zone); **BPE** = enlargement; **BPO** = obstruction from BPE; presents as **LUTS** (storage, voiding, post-micturition).
- Goals of therapy: relieve bother, improve QoL, prevent progression (AUR, surgery, renal damage).
- Sources: Campbell: BPH – evaluation, medical and surgical management chapters; **AUA guideline 2021, amended 2023**; **EAU Non-neurogenic Male LUTS guideline**.

### Pathophysiology relevant to therapy
- **Static component** – prostate bulk (androgen/DHT dependent) → target of 5-ARIs, surgery.
- **Dynamic component** – α1A-adrenergic smooth-muscle tone in prostate/bladder neck → target of α-blockers.
- **Bladder response** – detrusor overactivity/underactivity → antimuscarinics, β3-agonists.

### Evaluation (brief)
- History, **IPSS** (7 questions, score 0–35: mild 0–7, moderate 8–19, severe 20–35 + 1 QoL question 0–6), frequency-volume chart (3 days, for nocturia/polyuria).
- DRE, urinalysis, **PSA** (if life expectancy >10 y or if it changes management; also surrogate of volume).
- Optional: serum creatinine (if renal impairment suspected), uroflowmetry (Qmax <10 mL/s suggests BOO), PVR.
- **Before surgery** (AUA): measure prostate size and shape (TRUS/TAUS/cystoscopy/CT/MRI) – choice of modality depends on it; PVR, Qmax; pressure-flow study if diagnosis uncertain (e.g. Qmax >10, neurological disease, young men, large PVR).

### Medical management
**1. Watchful waiting / behavioural (WW)**
- For mild or non-bothersome symptoms: education, fluid timing, reduce caffeine/alcohol, double voiding, urethral milking, bladder retraining, review drugs (diuretics, anticholinergics, decongestants).

**2. α1-adrenergic blockers** (first line for moderate–severe bothersome LUTS; act within days)

| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Terazosin | 1→10 mg OD (titrate) | Non-selective; orthostatic hypotension; bedtime dosing |
| Doxazosin | 1→8 mg OD (GITS 4–8 mg) | Non-selective; titrate |
| Alfuzosin | 10 mg OD (ER) | "Clinically uroselective" – least ejaculatory dysfunction |
| Tamsulosin | 0.4 mg OD | α1A/α1D selective; ejaculatory dysfunction |
| Silodosin | 8 mg OD (4 mg in renal impairment) | Most α1A selective; highest anejaculation |

- Improve IPSS ~30–40% and Qmax modestly; **do not reduce prostate volume or AUR/surgery risk** in long term.
- Side effects: dizziness, postural hypotension, asthenia, nasal congestion, ejaculatory dysfunction.
- **IFIS (Intraoperative Floppy Iris Syndrome)** – billowing iris, progressive miosis, iris prolapse during cataract surgery; strongest with tamsulosin; **advise men planning cataract surgery to defer starting α-blocker** (stopping beforehand does not reliably prevent it; inform ophthalmologist).

**3. 5α-reductase inhibitors (5-ARIs)**
- **Finasteride 5 mg OD** (type 2 isoenzyme); **Dutasteride 0.5 mg OD** (types 1 and 2).
- Indicated with **enlarged prostate** (AUA: palpable enlargement, PSA >1.5 ng/mL or volume >30 mL; EAU: >40 mL).
- Reduce volume by **~20–25%** over 6–12 months; **halve PSA** (multiply by 2 for interpretation after 6 months); reduce AUR and BPH surgery risk by ~50%.
- Also used for BPH-related haematuria (finasteride).
- Side effects: decreased libido, ED, ejaculatory disorder, gynaecomastia. PCPT and REDUCE showed increased detection of high-grade cancer → FDA label warning (2011).

**4. Combination α-blocker + 5-ARI**
- **MTOPS (McConnell, NEJM 2003)**: doxazosin + finasteride vs either vs placebo; combination reduced overall clinical progression risk by **66%** vs placebo (doxazosin 39%, finasteride 34%); only finasteride/combination reduced AUR and invasive therapy.
- **CombAT (Roehrborn, 2010, 4-yr)**: dutasteride + tamsulosin in men with volume ≥30 mL, PSA 1.5–10; combination superior for symptoms; reduced AUR/BPH surgery by **~66% vs tamsulosin** (not significantly different from dutasteride alone).
- Indicated in men with **enlarged prostate at risk of progression**; consider stopping α-blocker after 6–12 months.

**5. Drugs for storage symptoms**
- **Antimuscarinics** (solifenacin 5–10 mg, tolterodine 4 mg ER, fesoterodine, oxybutynin) – alone or add-on to α-blocker for persistent OAB; caution if **PVR >250–300 mL** (retention risk); cognitive side-effects in elderly (anticholinergic burden).
- **β3-agonists**: mirabegron 25–50 mg OD, vibegron 75 mg OD – add-on to α-blocker; no cognitive burden; monitor BP (mirabegron).

**6. PDE5 inhibitor**
- **Tadalafil 5 mg OD** – improves LUTS (±ED), not Qmax significantly; contraindicated with nitrates; combination with α-blocker not recommended by AUA for LUTS (no added benefit, hypotension).

**7. Nocturnal polyuria**
- **Desmopressin** (low-dose; oral/sublingual/nasal) for nocturia due to nocturnal polyuria; check **serum sodium** at baseline, within first week and periodically (hyponatraemia risk higher in elderly).

**8. Phytotherapy**
- Saw palmetto (Serenoa repens), Pygeum africanum, beta-sitosterol – AUA: **not recommended** (no proven benefit; CAMUS/STEP trials of saw palmetto negative). EAU: hexane-extracted Serenoa may be offered with caution.

```
Male LUTS → evaluation (IPSS, DRE, U/A, PSA, FVC)
  Mild/non-bothersome ──────────────→ Watchful waiting + lifestyle
  Bothersome:
    Voiding predominant ──→ α-blocker (± 5-ARI if prostate >30–40 mL)
    Storage persists ─────→ add antimuscarinic / β3-agonist (PVR check)
    Nocturnal polyuria ───→ desmopressin (Na monitoring)
    With ED ──────────────→ tadalafil 5 mg
  Failure / complications / patient choice → Surgery (size-based)
```

### Surgical management
**Indications (AUA 2023 / EAU)**
- Absolute: **refractory urinary retention** (failed TWOC), **recurrent UTI**, **recurrent gross haematuria** refractory to 5-ARI, **bladder stones**, **renal insufficiency/upper tract dilatation** due to BPO, large bladder diverticula (relative).
- Relative: moderate–severe bothersome LUTS failing or declining medical therapy.

**Modalities (sizes per AUA 2021/2023 guideline)**

| Modality | Prostate size | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **TURP** – monopolar (M-TURP) / bipolar (B-TURP) | **30–80 mL** | Reference standard; durable | Bleeding, retrograde ejaculation (~65–75%), TUR syndrome (M-TURP), stricture, BNC |
| **TUIP** | **≤30 mL** (no median lobe) | Quick, less retrograde ejaculation | Higher retreatment rate |
| **TUVP** (vaporization) | 30–80 mL | Haemostatic | Irritative symptoms, no tissue (limited) |
| **PVP – GreenLight 180W XPS** | 30–80 mL | Anticoagulated/high-risk patients, day care | No histology; dysuria |
| **HoLEP / ThuLEP / bipolar enucleation** | **Size-independent** | Lowest retreatment, least bleeding, short stay, tissue for histology | Learning curve; transient stress incontinence; retrograde ejaculation (high) |
| **Simple prostatectomy** – open / laparoscopic / **robotic** | **>80–100 mL** | Complete adenoma removal; deal with stones/diverticulum | Morbidity, transfusion, longer stay |
| **Aquablation (robotic waterjet)** | **30–80 mL**; may consider **80–150 mL** | Image-guided, heat-free, better ejaculatory preservation | Post-op bleeding (needs haemostasis), GA, cost |
| **Rezūm (WVTT)** | **30–80 mL** | Office/day care, ejaculation preserved, treats median lobe | Delayed relief, catheter 3–7 days |
| **PUL – UroLift** | **30–80 mL**, **no obstructing median lobe** | Local anaesthesia, sexual function preserved | Lower efficacy, retreatment higher, implants complicate later surgery |
| **TIUD – iTIND** | 25–75 mL, no obstructing median lobe | Temporary device (5–7 days), no tissue left | Modest efficacy; limited long-term data |
| **PAE** (interventional radiology) | Typically larger glands (AUA: may be offered; EAU: in selected men after counselling) | No GA, preserves sexual function | Less effective than TURP; radiation, non-target embolisation; needs IR expertise |

**TURP points**
- M-TURP uses 1.5% glycine (hypo-osmolar) → **TUR syndrome** (dilutional hyponatraemia, fluid overload, glycine toxicity: confusion, bradycardia, hypertension, visual disturbance); risk ↑ with resection >60–90 min, large gland, capsular perforation, high irrigation pressure. Treat: stop surgery, diuretics, hypertonic (3%) saline for severe hyponatraemia, slow correction.
- **B-TURP** uses saline → virtually eliminates TUR syndrome; allows larger glands; efficacy similar to M-TURP.

**Landmark trials**
- **GOLIATH** (PVP 180W XPS vs TURP; 2 yr): PVP **non-inferior** to TURP for IPSS and Qmax; shorter catheter/stay.
- **WATER** (Aquablation vs TURP, 30–80 mL; Gilling 2018): non-inferior IPSS improvement, significantly less anejaculation; **WATER II**: single-arm, **80–150 mL**, durable results, acceptable safety.
- **L.I.F.T. study** (Roehrborn, 2013): PUL vs sham, 206 men – significant IPSS improvement, sexual function preserved; 5-yr durable with ~14% retreatment.
- **Rezūm II** RCT vs sham: sustained IPSS improvement to 5 years, no de-novo ED.

**Ejaculation-preserving options**: PUL, Rezūm, Aquablation, iTIND, PAE; ejaculation-sparing TURP/enucleation (preserving verumontanum/para-collicular tissue).

### Complications of surgery (general)
- Early: bleeding/clot retention, TUR syndrome, UTI/sepsis, capsular perforation, failed TWOC.
- Late: retrograde ejaculation, ED (rare), incontinence (stress after enucleation – usually transient), urethral stricture, bladder neck contracture, residual/recurrent adenoma.

### Follow-up
- Medical therapy: IPSS at 4–12 weeks (α-blocker, antimuscarinic), 3–6 months (5-ARI); then yearly; PSA adjustment on 5-ARI.
- After surgery: IPSS, Qmax, PVR at 4–12 weeks; histology review; retreatment surveillance.

### Recent advances
- **Robotic waterjet (Aquablation)** expanding to >150 mL; **Optilume BPH** (paclitaxel-coated balloon dilation, PINNACLE trial) FDA-approved 2023.
- **Vibegron** approved for OAB in men with BPH on therapy (2024).
- **Early apical release/en-bloc HoLEP**, thulium fibre laser enucleation, **MOSES** holmium technology.
- Office-based MISTs (Rezūm, PUL, iTIND) for ejaculation-preserving outpatient treatment.

**Diagram to draw:** (1) McNeal zonal anatomy showing transition zone enlargement; (2) Size-based surgical algorithm (≤30 TUIP → 30–80 TURP/PVP/MIST → >80 enucleation/simple prostatectomy).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - IPSS 0–35: 0–7 mild, 8–19 moderate, 20–35 severe.
> - 5-ARI: ~25% volume ↓, PSA halved, AUR/surgery ↓ ~50%; MTOPS combination ↓ progression 66%.
> - AUA sizes: TUIP ≤30; TURP/PVP/PUL/Rezūm/Aquablation 30–80; Aquablation 80–150 considered; HoLEP/ThuLEP size-independent; simple prostatectomy >80–100 mL.
> - IFIS with tamsulosin – defer α-blocker before cataract surgery.
> - B-TURP (saline) eliminates TUR syndrome; GOLIATH: PVP non-inferior to TURP.

## II-2. Discuss metabolic evaluation in urinary calculus disease.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks) | ~1500 words

### Introduction
- Urolithiasis is a systemic/metabolic disease; recurrence ~50% at 5–10 years without prevention.
- Purpose of metabolic evaluation: identify correctable risk factors → targeted dietary + pharmacological prevention.
- Sources: Campbell: Evaluation and medical management of urinary lithiasis chapter; **AUA Medical Management of Kidney Stones guideline (2014, amended 2019)**; **EAU Urolithiasis guideline (2024/25)**.

### Physicochemical basis
- Stone formation requires **supersaturation** → nucleation → growth → aggregation → retention.
- Urine zones: undersaturated → metastable (inhibitors prevent crystallisation) → unstable/supersaturated (spontaneous nucleation).
- Promoters: Ca, oxalate, uric acid, low volume, abnormal pH; **Inhibitors**: citrate, magnesium, pyrophosphate, Tamm-Horsfall protein, osteopontin, glycosaminoglycans.
- **Relative supersaturation (RSS)** – computed (EQUIL2 / Litholink profiles) for calcium oxalate, calcium phosphate and uric acid from 24-h urine; used to guide and monitor therapy (target RSS <1 / reduction from baseline).

### Who needs metabolic evaluation
- **Basic evaluation – every stone former** (AUA, EAU).
- **Comprehensive (24-h urine) evaluation – high-risk / interested first-time formers** (AUA: offer to high-risk and interested first-time stone formers and all recurrent).

**High-risk stone formers (EAU)**
- General: early onset (esp. children/teenagers), family history, recurrent stones, short interval recurrence, solitary kidney (functional/anatomical), bilateral/large stone burden, residual fragments.
- Stone type: **brushite (CaHPO4·2H2O)**, uric acid/urate, **infection stones**, cystine, 2,8-dihydroxyadenine, xanthine.
- Diseases: **primary hyperparathyroidism**, metabolic syndrome, nephrocalcinosis, PKD, **GI disease** (Crohn's, ileal resection, jejuno-ileal bypass, bariatric surgery, malabsorption), sarcoidosis, spinal cord injury/neurogenic bladder.
- Genetic: cystinuria, primary hyperoxaluria, **RTA type 1**, Dent disease, Bartter syndrome, familial hypomagnesaemia-hypercalciuria-nephrocalcinosis.
- Drugs: topiramate, acetazolamide, indinavir/atazanavir, triamterene, sulfadiazine, high-dose vitamin C/D, calcium supplements.
- Anatomy: medullary sponge kidney, PUJO, calyceal diverticulum, horseshoe kidney, ureteric stricture, urinary diversion.
- Occupation/special groups: pilots, military, solitary kidney donors.

### Basic metabolic evaluation (all patients)
- **History**: age at onset, number of episodes, family history, diet (fluid, salt, animal protein, oxalate-rich foods, supplements), medications, bowel disease/surgery, gout, bone disease, UTIs.
- **Imaging**: NCCT (burden, density – uric acid typically <500 HU), nephrocalcinosis.
- **Serum**: creatinine, **calcium** (ionized/albumin-corrected), **uric acid**, sodium, potassium, chloride, **bicarbonate** (low → RTA, diarrhoea), phosphate; **intact PTH** if serum calcium high-normal/raised or hypercalciuria (detect primary hyperparathyroidism – Ca high, PTH high/inappropriately normal); 25-OH vitamin D as needed.
- **Urine**: dipstick/microscopy – **pH** (persistently >6.5–7 suggests RTA/infection; <5.5 favours uric acid), crystals (hexagonal = cystine; coffin-lid = struvite), leucocytes, culture (urease organisms – Proteus, Klebsiella, Providencia).
- **Stone analysis** – mandatory at first episode and if recurrence on therapy/early recurrence: **infrared spectroscopy or X-ray diffraction** (preferred); wet chemistry obsolete. Polarisation microscopy acceptable adjunct.
- **Cystine screening**: **sodium cyanide-nitroprusside test** (qualitative; positive >75 mg/L) – confirm with quantitative 24-h cystine.

### Comprehensive evaluation – 24-h urine
- **Two 24-h collections** (AUA: one or two; EAU recommends two), on usual diet, ≥2–4 weeks after stone event/intervention, stone-free and infection-free.
- Check adequacy using **urinary creatinine** (men ~20–25 mg/kg/d; women ~15–20 mg/kg/d).
- Parameters: volume, calcium, oxalate, citrate, uric acid, sodium, potassium, magnesium, phosphate, sulphate, ammonium, pH, creatinine, ± cystine; urea nitrogen (protein intake); supersaturation.

**Reference / target values (adult, per day)**

| Parameter | Desirable / normal | Abnormality |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | **>2.5 L** (target output) | Low urine volume <2 L – commonest finding |
| Calcium | <250 mg (women), <300 mg (men), or **<4 mg/kg** | Hypercalciuria |
| Oxalate | **<40–45 mg** | Hyperoxaluria |
| Citrate | **>320 mg** (>450 F / >350 M used by some labs) | Hypocitraturia |
| Uric acid | <750 mg (women), **<800 mg (men)** | Hyperuricosuria |
| Sodium | <100 mmol/d (≈2.3 g Na intake) | High salt → ↑ Ca excretion |
| pH | 5.8–6.2 | <5.5 uric acid; >6.5–7 CaP/RTA/infection |
| Magnesium | >70 mg | Hypomagnesiuria (GI) |
| Sulphate | Marker of animal-protein intake | ↑ = high animal protein (acid load) |
| Cystine | Not normally detectable/very low | Cystinuria (homozygous typically **>400 mg/d**) |

### Interpretation and diagnostic categories
**1. Hypercalciuria**
- **Absorptive** (↑ gut Ca absorption; types I–III in Pak classification – type III renal phosphate leak), **renal leak** (↑ PTH secondary, normal serum Ca), **resorptive** (primary hyperparathyroidism – ↑ serum Ca, ↑ PTH).
- Pak's fast/calcium-load test now rarely used; classification has limited therapeutic impact (thiazide works for all idiopathic hypercalciuria).
- Others: sarcoidosis, vitamin D excess, immobilisation, RTA 1, malignancy, glucocorticoids.

**2. Hyperoxaluria**
- **Dietary** (spinach, nuts, tea, vitamin C) – mild (45–60 mg/d).
- **Enteric** – fat malabsorption (Crohn's, ileal resection, bariatric RYGB): unbound fatty acids saponify calcium → free oxalate absorbed in colon; also hypocitraturia, low volume, hypomagnesiuria, acidic urine.
- **Primary hyperoxaluria** (PH1 – AGXT/AGT deficiency, liver; PH2 – GRHPR; PH3 – HOGA1): usually markedly elevated (often >75–100 mg/d), childhood nephrocalcinosis, ESKD/oxalosis; confirm by genetic testing; urinary glycolate (PH1).

**3. Hypocitraturia**
- Idiopathic, **RTA type 1**, chronic diarrhoea, hypokalaemia, high animal protein, thiazide-induced hypokalaemia, acetazolamide/topiramate, UTI.

**4. Hyperuricosuria** – high purine diet, gout, myeloproliferative disease, uricosuric drugs; promotes **calcium oxalate** stones (heterogeneous nucleation).
- **Uric acid stones**: main determinant is **low urine pH (<5.5)** (metabolic syndrome, diabetes, gout, diarrhoea).

**5. Distal RTA (type 1)** – hyperchloraemic normal anion-gap metabolic acidosis, urine pH >5.5–6, hypocitraturia, hypercalciuria, CaP stones/nephrocalcinosis.
- Incomplete dRTA: normal serum bicarbonate → **ammonium chloride loading test** (0.1 g/kg orally; urine pH fails to fall **<5.5**) or **furosemide–fludrocortisone test**.

**6. Cystinuria** – AR defect (SLC3A1/SLC7A9) of dibasic amino acids (COLA – cystine, ornithine, lysine, arginine); cystine solubility ~250 mg/L at physiological pH → aim urine cystine **<250 mg/L**.

**7. Infection stones** – urease-producing organisms, urine pH >7, struvite/carbonate apatite; check for anatomic/neurogenic factors.

**8. Hypomagnesiuria, low volume, high sodium/protein** – dietary risk factors.

```
Stone episode → Basic evaluation (Hx, serum, urine, stone analysis)
        │
   Low risk ────────→ General preventive advice (fluids >2.5 L/d output, Na/protein ↓, normal Ca diet)
        │
   High risk / recurrent / interested
        ↓
   Two 24-h urines (+ serum PTH/bicarb) → specific abnormality
        ↓
   Targeted diet + drug therapy → repeat 24-h urine at ~6 months → then yearly (or per stability)
```

### Treatment linked to metabolic abnormality

| Abnormality | Diet | Drug |
|---|---|---|
| Low volume | Fluids to achieve >2.5 L output | – |
| Hypercalciuria (idiopathic) | Sodium <2.3 g/d, normal Ca (1,000–1,200 mg/d), ↓ animal protein | **Thiazide** (HCTZ 25–50 mg, chlorthalidone 25 mg, indapamide 2.5 mg) + K citrate to prevent hypokalaemia |
| Primary hyperparathyroidism | – | **Parathyroidectomy** |
| Hypocitraturia | ↑ fruits/vegetables | **Potassium citrate** (30–60 mEq/d) |
| Hyperoxaluria – dietary | ↓ oxalate, Ca with meals | – |
| Enteric hyperoxaluria | Low fat/oxalate, Ca supplements with meals | Calcium citrate, K citrate, cholestyramine |
| **PH1** | High fluid | **Pyridoxine** (B6-responsive), **lumasiran** (RNAi vs glycolate oxidase; approved 2020); **nedosiran** (RNAi vs LDH; PH1); liver ± kidney transplant |
| Hyperuricosuria (CaOx stones) | ↓ purine | **Allopurinol** (100–300 mg); febuxostat an alternative where allopurinol not tolerated (not first-line for stones) |
| Uric acid stones | – | **Potassium citrate** to pH 6.0–6.5 (first line), ± allopurinol if hyperuricosuria |
| dRTA | – | **Potassium citrate** |
| Cystinuria | High fluid (>3–4 L), Na and protein ↓ | Alkalinise to **pH 7.0–7.5** (EAU: ≥7.5); **tiopronin** (preferred over D-penicillamine) if refractory |
| Infection stones | – | Complete stone clearance, culture-specific antibiotics, **acetohydroxamic acid** (limited by side effects) |

### Special groups
**Children**
- **All children with stones need full metabolic evaluation** (high prevalence of metabolic/genetic abnormalities); stone analysis essential.
- Spot urine ratios (calcium/creatinine, oxalate/creatinine, citrate/creatinine) when 24-h collection impractical (age-specific normals); 24-h urine expressed per kg or per 1.73 m².
- Screen for cystinuria, PH (genetic testing), RTA, Dent disease, infections/anatomical anomalies.

**Pregnancy**
- Imaging: ultrasound first, MRU (no gadolinium) second line, low-dose CT reserved.
- Defer comprehensive 24-h urine evaluation until **after delivery/lactation** (physiological hypercalciuria and hypercitraturia of pregnancy make results misleading); avoid thiazides, allopurinol, tiopronin in pregnancy.

### Follow-up
- **Repeat 24-h urine within 6 months** of starting therapy to assess response (AUA), then annually (or as per stability); periodic serum chemistry for drug toxicity (K with thiazide, LFT/uric acid with allopurinol, CBC/proteinuria with tiopronin).
- Periodic imaging (US/low-dose CT) to detect new stone growth; repeat stone analysis if no response to therapy.

### Recent advances
- **RNAi therapy for PH1**: lumasiran, nedosiran – marked reduction in urinary oxalate.
- Investigational: **stiripentol** (primary hyperoxaluria); oxalate-degrading bacteria/enzymes (Oxalobacter formigenes, reloxaliase) for enteric hyperoxaluria.
- **Genetic panels** for monogenic stone disease in children/early-onset.
- Micro-CT, Raman spectroscopy and AI-based stone composition prediction from DECT.
- **NOSTONE trial (NEJM 2023)**: hydrochlorothiazide did not reduce recurrence vs placebo – questions routine thiazide use.

**Diagram to draw:** (1) Supersaturation zones (undersaturated–metastable–unstable); (2) Algorithm of basic vs comprehensive evaluation.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Two 24-h urines on usual diet, ≥4 weeks after event; check creatinine for adequacy.
> - Targets: volume >2.5 L, Ca <250/300 mg, oxalate <40 mg, citrate >320 mg, uric acid <750/800 mg.
> - dRTA: urine pH >5.5 despite acidosis; NH4Cl load fails to lower pH <5.5.
> - Cystinuria: keep cystine <250 mg/L, pH 7.0–7.5, tiopronin.
> - Repeat 24-h urine within 6 months; lumasiran for PH1.

## II-3a. Management of priapism
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks) | ~550 words

### Definition
- Full or partial erection persisting **>4 hours** beyond or unrelated to sexual stimulation (AUA/SMSNA guideline 2021).

### Classification
| Feature | **Ischaemic (low-flow)** | **Non-ischaemic (high-flow)** | **Stuttering** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Veno-occlusive, compartment syndrome | Unregulated arterial inflow (cavernous artery–sinusoidal fistula) | Recurrent self-limiting ischaemic episodes |
| Cause | ICI drugs, sickle cell disease, antipsychotics/trazodone, haematological malignancy, idiopathic | Perineal/penile trauma (straddle injury) | SCD commonest |
| Rigidity / pain | Fully rigid, painful | Partially rigid, painless | Painful episodes |
| Emergency | **Yes** | No | Prevent progression |

### Diagnosis
- History (duration, drugs, trauma, SCD), examination (glans soft in ischaemic).
- **Corporal blood gas**:

| | pO2 (mmHg) | pCO2 (mmHg) | pH |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ischaemic | **<30** | **>60** | **<7.25** |
| Normal arterial | >90 | <40 | 7.40 |
| Normal mixed venous | 40 | 50 | 7.35 |

- **Colour Doppler US**: absent cavernous flow (ischaemic); high flow/fistula ± pseudoaneurysm (non-ischaemic).
- CBC, haemoglobin electrophoresis, toxicology.

### Management of ischaemic priapism
```
Ischaemic priapism
  → Dorsal penile block → aspiration (16–18G, ±saline irrigation)
  → Intracavernosal phenylephrine 100–500 µg/mL, 1 mL every 3–5 min, up to ~1 h
     (monitor BP/pulse; lower conc. in cardiovascular disease)
  → Failure → Distal shunt → Proximal shunt
  → Duration >36 h / failed shunts → consider immediate penile prosthesis
```
- **Distal (cavernoglanular) shunts**: percutaneous – **Winter** (Tru-cut biopsy needle), **Ebbehoj** (scalpel through glans), **T-shunt** (Brant; scalpel turned 90°); open – **Al-Ghorab** (excision of tunica tips); **corporal tunnelling/snake manoeuvre** (dilator passed through Al-Ghorab or T-shunt).
- **Proximal shunts**: **Quackels** (cavernoso-spongiosal), **Grayhack** (cavernoso-saphenous), Barry (deep dorsal vein).
- Oral systemic therapy alone is not recommended for acute ischaemic priapism.
- Prolonged ischaemia (>24–48 h) → corporal fibrosis, ED in most; **early prosthesis** (AUA: may offer for priapism >36 h or failed shunts) is easier and preserves length vs delayed implant in fibrotic corpora (higher infection/erosion in acute setting – counsel).
- **SCD**: intracavernosal therapy first; **concurrent** haematology care (hydration, O2, exchange transfusion) – but do not delay urological intervention.

### Non-ischaemic priapism
- Not an emergency; **observation** first (many resolve spontaneously).
- Persistent/bothersome → **selective super-selective embolisation** (absorbable gelfoam/autologous clot preferred – lower ED; coils permanent).
- Surgical ligation of fistula rarely (when embolisation fails).

### Stuttering priapism
- Treat each acute episode as ischaemic.
- Prevention: **daily PDE5i** (paradoxical, in non-erect state), pseudoephedrine, self-injection phenylephrine; hormonal – GnRH agonists/antiandrogens (adults, not for those desiring fertility); in SCD – hydroxyurea, transfusion protocols.

### Recent advances
- SCD-targeted agents (e.g. crizanlizumab) being studied for priapism prevention; penile prosthesis early in refractory cases.

**Diagram to draw:** Penile cross-section showing distal (glans-corpora) vs proximal (corpora-spongiosum) shunts.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Ischaemic: pO2 <30, pCO2 >60, pH <7.25 – emergency.
> - Phenylephrine 100–500 µg/mL, 1 mL q3–5 min up to 1 h.
> - Distal shunts (Winter, Ebbehoj, T, Al-Ghorab) before proximal (Quackels, Grayhack).
> - Prosthesis for >36 h; high-flow → observe/embolise.

## II-3b. Renal tubular acidosis
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks) | ~550 words

### Definition
- Group of disorders with **hyperchloraemic, normal anion-gap metabolic acidosis** due to impaired renal bicarbonate reabsorption or H+ excretion, with relatively preserved GFR.

### Comparison of types

| Feature | **Type 1 (distal)** | **Type 2 (proximal)** | **Type 4 (hyperkalaemic)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Defect | Impaired H+ secretion in collecting duct (H+-ATPase/H+-K+-ATPase/anion exchanger; gradient defect) | Impaired HCO3- reabsorption in PCT (↓ threshold) | Aldosterone deficiency/resistance → ↓ NH4+ excretion |
| Urine pH | **>5.5 always** | <5.5 once serum HCO3 falls below threshold; >5.5 during bicarbonate loading | <5.5 |
| Serum K | Low | Low | **High** |
| Serum HCO3 | Very low (<10–15 possible) | 12–20 mmol/L | >15 mmol/L |
| Urine citrate | **Low** | Normal/high | Normal |
| Hypercalciuria | Yes | Yes (mild) | No |
| **Stones / nephrocalcinosis** | **Common (calcium phosphate)** | Rare | Absent |
| Associated | Osteomalacia, rickets | Fanconi syndrome, rickets | – |
| Causes | Genetic; Sjogren's, SLE, RA; amphotericin, lithium, ifosfamide; hypercalciuria, MSK, obstructive uropathy | Fanconi, cystinosis, Wilson, multiple myeloma, heavy metals, acetazolamide, tenofovir, ifosfamide | Diabetic nephropathy, **obstructive uropathy**, Addison's, ACEi/ARB, spironolactone, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin, calcineurin inhibitors |
| Treatment | **Potassium citrate/alkali 1–2 mEq/kg/d** | Large-dose alkali (10–15 mEq/kg/d) + K; thiazide | Low K diet, loop diuretic, **fludrocortisone**, stop offending drugs, bicarbonate |

- **Type 3**: rare (carbonic anhydrase II deficiency) – mixed proximal + distal, with osteopetrosis and cerebral calcification.
- **Incomplete dRTA**: normal serum HCO3 but inability to acidify urine – seen in many recurrent calcium phosphate stone formers.

### Diagnosis
- ABG/serum electrolytes: normal AG metabolic acidosis (AG = Na – [Cl + HCO3]).
- **Urine anion gap (UAG) = (Na + K) – Cl**: surrogate for NH4+ excretion.
  - **Negative** → appropriate NH4+ excretion (GI bicarbonate loss, proximal RTA).
  - **Positive** → impaired NH4+ excretion (distal RTA, type 4).
- Fresh **urine pH** (pH meter, morning sample under oil).
- **Ammonium chloride loading test** (0.1 g/kg orally; urine pH hourly ~6 h): failure to lower urine pH **<5.5** = distal RTA.
- **Furosemide–fludrocortisone test** (furosemide 40 mg + fludrocortisone 1 mg): better tolerated alternative; failure to acidify <5.5 confirms dRTA.
- Fractional excretion of HCO3 >15% during bicarbonate loading = proximal RTA.
- Urine–blood pCO2 gradient during bicarbonate loading (<20 mmHg in dRTA).
- Imaging: US/CT for nephrocalcinosis; 24-h urine citrate/calcium.

### Urological relevance
- dRTA → **recurrent calcium phosphate stones, nephrocalcinosis**, hypocitraturia; also predisposes to infection stones.
- **Medullary sponge kidney** often associated with incomplete dRTA.
- **Obstructive uropathy** (PUV, BPO) → type 4/hyperkalaemic distal RTA – improves after relief of obstruction.
- **Topiramate/acetazolamide** (carbonic anhydrase inhibitors) → CaP stones.
- **Urinary diversion** (ureterosigmoidostomy, ileal conduit, neobladder) → hyperchloraemic acidosis mimicking RTA.
- Post-transplant RTA (calcineurin inhibitors).
- Potassium citrate preferred over sodium bicarbonate (sodium increases calcium excretion).

### Recent advances
- Genetic testing (SLC4A1, ATP6V1B1, ATP6V0A4) for inherited dRTA; prolonged-release alkali formulations (e.g. ADV7103) for dRTA.

**Diagram to draw:** Nephron diagram showing site of defect – PCT (type 2), collecting duct α-intercalated cell (type 1), aldosterone action (type 4).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Type 1: urine pH >5.5, hypokalaemia, hypocitraturia, CaP stones/nephrocalcinosis.
> - Type 4: hyperkalaemia, no stones; seen in obstructive uropathy.
> - Positive UAG = distal RTA; NH4Cl 0.1 g/kg – pH fails to fall <5.5.
> - Treat dRTA with potassium citrate.

## II-3c. Millin's prostatectomy
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks) | ~550 words

### Definition / History
- **Retropubic extravesical simple prostatectomy**: enucleation of the obstructing adenoma through a **transverse incision in the anterior prostatic capsule**, without opening the bladder.
- Described by **Terence Millin (Lancet, 1945)**.
- Contrast: **Freyer's transvesical (suprapubic) prostatectomy** – adenoma enucleated through an opened bladder.
- Campbell: Simple prostatectomy (open and robotic) chapter.

### Indications
- Large gland, generally **>80–100 mL** (AUA), when endoscopic enucleation (HoLEP) unavailable.
- Indications for BPH surgery (retention, recurrent UTI/haematuria, renal impairment, bladder stones, refractory LUTS).
- Concomitant pathology: inguinal hernia repair through same incision.
- **Best for**: large lateral lobes with small/no median lobe.
- **Relative contraindications**: large intravesical median lobe, bladder diverticulum/large stones (transvesical preferred), small fibrous gland, previous pelvic surgery/radiation, prostate cancer.

### Steps
1. Supine/slight Trendelenburg; urethral catheter; Pfannenstiel or lower midline incision.
2. Extraperitoneal approach – develop **space of Retzius**; clear periprostatic fat.
3. **Haemostatic control**: suture ligatures across the anterior capsule proximal and distal to the planned incision (controls dorsal venous plexus of Santorini) ± ligation of lateral pedicles at bladder neck.
4. **Transverse capsulotomy** ~1–2 cm distal to bladder neck with diathermy.
5. Develop plane between adenoma and surgical capsule with scissors, then **finger enucleation**.
6. **Divide urethra at apex under direct vision** (protects external sphincter).
7. Inspect bladder neck and ureteric orifices; **wedge excision of posterior bladder neck lip** (prevents bladder neck contracture); advance mucosa (trigonisation) as needed.
8. Haemostasis: figure-of-eight sutures at **5 and 7 o'clock** at bladder neck.
9. Place 20–22 Fr three-way catheter; **close capsule** watertight; drain in Retzius; closure.

### Advantages over Freyer (transvesical)

| Aspect | **Millin (retropubic)** | **Freyer (transvesical)** |
|---|---|---|
| Bladder | Not opened | Opened |
| Apex/urethral division | Under direct vision – better continence | Blind finger fracture at apex |
| Haemostasis | Prostatic fossa directly visible | Fossa less visible; packing/purse-string needed |
| Bladder pathology (stone, diverticulum) | Difficult | Easily addressed |
| Large median lobe | Difficult | Ideal |
| Catheter time/stay | Shorter | Longer (suprapubic + urethral catheter) |
| Specific risk | Santorini bleeding, osteitis pubis | Bladder spasm, urinary leak/SP fistula |

### Complications
- Intra/early: **haemorrhage/transfusion**, clot retention, rectal injury (rare), UTI/sepsis, urinary leak, DVT/PE, wound infection.
- Late: **retrograde ejaculation** (most), bladder neck contracture, urethral stricture, stress incontinence (uncommon), ED (uncommon), **osteitis pubis**, residual adenoma.
- Excellent durable outcomes – large IPSS and Qmax improvement, low retreatment.

### Modern alternatives
- **HoLEP/ThuLEP/bipolar enucleation** – endoscopic equivalent, size-independent, less bleeding and shorter stay; EAU recommends as first choice for >80 mL where available.
- **Robotic-assisted simple prostatectomy (RASP)** – transvesical or Millin-type extravesical approaches; lower blood loss and shorter stay than open, similar functional outcome; allows concomitant diverticulectomy/stone removal.
- Laparoscopic simple prostatectomy; Aquablation (80–150 mL).

**Diagram to draw:** Sagittal section showing transverse capsulotomy below bladder neck with finger enucleation plane between adenoma and surgical capsule.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Millin 1945: retropubic, transverse anterior capsulotomy, bladder not opened.
> - Apical urethral division under vision; 5 and 7 o'clock haemostatic sutures; bladder neck wedge.
> - Freyer (transvesical) preferred for median lobe, stones, diverticulum.
> - HoLEP and robotic simple prostatectomy are modern alternatives for >80–100 mL.

## II-3d. Energy sources in laparoscopic urology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks) | ~550 words

### Introduction
- Energy devices achieve **cutting, coagulation and vessel sealing**; choice affects blood loss, speed and thermal injury.
- Tissue effects: ~60 °C – protein denaturation (coagulation); ~100 °C – water vaporization (cutting); >200 °C – carbonization.
- Campbell: Basics of laparoscopic and robotic surgery chapter.

### Types of energy sources

| Device | Principle | Uses / features | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Monopolar electrosurgery** | RF current (>100 kHz, typically 300 kHz–3 MHz) passes from active electrode through patient to return pad; cut (continuous, low-voltage) vs coag (interrupted, high-voltage) waveforms | Cheap, versatile; hook, scissors | Greatest lateral spread; stray current injury; pacemaker interference |
| **Conventional bipolar** | Current between two jaws only | Safer near nerves/bowel (e.g. nerve-sparing, small vessels) | Slower; no cutting; sticking/charring |
| **Advanced bipolar vessel sealers** (LigaSure, ENSEAL) | Pressure + impedance-controlled bipolar energy fusing collagen/elastin; integrated blade | Seal vessels **up to 7 mm**; nephrectomy, adrenalectomy, RPLND, cystectomy pedicles | Cost; lateral spread ~1–3 mm; jaw heat |
| **Ultrasonic** (Harmonic scalpel) | Blade vibrates at ~55.5 kHz → mechanical energy, protein denaturation (coaptive coagulation) at lower temperatures (<100 °C) | Simultaneous cut + seal; vessels **up to 5 mm** (newer models up to 7 mm); no current through patient; less smoke (mist) | Active blade remains hot; slower on thick tissue |
| **Hybrid** (Thunderbeat) | Ultrasonic + advanced bipolar | Fast dissection + seals up to 7 mm | Cost |
| **Lasers** | Holmium:YAG (2,120 nm), thulium (2,013 nm/TFL 1,940 nm), KTP (532 nm), diode | Partial nephrectomy (experimental), stricture incision, laser-assisted pyeloplasty, endourology | Smoke, eye safety |
| **Argon beam coagulator** | Monopolar current conducted through ionized argon gas – non-contact, superficial uniform eschar | Haemostasis of renal/liver/splenic parenchymal bed (partial nephrectomy) | **Gas embolism** (vent pneumoperitoneum), pressure rise |
| **Hydro-jet** (water-jet dissection) | High-pressure water jet separates parenchyma, preserves vessels | Partial nephrectomy, nerve-sparing | Not haemostatic |
| **Others** | Radiofrequency ablation, microwave, cryoablation | Ablation of small renal masses | – |

### Safety – mechanisms of electrosurgical injury
- Many injuries unrecognised intra-operatively (occur outside the field of view) → delayed bowel perforation presenting days later.
1. **Insulation failure** – defects in instrument insulation (repeated sterilisation, high-voltage coag) → current leaks to bowel.
2. **Direct coupling** – active electrode touches another metal instrument/laparoscope in contact with tissue.
3. **Capacitive coupling** – current induced in adjacent conductor through intact insulation; worse with high-voltage coag, long activation, small-port with metal; **avoid hybrid trocars** (metal cannula with plastic anchor) – use all-metal or all-plastic systems.
4. **Direct thermal spread** – lateral spread to ureter, nerves (neurovascular bundles), bowel; residual heat of instrument tips (ultrasonic blade).
5. **Return electrode burns** – poor pad contact (prevent by **return electrode contact quality monitoring, REM**).
6. **Smoke plume**; fires in oxygen-rich environment.

### Safety measures
- **Active Electrode Monitoring (AEM)** – shields instrument and shuts generator if insulation failure or capacitive coupling occurs.
- Use lowest effective power, cut waveform where possible, brief activation, avoid open-circuit activation.
- Keep active tip in view; do not activate near/in contact with other metal instruments.
- Inspect insulation; bipolar near critical structures; avoid thermal energy near neurovascular bundle (athermal nerve-sparing in prostatectomy).
- Pacemaker/ICD: bipolar preferred, cardiology input.

### Recent advances
- Smart generators with real-time impedance feedback; robotic vessel sealers (e.g. SynchroSeal, Vessel Sealer Extend); ultrasonic devices sealing up to 7 mm.

**Diagram to draw:** Monopolar circuit vs bipolar circuit; capacitive coupling across a hybrid trocar.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - LigaSure/advanced bipolar: vessels up to 7 mm; Harmonic: up to 5 mm at ~55.5 kHz.
> - Stray current injury: insulation failure, direct coupling, capacitive coupling.
> - Avoid hybrid trocars; use AEM and REM pads.
> - Argon beam – risk of gas embolism.

## II-3e. Role of vascular grafts in urology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks) | ~550 words

### Introduction
- Vascular grafts replace, bypass or extend vessels when primary repair is impossible; increasingly needed in transplantation and extended oncological resections.
- Campbell: Renal transplantation, Renovascular hypertension, and Renal tumours (IVC thrombus) chapters.

### Types of grafts

| Type | Examples | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Autologous vein** | Great saphenous vein, gonadal vein, donor/recipient iliac or renal vein | Low infection, best long-term patency in small vessels | Limited length/calibre, aneurysmal dilatation |
| **Autologous artery** | Internal iliac (hypogastric) artery, inferior epigastric artery, donor aortic (Carrel) patch | Ideal for renal artery reconstruction, matching size; growth in children | Limited availability |
| **Synthetic** | **PTFE (e-PTFE, Gore-Tex)**, **Dacron (polyester)** | Off-the-shelf, sizes available, large vessels (IVC, aorta) | Infection, thrombosis in low-flow/small calibre, contraindicated in contaminated field |
| **Biological / cadaveric** | Cryopreserved cadaveric vessels, deceased-donor iliac artery/vein; bovine pericardium patch | Useful in infected fields, deceased-donor transplant extension | Availability, degeneration |
| **Patches** | Bovine pericardium, PTFE patch | Partial IVC wall defects (cavotomy closure) | – |

### Applications in urology
**1. Renal transplantation**
- **Multiple donor arteries** (living donor): bench reconstruction – side-to-side conjoining, end-to-side implantation of small polar artery onto main artery, or inferior epigastric anastomosis to a lower-pole artery.
- Short renal vein (right kidney): extension with donor gonadal vein, saphenous vein or deceased-donor iliac/IVC segment.
- Deceased donor: Carrel aortic patch, donor iliac Y-grafts for reconstruction (e.g. pancreas/multiple vessels).
- Recipient atherosclerotic/aneurysmal iliacs: synthetic aorto-iliac bypass prior to/with transplant.

**2. Renovascular surgery**
- Renal artery stenosis (fibromuscular dysplasia, atherosclerosis), **renal artery aneurysm**, AV malformation: aortorenal bypass with saphenous vein, hypogastric artery or PTFE; splenorenal/hepatorenal bypass; ex vivo bench repair.

**3. Renal autotransplantation**
- Complex ureteric loss, renal artery branch disease, loin-pain haematuria; vascular extension grafts as needed.

**4. RCC with IVC tumour thrombus (Neves-Zincke levels)**
- Most managed by cavotomy and primary closure; **patch cavoplasty** (bovine pericardium/PTFE) if lumen narrowed >50%.
- **IVC resection and replacement** with ring-reinforced **PTFE graft** when wall invaded and collaterals inadequate; infrarenal IVC can often be ligated without graft when collaterals developed.
- Level III–IV thrombi may need cardiopulmonary bypass.

**5. Retroperitoneal tumours / post-chemotherapy RPLND**
- Residual masses encasing the aorta (testicular GCT) – **aortic resection with Dacron/PTFE interposition graft**; IVC resection ± reconstruction; renal artery reimplantation.
- Retroperitoneal sarcomas involving iliac vessels/IVC.

**6. Vascular access**
- **AV graft (PTFE)** for haemodialysis when native fistula impossible.

**7. Penile revascularisation**
- Inferior epigastric artery to dorsal artery/deep dorsal vein (microsurgical) – young men with arteriogenic ED after pelvic trauma; rarely performed.

**8. Vascular injury**
- Iatrogenic injury during laparoscopic nephrectomy, pelvic lymphadenectomy, cystectomy; trauma – interposition saphenous/PTFE graft.

### Principles
- Prefer **autologous** in contaminated/urine-exposed fields and small vessels; synthetic for large-calibre high-flow vessels.
- Tension-free, appropriately sized anastomosis; intimal-to-intimal apposition; spatulation; fine non-absorbable monofilament (polypropylene).
- Systemic heparinisation during clamping; postoperative antiplatelet/anticoagulation as indicated.
- Avoid kinking; omental wrap to isolate synthetic graft from urinary tract/bowel.
- Perioperative antibiotics; Doppler surveillance.

### Complications
- Thrombosis/occlusion, anastomotic stenosis, haemorrhage, pseudoaneurysm, **graft infection** (difficult – often needs explantation), graft-enteric/urinary fistula, lymphocele, distal embolism, limb oedema (IVC ligation).

### Recent advances
- Robotic IVC thrombectomy and robotic bench/vascular reconstruction; bioengineered acellular vessels (human acellular vessel) for dialysis access/trauma.

**Diagram to draw:** Bench reconstruction of two renal arteries (side-to-side conjoining and end-to-side implantation) before transplant.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Autologous (saphenous, hypogastric, gonadal) for small vessels/contaminated fields; PTFE/Dacron for IVC/aorta.
> - Transplant: Carrel patch, bench reconstruction of multiple arteries, gonadal/IVC extension of short right vein.
> - IVC: patch cavoplasty if narrowed; PTFE replacement if wall invaded.
> - Post-chemo RPLND may need aortic graft replacement.

## II-3f. Transrectal ultrasonography
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks) | ~550 words

### Introduction / Physics
- Real-time imaging of prostate, seminal vesicles and adjacent structures through the rectal wall.
- Piezoelectric crystals convert electrical to sound energy and back; higher frequency = better resolution but less penetration.
- Probes: **6–10 MHz (commonly 7.5 MHz)**, **biplanar** (transverse + sagittal) – end-fire or side-fire; colour/power Doppler, elastography.
- Campbell: Prostate biopsy – techniques and imaging chapter.

### Technique
- Left lateral decubitus (or lithotomy); DRE first; enema/rectal preparation.
- Systematic scanning: transverse base→apex, then sagittal midline → laterally.

### Normal anatomy (McNeal zones)
| Zone | % glandular volume | Echogenicity | Clinical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peripheral zone | ~70% | Homogeneous, isoechoic | ~70% of cancers |
| Transition zone | ~5–10% (enlarges in BPH) | Heterogeneous, hypoechoic | BPH, ~20–25% cancers |
| Central zone | ~25% | Slightly hyperechoic | ~5–10% cancers |
| Anterior fibromuscular stroma | Non-glandular | Hypoechoic | – |
- Seminal vesicles: paired "bow-tie" hypoechoic structures; ejaculatory ducts; verumontanum.

### Measurements
- **Prostate volume (ellipsoid formula) = 0.52 × L × W × H** (1 mL ≈ 1 g).
- **PSA density (PSAD) = PSA/volume**; >0.15 ng/mL/mL suspicious; used in deciding biopsy after MRI (e.g. PI-RADS 3).
- Classic cancer appearance: **hypoechoic peripheral-zone lesion** (low specificity; many cancers isoechoic).

### Indications
- **Guidance for prostate biopsy** (raised PSA, abnormal DRE, active surveillance, MRI lesion).
- Volume estimation – planning BPH surgery and choice of modality, PSAD.
- **Ejaculatory duct obstruction**: dilated seminal vesicles (>1.5 cm AP), dilated ejaculatory ducts, midline (utricular/Müllerian) cysts, calcification; TRUS-guided seminal vesicle aspiration/vesiculography.
- Prostatic abscess – diagnosis and transrectal/transperineal drainage.
- **Brachytherapy** (volume study, seed placement – transperineal under TRUS), **HIFU**, **cryotherapy**, focal therapy, fiducial marker/rectal spacer placement.
- Staging (ECE/SVI) – largely replaced by MRI.

### TRUS-guided biopsy
- **Systematic 12-core** (sextant medial + lateral at base, mid, apex) – lateral (far-lateral PZ) cores improve detection; ±targeted cores.
- Anaesthesia: **periprostatic nerve block** (lidocaine at junction of prostate base and seminal vesicles).
- Stop anticoagulants per risk (aspirin often continued).

**Complications**
- Haematospermia (most common), haematuria, rectal bleeding.
- **Infection** – UTI, prostatitis, **sepsis** (rising due to fluoroquinolone-resistant E. coli); urinary retention, vasovagal reaction.

**Prophylaxis (transrectal route)**
- Rectal cleansing with **povidone-iodine**; targeted prophylaxis based on rectal swab culture or augmented prophylaxis; fluoroquinolones restricted (EMA 2019) – EAU no longer recommends them as default.

### Current shift
- **EAU (2024/25)**: **transperineal biopsy recommended** over transrectal because of significantly lower infection risk; antibiotic prophylaxis may be omitted for transperineal biopsy. Freehand LA transperineal access systems (PrecisionPoint, CamPROBE) enable office-based procedure.
- **Pre-biopsy mpMRI** and **MRI-targeted biopsy** (cognitive, software fusion, in-bore):
  - **PROMIS (2017)**: mpMRI sensitivity for csPCa 93% vs TRUS biopsy 48%.
  - **PRECISION (2018)**: MRI-targeted biopsy detected more csPCa (38% vs 26%) and less insignificant cancer than standard TRUS biopsy.

### Recent advances
- **Micro-ultrasound (29 MHz, ExactVu; PRI-MUS scoring)** – higher resolution; **OPTIMUM RCT (2025)** reported micro-US–guided biopsy non-inferior to MRI-targeted biopsy for csPCa detection.
- Multiparametric US (contrast-enhanced, shear-wave elastography), AI-assisted TRUS segmentation, fusion platforms.

**Diagram to draw:** Transverse and sagittal TRUS views of zonal anatomy; 12-core biopsy template.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Biplanar probe 6–10 MHz; volume = 0.52 × L × W × H; PSAD >0.15 suspicious.
> - 12-core systematic biopsy with periprostatic block.
> - EAU: transperineal preferred (lower sepsis); FQ prophylaxis restricted.
> - PROMIS and PRECISION established MRI-first pathway.
