## Q1. Describe anatomy of lymphatic drainage of genitourinary system, etiopathogenesis and management of Chyluria
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### General plan of lymphatic drainage
- Lymph flows **cephalad and right → left**.
- Pelvic nodes (external iliac, internal iliac, obturator, presacral) → common iliac → lumbar (para-aortic, interaortocaval, paracaval) nodes → **lumbar trunks**. These join the intestinal trunk at the **cisterna chyli** (in front of L1–L2, behind the right side of the aorta). The **thoracic duct** rises behind the aorta to the left innominate vein.
- Pelvic lymph crosses freely between right and left. Organs supplied by lateral aortic branches (kidney, adrenal, ureter, gonad) drain to lumbar nodes.

### Drainage by organ
| Organ | Primary nodes | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Left kidney** | Left lateral para-aortic | Capsular and deep hilar networks drain to renal vein nodes; may go retrocrural |
| **Right kidney** | Right paracaval, interaortocaval | May cross to left para-aortic |
| **Ureter** | Abdominal: para-aortic (left), paracaval/interaortocaval (right); middle: common iliac; pelvic: common, external, internal iliac | Plexus in muscle and adventitia |
| **Bladder** | Mainly **external iliac**; also internal iliac, obturator | Begins in lamina propria; bilateral cross-drainage |
| **Prostate** | **Obturator and internal iliac**; presacral | Three routes: superolateral (side wall), posterior (presacral), apical (internal pudendal) |
| **Testis** | Left: left para-aortic; right: interaortocaval, then right paracaval | Follows gonadal vessels; crossover only right → left. Epididymal cauda and vas → external iliac |
| **Penis** | Skin → superficial inguinal (both groins); glans and corpora → superficial and deep inguinal, then pelvic | Shaft lymphatics converge dorsally |
| **Scrotum** | Ipsilateral superficial inguinal | Does not cross the raphe |
| **Urethra (male)** | Penile → inguinal; bulbomembranous and prostatic → internal/external iliac, obturator | Female: proximal → pelvic; distal → inguinal |

### Chyluria: definition
- Passage of **chyle** (intestinal lymph rich in chylomicrons) in urine, giving milky urine. Caused by an abnormal **lymphourinary (chylourinary) fistula**, usually at the renal fornices.

### Etiology
| Parasitic (commonest; India, China) | Non-parasitic |
|---|---|
| **Wuchereria bancrofti** (lymphatic filariasis) | Congenital lymphangiectasia, renal lymphangioma |
|  | Trauma, surgery (e.g. partial nephrectomy, RPLND (retroperitoneal lymph node dissection)) |
|  | Retroperitoneal tumour or fibrosis, tuberculosis, pregnancy |

### Pathogenesis (obstructive theory)
- Adult worms live in retroperitoneal lymphatics and cause lymphangitis and fibrosis. This **obstructs** flow between the intestinal lymph trunks and the thoracic duct.
- Proximal lymphatics dilate and their valves fail, so chyle **refluxes** into renal hilar lymphatics.
- Raised pressure ruptures them into a calyceal fornix → **pyelolymphatic fistula**. Rarely the fistula is in the ureter or bladder.
- Usually intermittent; may remit spontaneously.

### Clinical features
- Milky urine, worse after fatty meals; chylous clots (retention, colic); haematochyluria.
- Loss of fat and protein → weight loss, hypoproteinaemia, anaemia, lowered immunity.
- **Severity:** mild (intermittent, no systemic effect); moderate (persistent, clots); severe (haematochyluria, clot retention, weight loss, hypoalbuminaemia).

### Evaluation
- **Confirm chyle:** **ether (chloroform) test**: urine shaken with ether clears because fat dissolves. **Urine triglycerides** positive; Sudan III stains fat globules. Distinguish from phosphaturia (clears with acetic acid) and pyuria.
- **Filarial tests:** night blood smear (often negative at this stage), filarial antigen test, eosinophilia.
- **Lateralise the side:** **cystoscopy after a fatty meal** shows milky efflux from the ureteric orifice. Retrograde pyelography shows **pyelolymphatic backflow**.
- **Define fistula:** **lymphoscintigraphy** (non-invasive, shows tracer in kidney and bladder), pedal lymphangiography (historical gold standard), MR lymphangiography or CT.
- Serum albumin, haemoglobin, renal function.

### Management
```
Mild → diet + DEC
Persistent / moderate → add sclerotherapy (can repeat)
Severe or failed sclerotherapy → renal hilar lymphatic disconnection
```
**1. Conservative**
- **Low-fat, high-protein diet with MCT (medium-chain triglyceride) oil**, plenty of fluids. MCTs are absorbed straight into the portal blood, not into lacteals, so chyle formation falls.
- **DEC (diethylcarbamazine)** 6 mg/kg/day for 12 days kills microfilariae. Albendazole, ivermectin or doxycycline (anti-*Wolbachia*) are alternatives.

**2. Endoscopic sclerotherapy (renal pelvic instillation)**
- Ureteric catheter on the affected side, head-down. Instil **silver nitrate** (0.5–1%) or **povidone-iodine**, repeated over several sessions.
- Chemical lymphangitis and fibrosis seal the fistula. Good initial success; recurrence common.
- Complications: flank pain, haematuria, papillary necrosis, ureteric stricture, anuria; rarely renal failure or death (silver nitrate).

**3. Surgery: renal hilar lymphatic disconnection (chylolymphatic disconnection)**
- **Indications:** severe disease, failed sclerotherapy, haematochyluria with clot retention.
- **Steps:** full renal mobilisation (nephrolysis) → **skeletonise the renal artery and vein**, ligating or sealing all hilar lymphatics → strip the upper ureter → remove perinephric fat → nephropexy ± omental wrap around the hilum.
- Reflux occurs only through hilar lymphatics, so upper-pole dissection can be limited. On-table cystoscopy confirms clear urine.
- **Approach:** open, laparoscopic (transperitoneal or retroperitoneal) or robotic. Retroperitoneoscopic ligation beats open (shorter operation and stay, less blood loss, fewer complications).
- **Also know:** lymphovenous anastomosis; autotransplantation (rare); nephrectomy obsolete; prevent by clipping lymphatics during renal and nodal surgery.

**Diagram to draw:** Retroperitoneal lymphatics with cisterna chyli, obstruction and pyelolymphatic fistula at a calyceal fornix.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Right → left lymph flow; cisterna chyli at L1–L2.
> - Chyluria = pyelolymphatic fistula, usually filarial (*W. bancrofti*).
> - Ether test and triglycerides confirm; cystoscopy after fatty meal lateralises.
> - Diet (MCT) + DEC → sclerotherapy → laparoscopic hilar lymphatic disconnection.

## Q2. Discuss types of access, gases used in Laparoscopic urology and describe comparative analysis of open, Laparoscopic and Robotic Radical prostatectomy.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Access in laparoscopy
**A. Transperitoneal: closed (Veress)**
- Veress needle: 14-gauge, spring-loaded blunt stylet. Check flow and tip recoil first. Two "pops": fascia, then peritoneum.
- **Sites:** umbilicus (thinnest wall; aim towards pelvis in non-obese); lower quadrants; **Palmer point** (left subcostal, midclavicular line) with suspected adhesions; avoid scars.
- **Confirmation tests:** aspiration–injection–aspiration of saline; **drop test**; advancement test (1 cm free). Then insufflate: opening pressure <8–10 mmHg, set limit 15 mmHg.
- First trocar inserted blind, or under vision with an **optical trocar** (0° lens inside a clear tip).

**B. Open (Hasson)**
- 2-cm infra-umbilical incision; fascia and peritoneum opened under vision; stay sutures; blunt cannula.
- Safer with adhesions or previous surgery and early in training; more gas leak.

**C. Retroperitoneal**
- 2-cm incision below the tip of the 12th rib; finger dissection behind Gerota's fascia, in front of psoas → **balloon dilatation** (commercial or **Gaur** glove-on-catheter balloon) → balloon-tip port to prevent gas leak and emphysema.

**D. Extraperitoneal (pelvic):** infra-umbilical, behind rectus into the Retzius space with a balloon (used for extraperitoneal prostatectomy).

**E. Others:** hand-assisted (6.5–7.5 cm port); single-port/LESS (laparoendoscopic single-site); direct trocar entry without pneumoperitoneum.

- **Trocars:** **blunt dilating trocars** have replaced bladed ones (about fivefold less epigastric injury, fewer hernias). Close fascia of midline ports >10 mm or stretched sites (all ≥5 mm in children).
- **Access complications:** preperitoneal insufflation, vessel or bowel puncture, gas embolism, subcutaneous emphysema.

### Gases used
| Gas | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **CO₂ (carbon dioxide; standard)** | Non-combustible, cheap, **highly blood-soluble** (embolism less lethal), quickly absorbed | Absorbed → hypercarbia, acidosis, arrhythmia; dangerous in COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) |
| **Nitrous oxide** | Less peritoneal pain, fewer acid–base effects | **Supports combustion**: no electrosurgery |
| **Helium** | Inert, no hypercarbia; use in lung disease | Poorly soluble → embolism; costly |
| **Argon** | Inert | Embolism risk |
| Air, oxygen | – | Embolism, combustion: abandoned |

- **Physiology:** keep pressure 10–15 mmHg (12 mmHg more physiological). Pressure reduces venous return, raises afterload, causes **oliguria** (renal vein and parenchymal compression) and splints the diaphragm.
- CO₂ absorption plateaus at 30–60 minutes; higher with extraperitoneal insufflation. Hypercarbia peaks after extubation.
- **Gas embolism:** sudden collapse, millwheel murmur, end-tidal CO₂ rise then fall. Treat: stop insufflation, **left lateral, head-down**, 100% oxygen, aspirate via central line.

### Comparison of open (RRP), laparoscopic (LRP) and robotic (RALP) radical prostatectomy
- **Background:** anatomical nerve-sparing open RRP (Walsh, 1982) was the standard. First LRP (Schuessler, 1997) was too slow; French centres standardised it in 2000, but laparoscopic suturing of the anastomosis remained a barrier. The robot (da Vinci, 2000) removed that barrier, and RALP is now dominant in the USA.
| Parameter | Open RRP | LRP | RALP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vision | Direct, loupes | 2D, unstable | **3D, 10× magnified**, stable |
| Operating time | Shortest | Longest early | Longer than open; falls with experience |
| Instruments | Hands, haptic feedback | Rigid, fulcrum effect | **Wristed** (7 degrees of freedom); no haptics |
| Learning curve | Moderate | **Steepest** (suturing) | Shorter |
| Blood loss | 300–1000 mL | Less (pneumoperitoneum tamponade) | Least; **transfusion 1–2%** |
| Hospital stay, catheter | Longer; catheter ~2 weeks | Shorter | Usually 1 day; catheter ~1 week |
| Pain, return to work | More | Less | Less; normal activity 3–4 weeks |
| Positive margins | Similar | Similar | Similar (~15%; ~9% if pT2) |
| Continence (1 year) | ~93% (expert) | Similar | Similar; possibly earlier |
| Potency | Surgeon-dependent | Similar | Similar |
| Bladder neck stricture | Up to 16% (older series) | Lower | **<2%** (mucosa-to-mucosa) |
| Unique risks | Wound infection, hernia | Conversion, CO₂ effects | Steep Trendelenburg (eye, nerve), robot failure (<0.5%) |
| Cost | Lowest | Moderate | **Highest** |

### Evidence
- **Yaxley 2016 (randomised, one open vs one robotic surgeon):** equal continence, potency and early margins at 3 months; less blood loss and shorter stay with robot.
- **Coughlin 2018:** no difference in urinary or sexual function at 24 months.
- **LAP-01 (robotic vs laparoscopic):** earlier continence with robot.
- **Concept:** the **surgeon, not the platform**, decides outcome; margins depend mainly on tumour (Gleason ≥7, stage) and surgeon volume. The robot's advantages are recovery and wider reach (obese patients, prior surgery).
- **Also know:** transperitoneal vs extraperitoneal approach (no consistent difference); Retzius-sparing RALP (earlier continence, more margins); single-port (SP) robot; new robotic platforms; telesurgery.

**Diagram to draw:** Veress needle sites (umbilicus, Palmer point) and trocar layout for RALP.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Veress tests: aspiration, drop, advancement; opening pressure <10 mmHg.
> - CO₂ standard (soluble, non-flammable); helium in COPD; N₂O supports combustion.
> - Gas embolism: left lateral head-down, oxygen, aspirate.
> - Yaxley 2016: equal function and margins; robot less blood loss and stay.

## Q3a. Non iodinated contrast materials
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- Used when iodinated contrast is risky (allergy, renal impairment) or with MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) and ultrasound.

### 1. Gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs)
- **Mechanism:** paramagnetic gadolinium **shortens T1 relaxation** of nearby water → bright enhancement. Free gadolinium is toxic, so it is **chelated** (linear or macrocyclic; macrocyclic is more stable). Usual dose 0.1 mmol/kg; renal excretion.
- **NSF (nephrogenic systemic fibrosis):** skin and organ fibrosis, joint contractures, no proven treatment. Risk: eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) <30 mL/min/1.73 m², dialysis, AKI (acute kidney injury).

| ACR (American College of Radiology) group | Agents | NSF risk |
|---|---|---|
| I (linear) | Gadodiamide, gadopentetate, gadoversetamide | Highest |
| **II** | Gadobenate, gadoteridol, gadoterate, gadobutrol | **Little or none, even in ESRD (end-stage renal disease)** |
| III | Gadoxetate (liver-specific agent) | Few NSF data; recent ACR editions have moved some agents between groups, so check the current manual |

- **Gadolinium deposition:** traces retained in brain (dentate nucleus) and bone, mainly with linear agents; no proven harm. Europe suspended most linear agents (2017).
- **Other:** allergic reactions rarer than with iodinated agents; not nephrotoxic at usual doses; avoid in pregnancy unless essential.
- **Uses:** renal mass, prostate mpMRI (multiparametric MRI), MR urography and angiography.

### 2. Ultrasound contrast (microbubbles)
- Gas core (perfluorocarbon or sulfur hexafluoride) in a lipid or albumin shell; strong harmonic echoes.
- Purely **intravascular**; gas exhaled by lungs. **No nephrotoxicity or radiation.** Rare cardiopulmonary reactions.
- **CEUS (contrast-enhanced ultrasound):** solid vs cystic renal mass (Bosniak), ablation monitoring, surveillance in CKD (chronic kidney disease). Intravesical use: **voiding urosonography for VUR (vesicoureteral reflux)**; ureteric patency after PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy).

### 3. Other agents
- **CO₂ angiography:** negative contrast; not allergenic or nephrotoxic. Useful in CKD; **never above the diaphragm** (cerebral embolism).
- **Ferumoxytol** (iron oxide): MRI agent in renal failure. Lymphotropic iron nanoparticles help nodal staging (nodal metastases fail to darken).
- **Barium:** GI (gastrointestinal) studies only; contraindicated if perforation suspected.
- **Also know:** air as negative contrast (historical); radiotracers (⁹⁹ᵐTc, PET (positron emission tomography)) are not contrast agents; MRI safety (pacemakers, metal).

**Diagram to draw:** Chelated gadolinium (macrocyclic vs linear ring).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - GBCAs shorten T1; NSF risk eGFR <30; group II agents almost no risk.
> - Microbubbles: intravascular, no nephrotoxicity; ceVUS for reflux.
> - CO₂ angiography: never above diaphragm.

## Q3b. Acetylcysteine.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- **NAC (N-acetylcysteine):** acetylated form of the amino acid cysteine; a **thiol (sulfhydryl, –SH) compound**. Given orally, intravenously or by nebuliser.

### Mechanisms of action
- **Glutathione precursor:** supplies cysteine, the rate-limiting substrate for **glutathione** synthesis.
- **Antioxidant:** the free –SH group scavenges reactive oxygen species.
- **Mucolytic:** –SH breaks **disulfide bonds** in mucoproteins, lowering mucus viscosity.
- **Thiol–disulfide exchange:** theoretically forms a soluble mixed disulfide with cystine.

### Clinical uses
**1. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose: main indication**
- Overdose saturates normal pathways; the toxic metabolite **NAPQI** (N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine) accumulates once glutathione is used up, causing hepatic necrosis.
- NAC restores glutathione. It is most effective **within 8 hours**, but still worth giving later.
- **IV (intravenous) 21-hour regimen:** 150 mg/kg, then 50 mg/kg over 4 h, then 100 mg/kg over 16 h. Shorter two-bag regimens are now used in some countries.

**2. Mucolytic:** COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), cystic fibrosis.

### Urological relevance
- **Contrast-induced AKI (acute kidney injury):** once widely used (early small trials, KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) 2012 suggestion). **PRESERVE (2018):** NAC gave no benefit over saline. It is **no longer recommended**; isotonic saline hydration is the only proven measure.
- **Cystinuria:** thiol action is theoretical; standard thiols are **tiopronin** and D-penicillamine, not NAC.
- **Cyclophosphamide cystitis:** **mesna** (also a thiol) is the agent used.
- **Mucus after enterocystoplasty or neobladder:** sometimes used orally or in irrigation; evidence weak.
- **Male infertility:** antioxidant for oxidative stress; limited evidence.

### Adverse effects
- **Anaphylactoid reactions** with IV loading (flushing, rash, bronchospasm): slow the infusion, give antihistamine.
- Nausea, vomiting; unpleasant sulphur smell; bronchospasm when nebulised in asthma.

**Diagram to draw:** Paracetamol → NAPQI → glutathione conjugation, with NAC replenishing glutathione.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Thiol; glutathione precursor, antioxidant, mucolytic.
> - Antidote for paracetamol overdose; best within 8 h.
> - PRESERVE 2018: no benefit in contrast AKI.
> - Cystinuria uses tiopronin, not NAC.

## Q3c. Health related quality of life instruments (HRQOL) in urology.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **HRQOL (health-related quality of life):** the parts of life affected by health: physical, emotional and social function and the patient's bother. It differs from a **symptom score**, which only counts symptoms.
- Must be patient-reported; doctors underestimate dysfunction.

### Types of instruments
| Type | Measures | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Generic** | Domains common to all diseases; allow comparison across diseases | **SF-36**, SF-12, **EQ-5D** (5 items + VAS (visual analogue scale); gives utilities for QALYs (quality-adjusted life-years)), Sickness Impact Profile |
| **Cancer-specific** | Cancer effects | **FACT-G** (Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–General), **EORTC QLQ-C30** (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer) |
| **Disease-specific** | Domains of one disease; more responsive | See below |

### Urology-specific instruments
- **Prostate cancer:** **EPIC (Expanded Prostate Cancer Index Composite)**, EPIC-26, UCLA (University of California, Los Angeles) Prostate Cancer Index, FACT-P.
- **Bladder cancer:** FACT-Bl, **Bladder Cancer Index**, EORTC QLQ-BLM30 and NMIBC-24, FACT-Vanderbilt Cystectomy Index.
- **Kidney cancer:** FKSI (FACT–Kidney Symptom Index).
- **LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms):** **IPSS (International Prostate Symptom Score)** 7 items + 1 quality-of-life item; BPH Impact Index.
- **Incontinence and OAB (overactive bladder):** **ICIQ** (International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire), **OAB-q**, King's Health Questionnaire, I-QOL.
- **Sexual function:** **IIEF** (International Index of Erectile Function; 15 items), SHIM (5 items; no bother, so not true HRQOL), FSFI (Female Sexual Function Index).
- **Others:** NIH-CPSI (chronic prostatitis), O'Leary–Sant (interstitial cystitis), Wisconsin Stone QOL, USSQ (ureteric stent).
- **PROMIS (Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System):** free item banks, **computer-adaptive testing**, common T-score.

### Psychometric properties (choosing a tool)
| Property | Meaning | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Internal consistency | Items agree | Cronbach α >0.7 (>0.9 = redundant) |
| Test–retest reliability | Stable over time | r >0.7 |
| Content (face) validity | Right items present | Expert judgement |
| Criterion validity | Agrees with gold standard | r ≥0.7 |
| Construct validity | Convergent and divergent | Correlations |
| **Responsiveness** | Detects real change | **MCID (minimal clinically important difference)**: AUA (American Urological Association) Symptom Index 3.1 points; otherwise ≥½ SD |

- Validity is population-specific; translations need revalidation.
- Development: define domains → generate items → pilot → psychometric testing (Likert scales).

**Diagram to draw:** Pyramid: symptoms → function → bother → overall HRQOL.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Generic (SF-36, EQ-5D) vs disease-specific (EPIC, IPSS, ICIQ, IIEF).
> - SHIM lacks bother; I-QOL lacks symptoms.
> - Reliable, valid, responsive; Cronbach α >0.7; IPSS MCID ~3 points.

## Q3d. Distal Renal tubular acidosis.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **Distal (type 1) RTA (renal tubular acidosis):** the collecting duct cannot secrete H⁺ despite systemic acidosis. Result: **hypokalaemic, hyperchloraemic, normal anion gap metabolic acidosis** with **urine pH >6**. The only acidosis with alkaline urine.

### Normal acidification and the defect
- **α-intercalated cells** secrete H⁺ by apical H⁺-ATPase; bicarbonate exits by basolateral Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger (AE1). Normally urine pH can fall to 4.5.
- In distal RTA, potassium is secreted in place of H⁺ → hypokalaemia.

### Causes
| Inherited | Acquired (secondary) |
|---|---|
| **SLC4A1** (AE1): mostly dominant | Sjögren syndrome, SLE (systemic lupus erythematosus) |
| **ATP6V1B1, ATP6V0A4** (H⁺-ATPase): recessive, **sensorineural deafness** | Obstructive uropathy, pyelonephritis, medullary sponge kidney |
| **CA2**: mixed proximal–distal | Hypercalciuria, hyperparathyroidism, amphotericin B |

### Why stones form (urological importance)
- **Stones in up to 70%**: **calcium phosphate** stones and **nephrocalcinosis**. Stones lead to diagnosis in up to half.
- **Profound hypocitraturia** (most important): acidosis increases proximal citrate reabsorption.
- **Hypercalciuria:** bone buffering releases calcium. **High urine pH** precipitates calcium phosphate.

### Clinical features
- Stones, nephrocalcinosis, weakness (hypokalaemia), osteomalacia or rickets; children fail to thrive.
- **Incomplete distal RTA:** normal bicarbonate, but urine pH cannot fall below 5.5 after an acid load.

### Diagnosis
- Low bicarbonate, high chloride, low potassium; urine pH >6 on a fasting morning sample.
- **UAG (urine anion gap) = (Na⁺ + K⁺) − Cl⁻:** **positive** (low ammonium excretion) → renal cause; negative in diarrhoea.
- **Ammonium chloride loading** (0.1 g/kg orally; hourly urine pH): **failure to fall below 5.5** confirms. Furosemide–fludrocortisone test is better tolerated.
- 24-hour urine: low citrate, high calcium; ultrasound for nephrocalcinosis.

### Treatment
- **Potassium citrate** (about 1–2 mEq/kg/day in divided doses): corrects acidosis, hypokalaemia and hypocitraturia, reduces calcium excretion and stone formation. Sodium alkali is less suitable because sodium raises urinary calcium.
- Treat the cause; manage stones endourologically.
- **Also know:** proximal (type 2) RTA (bicarbonate wasting, Fanconi, few stones); type 4 (hyperkalaemic, low urine pH; diabetes, obstruction); topiramate and acetazolamide mimic distal RTA.

**Diagram to draw:** α-intercalated cell with H⁺-ATPase, AE1 and the stone mechanism.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Urine pH >6 with acidosis; hypokalaemia; positive UAG.
> - Calcium phosphate stones in up to 70%; hypocitraturia key.
> - NH₄Cl test: pH fails to fall below 5.5.
> - Potassium citrate is the treatment.

## Q3e. PET scans in urology.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Principle
- **PET (positron emission tomography):** tracer emits a positron → annihilation with an electron → **two 511-keV photons at 180°** → coincidence detection. Fused with CT (computed tomography) or MRI.
- Quantitative (**SUV, standardised uptake value**); costly. Half-lives: ¹⁸F 110 min, ⁶⁸Ga 68 min, ¹¹C 20 min (on-site cyclotron).

### Tracers
| Tracer | Mechanism | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| **¹⁸F-FDG (fluorodeoxyglucose)** | Glucose analogue trapped after phosphorylation (Warburg effect) | Bladder, UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma), penile, seminoma |
| **PSMA (prostate-specific membrane antigen)** ligands (⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11, ¹⁸F-DCFPyL, ¹⁸F-PSMA-1007, rhPSMA-7.3) | Bind cell-surface PSMA | Prostate staging and recurrence |
| ¹¹C/¹⁸F-choline | Cell membrane synthesis | Prostate recurrence (older) |
| ¹⁸F-fluciclovine | Amino acid transport | Prostate recurrence |
| ¹⁸F-NaF (sodium fluoride) | Bone turnover | Bone metastases |

### Uses by organ
**Prostate cancer**
- FDG is poor (low glucose metabolism). PSMA has specificity >90%.
- **Primary staging (high risk):** **proPSMA (2020)**: PSMA PET/CT more accurate than CT plus bone scan (92% vs 65%). Nodal sensitivity only 30–40%, so a negative scan does not exclude micrometastases.
- **BCR (biochemical recurrence):** detection rises with PSA (prostate-specific antigen); about half positive below 0.5 ng/mL. PSMA beats fluciclovine (56% vs 26%). CONDOR: management changed in 63.9%.
- **Theranostics:** PSMA-positive mCRPC (metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer) → **¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA-617 (VISION 2021)**.
- NCCN (National Comprehensive Cancer Network): PSMA PET preferred; outcome benefit still unproven.

**Testis**
- **Seminoma residual mass >3 cm:** FDG-PET **≥6 weeks after chemotherapy**. Negative scan reliable (observe); positive → consider resection.
- **Not for NSGCT (non-seminomatous germ cell tumour):** teratoma is not FDG-avid.

**Bladder and UTUC**
- FDG for nodal and distant staging in MIBC (muscle-invasive bladder cancer): modest sensitivity, high specificity; urinary excretion hides the primary. Use when CT is equivocal.

**Kidney**
- FDG not routine (clear cell RCC (renal cell carcinoma) poorly avid). **⁸⁹Zr-girentuximab** (anti-CAIX, carbonic anhydrase IX) identifies clear cell RCC (ZIRCON).

**Penis**
- FDG confirms metastases in **palpable inguinal nodes**; poor for impalpable nodes.

- **Also know:** EMPIRE-1 (fluciclovine-guided salvage radiotherapy); false positives from inflammation; neuroendocrine cancer loses PSMA.

**Diagram to draw:** Positron annihilation with two 511-keV photons hitting a detector ring.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - proPSMA: PSMA PET 92% vs 65% accuracy.
> - Seminoma residual >3 cm: FDG-PET at ≥6 weeks; not NSGCT.
> - FDG poor in prostate and clear cell RCC.
> - VISION: ¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA-617 in mCRPC.

## Q3f. Complicated urinary tract infections.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **Complicated UTI (urinary tract infection):** infection in a **structurally or functionally abnormal tract** or a **compromised host**, or with a resistant organism; more likely to fail or recur.
- **FDA (US Food and Drug Administration) 2018:** pyuria + pathogen in urine or blood + fever, chills, flank pain, with an abnormality or catheter.
- **Uncomplicated:** healthy non-pregnant woman, normal tract.

### Complicating factors
| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Obstruction | BOO (bladder outlet obstruction), PUJ (pelviureteric junction) obstruction, stricture, stone, tumour |
| Voiding dysfunction | Neurogenic bladder, high PVR (post-void residual), VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) |
| Foreign body / iatrogenic | Catheter, stent, nephrostomy, recent instrumentation, hospital-acquired |
| Anatomical | Diverticulum, fistula, cystocele |
| Host | **Male sex**, pregnancy, diabetes, immunosuppression, renal failure, transplant, elderly |
| Other | Symptoms >7 days; recent antibiotics |

- Each causes stasis, biofilm or weak defences.

### Organisms
- ***E. coli*** under half; more *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Pseudomonas*, *Enterococcus*, *Candida*. Often **MDR (multidrug-resistant)** or ESBL (extended-spectrum β-lactamase) producers.

### Evaluation
- **Urine culture mandatory before antibiotics**; blood cultures if febrile.
- Renal function, glucose, lactate.
- **Imaging:** ultrasound first; **CT (computed tomography)** if no response in 72 hours, stones, abscess or emphysematous infection suspected.

### Treatment
- **Empirical IV (intravenous) therapy** by local resistance (EAU (European Association of Urology)): amoxicillin + aminoglycoside, 2nd-generation cephalosporin + aminoglycoside, or IV 3rd-generation cephalosporin. Avoid fluoroquinolones if used in the last 6 months or resistance >10%. Carbapenem or newer agents for ESBL (ceftolozane–tazobactam, ceftazidime–avibactam).
- **Source control is essential:** drain obstruction (**stent or nephrostomy, equally effective**), change catheter, drain abscess, treat stones later.
- Switch to oral when afebrile and stable; de-escalate by culture.
- **Duration:** usually 7–14 days (14 days in men if prostatitis not excluded); 7 days may suffice once afebrile ≥48 hours.
- **Sepsis:** antibiotics within 1 hour, fluids, vasopressors, ICU (intensive care unit).
- Correct the underlying cause, or infection recurs.

### Special forms
- **Also know:** emphysematous pyelonephritis and cystitis (diabetics); pyonephrosis; renal and perinephric abscess; XGP (xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis); CAUTI (catheter-associated UTI); funguria; unresolved bacteriuria (resistance, staghorn "critical mass").

**Diagram to draw:** Flowchart: suspect cUTI → culture → imaging → empirical IV → drain obstruction → oral step-down.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Complicated = abnormal tract or compromised host; male sex alone qualifies.
> - Culture mandatory; often MDR.
> - Drain obstruction: stent = nephrostomy.
> - 7–14 days; oral step-down when afebrile.
