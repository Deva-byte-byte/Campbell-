## Q1. Describe clinical implication of AIDS on the Genito-Urinary Tract?
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome) is the late stage of HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) infection. HIV is a retrovirus that destroys CD4 T lymphocytes. It spreads by blood, semen, vaginal fluid and breast milk.

| Stage | Features |
|---|---|
| 1. Acute | 2–4 weeks after infection; flu-like illness; high viral load; antibody tests may be negative |
| 2. Clinical latency | Years; usually no symptoms |
| 3. AIDS | **CD4 <200 cells/mm³** or an AIDS-defining condition |

- **Concept:** genitourinary (GU) disease follows the falling CD4 count. Organisms become opportunistic, infections become chronic and the GU tract becomes a reservoir. With HAART (highly active antiretroviral therapy), the burden shifts to drug toxicity and diseases of ageing.

### Diagnosis (urologist's role)
- Test anyone with, or at risk of, an STD (sexually transmitted disease), and every TB (tuberculosis) patient.
- Antigen–antibody immunoassay → antibody differentiation assay; if discordant, HIV RNA (acute infection).

### Manifestations by site
| Site | Manifestations |
|---|---|
| **Genital skin and STDs** | Ulcerative STDs (herpes, syphilis, chancroid) increase HIV acquisition and transmission; HSV-2 (herpes simplex virus 2) in 50–90%. Warts (HPV (human papillomavirus) 6, 11) in about 20%, may be giant; molluscum 5–18%. Perineal cellulitis (cover MRSA (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*)); Fournier gangrene commoner |
| **UTI (urinary tract infection)** | About fivefold commoner. Bacteriuria about 30% at CD4 <200, 11% at 200–500, none above 500. *E. coli* and other gram-negatives; *Pseudomonas* up to 30%. *Salmonella* UTI needs lifelong prophylaxis. Culture before treatment |
| **Opportunistic UTI** | No response, or CD4 <500: look for fungi (*Candida*, *Cryptococcus*, *Histoplasma*), TB, CMV (cytomegalovirus), adenovirus |
| **Kidney** | Opportunistic pyelonephritis (*Pneumocystis*, *Candida*, mycobacteria). AKI (acute kidney injury) from sepsis, dehydration, nephrotoxins (tenofovir, indinavir, amphotericin). Obstruction by stones, lymphoma, fungal balls |
| **GU TB** | HIV raises active TB 15–20-fold. Disease is disseminated and bilateral, with **less fibrosis, fewer strictures and fewer contracted bladders**, because fibrosis needs an intact immune response. Mortality is higher. Same 6-month regimen; rifampicin lowers protease inhibitor levels |
| **Prostate** | Bacterial, TB and fungal prostatitis; prostatic abscess and urosepsis; relapse up to 70%. Cryptococcal prostatitis is a reservoir for relapsing meningitis, so give long-term antifungals |
| **Testis and epididymis** | Recurrent, chronic epididymo-orchitis (including TB). Testis harbours opportunists. Testicular atrophy and hypogonadism |
| **Voiding** | LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) worsen with disease stage. Retention may herald seroconversion. Neurogenic causes: HIV encephalopathy, toxoplasmosis, **CMV polyradiculopathy** (bladder and bowel dysfunction with sciatic pain: lumbar puncture, treat early), neuropathy |
| **Sexual function** | ED (erectile dysfunction) in about half of men under 50; protease inhibitors worsen it. **Protease inhibitors raise sildenafil levels 3–10-fold: start low.** Low libido, ejaculatory disorders |

### HIVAN (HIV-associated nephropathy)
- Nephrotic-range proteinuria and rising creatinine, often without oedema or hypertension. Typically CD4 <200 and viral load >1000 copies/mL; about 12 times commoner in Black patients.
- Biopsy: **collapsing FSGS (focal segmental glomerulosclerosis)** with tubular microcysts.
- Treatment: HAART plus ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitor. Without it, dialysis follows within about 10 months.

### Stones
- **Indinavir** stones in up to 22% of treated patients. Indinavir precipitates in alkaline urine. Stones are radiolucent, and pure stones may be invisible even on CT (computed tomography).
- Stop the drug and hydrate: succeeds in up to 70%. Newer protease inhibitors rarely cause stones.

### Malignancy
| AIDS-defining | Non-AIDS-defining |
|---|---|
| **Kaposi sarcoma** (HHV-8 (human herpesvirus 8)), non-Hodgkin lymphoma, cervical cancer | Penile, anal, testicular, prostate cancers; Hodgkin lymphoma |
| Falls with HAART | Now about 70% of cancers in treated HIV |

- **Kaposi sarcoma:** genital in about 3% of AIDS; glans lesions can block the meatus. Treat with ART (antiretroviral therapy) first. Local excision, laser or intralesional vinblastine; radiotherapy 15–30 Gy; systemic chemotherapy if disseminated.
- **Penile cancer:** HIV raises HPV infection; about fourfold risk of death.

### Implications for urological practice
- **Universal precautions** for every patient: double gloves, no hand-to-hand passing of sharps.
- Occupational risk: percutaneous about 0.3%, mucous membrane about 0.09%.
- **PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis)** within 72 hours, for 28 days: tenofovir + emtricitabine plus raltegravir or dolutegravir. HIV test at 6 weeks, 3 and 6 months.
- CD4 <200: TMP-SMX (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) *Pneumocystis* prophylaxis also lowers UTI.
- **Prevention:** male circumcision cuts heterosexual acquisition by 50–60% (three African RCTs (randomised controlled trials)); consistent condom use cuts risk about 80%; PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis).

**Also know:** IRIS (immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome) on starting ART; serodiscordant couples: suppressed viral load with sperm washing and assisted reproduction; transplantation is possible with controlled HIV.

**Diagram to draw:** male GU tract outline labelled with the lesion at each site.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - AIDS = CD4 <200; UTI risk rises as CD4 falls.
> - HIV-TB: disseminated, less fibrosis, fewer strictures.
> - Indinavir stones: radiolucent, may be CT-invisible; stop drug and hydrate.
> - PEP within 72 hours for 28 days; circumcision cuts acquisition 50–60%.

## Q2. Describe embryological development of VU Junction and outline management of primary VUR?
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Part A: Embryology of the VUJ (vesicoureteric junction)
1. **Ureteric bud (weeks 4–6)** arises from the caudal mesonephric (Wolffian) duct: ureter and duct form a "Y", the common nephric duct is the stem.
2. **Reciprocal induction:** the bud enters the metanephric blastema, branches into the collecting system and induces nephrons.
3. **Weeks 5–6:** the urorectal septum divides the cloaca into urogenital sinus (UGS) and anorectal canal.
4. **Absorption:** the common nephric duct is taken into the UGS (current view: removed by **apoptosis**), so ureter and duct open separately.
5. **Rotation (Mackie and Stephens):** the ureteric orifice moves **cranially and laterally** to the bladder base; the duct stays caudal as the ejaculatory duct (Gartner duct in females).
6. **Trigone:** classically ureteral; lineage tracing shows mostly bladder muscle. **Chwalla membrane** over the orifice ruptures; persistence explains ureterocele.
7. **Maturation:** ureteric muscle joins the detrusor as **Waldeyer's sheath**; the tunnel lengthens with growth, so VUR resolves with age.

| Bud origin | Orifice | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | Trigone corner | Tunnel length to diameter about **5:1** (Paquin); refluxing UVJs about 1.4:1 |
| Too low (early contact) | High, **lateral** | Short tunnel → **primary VUR**; abnormal bud–blastema contact → dysplasia |
| Too high (late contact) | Caudal, **medial** | **Ectopic ureter** (bladder neck, urethra, Wolffian structures), often obstructed |
| Two buds | Duplex | **Weigert–Meyer rule:** upper pole caudal-medial (ectopia, ureterocele); lower pole lateral (reflux) |

- **Antireflux mechanism:** passive flap valve, ureteric peristalsis, low bladder pressure.
- **Concept:** bud position fixes both orifice and kidney, so high-grade VUR and dysplasia coexist.

### Part B: Management of primary VUR (vesicoureteral reflux)
- **Primary:** congenitally short tunnel with a normal bladder. **Secondary:** high bladder pressure (PUV (posterior urethral valves), neurogenic bladder, BBD (bladder and bowel dysfunction)).
- In about 30% of children with UTI and about 30% of siblings.
- **Infection, not reflux, injures the kidney.** The aim is to prevent febrile UTI and scarring while VUR resolves.

**International grading (VCUG, voiding cystourethrogram)**
| Grade | Findings |
|---|---|
| I | Ureter only, not dilated |
| II | Up to pelvis and calyces; no dilatation |
| III | Mild–moderate dilatation; minimal forniceal blunting |
| IV | Moderate dilatation and tortuosity |
| V | Gross dilatation and tortuosity; **papillary impressions lost** |

**Evaluation**
- Urine culture; BP (blood pressure), creatinine, urine protein; BBD history.
- **RBUS (renal and bladder ultrasound)**; VCUG (gold standard); nuclear cystogram for follow-up; **DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid) scan** for scars.

**Natural history:** grade II resolves in 63–85%; grade III about 50%; grades IV–V and bilateral III rarely. Younger age resolves better.

**Risk stratification:** high risk = female, under 1 year, bilateral, grade III–V, BBD, scars, multiple febrile UTIs.

**Medical management**
- **Treat BBD first:** timed voiding, constipation treatment, anticholinergics.
- **CAP (continuous antibiotic prophylaxis):** one low dose at night. Trimethoprim or amoxicillin under 2 months; TMP-SMX or nitrofurantoin over 2 months.
- Boys with phimosis: circumcision or topical steroid (about 4 circumcisions prevent one UTI).
- **RIVUR (2014):** recurrent UTI fell from 23.6% to 12.9% on TMP-SMX; **scarring unchanged**.
- **Swedish Reflux Trial** (grade III–IV): febrile UTI in girls 19% on CAP, 23% after endoscopy, 57% on surveillance; no difference in boys.
- IRSC (International Reflux Study in Children), Birmingham: surgery equalled medical care for scarring.
- Low-risk children: observation without CAP.

**Indications for correction:** breakthrough febrile UTI on CAP; new scars or falling function; persistent high grade; non-adherence; parental choice.

**Endoscopic injection:** Dx/HA (dextranomer/hyaluronic acid) by STING or HIT; about 90% for low grade after one injection (see 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q3c).

**Ureteric reimplantation:** open success **95.9%** (Elder 1997).
| Technique | Tunnel | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Cohen** (intravesical) | Cross-trigonal | Commonest; over 98%; later ureteroscopy difficult |
| Politano–Leadbetter | New hiatus above old | Long tunnel; blind hiatus risks bowel |
| Glenn–Anderson | Advanced towards bladder neck | Least kinking; short tunnel |
| **Lich–Gregoir** (extravesical) | Detrusorotomy | No haematuria or spasm; **retention up to 10% after bilateral** |
| RALUR-EV (robotic extravesical) | Y-shaped detrusorotomy | Less pain; approaching open results |

- Ultrasound at 6–12 weeks. Contralateral VUR in about 1 in 10, usually resolves.
- **Guidelines:** AUA (American Urological Association) 2010/2017 and EAU/ESPU (European Association of Urology/European Society for Paediatric Urology): treat BBD, CAP for high risk, correct after breakthrough infection.
- **Follow-up:** BP, growth; scarred women risk pre-eclampsia.

```
Treat BBD -> low risk: observe | high risk: CAP
Breakthrough UTI or new scar -> injection or reimplant
```

**Diagram to draw:** "Y" of ureteric bud and mesonephric duct with orifice migration; grades I–V.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Low (early) bud → lateral orifice → VUR plus dysplasia; high (late) bud → ectopia.
> - Tunnel length to diameter 5:1 (Paquin).
> - RIVUR: CAP halves recurrent UTI, not scarring.
> - Open reimplantation 95.9%; Cohen over 98%.

## Q3a. Berger's Disease.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Berger's disease is IgA (immunoglobulin A) nephropathy (IgAN).*

### Definition
- **Commonest primary glomerulonephritis (GN) worldwide**, in children too. Dominant **mesangial IgA deposits** on immunofluorescence.
- Commonest cause of **glomerular haematuria** (about one-third). Second–third decades; male predominance.
- **IgA vasculitis** (Henoch–Schönlein purpura) is the systemic form.

### Pathogenesis
- "Multi-hit" model: abnormal (galactose-deficient) IgA1 → autoantibodies → immune complexes → mesangial deposition, complement activation, proliferation. Mucosal (respiratory) infection triggers flares.

### Clinical features
- **Recurrent painless macroscopic haematuria during or within days of an upper respiratory infection** ("synpharyngitic"), or after exercise. Post-streptococcal GN follows infection by weeks.
- Persistent microscopic haematuria, proteinuria, hypertension; occasional loin pain; rarely nephrotic syndrome or RPGN (rapidly progressive GN).

### Evaluation (urologist's role)
- **Dysmorphic RBCs (red blood cells), RBC casts, proteinuria** → glomerular source. **Normal C3 and C4.**
- Without casts, complete the urological evaluation (imaging ± cystoscopy) to exclude a urinary tract cause.
- **Renal biopsy** confirms. Once diagnosed, repeat haematuria work-ups are not needed.

**Oxford MEST-C score (biopsy)**
| Item | Grades |
|---|---|
| M: mesangial hypercellularity | M0, M1 |
| E: endocapillary hypercellularity | E0, E1 |
| S: segmental sclerosis | S0, S1 |
| T: tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis | T0 ≤25%, T1 26–50%, T2 >50% |
| C: crescents | C0 none, C1 <25%, C2 ≥25% of glomeruli |

### Prognosis
- Often benign, but **20–30% reach ESKD (end-stage kidney disease)**.
- Poor: persistent proteinuria, hypertension, raised creatinine, older onset, high MEST-C.

### Management
- No cure. **Supportive:** ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitor or ARB (angiotensin receptor blocker), BP (blood pressure) control, low salt, stop smoking.
- **Newer drugs:** SGLT2 (sodium–glucose cotransporter 2) inhibitors; sparsentan (dual endothelin–angiotensin receptor blocker); targeted-release budesonide acting on gut mucosal lymphoid tissue.
- Persistent proteinuria >1 g/day despite supportive care: consider a 6-month steroid course (KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) 2021); weigh infection risk.
- Crescentic RPGN: steroids plus cyclophosphamide.
- **Transplant:** recurs histologically often, clinically rarely.

**Diagram to draw:** glomerulus with mesangial IgA deposits.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Commonest primary GN; haematuria with upper respiratory infection.
> - Normal complement; biopsy shows mesangial IgA.
> - Oxford MEST-C; 20–30% ESKD.
> - RAS blockade is the mainstay.

## Q3b. Describe instrumentation for development of extraperitoneal space in laparoscopy?
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- Extraperitoneal laparoscopy works outside the peritoneum: the **flank retroperitoneum** (kidney, adrenal, ureter) or the **pelvic prevesical (Retzius) space** (extraperitoneal prostatectomy, lymphadenectomy).
- There is no natural cavity, so a working space must be **dissected before insufflation**.

### Instruments
| Instrument | Description and use |
|---|---|
| Open (Hasson) access set | Scalpel, S-retractors, artery forceps |
| **Index finger** | Blunt first pocket; feels psoas behind, Gerota's fascia in front |
| **Commercial balloon dissector** | Transparent silicone balloon on a stiff hollow shaft; inflated with room air by bulb (maximum 800 mL, 40 pumps). Oblong for the retroperitoneum, round for the pelvis. Laparoscope inside confirms position |
| **Gaur balloon (1992)** | Size 7 glove (or glove fingers, condom) tied with 0 silk on an 8-Fr red rubber catheter; filled with saline (600–800 mL). Cheap, but no rigid shaft or view; latex can rupture into fragments |
| **Optical (visual) obturator trocar** with 0° scope | Enters under vision; yellow retroperitoneal fat confirms the plane; scope sweeps the space |
| **Blunt-tip balloon trocar** | Internal balloon plus external foam cuff gives an airtight seal; prevents gas leak, emphysema and hypercarbia |

### Technique
**Retroperitoneal**
1. Full flank, table flexed; 1.5–2.5-cm incision below the 12th rib tip.
2. Split muscles; open the lumbodorsal fascia; finger dissection between psoas and Gerota's fascia.
3. Balloon inflated cephalad then caudad, in front of psoas, behind Gerota's fascia.
4. Blunt-tip balloon trocar; insufflate; secondary ports under vision.

**Pelvic extraperitoneal**
- Infraumbilical incision → open anterior rectus sheath, separate recti → finger tunnels behind the rectus to the pubis → pierce transversalis fascia → balloon midline, then each side.

### Pitfalls
- Balloon in muscle → flank hernia. Too anterior → peritoneal tear or colon injury; too posterior → psoas or quadratus bleeding.
- Peritoneal tear shrinks the space; convert if needed. Extraperitoneal CO₂ needs more ventilation.

**Diagram to draw:** flank section with balloon between psoas and Gerota's fascia.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Space is created first: finger, then balloon.
> - Gaur balloon: glove on red rubber catheter.
> - Balloon trocar seals and prevents emphysema.
> - Balloon in muscle causes hernia.

## Q3c. Lichen sclerosis.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Taken as lichen sclerosus (LS), formerly balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO).*

### Definition
- Chronic, progressive, **scarring inflammatory disease of genital skin**; possibly premalignant.

### Epidemiology
- 6–10 times commoner in females (prepubertal and menopausal).
- Men: peak 30–50 years in one chapter, older men in another; also children. Commonly found at circumcision for phimosis after infancy. 15–20% have extragenital disease.

### Aetiology
- Autoimmune (thyroid disease, vitiligo, alopecia); familial clustering.
- **Chronic occlusion by urine** under the foreskin (dribbling, obesity) definitely increases risk.
- Infection and trauma proposed, unproven.

### Clinical features
- White, atrophic, sclerotic plaques; purpura, fissures; itch, pain, painful erection, poor stream.
- Sequence: prepuce → **phimosis** → glans → **meatal stenosis** (sentinel site) → fossa navicularis and penile urethral stricture.
- **Spares the proximal (bulbar and posterior) urethra.**
- Buried penis from loss of shaft skin.
- Mechanism of stricture: voiding against meatal stenosis forces urine into the glands of Littre → inflammation → spongiofibrosis.

### Evaluation
- Clinical diagnosis. **Biopsy** if doubtful or suspicious: hyperkeratosis, epidermal atrophy, basal vacuolation, dermal oedema with homogenised collagen, band-like lymphocytic infiltrate.
- Uroflowmetry; **RUG (retrograde urethrogram) before meatal surgery**, because a proximal stricture often coexists.

### Management
| Situation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Skin disease | **Clobetasol propionate 0.05%** daily for 1–3 months, then taper to twice-weekly maintenance; calcineurin inhibitors second line; retinoids for refractory disease |
| Phimosis | **Circumcision**, usually curative |
| Meatal stenosis | Dilatation with clobetasol; extended meatotomy; BMG (buccal mucosal graft) meatoplasty |
| Anterior stricture | **Oral mucosal graft, never genital skin**; staged or single stage |
| Panurethral or unfit | **Perineal urethrostomy** (easy because the proximal urethra is spared); self-dilatation |

- Recurrence 40–50% after medical treatment; restricture higher than in other aetiologies.

### Malignancy
- Penile SCC (squamous cell carcinoma) in **2.3–9%** on long follow-up; HPV (human papillomavirus)-independent type.
- **Lifelong follow-up**; biopsy any non-healing ulcer or nodule.

**Diagram to draw:** penis with LS at prepuce, glans, meatus and fossa, proximal urethra spared.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Genital skin disease; meatus is the sentinel site.
> - Spares proximal urethra; perineal urethrostomy easy.
> - Clobetasol first; oral mucosa, never skin, for repair.
> - SCC in 2.3–9%: lifelong follow-up.

## Q3d. Renal allograft rejection.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- Recipient immune attack on donor (allo) antigens: **HLA (human leucocyte antigen)** class I (A, B, C) and II (DR, DQ, DP), ABO and non-HLA antigens.
- Acute rejection has fallen from over 60% (pre-ciclosporin) to **below 10%**. **Chronic antibody injury** now limits graft survival.

### Mechanism
- **T-cell**: direct (donor antigen-presenting cells) and indirect pathways → cytotoxic T cells → tubulitis, arteritis.
- **Antibody**: DSA (donor-specific antibody) binds endothelium → complement, leaving **C4d** → microvascular inflammation.

### Types
| Type | Timing | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Hyperacute** | Minutes–hours | Preformed ABO/HLA antibody; thrombosis; graft turns blue. **Prevented by crossmatch** |
| Accelerated | 2–5 days | Memory response in sensitised recipient |
| **Acute TCMR (T-cell-mediated rejection)** | Mostly first 6 months | Interstitial infiltrate, tubulitis, arteritis |
| **Acute AMR (antibody-mediated rejection)** | Any time | DSA, C4d, capillaritis |
| **Chronic active AMR** | Months–years | Transplant glomerulopathy, arterial intimal fibrosis; main cause of late loss |
| Subclinical | — | Histological rejection, stable function |

- **Risk factors:** HLA mismatch, DSA, ABO incompatibility, long ischaemia and DGF (delayed graft function), **non-adherence**, retransplant, previous rejection.

### Diagnosis
- Rising creatinine, oliguria; fever and graft tenderness now rare.
- **Exclude:** obstruction, urine leak, lymphocele, renal artery stenosis or thrombosis (ultrasound with Doppler), CNI (calcineurin inhibitor) toxicity, BK virus nephropathy, recurrent disease.
- **Biopsy is the gold standard (Banff):**

| Banff category | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Borderline | Foci of tubulitis with mild inflammation |
| TCMR IA/IB | Interstitial inflammation >25% with moderate/severe tubulitis |
| TCMR IIA/IIB | Mild-moderate/severe intimal arteritis |
| TCMR III | Transmural arteritis or fibrinoid necrosis |
| Active AMR | Tissue injury + antibody–endothelium interaction (C4d) + circulating DSA |

- **Monitoring:** de novo DSA predicts loss; dd-cfDNA (donor-derived cell-free DNA) has negative predictive value >90%; gene-expression panels.

### Treatment
- **TCMR:** IV (intravenous) pulse methylprednisolone; **ATG (antithymocyte globulin)** for Banff II–III or steroid-resistant.
- **AMR:** not standardised: steroids, **plasmapheresis, IVIG (intravenous immunoglobulin)**, rituximab; eculizumab in selected patients; anti-IL-6 (interleukin-6) for chronic active AMR.
- Optimise maintenance (tacrolimus, mycophenolate, steroids); address adherence.

**Diagram to draw:** Banff biopsy features: tubulitis, arteritis, peritubular capillary C4d.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Hyperacute rejection prevented by crossmatch.
> - Banff AMR = injury + C4d + DSA.
> - TCMR: pulse steroids, ATG; AMR: plasmapheresis, IVIG.
> - Chronic active AMR is the main cause of late loss.

## Q3e. Leak point pressures-clinical implication.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **LPP (leak point pressure):** pressure at which leakage is first seen, spontaneous or provoked. Two independent types:

| Feature | ALPP (abdominal LPP), incl. VLPP (Valsalva LPP) and CLPP (cough LPP) | DLPP (detrusor LPP) |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Total Pves (vesical pressure) at leak during raised abdominal pressure, **no detrusor contraction** | Lowest Pdet (detrusor pressure) at leak, **without contraction or straining** |
| Measures | Sphincter's resistance to abdominal pressure | Poorly compliant bladder's pressure against outlet resistance |
| Bad result | **Low** (weak sphincter) | **High** (danger to upper tracts) |
| Used in | SUI (stress urinary incontinence) | Neurogenic or low-compliance bladder |

### VLPP: technique
1. Urethral and rectal catheters; standing; fill to **150 mL**; graded Valsalva; record **total Pves** at visible leak.
2. No leak: repeat every 50 mL; at capacity remove the urethral catheter and read the rectal line.

### VLPP: clinical implications
| VLPP (cmH₂O) | Inference (McGuire 1993) |
|---|---|
| **<60** | **ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency)** |
| 60–90 | Equivocal |
| **≥90** | **Urethral hypermobility**, little ISD |

- Low VLPP or ISD: favours a sling (or bulking) over colposuspension; in men, guides sling versus AUS (artificial urinary sphincter).
- **Limitations:** VLPP < CLPP; falls with larger catheters and volumes; invalid with DO (detrusor overactivity); poor predictor of surgical outcome.
- **Guidelines:** EAU: do not use LPP to grade severity. AUA/SUFU (Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction) 2023: urodynamics may be omitted in the index SUI patient.

### DLPP: clinical implications
- **McGuire (1981):** DLPP **>40 cmH₂O** in myelodysplastic children → hydronephrosis or VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) in **85%**.
- A strong outlet keeps the patient dry but transmits pressure to the kidneys; a weak outlet "pops off" safely.
- **Hostile bladder** (≥40 cmH₂O) → CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) plus antimuscarinic; botulinum toxin, augmentation or vesicostomy if needed.
- **Caveat:** 40 is not validated as "safe"; 20 cmH₂O is more sensitive in children. Aim as low as possible.

**Also know:** DO leak point pressure (ICS (International Continence Society) 2017); MUCP (maximum urethral closure pressure) ≤20 cmH₂O suggests ISD.

**Diagram to draw:** Pves, Pabd and Pdet traces marking ALPP and DLPP.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - ALPP low = weak sphincter; DLPP high = upper-tract danger.
> - VLPP <60 ISD; ≥90 hypermobility.
> - DLPP >40: 85% upper-tract change (McGuire 1981).
> - Lower DLPP is safer; no validated safe cut-off.

## Q3f. Role of renal nephrometry in management of RCC.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **Nephrometry scores** quantify the anatomical complexity of a renal mass on CT (computed tomography) or MRI, to standardise reporting of RCC (renal cell carcinoma) and plan treatment.

### R.E.N.A.L. score (Kutikov and Uzzo, 2009)
| Component | 1 point | 2 points | 3 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **R**adius (maximum diameter) | ≤4 cm | >4 to <7 cm | ≥7 cm |
| **E**xophytic/endophytic | ≥50% exophytic | <50% exophytic | Entirely endophytic |
| **N**earness to collecting system or sinus | ≥7 mm | >4 to <7 mm | ≤4 mm |
| **A**nterior/posterior | Suffix a, p or x (no points) | | |
| **L**ocation to polar lines | Entirely above or below | Crosses a polar line | >50% across, crosses midline, or entirely between |
- Suffix "h" if touching the main renal artery or vein. **Total 4–12:** low 4–6, moderate 7–9, high 10–12.

### PADUA score (Ficarra, 2009)
- Polar location, exophytic rate, renal rim (lateral or medial), renal sinus, collecting system and size, plus anterior/posterior face. **Total 6–14:** 6–7 low, 8–9 intermediate, ≥10 high risk.

### C-index (centrality index)
- Distance from tumour centre to kidney centre ÷ tumour radius. **Lower value = more central, more complex**; 0 means the tumour is centred on the kidney centre.

### Role in management
| Decision | Use |
|---|---|
| **Partial vs radical nephrectomy** | High complexity favours RN (radical nephrectomy) when PN (partial nephrectomy) would be hazardous |
| **Approach** | Open vs minimally invasive; hilar clamping |
| **Predict outcomes** | Warm ischaemia, blood loss, complications, conversion, collecting-system entry; urine leak (R and N components) |
| **Function** | Parenchymal loss, eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) decline |
| **Ablation** | Cryoablation complications rise with complexity |
| **Pathology** | Higher scores: malignancy, higher grade |
| **Research** | Common language between series |

- **AUA (American Urological Association) 2021:** prefer RN for a complex tumour only when PN would be challenging even in experts, with normal baseline function and a normal opposite kidney.

### Limitations
- Interobserver variation; ignores patient factors, fat and surgeon experience.

**Also know:** ABC (arterial based complexity), DAP (diameter–axial–polar), contact surface area; **MAP (Mayo Adhesive Probability) score** for adherent perinephric fat; SPARE (simplified PADUA).

**Diagram to draw:** kidney with polar lines and the five R.E.N.A.L. components.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - RENAL 4–12: low 4–6, moderate 7–9, high 10–12.
> - PADUA 6–14; C-index lower = more central.
> - Predicts PN feasibility, ischaemia time and complications.
> - MAP score: adherent perinephric fat.
