## IV-1. Describe in brief intravesical therapy for transitional cell carcinoma of Urinary bladder.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Introduction
- NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer: Ta, T1, CIS [carcinoma in situ]) is 70–80% of bladder cancers. "Urothelial carcinoma" now replaces "transitional cell carcinoma".
- Two problems: **recurrence** (40–60%) and **progression** (about 6% in low-grade Ta to 17% in high-grade T1). Intravesical therapy follows complete TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour).
- **Rule:** chemotherapy reduces recurrence only. BCG (bacille Calmette-Guérin) is the only agent that also reduces progression.

### Risk-adapted use (AUA [American Urological Association]/SUO)
| Risk | Criteria | Therapy |
|---|---|---|
| Low | LG (low-grade) solitary Ta ≤3 cm; PUNLMP | Single post-op instillation only |
| Intermediate | LG Ta recurrent, >3 cm or multifocal; LG T1; HG (high-grade) Ta ≤3 cm | 6-week chemotherapy or BCG; maintenance 1 year |
| High | HG T1; HG Ta large, multifocal or recurrent; any CIS; LVI (lymphovascular invasion); variant histology | BCG induction + maintenance 3 years |

- EAU (European Association of Urology) adds a "very high" group; EORTC (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer) and CUETO risk tables.

### 1. Single immediate instillation
- **Rationale:** kills floating cells before they implant. So it must be early: **within 6 hours** (no benefit at 24 hours).
- Agents: MMC (mitomycin C) 40 mg, epirubicin 50 mg, **gemcitabine 2 g** (standard in USA). **SWOG S0337:** 4-year recurrence 35% vs 47% with saline.
- **Withhold** after perforation or extensive resection. **Never give BCG** now (intravasation, sepsis).

### 2. Adjuvant chemotherapy
- **MMC** (alkylating), 40 mg in 20 mL weekly ×6. Optimise it (Au, 2001): empty bladder, fluid restriction, sodium bicarbonate, concentrated dose. Skin rash.
- **Gemcitabine** (nucleoside analogue): minimal absorption, well tolerated. **Anthracyclines** (epirubicin, doxorubicin) cause chemical cystitis.
- Intermediate risk: 6-week induction, then monthly maintenance for 1 year.
- **Gem/Doce:** gemcitabine 1 g, drain, then docetaxel 37.5 mg; weekly ×6, then monthly for 2 years. Used in BCG-unresponsive disease and during BCG shortage.

### 3. BCG immunotherapy
- **Mechanism:** BCG binds **fibronectin**. Cytokines follow (IFN-γ [interferon-gamma], IL [interleukin]-2, IL-12, TNF-α [tumour necrosis factor-alpha]). A **Th1 (T-helper 1)** response gives cell-mediated tumour kill; TRAIL release is important.
- **Technique:** 1 vial in 50 mL saline. Start **2–4 weeks** after TURBT (re-epithelialisation). Urinalysis first; retain 1–2 hours.
- **SWOG schedule (Lamm, 2000):** 6 weekly induction doses, then 3 weekly doses at 3 and 6 months, then every 6 months to 3 years. Median recurrence-free survival 76.8 vs 35.7 months.
- **EORTC 30962:** full dose; 1 year for intermediate, 3 years for high risk. **NIMBUS:** reduced frequency was inferior.
- CIS complete response up to 84%; BCG beats MMC only with maintenance.
- **Contraindications:** immediately after TURBT, gross haematuria, traumatic catheterisation (all risk intravasation); previous BCG sepsis; total incontinence; immunosuppression (now relative). Relative: active UTI (urinary tract infection), liver disease, previous TB (tuberculosis).

### BCG toxicity
| Grade | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cystitis, fever <38.5 °C, <48 h | Anticholinergics, analgesics, NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) |
| 2 | Severe or >48 h | Culture; isoniazid + rifampicin; resume at ½–⅓ dose |
| 3 | Sepsis, organ involvement | Stop BCG; isoniazid + rifampicin + ethambutol; steroids only with cover |

- BCG is resistant to **pyrazinamide**. BCG sepsis 0.5%.

### BCG failure
- **Adequate BCG:** ≥5 of 6 induction + ≥2 of 3 maintenance doses.
- **BCG-unresponsive:** persistent or recurrent CIS or HG Ta/T1 despite adequate BCG, or HG relapse within 6 months (Ta/T1) or 12 months (CIS) of the last dose.
- **Also know:** refractory, intolerant, exposed, delayed relapse.

### BCG-unresponsive options
- **Timely radical cystectomy** is the standard (EAU, AUA).
- If unfit or refusing:
  - **Pembrolizumab** (KEYNOTE-057): IV every 3 weeks; CIS complete response 41%.
  - **Nadofaragene firadenovec:** adenovirus with interferon-α2b gene, every 3 months; CIS complete response 53%.
  - **N-803:** IL-15 superagonist with BCG; CIS complete response 71%.
  - Gem/Doce; valrubicin; **TAR-200** (gemcitabine-releasing device); cretostimogene (trials).

### Device-assisted therapy
- **EMDA (electromotive drug administration):** current increases MMC tissue delivery 4–7-fold. Sequential BCG + EMDA-MMC improved outcomes in T1.
- **Chemohyperthermia:** RITE (radiofrequency, 42 °C); HIVEC (heated recirculating MMC).
- **Also know:** UGN-102 MMC gel; interferon with low-dose BCG.

**Diagram to draw:** BCG mechanism (fibronectin → cytokines → Th1 → kill) with SWOG schedule timeline.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Single instillation within 6 h; never BCG immediately (SWOG S0337 gemcitabine).
> - Only maintenance BCG reduces progression; SWOG 3-year schedule.
> - BCG-unresponsive: cystectomy; pembrolizumab, nadofaragene, N-803.

## IV-2. Describe applications of various LASERs in urology.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)

### Principle
- LASER (light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation): a "pumped" gain medium releases identical photons. A mirror resonator amplifies them, and a partial mirror lets the beam out.
- Light is **monochromatic, coherent and collimated**, so it passes down thin fibres.
- **Chromophore** decides the effect. Water absorbs holmium and thulium light. Haemoglobin absorbs KTP (potassium titanyl phosphate) light.
- Absorption sets **penetration**. Shallow penetration vaporises and cuts. Deep penetration coagulates and later sloughs.
- Coagulation occurs at about 60 °C; vaporisation at ≥100 °C.
- **Pulse duration** decides the stone mechanism. Short pulses (<10 µs) act photomechanically (shock waves). Long pulses (>250 µs: Ho:YAG [holmium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet], TFL [thulium fibre laser]) act **photothermally**.

### Lasers used
| Laser | Wavelength (nm) | Chromophore | Penetration | Main uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ho:YAG | 2100 | Water | 0.3–0.4 mm | Stones, HoLEP, incisions, tumours |
| TFL | 1940 | Water (peak) | <0.1 mm | Dusting, enucleation |
| Tm:YAG | ~2010 | Water | 0.2 mm | Vaporesection, enucleation |
| KTP/LBO | 532 | Haemoglobin | 0.8 mm | PVP |
| Diode | 940–1470 | Water + haemoglobin | 0.5–5 mm | Prostate vaporisation |
| Nd:YAG | 1064 | Neither strongly | Up to 10 mm | Coagulation of tumours |
| CO₂ | 10,600 | Water | ~0.1 mm | Skin and penile lesions |

### 1. Stones
- **Ho:YAG** is the gold standard. It breaks all stone types. Used in URS (ureteroscopy), RIRS (retrograde intrarenal surgery), PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) and for bladder stones.
- **Moses effect:** the start of each pulse makes a vapour channel; the rest crosses it to the stone. So keep the fibre close and perpendicular.
- Fragmenting: 0.6–1.0 J × 6–10 Hz. Dusting: 0.2–0.3 J × 40–80 Hz.
- **Retropulsion** rises with pulse energy and falls with longer pulses or pulse modulation.
- **TFL:** higher water absorption and low peak power give finer dust and less retropulsion. It beat low-power holmium in RCTs (randomised controlled trials) and equalled 120 W pulse-modulated holmium.
- **Heat** depends on power, not laser type. Keep ≤10 W in the ureter; use good flow and an access sheath.

### 2. BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia)
- **HoLEP (holmium laser enucleation of prostate):**
  - Endoscopic equivalent of open prostatectomy, **size-independent**. Enucleate at about 2 J × 40–50 Hz, then morcellate.
  - Only endoscopic procedure superior to TURP (transurethral resection of prostate). Less bleeding, shorter catheter time.
- **PVP (photoselective vaporisation, KTP/LBO 120–180 W):**
  - Haemoglobin absorption gives bloodless vaporisation, so anticoagulants can continue.
  - **GOLIATH RCT:** non-inferior to TURP. Avoid >80 cc (retreatment).
- **Thulium:** vaporisation, vaporesection, ThuLEP (equivalent to HoLEP). Diode vaporisation also used.
- Nd:YAG coagulation was abandoned (1 cm penetration, sloughing).
- AUA (American Urological Association): HoLEP, ThuLEP or PVP for **high bleeding risk**.

### 3. Incisions
- Laser urethrotomy has no advantage over cold knife.
- Holmium incision of bladder neck contracture, ureteric stricture, endopyelotomy, ureterocele and calyceal diverticulum neck.

### 4. Urothelial tumours
- **NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer):** laser **en bloc resection** (Ho, Tm, KTP) gives detrusor in the specimen and no obturator jerk. Office ablation of small low-grade recurrences.
- **UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma):** biopsy, then ablate by ureteroscopy:
  - Ho:YAG: <0.5 mm, haemostatic.
  - Nd:YAG: 5–6 mm coagulation, so stricture risk.
  - Thulium: <0.2 mm.
- **PDT (photodynamic therapy):** a photosensitiser plus laser light makes singlet oxygen.

### 5. Penile and genital lesions
- PeIN (penile intraepithelial neoplasia) and small Ta/T1: **Nd:YAG preferred**, with base biopsies.
- CO₂ is too superficial for T1 (recurrence up to 50%). CO₂ or Nd:YAG for genital warts.

### Safety
- Wavelength-specific eyewear for all, including the patient. Door signage, covered windows, trained staff and a laser safety officer.
- Check fibres; never fire inside the scope. Guard against fire and thermal injury.

### Recent advances
- Pulse modulation (Moses) and high-frequency holmium. TFL and thin fibres. Suction sheaths. AI (artificial intelligence) settings.

**Diagram to draw:** water and haemoglobin absorption curves marking 532, 1064, 1940 and 2100 nm.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Chromophore sets penetration: TFL shallowest, Ho 0.4 mm, Nd:YAG deepest.
> - Holmium lithotripsy is photothermal (Moses); TFL gives less retropulsion.
> - HoLEP size-independent, superior to TURP; PVP for anticoagulated men (GOLIATH).
> - Nd:YAG for PeIN; eyewear for everyone.

## IV-3a. Prostate cancer prevention trials
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Rationale
- Prostate cancer is common, slow and androgen-dependent. Men with 5α-reductase deficiency have rudimentary prostates. This supported **5ARI (5α-reductase inhibitor)** trials.

### Landmark trials
| | PCPT (2003) | REDUCE (2010) | SELECT (2009) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agent | Finasteride 5 mg | Dutasteride 0.5 mg | Selenium 200 µg ± vitamin E 400 IU |
| Men | 18,882; PSA (prostate-specific antigen) ≤3 | PSA 2.5–10, prior negative biopsy | 35,533 |
| Design | 7 years; end-of-study biopsy | Biopsy at 2 and 4 years | Stopped early for futility |
| All cancers | ↓24.8% | ↓22.8% | No benefit |
| High grade | Gleason 7–10 ↑ (6.4% vs 5.1%) | Gleason 8–10 ↑ (0.9% vs 0.6%) | Vitamin E alone ↑ risk |

### The high-grade controversy
- Proposed artefacts: better PSA performance and a smaller gland make cancer easier to find.
- The FDA (Food and Drug Administration) did **not accept** these. In 2011 it refused a prevention licence and added a **high-grade warning** to 5ARI labels.
- PCPT at 18.4 years: non-significant 25% fewer prostate cancer deaths. Real-world data show no mortality effect.
- **Conclusion:** 5ARIs reduce detection of low-grade cancer, not death.

### Vitamins and other agents
- **ATBC** trial: low-dose vitamin E cut prostate cancer 32% in smokers (secondary end point). SELECT then failed.
- **VITAL** (vitamin D): no prostate effect. High-dose zinc, calcium and antioxidants may harm.
- **Aspirin/NSAIDs** (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs): small effect. **Statins:** observational benefit only. **Metformin:** none.
- Diet: whole foods; avoid obesity.

### Current position
- No agent is approved for prevention. 5ARIs may be discussed with men taking them for BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia).

**Diagram to draw:** bar chart: all cancers ↓ vs high-grade ↑ in PCPT and REDUCE.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - PCPT ↓24.8%; REDUCE ↓22.8%; both ↑ high grade.
> - FDA warning; no approved preventive agent.
> - SELECT negative; vitamin E alone harmful.

## IV-3b. Prepucioplasty.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Read as: preputioplasty, a foreskin-preserving operation for phimosis.*

### Definition
- An operation that widens the tight preputial ring but keeps the foreskin. It is an alternative to circumcision.

### Background
- **Physiological phimosis** at birth is due to glans–preputial adhesions. Smegma and erections separate them. About 90% retract by 3 years; fewer than 1% are phimotic at 17 years.
- **Secondary phimosis** follows scarring (forced retraction, balanitis) or BXO (balanitis xerotica obliterans).

### Indications
- Persistent symptomatic phimosis: recurrent balanoposthitis, UTI (urinary tract infection), paraphimosis, painful erection.
- Failed **topical steroid** (betamethasone 0.05% twice daily for about 4 weeks). About 1 in 6 fail.
- Wish to keep the foreskin (cultural reasons, cosmesis).

### Contraindications
- **BXO** with scarring: circumcision is preferred. Triradiate incision with triamcinolone has been reported in selected boys.
- Physiological non-retractility needs only reassurance. Treat active infection first.

### Techniques
| Technique | Method |
|---|---|
| Dorsal slit, transverse closure | Longitudinal incision through the ring, closed transversely (Heineke–Mikulicz) |
| Triple incision | Three longitudinal cuts over the ring, each closed transversely |
| Y–V plasty | V-flap of outer skin advanced into a Y-cut |

- General anaesthesia or penile block; confirm full retraction; absorbable sutures.

### Postoperative care
- **Daily retraction from the first week**, continued for weeks, prevents re-stenosis. A topical steroid may help.

### Outcomes and complications
- Most achieve a retractile foreskin. It is quicker and less painful than circumcision.
- Complications: re-phimosis (needs circumcision), bleeding, infection, poor cosmesis, paraphimosis.
- **Also know:** preputial reconstruction in hypospadias repair is also called preputioplasty; dehiscence is its risk.

**Diagram to draw:** dorsal incision through the ring, closed transversely.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Steroid first; about 1 in 6 fail.
> - Cut lengthwise, close crosswise; daily retraction after.
> - BXO: circumcision preferred.

## IV-3c. Optical coherence tomography in carcinoma bladder.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- WLC (white-light cystoscopy) misses flat lesions and cannot show depth.
- **OCT (optical coherence tomography)** is an "optical biopsy": real-time cross-sectional imaging of the bladder wall. It works like ultrasound but uses light.

### Principle
- **Near-infrared light** (about 1300 nm) is shone on tissue. Back-scattered light is measured by **low-coherence interferometry** against a reference beam.
- The delay of reflected light gives depth. Scanning across builds a cross-sectional image.
- **Resolution about 10–20 µm** (near histology). **Depth about 2–3 mm**: urothelium, lamina propria and superficial muscle.
- A probe passes through the cystoscope channel and touches the mucosa.

### Appearance
| Normal | Tumour |
|---|---|
| Thin, dark urothelium | Thickened urothelium |
| Bright lamina propria; clear boundary | Layers lost |
| Muscle visible below | Broken boundary = invasion (T1) |

### Uses
- Tell tumour from inflammation at suspicious areas; fewer unnecessary biopsies.
- **Staging Ta vs T1** (lamina propria invasion); helps in diverticula and thin walls.
- Check margins and completeness of TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour).

### Accuracy and limitations
- High sensitivity; moderate specificity. Inflammation, scar, recent TURBT or BCG (bacille Calmette-Guérin) give false positives. Adding fluorescence cystoscopy improves specificity.
- Small field: needs WLC or PDD (photodynamic diagnosis) to guide it.
- Cannot judge deep muscle invasion; grading unreliable; cost.
- Still investigational; not in routine guideline use.

### Other enhanced cystoscopy
- **PDD (blue light, hexaminolevulinate):** tumour porphyrins fluoresce red; finds about one-third more CIS (carcinoma in situ).
- **NBI (narrow-band imaging):** 415/540 nm light shows vessels; many false positives.
- **Confocal laser endomicroscopy:** fluorescein; cell-level surface image.
- **Also know:** Raman spectroscopy.

**Diagram to draw:** OCT image of normal layers vs tumour with lost layers.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Near-infrared interferometry; 10–20 µm resolution; 2–3 mm depth.
> - Role: Ta vs T1; cannot assess deep muscle.
> - High sensitivity, moderate specificity; investigational.

## IV-3d. Re-look TUR bladder tumour.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- Second (restaging) TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour) of the primary site, including detrusor, a few weeks after the first. It is a quality indicator of NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer) care.

### Rationale
- **Incomplete resection:** residual tumour at the site in ≥40%; tumour at first check cystoscopy in up to 45%.
- **Understaging:** in HG (high-grade) T1, re-resection shows worse disease or muscle invasion in 25–30%. This rises to about half if the first specimen had **no muscle**.

### Indications (AUA [American Urological Association] / EAU [European Association of Urology])
- **Recommended:**
  - Incomplete first resection.
  - **All T1.**
  - No muscle in specimen (except low-grade Ta and primary CIS [carcinoma in situ]).
- **Consider:** large or multifocal HG Ta (residual up to 50%, upstaging up to 15%).

### Not needed
- Low-grade Ta, primary CIS, clear muscle invasion, or planned cystectomy.

### Timing and technique
- **2–6 weeks** after the first TURBT (AUA: within 6 weeks), before BCG (bacille Calmette-Guérin).
- Resect the scar and base deep into detrusor; send the base separately. Use blue light or NBI (narrow-band imaging) if available.

### Benefits
- Corrects stage and **changes treatment in about one-third**.
- **Divrik RCT (randomised controlled trial, 2010):** re-TUR in T1 improved recurrence-free and progression-free survival.
- Better BCG response with minimal residual tumour.

### Result-based plan
- **Residual T1** means progression up to 80%: consider early cystectomy.
- T0 or Ta: BCG induction + maintenance. T2: treat as muscle-invasive.

### Recent advances
- **En bloc resection** gives muscle more often and may reduce re-TUR. It is not yet a proven substitute.

**Diagram to draw:** flowchart: T1 or no muscle → re-TUR 2–6 weeks → T2: cystectomy pathway / residual T1: consider cystectomy / T0–Ta: BCG.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Re-TUR: all T1, incomplete resection, no muscle; consider HG Ta.
> - 2–6 weeks; residual ≥40%; upstaging 25–30% (half if no muscle).
> - Residual T1 → progression up to 80%.

## IV-3e. Male menopause.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Read as: late-onset hypogonadism (testosterone deficiency of ageing men, "andropause").*

### Definition
- Symptoms plus **consistently low serum testosterone** in an ageing man.
- "Menopause" is a misnomer: the fall is gradual and partial. Preferred terms: PADAM (partial androgen deficiency of the ageing male), LOH (late-onset hypogonadism).

### Pathophysiology
- Testosterone in the 70s is about one-third lower than in young men.
- **Testis:** fewer Leydig cells. **Centre:** blunted feedback, irregular GnRH (gonadotrophin-releasing hormone) pulses.
- **SHBG (sex hormone-binding globulin) rises**, so free testosterone falls more.
- Worse with obesity, diabetes and opioids.

### Clinical features
- **Sexual (most specific):** low libido, ED (erectile dysfunction), fewer morning erections.
- Fatigue, low muscle mass, osteoporosis, anaemia, low mood.
- **ADAM (Androgen Deficiency in the Aging Male)** questionnaire: sensitive, not specific.

### Diagnosis
- **Morning (7–11 am) total testosterone, twice.** AUA (American Urological Association): **<300 ng/dL**. EAU (European Association of Urology): about **12 nmol/L**; calculated free testosterone if borderline.
- **LH (luteinising hormone):** high in primary, low or normal in secondary hypogonadism. Check **prolactin** if LH is low.
- Baseline: PSA (prostate-specific antigen), DRE (digital rectal examination), haematocrit.

### Treatment
- Lifestyle first: weight loss, exercise.
- **TRT (testosterone replacement therapy)** for symptoms + confirmed low level: gels, injections (enanthate every 2–3 weeks; undecanoate every 10–14 weeks), oral undecanoate. Stop if no benefit at 3–6 months.
- **Contraindications:**
  - Prostate or breast cancer.
  - **Wish for fertility** (TRT stops sperm production; use hCG [human chorionic gonadotrophin] or clomiphene).
  - Haematocrit >50%.
  - Untreated severe sleep apnoea; recent MI (myocardial infarction) or stroke.

### Monitoring
- At 3–6 months, then yearly: testosterone, **haematocrit** (stop if >54%), PSA.

### Safety evidence
- **TRAVERSE (2023):** 5,246 men aged 45–80 at high cardiovascular risk. TRT was **non-inferior for major cardiac events**; more atrial fibrillation and pulmonary embolism.

**Diagram to draw:** hypothalamic–pituitary–testis axis with ageing defects.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Symptoms + low morning testosterone twice (AUA <300 ng/dL; EAU 12 nmol/L).
> - LH separates primary from secondary; prolactin if LH low.
> - TRT harms fertility; watch haematocrit; TRAVERSE: cardiac non-inferiority.

## IV-3f. Acute urinary retention in females.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Introduction
- AUR (acute urinary retention) is sudden inability to void. It is uncommon in women and is often non-obstructive, so search for the cause.

### Causes
| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Obstructive | Large cystocele or prolapse; tight sling after incontinence surgery; urethral diverticulum, stricture; pelvic mass, **retroverted gravid uterus**, haematocolpos; cancer |
| Functional | **Fowler syndrome**; dysfunctional voiding; primary bladder neck obstruction (rare) |
| Neurogenic | MS (multiple sclerosis; may present first with retention), cauda equina, transverse myelitis, diabetes |
| Inflammatory | Cystitis, vulvovaginitis, **genital herpes**, Bartholin abscess |
| Drugs | Anticholinergics, opioids, tricyclics, calcium-channel blockers |
| Postoperative/postpartum; psychogenic | Epidural, pain; diagnosis of exclusion |

### Fowler syndrome
- Young women (20–35) with painless retention and bladder capacity over 1 litre.
- **Urethral sphincter EMG (electromyography):** complex repetitive discharges, so the sphincter fails to relax. Urethral closure pressure >100 cm H₂O.

### Evaluation
- **History:** drugs, surgery, delivery, neurological and bowel symptoms, genital lesions.
- **Examination:** pelvic (prolapse, mass); neurological (perineal sensation, anal tone).
- **Tests:** drained volume; urine culture; creatinine; pregnancy test; ultrasound.
- MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) spine if neurological signs; cystoscopy.
- **Later:** video-urodynamics (female obstruction, e.g. Qmax ≤12 mL/s with Pdet ≥20 cm H₂O); sphincter EMG.

### Management
- **Immediate:** urethral catheter; suprapubic if urethral access fails.
- **Treat the cause:** stop drugs; treat infection or herpes; pessary or prolapse repair; reposition the uterus; sling division; diverticulectomy.
- **TWOC (trial without catheter)** after correction.
- **Persistent retention:**
  - **CISC (clean intermittent self-catheterisation)** rather than an indwelling catheter.
  - **SNM (sacral neuromodulation):** about 70% success in Fowler syndrome.
  - Bethanechol not recommended. Bladder neck incision only with caution (incontinence).

**Diagram to draw:** flowchart: AUR → catheterise → find cause → treat → TWOC → CISC or SNM.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Look for prolapse, sling obstruction, pelvic mass, neurology and drugs.
> - Fowler: abnormal sphincter EMG; SNM about 70%.
> - CISC for persistent retention.
