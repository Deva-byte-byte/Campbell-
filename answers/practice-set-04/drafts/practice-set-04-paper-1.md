## Q1. Discuss pathophysiology of renal stones and also describe how to prevent its recurrence
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q2)

### Introduction
- Stone disease is a **chronic metabolic disease** with recurrence up to **50% within 5 years**. Removal is surgical; recurrence is metabolic.
- Calcium oxalate (with or without apatite) forms about 80%; uric acid and struvite about 7% each; cystine 1–3%.

### Risk factors
- **Epidemiological:** male sex, hot climate (low urine volume), animal protein and salt, family history.
- **Systemic:** metabolic syndrome and diabetes (insulin resistance lowers urine pH), gout, hyperparathyroidism, RTA (renal tubular acidosis), bowel disease or bypass, sarcoidosis.
- **Stasis:** PUJ (pelviureteric junction) obstruction, horseshoe kidney, medullary sponge kidney. **Drugs:** indinavir, topiramate, vitamin C and D excess.

### Physicochemistry: state of saturation
| State | Concentration product (CP) | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Undersaturated | Below Ksp (solubility product) | No crystals; stones may dissolve |
| **Metastable** | Between Ksp and Kf (formation product) | No spontaneous nucleation, but growth on existing crystals; inhibitors act here. **Zone of therapy** |
| Unstable | Above Kf | Spontaneous nucleation, growth, aggregation |


### Steps of stone formation
1. **Supersaturation:** low volume; high calcium, oxalate or uric acid; low citrate.
2. **Nucleation:** homogeneous, or **heterogeneous** on debris or other crystals (needs less energy).
3. **Growth and aggregation:** aggregates, not single crystals, can block a tubule within the 5–7 minute transit (**free-particle theory**).
4. **Retention (fixed-particle theory):** **Randall plaque** (calcium apatite) starts in the basement membrane of the thin loop of Henle, spreads through the interstitium, erodes the papillary urothelium and anchors calcium oxalate growth (idiopathic formers). **Ductal plugs** (crystals in collecting ducts) point to a secondary cause: enteric hyperoxaluria, distal RTA, hyperparathyroidism, cystinuria.

### Inhibitors and matrix
- **Inhibitors:** **citrate** (complexes calcium), **magnesium** (complexes oxalate), pyrophosphate, glycosaminoglycans, nephrocalcin, **Tamm–Horsfall protein**, osteopontin. **No inhibitor of uric acid** exists. Matrix is about 2.5% of stone weight.

### Pathophysiology by urinary abnormality
| Abnormality (definition) | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Hypercalciuria** (>200 mg/day, or >4 mg/kg/day) | Absorptive (gut), renal leak (secondary high PTH [parathyroid hormone]), resorptive (primary hyperparathyroidism): Pak classification |
| Hyperoxaluria (>40 mg/day) | **Enteric:** malabsorbed fat binds calcium, freeing oxalate; **primary:** hepatic enzyme defect (PH1, AGXT gene) |
| Hyperuricosuria (>600 mg/day) | Urate promotes calcium oxalate nucleation |
| **Hypocitraturia** (<320 mg/day) | Distal RTA, diarrhoea, hypokalaemia, animal protein (acid load) |
| **Uric acid stones** | **Low urine pH (<5.5)** more than excess uric acid; pKa 5.5 |
| **Cystine** | Autosomal recessive defect of tubular cystine reabsorption |
| **Struvite** | **Urease** (Proteus) splits urea → ammonia, pH >7.2 → magnesium ammonium phosphate and carbonate apatite |

### Prevention of recurrence
**1. Screen every first-time former (AUA [American Urological Association], EAU [European Association of Urology])**
- History, serum electrolytes, creatinine, calcium, uric acid; urine pH and culture; **stone analysis**; imaging. This sorts patients into high and low risk.

**2. General measures (all formers)**
- **Fluid for urine ≥2.5 L/day** (drink about 3 L). Borghi 1996: 5-year recurrence 12.1% vs 27%.
- **Normal calcium**, low sodium, less animal protein, more fruit and vegetables (Borghi 2002: halved recurrence versus a low-calcium diet, which raises oxalate absorption).
- Limit high-oxalate foods (spinach, nuts, chocolate); weight loss.

**3. Metabolic evaluation (high-risk or recurrent formers, children)**
- One or two **24-hour urines**: volume, pH, calcium, oxalate, uric acid, citrate, sodium, potassium, creatinine. Therapy targeted to these results reduces stone events.

**4. Selective drug therapy**
| Abnormality | Treatment | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Hypercalciuria | **Thiazide:** hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg twice daily, chlorthalidone 25–50 mg, indapamide 2.5 mg | Distal calcium reabsorption; add potassium citrate (hypokalaemia causes hypocitraturia) |
| Hypocitraturia, distal RTA | **Potassium citrate 30–60 mEq/day** | Alkali load reduces tubular citrate reabsorption |
| Hyperuricosuria with calcium stones | Allopurinol 300 mg/day | Watch hypersensitivity |
| Uric acid stones | Potassium citrate to **pH 6.0–6.5** (avoid >7.0) | pH disease; allopurinol only for hyperuricosuria |
| Enteric hyperoxaluria | **Calcium with meals**, low-oxalate, low-fat diet, magnesium, liquid citrate | Binds oxalate in gut |
| Primary hyperoxaluria type 1 | Pyridoxine; **lumasiran** (RNA [ribonucleic acid] interference) | ILLUMINATE-A |
| Cystinuria | Urine ≥3 L/day, pH **7.0–7.5**; **tiopronin** | Cystine <250 mg/L; thiol forms soluble mixed disulfide |
| Struvite | **Complete clearance**, antibiotics; AHA (acetohydroxamic acid) 250 mg three times daily if unfit for surgery | Urease inhibitor; thrombosis |


**5. Monitoring**
- 24-hour urine within **6 months** of starting therapy, then yearly; reanalyse any new stone.

### Recent advances
- **ROKS (Recurrence of Kidney Stone) nomogram** predicts individual risk. **Also know:** NOSTONE (2023): hydrochlorothiazide did not reduce recurrence; citrus juices are adjuncts; *Oxalobacter formigenes* loss.

**Diagram to draw:** saturation zones (Ksp, Kf) and Randall plaque on a renal papilla.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Metastable zone between Ksp and Kf: where inhibitors and therapy act.
> - Randall plaque anchors idiopathic calcium oxalate stones; ductal plugs suggest a secondary cause.
> - Urine ≥2.5 L/day; normal calcium, low salt and animal protein (Borghi).
> - Uric acid pH 6.0–6.5; cystine pH 7.0–7.5 and <250 mg/L.

## Q2. Describe pathophysiology of OAB. Briefly outline management &describe nonmedical non-pharmacological management
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(neuromodulation also answered in 2025-Winter Paper 3, Q3e and 2023-Winter Paper 2, Q3b)

### Definition
- **OAB (overactive bladder), ICS (International Continence Society):** urgency, usually with frequency and nocturia, with or without UUI (urgency urinary incontinence), without UTI (urinary tract infection) or other obvious pathology.
- OAB is a **symptom** diagnosis. DO (detrusor overactivity) is a **urodynamic** finding; only 44–69% of patients with urgency show DO.

### Pathophysiology
All mechanisms converge on **more afferent signal reaching awareness**: the bladder turns the signal up, or the CNS (central nervous system) fails to turn it down.

| Mechanism | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Afferent (sensory)** | Aδ fibres normally sense filling. Unmyelinated **C fibres** are "silent", but become spontaneously active and hypersensitive in pathological states |
| **Urothelial** | Urothelium acts as a transducer: mechanical, inflammatory or chemical stimuli release ATP (adenosine triphosphate), acetylcholine and other mediators onto suburothelial afferents |
| **Myogenic** | Patchy denervation causes receptor upregulation and spontaneous contraction; exaggerated **micromotions** cause urgency, and coordinated spread across the wall gives DO |
| **Neurogenic / central** | Forebrain and PAG–PMC (periaqueductal grey–pontine micturition centre) normally inhibit voiding; loss of this inhibition lets "afferent noise" be felt as urgency |
| **Other** | Bladder–bowel crosstalk (constipation), ischaemia–reperfusion, chronic inflammation, altered urinary microbiome |

- **Risk factors:** ageing, female sex, BOO (bladder outlet obstruction: an animal model, but only an indirect cause in humans; DO persists in up to 60% after TURP [transurethral resection of the prostate]), anxiety and depression, metabolic syndrome, poor toileting habits.

### Brief outline of management
- **Assessment:** history, examination, **urinalysis** (minimum); bladder diary (3–7 days); questionnaires; PVR (post-void residual) when indicated. Urodynamics only if it will change an invasive decision.
- **Ladder (AUA/SUFU [American Urological Association/Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction]):** not a compulsory sequence.
```
Education + behavioural therapy (first line)
        ↓  (4–12 weeks)
Antimuscarinic or β3-agonist (mirabegron, vibegron)
        ↓
PTNS / onabotulinumtoxinA / SNM (third line)
        ↓
Augmentation cystoplasty or urinary diversion
```
- Antimuscarinics: dry mouth, constipation; **dementia risk with use >3 months**, so β3-agonists are preferred in the elderly.

### Non-pharmacological management
**1. Education and lifestyle**
- Explain normal bladder function; set realistic goals.
- Reduce fluid intake by about 25% if excessive; cut caffeine; treat constipation.
- **Weight loss:** about 8 kg loss in obese women reduced UUI episodes.

**2. Bladder training (BT)** (breaks the cycle of urgency, frequent voiding, smaller capacity)
1. Keep a bladder diary.
2. Start a fixed interval (often 1 hour while awake).
3. Void by the clock, not by urge.
4. Increase by 15–30 minutes a week as control improves.
5. Target interval 3–4 hours.
- Works by **uncoupling the void from the urge**.

**3. PFMT (pelvic floor muscle training) and urge suppression**
- Kegel exercises: quick 1–2 s flicks and 5–10 s holds, twice daily.
- A pelvic floor contraction **reflexly inhibits the detrusor**. Urge suppression: stop, stay still, squeeze quickly several times, relax, let the urge pass, then walk calmly to the toilet.
- PFMT and BT reduce incontinence episodes by **50–80%**.
- **Also know:** biofeedback helps patients who cannot find the muscle; electrical stimulation at 5–20 Hz inhibits the detrusor; timed and prompted voiding for cognitively impaired patients.

**4. PTNS (percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation)**
- 34-gauge needle 3–4 cm above the medial malleolus; 20 Hz, 200 μs; confirm great-toe flexion or sole tingling. **30 min weekly for 12 weeks**, then maintenance.
- Posterior tibial nerve (L4–S3) shares sacral roots with the bladder. **SUmiT trial:** 55% vs 21% (sham) responders.

**5. SNM (sacral neuromodulation)**
- Mechanism: stimulates **afferents** that modulate brainstem and forebrain control.
- **Test phase:** PNE (percutaneous nerve evaluation, 5–7 days) or **staged tined lead** at **S3** (anal "bellows" and great-toe flexion). ≥50% improvement on diary → implant pulse generator in the buttock.
- **InSite trial:** 61% vs 42% (standard medical therapy). Revision >30% at 5 years; MRI (magnetic resonance imaging)-conditional rechargeable devices now available.

**6. Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA**
- Cleaves SNAP-25 (synaptosomal-associated protein 25), blocking acetylcholine release; also reduces afferent signalling.
- **100 U in 10 mL at 20 sites** cystoscopically; effect lasts about 6–9 months.
- About 25% become completely dry. Risks: UTI about 20%, retention needing CISC (clean intermittent self-catheterisation). **ROSETTA:** greater UUI reduction than SNM at 6 months, but more UTI.

**7. Last resort:** augmentation cystoplasty or urinary diversion.

**Diagram to draw:** bladder showing urothelial afferents, C fibres and micromotions, with the brainstem and forebrain inhibitory pathways.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - OAB is defined by urgency; DO is a urodynamic finding.
> - Final pathway: increased afferent signal (urothelial, myogenic) or failed central inhibition.
> - BT and PFMT with urge suppression first: 50–80% fewer leaks.
> - PTNS (SUmiT 55% vs 21%), SNM (InSite 61% vs 42%), Botox 100 U.

## Q3a. Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- XGP (xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis) is a rare, severe, **chronic** infection causing diffuse destruction of an enlarged, non-functioning kidney, with **lipid-laden foamy macrophages** (xanthoma cells).
- Up to a fifth of nephrectomies for pyelonephritis. Peak age 50–70 years; women; diabetes is a risk factor.

### Pathogenesis
- **Triad: stone, obstruction, infection.** Stones in up to 80% (half staghorn).
- Low-virulence organisms (**Proteus** commonest, *E. coli*) in an obstructed, ischaemic kidney with an inadequate acute inflammatory response. Lipid probably comes from lysed red cells.

### Pathology
- Diffuse (>80%) or segmental (focal).
- Gross: yellow nodules, pus-filled dilated calyces, a **contracted pelvis** encased by fibrosis, frequent **perinephric extension**.
- Microscopy: sheets of foamy histiocytes with lymphocytes, plasma cells and giant cells.
- **Malek staging:** I confined to kidney; II into perinephric fat; III into paranephric tissue or retroperitoneum.

### Clinical features
- Flank pain, fever (about two-thirds), flank mass (about 60%), weight loss; persistent bacteriuria.
- Labs: anaemia, raised inflammatory markers, pyuria; liver dysfunction in up to half. One-third have **sterile urine** (prior antibiotics).

### Imaging
- **CT (computed tomography), most useful:** enlarged kidney, little or no function, central calculus in a contracted pelvis; low-density cavities with rim enhancement (**"bear paw" sign**); extrarenal spread.
- Ultrasound: enlarged kidney with hypoechoic areas. DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid) scan quantifies lost function.
- **Differentials:** RCC (renal cell carcinoma), pyonephrosis, malakoplakia, lymphoma, tuberculosis.

### Management
- Antibiotics to stabilise; drain if septic.
- **Nephrectomy** (with perinephric fat) for diffuse disease; partial nephrectomy for focal disease.
- Surgery is difficult: dense adhesions to psoas, great vessels, bowel and diaphragm. Remove the whole inflammatory mass, because simple drainage leads to **nephrocutaneous fistula**.
- Laparoscopic nephrectomy is feasible; conversion rates are high.
- Frozen section can mimic clear cell carcinoma, and XGP can **coexist with RCC** or urothelial carcinoma. If cancer is not excluded, do nephrectomy.

**Diagram to draw:** CT sketch of an enlarged kidney with central staghorn and "bear paw" cavities.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Stone, obstruction, Proteus infection → foamy macrophages.
> - CT: non-functioning enlarged kidney, contracted pelvis, "bear paw" sign.
> - Mimics RCC; treat by complete nephrectomy, not drainage.

## Q3b. Doppler in urology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Principle
- **Doppler shift:** sound reflected from moving red cells changes frequency in proportion to velocity and the cosine of the insonation angle: Δf = 2f₀v cosθ / c. Keep the angle **below 60°**.
- **RI (resistive index) = (PSV − EDV) / PSV** (peak systolic and end-diastolic velocity); reflects downstream resistance.

### Modes
| Mode | Shows | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Colour** | Velocity and direction (red towards, blue away by convention) | Flow in kidney, testis, ureteric jets |
| **Spectral (duplex)** | Waveform at one point; velocities, RI | Penile and renal arteries |
| **Power** | Amplitude only; 3–5 times more sensitive, less angle-dependent | Low flow: torsion, prostate lesions |

### Applications
| Organ | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Scrotum** | **Torsion:** absent intratesticular flow (compare with the other side); epididymitis: increased flow; **varicocele:** venous reflux on Valsalva; intratesticular RI >0.6 suggests poor spermatogenesis |
| **Penis (after intracavernosal injection)** | **PSV <25 cm/s = arterial ED (erectile dysfunction)**, 25–35 equivocal, >35 normal; **EDV >5 cm/s and RI <0.75 = veno-occlusive dysfunction**; Peyronie plaque assessment |
| **Priapism** | Ischaemic: absent cavernosal flow; non-ischaemic: high flow (PSV >50 cm/s), locates the fistula |
| **Kidney** | Normal RI 0.47–0.70; **raised RI in acute obstruction** (useful in pregnancy, where change in RI is more accurate); renal artery stenosis: high velocity and parvus-tardus waveform; arteriovenous fistula after biopsy or PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy) |
| **Transplant** | Arterial thrombosis: absent signal; **venous thrombosis: reversed diastolic flow**; stenosis: high velocity; rejection: loss of diastolic flow, high RI |
| **Bladder** | Ureteric jets (absent on the obstructed side) |
| **Tumours** | Vascularity of renal and testicular masses; tumour thrombus; hypervascular prostate cancer foci |

- **Also know:** intraoperative micro-Doppler in microsurgical varicocelectomy; contrast-enhanced ultrasound and elastography in multiparametric ultrasound.

### Limitations
- Operator- and angle-dependent; poor in obesity and deep vessels; early torsion may show collateral flow, so **Doppler cannot exclude torsion**.

**Diagram to draw:** spectral waveform marking PSV and EDV with the RI formula.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - RI = (PSV − EDV)/PSV; normal renal 0.47–0.70.
> - Penile PSV <25 cm/s arterial; EDV >5 cm/s veno-occlusive.
> - Absent intratesticular flow = torsion, but explore on clinical grounds.

## Q3c. Christian Chaussey
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Taken as Christian Chaussy, the Munich urologist who pioneered extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy; answered as the history and principles of SWL.*

### Contribution and history
- Engineers at **Dornier** (aircraft industry) found by chance that shock waves from raindrop and micrometeorite research affected tissue.
- Chaussy, with Brendel and Schmiedt in **Munich**, developed the technique in animals and performed the **first human SWL (shock wave lithotripsy) on 7 February 1980 on the Dornier HM1**. They reported it in the *Lancet* in 1980.
- **HM3** (water bath, electrohydraulic spark, biplanar fluoroscopy): first installations Stuttgart 1983 and Indianapolis 1984. It remains the reference for efficacy.
- SWL made most open stone surgery obsolete.

### Principles
- A shock wave is a short acoustic pulse: **peak positive 30–100 MPa, then a negative tail of about −10 MPa**.
- Generated at **F1**, focused onto the stone at **F2** through water or gel coupling (water and tissue have similar acoustic impedance); targeting by fluoroscopy or ultrasound.

| Energy source | Feature |
|---|---|
| Electrohydraulic (HM1, HM3) | Underwater spark, ellipsoid reflector; most effective; electrode wear |
| Piezoelectric | Ceramic elements; long life, little pain; small focal zone, low energy |
| **Electromagnetic** (modern) | Coil and membrane with lens; consistent; dominant today |

### Mechanisms of fragmentation
- **Spallation:** reflected wave at the back surface creates tension.
- **Shear and tear** inside the stone.
- **Quasistatic squeezing** and **dynamic squeezing** (strongest force in initial breakage).
- **Cavitation:** collapsing bubbles erode the surface; excess bubbles shield the stone and injure tissue.

### Practical points
- Better results at **60–90 shocks/min**, with power **ramping** and air-free coupling.
- Poor results: stone >1000 HU (Hounsfield units), skin-to-stone distance >10 cm, lower pole >1 cm.
- **Contraindications:** pregnancy, uncorrected coagulopathy, untreated UTI (urinary tract infection), distal obstruction, aortic aneurysm >4 cm.
- **Also know:** burst wave lithotripsy and ultrasonic propulsion are newer descendants.

**Diagram to draw:** ellipsoid reflector with spark at F1 and stone at F2.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Chaussy, Munich: first human SWL 7 February 1980, Dornier HM1.
> - HM3 (1983) is still the efficacy benchmark.
> - Spallation, squeezing, shear, cavitation; electromagnetic sources today.

## Q3d. Creatinine clearance
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(GFR assessment also answered in 2023-Winter Paper 2, Q3a)

### Definition
- Creatinine clearance (CrCl) is the volume of plasma cleared of creatinine per minute. It estimates **GFR (glomerular filtration rate)** because creatinine is freely filtered and not reabsorbed.
- Normal about 120 mL/min/1.73 m² in young men, slightly lower in women; falls with age.

### Measured 24-hour creatinine clearance
- **CrCl = (U × V) / P**: U = urine creatinine, V = urine flow (mL/min), P = plasma creatinine (same units).
- **Method:** discard the first morning void, collect all urine for 24 hours including the next morning's first void; take serum creatinine during the collection.
- **Completeness check:** creatinine excretion should be **20–25 mg/kg/day in men, 15–20 mg/kg/day in women**.

### Limitations
- **Tubular secretion** of creatinine: small in health but rising as function falls, so CrCl **overestimates GFR** in CKD (chronic kidney disease).
- Collection errors are common. Creatinine generation depends on **muscle mass**, diet (cooked meat, creatine supplements) and age.
- Drugs blocking secretion (**trimethoprim, cimetidine**) raise serum creatinine without real injury.
- Invalid in AKI (acute kidney injury). **Also know:** cimetidine pretreatment blocks secretion to improve accuracy; averaging urea and creatinine clearance helps in advanced CKD.

### Estimated clearance: Cockcroft–Gault
- **CrCl = (140 − age) × weight (kg) / (72 × serum creatinine mg/dL)**, × 0.85 in women.
- Used for **drug dosing**; not normalised to body surface area; inaccurate in obesity.

### Comparison with other GFR measures
| Method | Comment |
|---|---|
| eGFR (CKD-EPI [Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration] 2021) | Age and sex, no race; used for staging |
| Cystatin C | Independent of muscle; combined equation most accurate |
| Measured GFR (inulin, iohexol, ⁵¹Cr-EDTA, ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DTPA) | Reference standard; DTPA also gives split function |

### Clinical uses
- Drug dosing (antibiotics, chemotherapy); **cisplatin eligibility** (CrCl <60 mL/min usually unfit).
- Extremes of body size, amputees and pregnancy, where eGFR is unreliable.
- Living donor evaluation (confirm with measured GFR).

**Diagram to draw:** nephron showing filtration plus a small secreted fraction of creatinine.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - CrCl = UV/P; check completeness by creatinine excreted per kg.
> - Secretion makes CrCl overestimate GFR as function falls.
> - Cockcroft–Gault: (140 − age) × weight / (72 × SCr), × 0.85 women.

## Q3e. Radionuclear studies in PUJ obstruction
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 1, Q3e)

### Introduction
- Dilatation is not obstruction. **Diuretic renography** is the functional gold standard for PUJ (pelviureteric junction) obstruction and the best follow-up study.

### Tracers
| Tracer | Handling | Use |
|---|---|---|
| ⁹⁹ᵐTc-**MAG3** (mercaptoacetyltriglycine) | Tubular secretion; extraction about 60% | **Agent of choice**: split function and drainage, good images even with poor function |
| ⁹⁹ᵐTc-**DTPA** (diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid) | Glomerular filtration | GFR (glomerular filtration rate); poor in renal impairment |
| ⁹⁹ᵐTc-**DMSA** (dimercaptosuccinic acid) | Cortical retention | Static split function and scars; no drainage data |

### "Well-tempered" diuretic renogram
| Element | How | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydration** | Oral, or IV (intravenous) in children | Dehydration mimics obstruction |
| **Bladder catheter** | Urethral catheter | Removes full-bladder and reflux effects |
| **Furosemide** | 0.5 mg/kg (adult maximum 40 mg); 1 mg/kg in children | Maximal diuresis |
| **Timing** | **F+20** (pelvis full; Campbell authors use F+15), **F0** or **F−15** | Diuretic before filling misleads; always state timing |

### Outputs
- **DRF (differential renal function)** from counts at 1–3 min; normal 45–55%.
- **Drainage half-time (T½)** after furosemide:

| T½ | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| <10 min | Not obstructed |
| 10–20 min | Equivocal |
| >20 min | Obstructed |

- **O'Reilly curves:** I normal; II obstructed (keeps rising); IIIa dilated, non-obstructed (falls after furosemide); IIIb equivocal.
- **Also know:** P40 (activity left at 40 min) raises sensitivity; biphasic curve and poor upright drainage predict progression.

### Indications for pyeloplasty
- **DRF <40%**, **fall in DRF** on serial comparable scans (≥5% in children; 10% in older series), poor drainage with symptoms, infection or increasing dilatation.
- Adult DRF <15%: likely non-salvageable; nephrostomy and reassess, or nephrectomy.

### Pitfalls
- Dehydration, early diuretic, full bladder, very poor function.
- No agreed paediatric T½ cut-off (false positives up to 44%); one Campbell chapter uses **T½ >40 min** in adults. Serial trends decide.

### Follow-up and alternatives
- After pyeloplasty: renogram 1 month after stent removal. Equivocal cases: **Whitaker test** or MRU (magnetic resonance urography).

**Diagram to draw:** O'Reilly curves I, II, IIIa and IIIb.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - MAG3 tracer of choice; hydrate and catheterise.
> - DRF normal 45–55%; T½ <10 normal, >20 min obstructed.
> - Operate for DRF <40%, falling DRF, or symptoms.

## Q3f. Cyclosporins
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(immunosuppressants also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 1, Q3f; tacrolimus in 2017-Winter Paper 3, Q3c)

*Taken as ciclosporin (cyclosporin A).*

### Introduction
- Ciclosporin is a cyclic peptide of 11 amino acids from the fungus *Tolypocladium inflatum*. It was the first **CNI (calcineurin inhibitor)**.
- Its introduction in the early 1980s cut acute rejection from at least 60% (pre-ciclosporin era) towards today's <10%. Tacrolimus has now largely replaced it.

### Mechanism
```
Ciclosporin + cyclophilin (cytoplasm)
   → complex inhibits calcineurin
   → NFAT (nuclear factor of activated T cells) stays phosphorylated
   → no IL-2 (interleukin-2) transcription → T-cell activation blocked (signal 1)
```

### Pharmacokinetics and monitoring
- Variable oral absorption; the **microemulsion form** absorbs more predictably.
- Metabolised by **CYP3A4** (cytochrome P450 3A4) and P-glycoprotein; narrow therapeutic index, so whole-blood **trough (C0) or 2-hour (C2) levels** guide dosing.

### Uses
- **Transplantation:** maintenance with an antimetabolite and steroids, when tacrolimus is not tolerated.
- **IC/BPS (interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome):** refractory **Hunner lesions**, 1–3 mg/kg/day; better than pentosan polysulfate.
- **Others:** retroperitoneal fibrosis after steroid failure; frequently relapsing nephrotic syndrome; severe psoriasis and other dermatoses.

### Toxicity
| System | Effect |
|---|---|
| Kidney | **Nephrotoxicity**: acute afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction (reversible); chronic striped fibrosis, arteriolar hyalinosis; TMA (thrombotic microangiopathy) |
| Metabolic | Hypertension, hyperkalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, **hyperuricaemia** (gout), dyslipidaemia |
| Cosmetic | **Gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism** |
| Neurological | Tremor, PRES (posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome) |
| Immune | Infection, skin cancer, PTLD (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder) |

### Interactions
- **Levels rise:** azoles, erythromycin and clarithromycin, diltiazem, verapamil, grapefruit.
- **Levels fall:** rifampicin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, St John's wort.
- More nephrotoxicity with NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) and aminoglycosides.

### Versus tacrolimus
- Ciclosporin: more hirsutism, gum hypertrophy, hypertension and hyperlipidaemia; less diabetes and tremor.
- **ELITE-Symphony (2007):** low-dose tacrolimus gave less rejection and better GFR (glomerular filtration rate) than ciclosporin regimens.
- **Urological note:** CNIs promote transplant stones (hyperuricaemia, hyperoxaluria, hypocitraturia).

**Diagram to draw:** T cell with the cyclophilin–calcineurin–NFAT–IL-2 pathway.

> **Key points to remember:**
