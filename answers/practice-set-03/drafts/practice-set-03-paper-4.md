## Q1. Immunobiology of renal transplant, describe. Also, describe newer immunosuppressant drugs
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q1)

### Introduction
- The recipient's immune system recognises donor antigens as foreign. Immunosuppression has cut acute rejection from at least 60% (pre-cyclosporine era) to **below 10%**. Slow, subclinical **antibody-mediated injury** is now the main cause of late graft loss.

### Transplant antigens
| Antigen | Features |
|---|---|
| **HLA (human leucocyte antigen) = MHC (major histocompatibility complex)** | Class I (A, B, C) on all nucleated cells; class II (DR, DQ, DP) on antigen-presenting and endothelial cells. Class II mismatch is most immunogenic |
| **ABO** blood group | On endothelium; incompatibility causes hyperacute rejection unless desensitised |
| Minor and non-HLA antigens | Rejection despite HLA identity |

- **Sensitisation**: transfusion, pregnancy or previous transplant create preformed anti-HLA antibodies.
- **Testing**: CDC (complement-dependent cytotoxicity) and flow crossmatch (donor lymphocytes with recipient serum); **Luminex single-antigen beads** define each recipient's antibodies and allow a **virtual crossmatch**; cPRA (calculated panel-reactive antibody) expresses sensitisation.

### Innate immunity: the first trigger
- **Ischaemia–reperfusion injury** releases danger signals (DAMPs, damage-associated molecular patterns). These activate **Toll-like receptors**, complement and macrophages, and up-regulate MHC on the graft. Hence DGF (delayed graft function) raises rejection risk.
- **Dendritic cells** bridge innate and adaptive immunity: activated, they up-regulate MHC and costimulatory B7 and migrate to lymph nodes.

### Allorecognition
| Pathway | Mechanism | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | Recipient T cells recognise **intact donor MHC** on donor "passenger" dendritic cells | Very strong (high T-cell frequency); early acute T-cell rejection |
| **Indirect** | Recipient APCs (antigen-presenting cells) present **processed donor peptides on self-MHC** | Lifelong; helps B cells make DSA (donor-specific antibody); chronic rejection |
| **Semi-direct** | Recipient APCs acquire intact donor MHC by membrane transfer | Links both pathways |

### Three-signal model of T-cell activation
1. **Signal 1**: TCR (T-cell receptor) binds MHC–peptide → **calcineurin** dephosphorylates NFAT (nuclear factor of activated T cells) → IL-2 (interleukin-2) transcription.
2. **Signal 2 (costimulation)**: **CD28 binds CD80/86 (B7)**. Signal 1 without signal 2 causes **anergy**. CTLA-4 (cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen-4) is the natural brake.
3. **Signal 3**: IL-2 binds **CD25** (IL-2 receptor) → **mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin)** → cell cycle G1 to S → clonal proliferation, which needs de novo purine synthesis.

### Effector mechanisms
- **Cellular**: CD4 Th1 cells (IL-2, IFN-γ (interferon-γ)) recruit macrophages; CD8 cytotoxic T cells kill by perforin and granzyme → **tubulitis, endarteritis**.
- **Humoral**: B cells with T-cell help become plasma cells → **DSA** binds endothelium → complement (leaves **C4d** in peritubular capillaries) → **microvascular inflammation**. NK (natural killer) cells cause complement-independent injury.
- **Regulation**: **Tregs** (regulatory T cells, FoxP3+) suppress responses; the basis of tolerance research.

### Rejection (brief; detail in 2020-Summer Paper 1, Q3d)
- **Hyperacute** (preformed antibody; prevented by crossmatch); **acute TCMR** (T-cell-mediated rejection); **acute AMR** (antibody-mediated rejection); **subclinical**; **chronic active AMR** (transplant glomerulopathy). **Banff** classification grades biopsy findings.
- **Monitoring**: creatinine, **de novo DSA**, biopsy, **dd-cfDNA (donor-derived cell-free DNA)** (negative predictive value >90%), gene-expression panels.
- **Treatment**: TCMR: IV (intravenous) steroids, ATG (antithymocyte globulin) for Banff II–III. AMR: steroids, IVIG (intravenous immunoglobulin), plasmapheresis, rituximab.

### Standard immunosuppression
- **Induction** (>90%) plus **tacrolimus + mycophenolate** ± steroids.

### Newer immunosuppressant drugs
| Drug | Mechanism (site) | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Belatacept** | **CTLA4-Ig**: binds CD80/86, blocks **signal 2** | IV monthly; CNI (calcineurin inhibitor)-free. **BENEFIT**: better GFR (glomerular filtration rate), more early acute rejection; 7-year better patient and graft survival. **Contraindicated if EBV (Epstein–Barr virus)-seronegative** (PTLD (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder)) |
| **Extended-release tacrolimus** | Binds FKBP12 → inhibits calcineurin (**signal 1**) | Once daily, better adherence, steadier levels |
| **mTOR inhibitors** (sirolimus, everolimus) | Bind FKBP12 → block mTOR (**signal 3**) | Not nephrotoxic; fewer CMV (cytomegalovirus) infections; antitumour effect. **TRANSFORM (2018)**: everolimus + reduced CNI non-inferior. Poor wound healing, **lymphocele**, proteinuria, dyslipidaemia |
| **Basiliximab** | Anti-CD25, non-depleting (**signal 3**) | Low-risk induction, days 0 and 4 |
| **rATG** (rabbit ATG); **alemtuzumab** | T-cell depletion; anti-CD52 | High-risk induction; infection, PTLD |
| **Rituximab; bortezomib** | Anti-CD20 (B cells); proteasome inhibitor (plasma cells) | AMR, desensitisation |
| **Eculizumab** | Anti-C5 (complement) | Selected AMR; aHUS (atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome) |
| **Imlifidase** | Enzyme that **cleaves IgG** | EMA (European Medicines Agency) 2020: highly sensitised, crossmatch-positive |
| **Anti-IL-6** (tocilizumab, clazakizumab) | Blocks IL-6 pathway | Chronic active AMR |

- **Also know:** mycophenolate inhibits IMPDH (inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase), blocking lymphocyte purine synthesis; anti-CD40 costimulation blockade under trial.

### Recent advances
- **Tolerance induction** by mixed chimerism (combined kidney and donor stem-cell transplant) and Treg therapy; **pig-kidney xenotransplant** into a living human (2024).

**Diagram to draw:** APC–T-cell synapse showing signals 1–3 with drug sites (CNI at 1, belatacept at 2, basiliximab and mTOR inhibitors at 3, mycophenolate at DNA synthesis).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Direct pathway drives early TCMR; indirect pathway drives DSA and chronic rejection.
> - Signal 1 calcineurin, signal 2 CD28–B7, signal 3 IL-2R–mTOR.
> - Belatacept blocks signal 2; better GFR, avoid if EBV-negative.
> - Imlifidase (IgG cleavage) for highly sensitised; dd-cfDNA NPV >90%.

## Q2. Describe principles of continent urinary diversions. Current status of orthotopic neobladder.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(neobladder also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q3a)

### Introduction
- A continent diversion stores urine in a bowel reservoir and empties it **at will**: through the native urethra (**orthotopic neobladder**) or by CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) of a stoma (**continent cutaneous pouch**).
- Evolution: ileal conduit (Bricker, 1950) → continent cutaneous (Kock, 1982) → orthotopic (Camey, 1979; Hautmann, 1988; Studer, 1989).

### Basic principles
1. **Reliable outlet.** Orthotopic: intact rhabdosphincter (pudendal nerve) and a healthy urethra. Cutaneous: a stable catheterisable channel with a dependable continence mechanism.
2. **Low pressure throughout filling.** Complete **detubularisation** and cross-folding abolish coordinated peristaltic contractions. A **sphere** gives the greatest volume for its surface area (Laplace: wall tension = pressure × radius).
3. **Adequate volume: ≥500 mL when mature.** Bowel stretches with time (ileum more than colon); 44 cm of double-folded ileum holds <200 mL at first and 500–600 mL by 1 year.
4. **Unobstructed ureterointestinal anastomosis** to protect the kidneys.
5. **Enough renal function** to clear the acid load reabsorbed by bowel mucosa.
- Continence is a **pressure balance**: low reservoir pressure against adequate outlet resistance.

### Choice of bowel segment
| Segment | Points |
|---|---|
| **Ileum** (default) | Most compliant, lowest pressure; mesentery reaches the urethra; mucosa atrophies, so absorption falls |
| Ileum + caecum (Mainz) | Near-ileal compliance; loses ileocaecal valve |
| Colon (right, sigmoid) | Less distensible, pressure waves → more night leakage; when ileum is short or diseased |
| Stomach | Secretes acid: helps in renal impairment; haematuria–dysuria; rarely used |

### Reflux versus obstruction
- Voiding by Valsalva raises pressure equally throughout, so reflux into a low-pressure pouch is benign, while **every antireflux valve can obstruct** (Le Duc tunnel stricture 7–29%; late Kock nipple stenosis).
- Direct refluxing anastomosis (Wallace, Leadbetter): stricture about 3–6%. **USC (University of Southern California) RCT (randomised controlled trial)**, 484 patients: Studer vs T pouch, no difference in renal function or infection at 3 years; T pouch needed more secondary procedures.

### Continent cutaneous mechanisms
| Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|
| Flap valve: appendix tunnelled in taenia | **Mitrofanoff** (Penn, right colon pouch); Yang–Monti ileal tube if no appendix |
| Imbricated ileocaecal valve + tapered ileum | **Indiana** pouch |
| Intussuscepted nipple | Kock (historical) |
- Used when the urethra is unusable; umbilical stoma preferred.

### Patient selection
- **Contraindications to any continent diversion**: severe renal impairment (eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) below about 35–40 mL/min), severe hepatic dysfunction, inability to self-catheterise. **Orthotopic only**: tumour at the urethral margin, significant stricture.
- **Relative**: poor sphincter, frailty, prior radiation or bowel resection, prostatic stromal invasion, bladder-neck or anterior vaginal tumour. **Age and obesity alone are not bars.**

### Current status of orthotopic neobladder
**1. Preferred where suitable:** closest to a native bladder. AUA/SUO (American Urological Association/Society of Urologic Oncology) and EAU (European Association of Urology): counsel every cystectomy patient on all options and refer to an experienced surgeon.

**2. Under-used.** 80–90% of men and about 75% of women are eligible, yet only about 9% of US patients receive any continent diversion, and this is falling; continent diversion in RAZOR 21%, iROC 11%. Causes: comorbid patients, surgeon training, intracorporeal robotics.

**3. Designs in use.** **Studer** (40–44 cm U plus isoperistaltic afferent limb) and **Hautmann W** (about 70 cm; refluxing since 1997) dominate; T pouch or serous-lined tunnel when antireflux is wanted.

**4. Oncological safety.**
- Men: urethral recurrence about 7% at 5 years; main risk is prostatic stromal involvement. The neobladder seems protective (meta-analysis HR (hazard ratio) 0.44).
- Women: risk lies with bladder-neck tumour; no skip lesions, so preserve the urethra. **Frozen section** of the urethral margin decides in both sexes.
- Local recurrence <10%, rarely disabling the pouch; nodal disease is not a bar. Selected **post-radiation** patients do well.

**5. Function**
| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Day continence | 80–90% (most by 3–6 months) |
| Night incontinence | 20–50%: no sphincter reflex, mucosal water secretion |
| Retention needing CIC | Men 4–10%; **women 20–30%** (pouch angulation) |

**6. Complications.** Early morbidity equals conduit. Late: hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis, vitamin B12 deficiency, mucus, ureteroileal stricture, stones, pouch rupture, pouch–vaginal fistula (5–10%), hernia.

**7. Robotic intracorporeal neobladder:** comparable to open; long-term data lacking.

**8. Quality of life:** no diversion proven superior; body image favours neobladder.

- **Also know:** nerve sparing and uterine preservation aid continence; lifelong follow-up (metabolic panel, B12, upper tracts, urethral wash cytology if CIS (carcinoma in situ)).

**Diagram to draw:** Studer pouch with afferent limb, ureters and urethral anastomosis.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Three principles: reliable outlet, low pressure (detubularise, sphere), ≥500 mL.
> - Obstruction, not reflux, destroys kidneys; USC RCT: Studer = T pouch.
> - Eligible 80–90% men, 75% women; actual uptake about 9% in the US.
> - Day continence 80–90%; night leakage 20–50%; women retain (20–30%).

## Q3a. Advances in lap surgeries
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(related: LESS in 2023-Winter Paper 4, Q1; energy sources in 2015-Summer Paper 2, Q3d)

### Introduction
- Since the first laparoscopic nephrectomy (Clayman, 1991), advances aim at **less access trauma, better vision, safer haemostasis, gentler physiology and faster training**.

### 1. Access and insufflation
- **Non-bladed dilating and optical trocars**: port-site hernia fell from 1.83% to 0.19%, wall bleeding from 0.83% to 0.16%.
- **Balloon dilators** for retroperitoneal and extraperitoneal space.
- **AirSeal** valveless system: stable pneumoperitoneum at **8–12 mmHg** despite suction, smoke evacuation, less CO₂ absorption.
- Lower pressure (12 rather than 15 mmHg): less pain and oliguria.

### 2. Fewer and smaller incisions
| Technique | Gain | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **LESS** (laparoendoscopic single-site surgery) | Cosmesis, less analgesia | No triangulation, clashing |
| **NOTES** (natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery) | No abdominal scar (hybrid transvaginal extraction) | No proven benefit |
| Mini-laparoscopy (2–3 mm instruments) | Less pain, cosmesis | Instrument flexibility |
| Hand-assisted | Tactile feedback, fast bleeding control | Larger wound |

### 3. Vision
- Chip-on-tip HD (high-definition) and **3D** laparoscopes; deflectable tips.
- **ICG (indocyanine green) near-infrared fluorescence**: vessel mapping for selective clamping in partial nephrectomy, ureteric identification, sentinel nodes.
- Laparoscopic ultrasound; 3D-printed planning models.

### 4. Haemostasis and suturing
- **Advanced bipolar sealers** (LigaSure: vessels ≤7 mm, 1–3 mm spread) and **ultrasonic shears** (about 1 mm spread, but very hot jaws).
- **Hem-o-lok** clips, **barbed sutures** (shorter anastomosis), Lapra-Ty clips, powered articulating staplers.

### 5. Perioperative care
- **ERAS (enhanced recovery after surgery)**: stay about 30% shorter, complications halved; no routine bowel preparation.

### 6. Training
- Box trainers, VR (virtual reality) simulators, EAU **E-BLUS** (European Basic Laparoscopic Urological Skills) certification, 5-day mini-residencies.

### 7. Robot-assisted laparoscopy
- Wristed 3D platforms (da Vinci Xi, SP 2018; Hugo, Versius, SSI Mantra) solved laparoscopic suturing (**see Q3b**).
- **Also know:** augmented reality, artificial intelligence, telesurgery.

**Diagram to draw:** LESS multichannel port in the umbilicus with articulating instruments.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Blunt trocars cut port-site hernia to 0.19%.
> - AirSeal allows 8–12 mmHg working pressure.
> - ICG fluorescence guides selective clamping.
> - LESS: cosmesis at the cost of triangulation.

## Q3b. Robotic vs std lap surgeries
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(robotics also answered in 2017-Winter Paper 4, Q1 and 2015-Summer Paper 4, Q1)

### Introduction
- Robotic surgery is **robot-assisted laparoscopy** with a **master–slave telemanipulator** (surgeon console, patient-side cart, vision cart). Access and physiology are the same; the interface differs.

### Comparison
| Feature | Standard laparoscopy | Robotic |
|---|---|---|
| Vision | Mostly 2D; assistant holds camera | **3D**, 10× magnified, surgeon controls camera |
| Instruments | Rigid, **4 DOF (degrees of freedom)**; fulcrum effect reverses movement | **EndoWrist, 7 DOF**, 90° articulation; intuitive movement |
| Precision | Tremor transmitted | **Tremor filtration, motion scaling** |
| Ergonomics | Standing, awkward | Seated, less fatigue |
| Suturing | Hard; steep learning curve | Easy; shorter learning curve |
| Haptic feedback | Present (limited) | Absent (force feedback only in newest system) |
| Port placement | Flexible | Ports 8–10 cm apart; docking time |
| Bedside | Surgeon at patient | Surgeon remote; emergency undocking delays |
| Cost | Low, reusable | High capital; instruments limited to about 10 uses |
| Failure | — | Robot failure about 0.4% |

### Evidence by procedure
- **Radical prostatectomy:** laparoscopic prostatectomy stalled on the vesicourethral anastomosis; the robot made it routine and dominant. A small RCT (randomised controlled trial; Porpiglia, 2013) favoured robotic continence and potency.
- **Partial nephrectomy:** robotic vs laparoscopic gives fewer conversions, **shorter warm ischaemia**, smaller eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) fall and shorter stay; complications and margins similar. So more complex tumours are done minimally invasively.
- **Radical nephrectomy, adrenalectomy:** no outcome advantage; robot only adds cost.
- **Reconstruction** (pyeloplasty, reimplantation): similar success; easier suturing, also in children.
- Outcomes depend more on the **surgeon** than on the platform.

### Choosing
- Robot: complex excision plus reconstruction. Laparoscopy: extirpation, low-resource settings.

### Recent advances
- New platforms (Hugo RAS, Versius, Indian SSI Mantra) may cut costs; da Vinci SP (single port); telesurgery.
- **Also know:** dual console and simulators for training.

**Diagram to draw:** EndoWrist (7 DOF) beside a rigid instrument (4 DOF).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Robot: 3D, 7 DOF, tremor filtration, but no haptics and high cost.
> - Robotic partial nephrectomy: shorter warm ischaemia, fewer conversions.
> - Robot made prostatectomy routine; no benefit for simple extirpation.
> - Surgeon matters more than platform.

## Q3c. Metallic stents in urology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(urethral stents also answered in 2015-Summer Paper 4, Q3d; ureteric stents in 2015-Summer Paper 3, Q1)

### Introduction
- Polymer stents (polyurethane, silicone) fail early under **extrinsic compression** and need frequent changes. Metal stents **resist compression** and stay longer, but suffer **encrustation, tissue ingrowth and migration**.

### Classification
| Site | Type | Example | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ureter | Full-length coiled metal, double pigtail | **Resonance** (2006) | Tightly coiled alloy; changed every 12 months |
| Ureter | Segmental self-expanding mesh, bare or covered | Wallstent-type; covered nitinol (Allium) | Placed across malignant stricture; bare mesh allows ingrowth |
| Ureter | Thermo-expandable nitinol coil | Memokath 051 | Removable |
| Prostate | Temporary thermo-expandable nitinol coil | **Memokath** | Not epithelialised; removable |
| Prostate, bulbar urethra | Covered self-expanding, removable | Allium | Left 6–12 months |
| Bulbar urethra | Permanent epithelialising mesh | **UroLume** | Withdrawn |

### Ureteric metal stents
- **Indications:** malignant extrinsic obstruction; retroperitoneal fibrosis (first Resonance use); benign stricture in a patient unfit for repair; ureteroenteric stricture with limited life expectancy; failed tandem polymer stents.
- **Outcomes:** full-length metal stent: up to 3 years of drainage, pooled success about 72.5%, migration about 1%, but about one-third failed in one malignant series. Ureteroenteric strictures: patency >80% at 6–22 months.
- **Problems:** encrustation, difficult exchange, ingrowth, migration.

### Urethral and prostatic stents
- **Indications:** BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) retention in men unfit for surgery; recurrent bulbar stricture unfit for urethroplasty; formerly DSD (detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia), now abandoned.
- **Technique:** Memokath expands with warm water and softens with cold water for removal. A bulbar stent must **not cross the external sphincter**.
- **Outcomes:** Memokath variable; UroLume: good early results, then **hyperplastic ingrowth** and restenosis, so withdrawn. No stent removes spongiofibrosis.
- **AUA (American Urological Association):** no urethral stent if urethroplasty is planned.

### Recent advances
- **Also know:** biodegradable and drug-eluting stents (experimental); Optilume paclitaxel balloon (ROBUST III); iTind (temporary nitinol prostatic device).

**Diagram to draw:** prostatic Memokath ending above the external sphincter.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Metal resists extrinsic compression; weakness is ingrowth and encrustation.
> - Resonance: coiled, full-length, changed every 12 months.
> - Memokath: thermo-expandable nitinol, removable.
> - UroLume withdrawn; AUA: no stent if urethroplasty planned.

## Q3d. Gracilis myocutaneous flap in Uro.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(gracilis-assisted urethroplasty also in 2023-Winter Paper 4, Q3b)

### Definition
- A pedicled **axial** flap of gracilis muscle, with or without skin, rotated from the medial thigh to bring **vascularised bulk** to the perineum, groin or vagina.

### Anatomy
| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Origin–insertion | Inferior pubic ramus and body of pubis → medial proximal tibia (pes anserinus) |
| Blood supply | **Mathes–Nahai type II**: dominant pedicle from the **medial circumflex femoral artery** (profunda femoris), entering about 8–10 cm below the pubic tubercle; minor distal pedicles from the superficial femoral artery |
| Nerve | Anterior branch of the **obturator nerve** |
| Skin paddle | Reliable over the proximal two-thirds only |
| Function | Expendable (other adductors compensate) |

### Why it is used
- Brings blood supply into **irradiated or scarred** fields, fills dead space and **separates suture lines**. Near the perineum, easy to tunnel.

### Uses in urology
1. **Rectourethral fistula** (perineal): non-irradiated all closed; irradiated mostly closed, but more strictures and permanent colostomy.
2. **Complex or irradiated VVF (vesicovaginal fistula)** with tissue loss.
3. **Urethroplasty** in a poor bed: muscle as bed for BMG (buccal mucosal graft).
4. Extensive perineal or scrotal loss after **Fournier gangrene**.
5. **Groin cover** of femoral vessels after inguinal node dissection with skin excision.
6. Vaginal reconstruction after exenteration.

### Technique
1. Lithotomy; line from pubic tubercle to medial femoral condyle; gracilis lies just behind it.
2. Medial thigh incision; skin paddle over the proximal muscle if needed.
3. Divide the distal tendon; ligate minor pedicles; **preserve the dominant pedicle**.
4. Tunnel subcutaneously to the perineum; rotate without twisting.
5. Interpose and fix; drain the donor site.

### Complications
- Donor seroma, dehiscence, **distal skin-paddle necrosis**, thigh numbness; flap loss rare.

- **Also know:** alternatives: Martius, omentum, VRAM (vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous), Singapore flap.

**Diagram to draw:** gracilis tunnelled on its proximal pedicle between urethra and rectum.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Type II flap: medial circumflex femoral pedicle 8–10 cm below the pubic tubercle.
> - Skin paddle unreliable over the distal third.
> - Main uses: rectourethral fistula, complex VVF, Fournier, groin cover.

## Q3e. Newer generation laser technologies.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2024-Winter Paper 4, Q3c; stone lasers in 2025-Winter Paper 4, Q3b)

### Background
- **Ho:YAG (holmium:yttrium-aluminium-garnet), 2120 nm** (often quoted as 2100 nm) is the standard for stones and enucleation: water-absorbed, pulsed, **photothermal**; penetration 0.31 mm.
- Aims: less retropulsion, finer dust, faster work.

### 1. High-power holmium (100–120 W)
- **Dusting** (low energy, high frequency) and **popcorning**; long-pulse modes reduce retropulsion.

### 2. Pulse modulation (Moses technology)
- **Moses effect:** the first part of a pulse boils a **vapour channel**; vapour absorbs less light, so the rest reaches the target.
- Moses technology splits each pulse in two: the first opens the channel, the second strikes. Less retropulsion; may shorten **HoLEP (holmium laser enucleation of the prostate)**.

### 3. TFL (thulium fibre laser), 1940 nm
- Diode-pumped silica fibre at the **water absorption peak**: about **4× holmium absorption**; penetration **0.08 mm**.
- **Low peak power, long pulses, very high frequency (above 2000 Hz)**: fine dust, **minimal retropulsion**.
- **Thin fibres** (about 150 µm): better irrigation and deflection.
- **RCTs (randomised controlled trials):** TFL beat **low-power (30 W) holmium**; it **equalled 120 W pulse-modulated holmium**.
- **Prostate:** ThuFLEP (thulium fibre laser enucleation).

### 4. Thulium:YAG, 2013 nm
- Originally continuous wave (0.2 mm penetration, carbonisation): ThuVARP (vaporesection), ThuVEP (vapoenucleation), **ThuLEP** (enucleation, size-independent like HoLEP). Newer **pulsed Tm:YAG** also does lithotripsy.

### Comparison
| | Ho:YAG | TFL |
|---|---|---|
| Wavelength | 2120 nm | 1940 nm |
| Penetration | 0.31 mm | 0.08 mm |
| Peak power | High | Low |
| Retropulsion | More | Minimal |

### Settings and safety
- **Retropulsion rises with pulse energy**, not frequency; fibre close and perpendicular.
- **Heat follows power, not laser type:** ≤10 W in the ureter, ≤25 W in the kidney; injury above 43 °C; use an access sheath.

- **Also know:** 180 W GreenLight (532 nm) vaporisation; diode lasers; suction sheaths.

**Diagram to draw:** water absorption curve marking 1940 and 2120 nm.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - TFL 1940 nm: water peak, low peak power, minimal retropulsion.
> - Moses: vapour channel; two-part pulse.
> - TFL beats low-power, equals high-power pulse-modulated holmium.
> - Heat depends on power: ≤10 W ureter, ≤25 W kidney.

## Q3f. New LASER for stone Treatment
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

**See Practice Set 3 Paper 4, Q3e (full answer).**

> **Key points to remember (stone angle):**
> - TFL (1940 nm): fine dust, minimal retropulsion, thin fibres; settings ≤40 Hz often enough.
> - Pulse-modulated (Moses) high-power holmium: vapour channel, less retropulsion.
> - Safety: ≤10 W in the ureter, ≤25 W in the kidney; access sheath; damage above 43 °C.
