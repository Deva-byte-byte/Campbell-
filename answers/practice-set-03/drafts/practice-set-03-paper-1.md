## Q1. Describe hemodynamic & physiologic changes d/t pneumoperitoneum during lap.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered, with Q2, in 2019-Summer Paper 1, Q2)

*"d/t" is read as "due to" and "lap" as laparoscopy.*

### Introduction
- **Pneumoperitoneum** is insufflation of gas, usually CO₂ (carbon dioxide), into the peritoneal cavity at 10–15 mmHg to create a working space.
- Its effects have **two drivers**: **pressure** (compression of the cava, renal veins, kidneys and diaphragm) and **absorbed CO₂** (hypercarbia, acidosis, sympathetic drive). Patient position and volume status modify both.

### Choice of insufflant
| Gas | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **CO₂ (standard)** | Non-combustible; highly blood-soluble, so embolism is less lethal; cheap | Hypercarbia, acidosis, arrhythmia; COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) patients cannot clear it |
| Helium, argon | Inert; no hypercarbia | Poorly soluble, so gas embolism risk; costly. Helium used in lung disease or to salvage hypercarbia |
| N₂O (nitrous oxide) | Less peritoneal irritation | Supports combustion; no electrosurgery |
| Room air, oxygen | — | Embolism, combustion; abandoned |

### Effects by pressure (supine, Campbell Table 18.1)
| Effect | 5 mmHg | 10 mmHg | 20 mmHg | 40 mmHg |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ | ↓ |
| MAP (mean arterial pressure), SVR (systemic vascular resistance) | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ |
| Cardiac output | ↔/↓ | ↔/↑ | ↔/↓ | ↓ |
| GFR (glomerular filtration rate), urine output | ↔ | ↓ | ↓↓ | ↓↓ |
| Arterial PCO₂ | ↔ | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ |
| Arterial pH | ↔ | ↔/↓ | ↓ | ↓ |

- Renal function is the first casualty (from 10 mmHg). Afterload rises at every pressure. Output collapses at 40 mmHg. Acidosis builds from 20 mmHg.

### Cardiovascular (haemodynamic) changes
- **Afterload rises**: aortic and splanchnic compression plus catecholamine and **vasopressin** release raise MAP and SVR.
- **Preload depends on volume status.** In normovolaemia or hypovolaemia, pressure up to 20 mmHg compresses the IVC (inferior vena cava) and venous return falls. In hypervolaemia the cava resists and venous return may rise. At ≥40 mmHg venous return falls sharply in everyone.
- **Arrhythmias**: hypercapnia causes tachycardia and ventricular ectopics; peritoneal stretch causes **vagal bradycardia** or asystole. A new arrhythmia may warn of pneumothorax, hypoxia or gas embolism.
- **CVP (central venous pressure) reads falsely high** because intrathoracic pressure rises; do not guide fluids by it.
- Leg venous return falls: theoretical DVT (deep vein thrombosis) risk.

### Respiratory changes
- **Diaphragm splinted**: FRC (functional residual capacity) and compliance fall, peak airway pressure rises; atelectasis and V/Q (ventilation–perfusion) mismatch. Dead space is unchanged. PEEP (positive end-expiratory pressure) helps in lung disease.
- **Head-down** adds a further fall in vital capacity, endobronchial tube slip and facial oedema.
- **Barotrauma** (pressure >15 mmHg for long): pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum.

### Acid–base changes (CO₂ absorption)
- CO₂ is absorbed through the peritoneum and, extraperitoneally, through fat and disrupted lymphatics. Absorption rises over 30–60 minutes, then plateaus.
- **Respiratory acidosis** and rising end-tidal CO₂. Treat by raising minute ventilation and lowering pressure.
- **COPD**: arterial gases if insufflation exceeds 1 hour, continued in recovery. **Hypercarbia peaks after extubation**, as stored CO₂ is released.

### Renal changes
- **Oliguria** comes from **renal vein and parenchymal compression**, not ureteric compression or hormones. Cortical flow falls; medullary flow rises up to 20 mmHg, then falls. It reverses on desufflation.
- Keep pressure ≤10 mmHg. If needed give furosemide, mannitol 12.5–25 g or dopamine 2 µg/kg/min to keep urine >100 mL/hour, rather than fluid boluses (overload, oedema). Important in live donor nephrectomy.

### Other systems
- **Splanchnic**: hepatic and mesenteric flow fall (rare mesenteric thrombosis); less ileus than open surgery; no rise in reflux.
- **ICP (intracranial pressure) and IOP (intraocular pressure)** rise with hypercarbia and head-down tilt: caution in head injury, shunts and glaucoma.
- **Stress and immune response**: cortisol, catecholamines and prolactin rise as in any surgery, but hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance, catabolism and IL-6 (interleukin-6) rise less than after open surgery; oncological benefit unproven.
- **Also know:** hypothermia from cold dry gas; shoulder-tip pain from diaphragmatic CO₂ irritation.

### Effects of position and approach
| Position | Heart rate | MAP | Cardiac output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head-up | ↑ | ↓ | ↓ |
| Head-down (Trendelenburg) | ↓ | ↑ | ↑ (but lungs worse) |
| Lateral flank, kidney rest | — | — | Lung V/Q mismatch; rhabdomyolysis risk |

- **Retroperitoneal** insufflation causes smaller swings in output and venous pressures than transperitoneal; pelvic extraperitoneal insufflation raised arterial CO₂ more.

### Safe practice
- Preoperative cardiopulmonary assessment; correct arrhythmias; optimise volume.
- **Work at 10–12 mmHg** (12 mmHg keeps stroke volume unchanged); 20 mmHg only briefly (first trocar, venous bleeding).
- Monitor end-tidal CO₂, saturation, airway pressure and urine output.
- **Recent advances:** low-pressure (8 mmHg) surgery with valveless insufflators (AirSeal); helium for lung disease.

**Diagram to draw:** abdomen under pressure with arrows to diaphragm (lungs), IVC (preload), aorta (afterload) and renal vein (oliguria), plus CO₂ absorption to blood.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Two drivers: pressure and absorbed CO₂; work at 10–12 mmHg.
> - Afterload rises at all pressures; output collapses at 40 mmHg.
> - Oliguria from renal vein compression; reversible.
> - Hypercarbia peaks after extubation; COPD needs arterial gases.

## Q2. Complications of retroperitoneoscopy.
**Type:** LAQ (25 marks)
(also answered, with Q1, in 2019-Summer Paper 1, Q2)

### Introduction
- **Retroperitoneoscopy** is laparoscopic or robotic surgery in the retroperitoneum without entering the peritoneum. It gives direct hilar access and avoids bowel, but the space must be **created**.
- **Why its complications differ:** no natural cavity; space made by finger and **balloon dissection** behind Gerota's fascia and in front of the psoas; fewer landmarks; dissection close to the lens; gas tracks along tissue planes; CO₂ (carbon dioxide) is absorbed through fat and lymphatics.
- Minimally invasive renal surgery: about 15% complications, mostly postoperative. Learning curve about 20 cases.

### Classification (by phase of surgery)
| Phase | Complications |
|---|---|
| Access and space creation | Wrong plane, balloon rupture, peritoneal tear, muscle bleeding |
| Insufflation | Subcutaneous emphysema, hypercarbia, pneumothorax, gas embolism |
| Dissection | Vascular, visceral, nerve, thermal injury |
| Exit and recovery | Port bleeding, hernia, rhabdomyolysis, urinoma, lymphocele |

### 1. Access and balloon-related
- **Balloon in muscle** → postoperative **flank hernia** or bulge.
- **Too anterior** → peritoneal breach or colon injury; **too posterior** → bleeding from quadratus lumborum or psoas.
- **Balloon rupture**: latex glove balloon breaks into many fragments (silicone leaves one) that must be retrieved.
- **Peritoneal tear**: CO₂ enters the peritoneum and collapses the small working space. Manage by venting with a Veress needle or port, enlarging the tear, or converting to transperitoneal.
- Disorientation: keep the **psoas horizontal** as the landmark.

### 2. Gas-related
- **Subcutaneous emphysema** (commonest; commoner than transperitoneal): crepitus over flank, chest and neck; pneumoscrotum. Cause: leak around the port. Prevent with a **balloon-tip (blunt-tip) trocar**, tight incisions and 12 rather than 15 mmHg.
- **Hypercarbia**, especially with emphysema; a sealed port removes the difference from transperitoneal surgery.
- **Pneumothorax (1.6–4%, commoner retroperitoneally)**: pleura is near the upper pole and 12th rib. Signs: neck and chest emphysema; tension gives hypotension, absent breath sounds and high airway pressure. Treat with a 16-G needle in the second or third intercostal space, midclavicular line, then chest drain. Pneumomediastinum and pneumopericardium (about 0.8%) also occur.
- **Gas embolism** (gas into an opened renal vein, IVC [inferior vena cava] or lumbar vein): sudden collapse; end-tidal CO₂ rises abruptly, saturation falls, then end-tidal CO₂ falls; "millwheel" murmur. Stop insufflation, **left lateral, head down**, 100% oxygen, aspirate through a central line.

### 3. Vascular injury
- Commonest complication of minimally invasive urology; major injury <1 in 1000 but fatal in 8–17%. Upper-tract (retroperitoneal-organ) work carries about five times the pelvic vascular risk.
- Sites: renal artery and vein, **lumbar and gonadal veins**, adrenal vein, IVC, aorta. In the small posterior space the IVC or aorta can be mistaken for hilar vessels.
- **Management of venous bleeding**: raise pressure to 25 mmHg, suction to see the site, tamponade with a sponge, haemostatic sealant, extra 5-mm port for suturing or a Satinsky clamp, hand-assist, or convert. Arterial: clip or staple; aortic injury needs conversion. Never clip blindly.

### 4. Visceral injury
- **Peritoneum and colon**; duodenum (right); spleen, pancreas (left); liver; pleura; ureter; adrenal.
- **Thermal bowel injury** by four mechanisms: direct activation, direct coupling, capacitive coupling, insulation failure. It may present up to 18 days later with low-grade fever and pain; diagnose by CT (computed tomography) with oral contrast.

### 5. Positioning and nerve injury
- **Rhabdomyolysis** (about 1% retroperitoneal; 0.4% overall): men, BMI (body mass index) ≥25, kidney rest, full flexion, long cases. Brown urine, hip pain, CK (creatine kinase) >5,000 units. Prevent: kidney rest <1 hour, cases <5 hours, avoid hypotension.
- **Nerves**: brachial plexus (axillary roll), sciatic; in the field, **genitofemoral and lateral femoral cutaneous** on the psoas, ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric (thigh numbness, flank bulge).

### 6. Postoperative
- Bleeding or haematoma; port-site hernia; wound infection.
- **Urinoma** (partial nephrectomy, pyeloplasty); **lymphocele** or chylous leak after hilar or node dissection.
- Delayed bowel injury; DVT (deep vein thrombosis).
- **Conversion** to transperitoneal or open surgery for bleeding, peritoneal tear or lost orientation.

### Retroperitoneal versus transperitoneal
| Retroperitoneal more | Retroperitoneal less |
|---|---|
| Emphysema, pneumothorax, hypercarbia, disorientation | Bowel injury, ileus, adhesions; peritoneum spared |

### Prevention
- Open (Hasson) access below the 12th rib tip; balloon wholly in the retroperitoneum; balloon-tip port; 12 mmHg; trocars under vision; respect the 12th rib and pleura; check haemostasis at low pressure.

**Diagram to draw:** flank cross-section showing balloon between psoas and Gerota's fascia, with peritoneum pushed medially and port positions.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Space is created, not natural: wrong plane gives hernia, peritoneal tear or bleeding.
> - Emphysema commonest; balloon-tip port and 12 mmHg prevent it.
> - Pneumothorax 1.6–4%, commoner retroperitoneally.
> - Rhabdomyolysis about 1%: limit kidney rest to <1 hour.

## Q3a. Clinical trial methodology
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- A **clinical trial** is a prospective study that assigns people to an intervention to test its effect on health outcomes. The **RCT (randomised controlled trial)** is the gold standard because randomisation balances known and unknown confounders.

### Phases
| Phase | Purpose | Subjects |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Microdose pharmacokinetics | Very few |
| I | Safety, dose, toxicity | 20–80, often volunteers |
| II | Activity, efficacy signal | Patients, about 100–300 |
| III | Comparative efficacy versus standard | Hundreds to thousands; basis of approval |
| IV | Post-marketing surveillance | General population |

### Design
- **Parallel**, **crossover** (own control; stable chronic disease), **factorial** (two questions at once), **cluster** or **adaptive**.
- Hypothesis: **superiority**, **non-inferiority** (pre-set margin) or equivalence.
- Surgical innovation follows the **IDEAL** framework (Idea, Development, Exploration, Assessment, Long-term study).

### Randomisation and allocation
- Simple, **block** (equal numbers), **stratified** (by prognostic factors) or **minimisation**.
- **Allocation concealment** (central or web-based) stops selection bias.

### Blinding
- Single (patient), double (plus assessor) or triple (plus analyst). Surgery: sham procedures or blinded assessors.

### Endpoints
- One **primary endpoint** sets sample size; secondary endpoints are exploratory.
- **Hard** (overall survival) versus **surrogate** endpoints, valid only if they meet the **Prentice criteria**; in prostate cancer only MFS (metastasis-free survival) does.
- **PROs (patient-reported outcomes)** with validated tools; complications graded by **Clavien–Dindo** or CTCAE (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events).

### Sample size and analysis
- Set α (type I error, usually 0.05), power (80–90%) and expected effect.
- **ITT (intention-to-treat)** preserves randomisation; per-protocol supports non-inferiority claims.
- Kaplan–Meier curves for time-to-event data.

### Ethics and reporting
- Declaration of Helsinki, ICH-GCP (International Council for Harmonisation–Good Clinical Practice), informed consent, ethics committee, DSMB (data safety monitoring board).
- **Registration** before enrolment (CTRI [Clinical Trials Registry–India]).
- **CONSORT** (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials): checklist plus **participant flow diagram** (25 items in the 2010 version; updated 2025).
- **Also know:** lead-time bias; Will Rogers phenomenon.

**Diagram to draw:** CONSORT flow diagram: enrolment → randomisation → allocation → follow-up → analysis.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Phase III RCT with concealed allocation and blinding is the gold standard.
> - Primary endpoint drives sample size; surrogates need the Prentice criteria.
> - ITT analysis; CONSORT flow diagram for reporting.

## Q3b. Classify immunosuppresants. Describe few common protocols.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2021-Summer Paper 1, Q3f and 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q1)

*Taken as immunosuppressants in renal transplantation.*

### Principle
- T-cell activation needs **signal 1** (TCR [T-cell receptor] → calcineurin → IL-2 [interleukin-2]), **signal 2** (costimulation, CD28–CD80/86) and **signal 3** (IL-2 receptor → mTOR [mammalian target of rapamycin] → proliferation). Drugs block these steps.

### Classification
| Class | Drugs | Action | Main toxicity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CNI (calcineurin inhibitors)** | **Tacrolimus**, ciclosporin | Signal 1 | Nephrotoxicity, diabetes, tremor |
| **Antiproliferatives** | **Mycophenolate**, azathioprine | Purine synthesis | Diarrhoea, leucopenia |
| **mTOR inhibitors** | Sirolimus, everolimus | Signal 3 | Lymphocele, poor wound healing |
| **Corticosteroids** | Methylprednisolone, prednisolone | Cytokine transcription | Diabetes, bone loss |
| **Costimulation blocker** | Belatacept | Signal 2 | PTLD (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder) if EBV (Epstein–Barr virus)-negative |
| **Depleting antibodies** | rATG (rabbit antithymocyte globulin), alemtuzumab | T-cell depletion | Infection, PTLD |
| **Non-depleting antibody** | **Basiliximab** | Anti-CD25 (IL-2 receptor) | Few |
| **Antibody/B-cell agents** | Rituximab, IVIG (intravenous immunoglobulin), plasmapheresis, eculizumab, imlifidase | Antibody removal or blockade | — |

### Phases
- **Induction** (peri-transplant), **maintenance** (lifelong), **treatment of rejection**.

### Common protocols
1. **Standard-risk (most recipients):** basiliximab 20 mg IV (intravenous) on days 0 and 4 + intraoperative methylprednisolone; then **tacrolimus + mycophenolate + tapering prednisolone**. Over 90% receive induction and tacrolimus–mycophenolate; steroids in more than two-thirds.
2. **High-immunological-risk** (sensitised, retransplant, DGF [delayed graft function] expected): **rATG** induction, then triple therapy.
3. **Steroid-avoidance or early withdrawal:** low-risk recipients, usually after depleting induction.
4. **CNI-sparing:** belatacept- or mTOR-based regimens to spare nephrotoxicity; TRANSFORM: everolimus with reduced CNI non-inferior.
5. **ABO-incompatible or HLA (human leucocyte antigen)-sensitised:** desensitisation with rituximab, plasmapheresis and IVIG; imlifidase for crossmatch-positive.
6. **Rejection:** T-cell-mediated → pulse IV methylprednisolone, rATG for Banff grade II–III; antibody-mediated → steroids, plasmapheresis, IVIG, rituximab.

- **Monitoring:** tacrolimus trough levels (higher early, lower later); CYP3A4 (cytochrome P450 3A4) interactions.
- **Urological relevance:** BK virus ureteric stricture; bladder cancer about 3-fold; lymphocele with mTOR inhibitors.

**Diagram to draw:** APC (antigen-presenting cell)–T-cell synapse with drugs at signals 1–3.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Basiliximab or rATG induction; tacrolimus + mycophenolate ± steroids maintenance.
> - CNI nephrotoxic; mTOR inhibitors cause lymphocele.
> - Rejection: steroids for TCMR; plasmapheresis, IVIG, rituximab for AMR.

## Q3c. Assessment of quality of life after urinary diversion
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **HRQoL (health-related quality of life)**: the patient's view of how disease and treatment affect physical, emotional and social function, plus **bother**. It must be **patient-reported**; doctors underestimate urinary, sexual and bowel dysfunction.

### Instruments
| Type | Examples | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Generic** | SF-36 (Short Form-36), SF-12, EQ-5D (EuroQol five-dimension) | Insensitive to diversion-specific issues |
| **Cancer-specific** | EORTC QLQ-C30 (European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer core questionnaire), FACT-G (Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–General) | General cancer domains |
| **Bladder-cancer-specific** | **FACT-Bl** (40 items), **EORTC QLQ-BLM30** (muscle-invasive module, 30 items), **BCI (Bladder Cancer Index**, 34 items: urinary, bowel, sexual domains), FACT-VCI (Vanderbilt Cystectomy Index) | Weight symptoms of one diversion, so biased in cross-diversion comparisons |
| **Neobladder-specific** | IONB-PRO (23 items) | Not usable across diversions |

- A good tool is **reliable** (Cronbach α >0.7), **valid** in that population and **responsive** to change.

### Domains to assess
- Urinary: continence, catheterisation, stoma problems.
- **Body image**, sexual function, bowel function, social and emotional function, global HRQoL.

### Evidence: ileal conduit versus neobladder
- **No diversion proven superior.** Studies are mostly cross-sectional and retrospective, without baseline data or randomisation.
- Verdict depends on instrument: generic tools mostly show **no difference** (largest group of studies); EORTC tools tend to **favour neobladder**; the urinary-focused BCI **penalises neobladder** for leakage.
- **Consistent signal:** body image favours neobladder; urinary function favours conduit.
- Large prospective cohort: global HRQoL exceeded baseline in all diversions by 6 months.
- Neobladder: daytime continence improves over 6–12 months; **night-time incontinence** (up to 50%) is the main bother.
- **Also know:** informed patients have less decision regret; most adapt to their diversion; PROMIS (Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System).

### Practical approach
- Baseline and serial validated questionnaires (3, 6, 12 months), a disease-specific plus a generic tool, and realistic counselling.

**Diagram to draw:** bar sketch: body image (neobladder better) versus urinary function (conduit better).

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Generic (SF-36, EQ-5D) plus bladder-specific (FACT-Bl, BLM30, BCI) tools.
> - No diversion proven superior; studies flawed.
> - Neobladder better body image; conduit better urinary function.

## Q3d. Describe evidence based medicine
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)
(also answered in 2020-Summer Paper 4, Q3f)

### Definition
- **EBM (evidence-based medicine)**: "the conscientious, explicit and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients" (**Sackett, 1996**).
- It integrates best research evidence, **clinical expertise** and **patient values**.

### Steps ("5 As")
1. **Ask** a focused question in **PICO** form (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome).
2. **Acquire**: PubMed, Cochrane Library, guidelines.
3. **Appraise**: validity (randomisation, blinding), effect size (ARR [absolute risk reduction], **NNT [number needed to treat] = 1/ARR**), precision (95% CI [confidence interval]), applicability.
4. **Apply** with expertise and patient preference.
5. **Assess** (audit) the outcome.

### Levels of evidence (Oxford CEBM [Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine], as adapted by EAU [European Association of Urology])
| Level | Type of evidence |
|---|---|
| **1a** | Systematic review or meta-analysis of RCTs (randomised controlled trials) |
| **1b** | At least one RCT |
| 2a | Well-designed controlled study without randomisation |
| 2b | Quasi-experimental (cohort) study |
| 3 | Non-experimental: case-control, correlation, case reports |
| 4 | Expert committee reports or opinion |

- **Grades**: A (consistent level 1), B (level 2–3), C (level 4 or consensus).
- **GRADE** (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation): certainty high to very low; RCTs start high, observational studies low; **downgrade** for bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias; **upgrade** for large effect or dose–response. Recommendations **strong or weak** (used by EAU). AUA (American Urological Association): evidence strength A/B/C; strong, moderate or conditional recommendations.

### Pitfalls in appraisal
- **Bias**: selection, confounding, **lead-time**, **length-time**, stage migration (Will Rogers).
- **Surrogate endpoints** must meet the **Prentice criteria**.
- **Also know:** CONSORT, PRISMA, STROBE reporting checklists; validated PROs (patient-reported outcomes).

### Limitations
- Few surgical RCTs; trial patients differ from real ones; publication bias; "cookbook medicine" if patient values are ignored.

**Diagram to draw:** evidence pyramid and the three overlapping circles of EBM.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Sackett 1996: best evidence + clinical expertise + patient values.
> - 5 As: Ask (PICO), Acquire, Appraise, Apply, Assess.
> - Level 1a = systematic review of RCTs; GRADE: strong or weak.
> - NNT = 1/ARR; beware lead-time, length-time and confounding bias.

## Q3e. What are nanobacteria? Describe their role in urolithiasis.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

### Definition
- **Nanobacteria**, now called **CNPs (calcifying nanoparticles)**, are spherical particles far smaller than bacteria with a **calcium apatite shell**, described in bovine and human serum by **Kajander and Çiftçioglu (1998)** as *Nanobacterium sanguineum*.
- **Their living nature is disputed.** Proposed as the smallest self-replicating bacteria, but later work showed "replication" is mineral nucleation, and that they are **mineralo-protein complexes** (apatite with serum proteins such as fetuin-A). Their bacterial DNA proved to be contamination. Most now regard them as **non-living**.

### Claimed properties
| Property | Claim |
|---|---|
| Size | Pass through bacteria-retaining filters |
| "Growth" | Very slow, in serum-containing medium |
| Shell | Apatite; resistant to heat and gamma irradiation |
| Detection | Electron microscopy, von Kossa and Hoechst staining, specific antibodies, culture |
| Susceptibility | Tetracycline and EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) inhibit them |

### Proposed role in urolithiasis
- **Nidus theory:** CNPs in blood or urine are filtered or excreted, deposit apatite in renal tubular cells and interstitium, and act as a **nucleating nidus** for crystal growth.
- **Link to Randall plaque:** idiopathic calcium oxalate stones grow on **Randall plaque**, an interstitial **apatite** deposit that starts in the basement membrane of the thin loop of Henle and erodes through the papilla. CNPs are **associated with**, but **not proven to cause**, Randall plaque (Campbell, Chapter 95).
- Early studies found apatite nanoparticles in a large majority of stones; rat studies reported renal calcification after injection.
- Also implicated in atherosclerotic calcification, polycystic kidney cysts and chronic prostatitis.

### Therapeutic implications (unproven)
- Tetracycline-based regimens with EDTA were tried in stones and chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome; no RCT (randomised controlled trial) evidence supports routine use.

### Current position
- Not in stone guidelines; pathogenesis is explained by supersaturation, inhibitors, Randall plaque and ductal plugs.

**Diagram to draw:** renal papilla with interstitial apatite (Randall plaque) eroding through urothelium, CNPs as nidus, and calcium oxalate stone attached.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Nanobacteria = calcifying nanoparticles; apatite shell; living nature disputed.
> - Proposed nidus for apatite and Randall plaque; association, not causation.
> - Tetracycline/EDTA therapy unproven.

## Q3f. Secondary malignancies in Urology.
**Type:** SAQ (10 marks)

*Interpreted as second cancers caused by urological or oncological treatment (radiotherapy, chemotherapy, urinary diversion, immunosuppression).*

### Definition
- A **second (treatment-related) malignancy** is a new, histologically distinct cancer caused by treatment of a first disease. It appears after a latency of years, often **in the radiation field**.

### Causes and risks
| Cause | Second cancer | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Pelvic radiotherapy** | **Bladder cancer**, rectal cancer, sarcoma | Bladder RR (relative risk) 2–4 after cervical EBRT (external beam radiotherapy), 1.5–2 after prostate EBRT, smaller after brachytherapy, little with IMRT (intensity-modulated radiotherapy). Latency 15–30 years |
| **Cyclophosphamide** | **Bladder cancer**, UTUC (upper tract urothelial carcinoma) | Only chemotherapy proven to cause bladder cancer; metabolites phosphoramide mustard and acrolein. About 4.5-fold, dose-dependent (>20 g cumulative) |
| **Urinary diversion / augmentation** | Adenocarcinoma at the uro-intestinal junction | Ureterosigmoidostomy 6–29% after 10–20 years (registry 2.6%); cystoplasty 1.6%; ileal conduit 0.02%. Risk tracks colonic mucosa exposed to urine and faeces |
| **Transplant immunosuppression** | Skin cancer, PTLD (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder), RCC (renal cell carcinoma), bladder cancer | RCC mostly in native kidneys; bladder cancer about 3-fold and aggressive |
| **Testicular GCT (germ cell tumour) treatment** | Solid tumours, leukaemia | 60–100% excess after cisplatin or radiotherapy, 200% after both; etoposide-related leukaemia |
| **Paediatric tumours** | In-field solid tumours, leukaemia | Wilms tumour 1% at 10 years |

### Mechanisms
- Radiation and alkylating or topoisomerase-inhibitor chemotherapy: mutagenic DNA damage in normal tissue.
- Immunosuppression: loss of immune surveillance and oncogenic viruses (EBV [Epstein–Barr virus], HPV [human papillomavirus]).
- Diversion: urine–faeces contact at the anastomosis.

### Prevention and surveillance
- IMRT, smaller fields; **mesna** with cyclophosphamide (reduces cystitis).
- **Haematuria after pelvic RT or cyclophosphamide: always cystoscopy.**
- Endoscopy from 5 years after ureterosigmoidostomy; augmentation surveillance from about 10 years; excise a defunctioned ureterosigmoid anastomosis.
- Testis: fewer modalities; low-dose CT (computed tomography) in surveillance.
- **Also know:** pioglitazone, aristolochic acid and phenacetin as drug-related urothelial carcinogens.

**Diagram to draw:** ureterosigmoid anastomosis showing adenocarcinoma at the junction.

> **Key points to remember:**
> - Pelvic EBRT raises bladder cancer 1.5–4-fold; IMRT little.
> - Cyclophosphamide: only chemotherapy proven to cause bladder cancer.
> - Ureterosigmoidostomy cancer 6–29% after 10–20 years.
> - Transplant: bladder cancer about 3-fold.
