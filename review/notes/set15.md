## Ch 44 External genitalia in boys

### Development and prepuce
- DHT (dihydrotestosterone) acting on androgen receptors at 9–13 weeks turns the genital tubercle, genital folds and genital swellings into glans, penile shaft and scrotum. <!-- c044-a01 -->
- Primary phimosis resolves spontaneously in most boys: 90% retractable by 3 years; fewer than 1% have phimosis at 17 years. <!-- c044-a02 c044-r01 -->
- Reasons to help retraction: persistent primary phimosis, secondary phimosis, balanitis, posthitis, BXO (balanitis xerotica obliterans) and UTI (urinary tract infection). <!-- c044-r02 -->
- Topical corticosteroids relax the phimotic ring in 70%–80%, but they do not resolve penile adhesions. <!-- c044-r07 c044-r11 -->
- BXO is a chronic infiltrative, scarring (cicatrix) skin condition. Steroids help only mild preputial BXO; circumcision is the preferred treatment. <!-- c044-a05 c044-r12 -->

### Circumcision
- The AAP (American Academy of Pediatrics) says benefits justify access to newborn circumcision and third-party payment. <!-- c044-r03 -->
- Benefits: fewer penile cancers, STIs (sexually transmitted infections) including HIV, UTIs, balanitis and phimosis. <!-- c044-r06 -->
- Give local or topical anaesthesia routinely in neonates. Revisions need general anaesthesia. <!-- c044-r04 c044-r09 -->
- Defer it if the newborn has a penile anomaly that may need surgery: hypospadias, penile curvature, dorsal hood, buried or webbed penis. <!-- c044-a03 c044-r05 -->
- Complications occur in 0.2%–5%. Bleeding is the commonest (about 0.1%, more in older boys). Meatal stenosis occurs only in the circumcised penis. <!-- c044-a04 c044-r08 c044-r10 -->

### Penile anomalies
- Penile agenesis (aphallia, failed genital tubercle) occurs in 1 in 10–30 million births. It is almost always 46,XY, with a normal scrotum, descended testes and an anteriorly displaced anus. Associations: cryptorchidism, VUR (vesicoureteral reflux), horseshoe kidney, renal agenesis, imperforate anus, skeletal and cardiopulmonary defects (not UPJ obstruction). <!-- c044-a06 -->
- Buried penis has three causes: poor penopubic skin fixation, obesity, and a trapped penis from a cicatrix after surgery (usually circumcision). It is a normal-length penis, so not micropenis. <!-- c044-a07 c044-r14 -->
- A trapped penis in a young child: office dilation of the cicatrix under local anaesthesia, or topical betamethasone with manual retraction. <!-- c044-a08 -->
- Stretched length is measured from the pubis to the glans tip with the fat pad fully depressed. A term neonate should be at least 1.9 cm (2.5 SD below the mean of 4.0 cm). <!-- c044-a09 -->
- Micropenis: a normally formed penis at least 2.5 SD below the mean stretched length (under 1.9 cm in neonates). The length-to-girth ratio is usually normal; the testes are often small and undescended and the scrotum small. <!-- c044-a10 c044-r13 -->
- Causes: hypogonadotropic hypogonadism is commonest (low GnRH: Prader-Willi, Kallmann, Laurence-Moon-Biedl, CHARGE), then hypergonadotropic hypogonadism. <!-- c044-a11 c044-r15 -->
- Work-up: karyotype, LH and FSH, testosterone before and after hCG stimulation, anterior pituitary tests and brain MRI. Most adults with micropenis have male identity and satisfactory sexual function. <!-- c044-r16 c044-r17 -->
- Congenital penile naevi are superficial and benign; they are excised. <!-- c044-a12 -->
- Penoscrotal transposition usually accompanies perineal, scrotal or penoscrotal hypospadias with chordee. It is linked to caudal regression, sex chromosome anomalies and Aarskog syndrome. Up to 75% with complete transposition have significant urinary tract anomalies (renal agenesis, dysplasia). <!-- c044-a15 -->

### Priapism in boys (see Ch 70 for full details)
- Types are ischaemic (low flow), non-ischaemic (arterial, high flow) and stuttering. Sickle cell disease is the commonest cause in children. <!-- c044-r18 c044-r19 -->
- Sickle cell low-flow priapism (book): hydration, oxygen, alkalinisation, analgesia and transfusion, with aspiration, irrigation and α-adrenergic (not β) injection concurrently or after failure; shunts if these fail. **Update:** AUA/SMSNA say intracavernous treatment should not wait for systemic therapy. <!-- c044-a13 c044-r20 -->
- High-flow priapism usually follows perineal (straddle) trauma. Aspirate is bright red like arterial blood; colour Doppler often shows the fistula. Observe first, as many resolve; embolise if it persists. <!-- c044-a14 c044-r21 -->
- Stuttering episodes (under 2 hours) can be treated with bedtime pseudoephedrine. <!-- c044-r22 -->

### Acute scrotum
- Delay in presentation comes from embarrassment, denial and poor public awareness. <!-- c044-r36 -->
- A careful history and examination give the diagnosis in most cases; ultrasound only confirms. <!-- c044-r37 -->
- Torsion risk relates to an abnormal tunica vaginalis (bell clapper), not a patent processus. Irreversible damage can begin 4 hours after cord occlusion. <!-- c044-a16 c044-a18 -->
- Ultrasound signs: absent flow, the "snail sign" (twisted cord) and heterogeneous parenchyma. A cord twist on high-resolution Doppler is the most specific sign. <!-- c044-a19 c044-r38 -->
- Salvage falls with duration and relates to the degree of twist, so do not delay surgery for imaging. <!-- c044-r39 -->
- Manual detorsion ("open book", outward) may be incomplete, and one third twist the opposite way. Exploration is still mandatory and detorsion must never delay theatre. <!-- c044-a20 -->
- Suspected intermittent torsion: elective exploration with bilateral fixation (a bell clapper is expected). <!-- c044-a21 -->
- Perinatal torsion is rarely salvageable when found in the nursery. A change after birth needs emergency exploration with contralateral fixation. <!-- c044-a24 c044-r40 -->
- Appendage torsion is the commonest acute scrotum before puberty. A "blue dot" may show at the upper pole. It is self-limiting: rest and analgesia. <!-- c044-a22 c044-r41 -->
- Infant epididymitis: most have sterile urine and normal urinary tracts on imaging. <!-- c044-a23 -->
- Abdominoscrotal hydrocele has a closed processus. It is linked to an elongated, dysmorphic testis, raised tunical pressure and hydronephrosis. Epididymal anomalies are not a typical association. <!-- c044-a17 -->

### Varicocele
- Prevalence is about 15% in adolescents and adults alike. It is commoner in tall thin boys (genetic tendency). <!-- c044-r23 c044-r24 -->
- Most are asymptomatic and found on examination; pain is the least common presentation. <!-- c044-a25 c044-r25 -->
- Testes should differ by no more than 15%–20% in volume; growth can be asynchronous in puberty. Ultrasound measures volume most accurately. <!-- c044-a26 c044-r26 -->
- Hypotrophy of 15%–20% may catch up after repair, or spontaneously. Grade and catch-up growth do not reliably predict semen quality. A poorer sperm trend may affect only a subset. <!-- c044-r27 c044-r28 -->
- Repair indications: hypotrophy of at least 15%–20% on serial yearly exams, significant pain, small total testicular volume, or oligospermia. Semen analysis is the best fertility indicator. Continuous reflux on Doppler alone is not an indication. <!-- c044-a28 -->
- Retroperitoneal (open or laparoscopic) and subinguinal repairs have similar success. Hydrocele is commonest after retroperitoneal mass ligation and least likely after transvenous embolisation. Lymphatic-sparing reduces it, and most post-repair hydroceles need surgery. <!-- c044-a27 c044-r29 -->

### Inguinal hernia and hydrocele
- Hernias occur in 1%–5% of children, more in boys and premature infants. The right side predominates (book 3:1, from later right descent); usually quoted as about 60% right, 30% left, 10% bilateral. Bilaterality is more likely in girls, prematurity, age under 1 year and cryptorchidism. <!-- c044-r30 -->
- An intermittent bulge separates hernia or communicating hydrocele from a scrotal or cord hydrocele. Parents' photographs are reliable. <!-- c044-r31 -->
- Repair hernias soon after diagnosis. Observe hydroceles, which often resolve. <!-- c044-r32 -->
- Approaches are inguinal (highest success), scrotal or laparoscopic. Transperitoneal laparoscopy inspects the contralateral ring quickly and accurately. <!-- c044-r33 c044-r35 -->
- Recurrence (0.5%–1%) follows a missed or poorly ligated sac, a torn sac, floor damage, a weak internal ring or a missed direct hernia. <!-- c044-r34 -->

## Ch 70 Priapism

### Types and causes
- Ischaemic priapism: a rigid erection with little or no cavernous arterial inflow. Progressive hypoxia, hypercarbia and acidosis occur; pain starts after 6–8 hours. The aspirate is dark, not bright red. <!-- c070-a01 c070-r01 -->
- Pathology: interstitial oedema by 12 hours, then endothelial loss and platelet adherence. By 48 hours there is sinusoidal thrombus and smooth muscle necrosis. Hypoxic smooth muscle stops contracting to α-agonists. <!-- c070-a07 -->
- Most ischaemic priapism is idiopathic. Associations: psychotropics, alcohol and drugs, blood dyscrasias, metastases, perineal trauma, intracavernous injection and SCD (sickle cell disease). <!-- c070-r05 -->
- In men over 40, intracavernous ED (erectile dysfunction) drugs are the usual cause. With alprostadil, prolonged erection (4–6 h) occurred in 5% and priapism (over 6 h) in 1%; with trimix programmes prolonged erections occur in 5%–35%. <!-- c070-a04 -->
- PDE5 (phosphodiesterase type 5) inhibitors rarely cause priapism, usually with other risks. Daily tadalafil caused none in trials, and no PDE5 inhibitor is more likely than another. Risk factors: SCD, spinal cord injury, recreational use, use with injections, penile trauma, psychotropics and narcotic abuse. <!-- c070-a05 c070-a24 c070-r06 c070-r17 -->
- Non-ischaemic (high-flow) priapism is much rarer. It usually follows trauma that lacerates the cavernous artery, most often a straddle injury to the crura. Other causes: coital trauma, kicks, pelvic fracture, birth trauma, needle injury and metastatic erosion (not haemodialysis). The corpora are tumescent but not rigid. <!-- c070-a02 c070-a06 c070-r04 -->
- Stuttering priapism: recurrent, unwanted, painful erections, usually in SCD. <!-- c070-r03 -->

### Pathophysiology in SCD
- HbS polymerises when deoxygenated. This causes haemolysis, vaso-occlusion and adhesion of sickle cells, endothelium and leukocytes. Free haemoglobin scavenges NO (nitric oxide), and arginase removes NO substrate. Decreased plasma HbS is not a mechanism. <!-- c070-a03 -->
- Haemolysis and low NO drive priapism, pulmonary hypertension, leg ulcers and stroke. Viscosity drives pain crises, osteonecrosis and acute chest syndrome. SCD priapism carries a fivefold risk of pulmonary hypertension, lower haemoglobin and higher haemolytic markers (reticulocytes, bilirubin, LDH, AST). <!-- c070-a21 -->
- Ischaemic and stuttering priapism reflect NO imbalance with PDE5 downregulation: low endothelial NO gives low basal cGMP, and so little PDE5. <!-- c070-a08 c070-r08 -->

### Evaluation
- Blood gas: ischaemic is hypoxic, hypercarbic and acidotic. Normal mixed venous values (e.g. PO2 40, PCO2 50, pH 7.35) after treatment with a turgid penis mean oedema, so observe. <!-- c070-a12 -->
- CDU (colour duplex ultrasound) is the most reliable, least invasive test. Ischaemic shows absent cavernous flow (the waveform returns with detumescence); high-flow shows normal or high velocities and may show the fistula. Scan the shaft and the perineum (crura) after trauma. <!-- c070-a10 c070-a11 -->
- Use CDU for a persisting erection after treatment: resolved ischaemia with oedema, persistent ischaemia, or conversion to high flow (a rare event). <!-- c070-r07 -->
- Arteriography is too invasive for diagnosis; keep it for embolisation. <!-- c070-a19 -->

### Treatment of ischaemic priapism
- First step: aspiration to decompress, repeated until bright red blood returns. It relieves pain and clears acidotic blood and cures about a third (36%). Irrigation is not proven better than aspiration alone. Oral agents have no role in acute priapism over 4 hours. <!-- c070-a09 c070-a23 c070-r09 -->
- If aspiration fails, inject phenylephrine 200 µg/mL in 0.5-mL aliquots (up to 1 mg in total). It is the preferred agent: α1-selective, with minimal cardiac β effects. Aspiration plus sympathomimetic is the AUA standard. <!-- c070-a13 c070-r10 -->
- SCD: hydration, alkalinisation and exchange transfusion are no longer first line (neurological complications; transfusion may be ineffective). Treat with aspiration and phenylephrine, with haematology input. Hydroxyurea raises HbF and NO. <!-- c070-a22 c070-r16 -->
- Shunt if medical treatment fails. All shunts reopen venous outflow so inflow returns. A distal cavernoglanular shunt comes first (easier; percutaneous under local anaesthetic is possible). If it fails, consider a proximal shunt. T-shunt with "snake" tunnelling works if under 24 hours, poorly after 48 hours. <!-- c070-a18 c070-r13 c070-r15 -->
- Function after reversal: 100% have erections if under 12 h, 78% at 12–24 h, 44% at 24–36 h, none after 36 h. Interventions after 48 h relieve pain but preserve little potency. <!-- c070-a17 c070-a25 c070-r02 -->
- Refractory or untreated priapism leads to fibrosis, length loss and complete ED (after 36–72 h). Early prosthesis preserves length and is easier than delayed implantation into fibrotic corpora. <!-- c070-a15 -->

### Stuttering priapism
- Aim: prevent episodes and preserve function. Options include daily oral sympathomimetic, daily PDE5 inhibitor (used in SCD) or self-injection of phenylephrine. GnRH agonists or antiandrogens are for adults only, not before full sexual maturity. <!-- c070-a16 c070-r12 -->

### Treatment of high-flow priapism
- It is not an emergency: observe, as about two thirds resolve spontaneously (up to 62%). Aspiration is diagnostic only. <!-- c070-a14 c070-r11 c070-r14 -->
- Embolise if the patient prefers after counselling on spontaneous resolution and on ED risk. Success is high but recurrence after one treatment is 30%–40%. Surgical ligation (under CDU guidance, after a pseudocapsule forms) if embolisation fails. <!-- c070-a20 -->

## Ch 87 Renal insufficiency and ischaemic nephropathy

### Acute renal failure
- ARF (acute renal failure; now AKI) is prerenal, intrarenal or postrenal. <!-- c087-r01 -->
- Prerenal: low renal blood flow or high nitrogen load. BUN-to-creatinine ratio over 10:1 (book), low urine volume, high osmolality and very low sodium. **Update:** usually quoted as above 20:1. <!-- c087-r02 -->
- Intrarenal: parenchymal disease of the glomerulus (acute GN), tubule (ATN, acute tubular necrosis) or interstitium (acute interstitial nephritis). Ratio about 10:1, variable output, urine sodium over 20 mEq/L (over 40 in ATN). <!-- c087-r03 -->
- Postrenal: obstruction of the whole nephron mass; ratio 10:1. Anuria suggests complete obstruction, cortical necrosis or bilateral vascular occlusion. <!-- c087-r04 -->
- A low FENa (fractional excretion of sodium) occurs in prerenal azotemia and in acute GN. The urinalysis separates them: bland in prerenal, protein and RBC casts in GN. <!-- c087-a07 c087-r26 -->
- ATN: renal blood flow falls 50% or more, worst in the outer medulla, with increased tubular permeability. ATP depletion is the sentinel event. The S3 proximal tubule and the medullary thick ascending limb are most injured. <!-- c087-a05 c087-a06 c087-r05 c087-r21 -->
- Angiotensin II constricts the efferent more than the afferent arteriole; prostaglandins dilate the afferent. ACE inhibitors, ARBs and NSAIDs can therefore cause ARF (efferent dilatation, loss of afferent dilatation). <!-- c087-a02 -->
- Creatinine and GFR are not linear: a rise from 0.9 to 1.2 mg/dL can mean GFR falling from 120 to 60 mL/min. <!-- c087-a01 -->
- Rhabdomyolysis (prolonged exaggerated lithotomy or lateral decubitus) causes myoglobin injury to the proximal tubule. Recognise it early and give forced alkaline diuresis. <!-- c087-a04 c087-r20 c087-r25 -->
- Urinary leak can be shown by drain fluid creatinine, IV indigo carmine or methylene blue, or imaging (isotope renography, RPG, cystogram, CT). A renal scan shows perfusion and extravasation. <!-- c087-a03 -->
- A bilateral persistent dense nephrogram at 24 hours after contrast means ATN (contrast nephropathy). Arterial occlusion is unilateral with absent nephrogram. <!-- c087-a26 -->

### Management of ARF
- Prerenal: restore circulating volume with isotonic fluid. <!-- c087-a08 -->
- Loop diuretics cut NaCl transport in the thick ascending limb, lowering metabolic demand. Dopamine is unproven. The book says mannitol before an ischaemic insult helps. **Update:** an RCT in partial nephrectomy showed no benefit from mannitol. <!-- c087-a09 c087-a11 c087-r06 -->
- Hyperkalaemia with ECG changes (peaked T, long PR, wide QRS): IV calcium first (immediate, short-lived), then insulin-glucose or bicarbonate, then removal with Kayexalate (risk of colonic necrosis) or dialysis. <!-- c087-a12 c087-r07 -->
- Dialysis indications: volume overload, severe hyperkalaemia, severe acidosis, pericarditis, some poisonings and uraemic symptoms. Peritoneal dialysis is gentler on haemodynamics. <!-- c087-r08 c087-r09 -->
- ATN mortality is about 50%; 5% of survivors need chronic dialysis. <!-- c087-r10 -->
- Contrast nephropathy: pre-hydrate with IV saline and use non-ionic contrast. Loop diuretic or mannitol adds nothing (Solomon). **Update:** PRESERVE found bicarbonate and N-acetylcysteine no better than saline. <!-- c087-a13 c087-r11 -->
- Gadolinium is not nephrotoxic, but it causes NSF (nephrogenic systemic fibrosis) in advanced CKD, which can be fatal. Use it only after careful thought. **Update:** the ACR allows group II agents when needed. <!-- c087-a10 c087-a24 c087-r27 -->

### Chronic kidney disease
- CKD is a GFR under 60 mL/min (book). **Update:** KDIGO: GFR under 60 or markers of damage (e.g. albuminuria), for over 3 months. <!-- c087-r12 -->
- An MDRD eGFR of about 52 after nephrectomy is stage 3 CKD: refer to a nephrologist and start protective measures. <!-- c087-a15 -->
- Causes: diabetes and hypertension most, then glomerular disease (FSGS, membranous), then systemic disease (SLE, Wegener). <!-- c087-a17 -->
- Lost nephron mass triggers hyperfiltration (high glomerular pressure), glomerular hypertrophy, sympathetic activation and interstitial fibrosis (not intrarenal vascular occlusion). Once GFR is under 50% of normal, decline continues even if the cause is gone. <!-- c087-a14 c087-r13 c087-r23 -->
- Fibrosis is promoted by many mediators; ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide) may oppose it. <!-- c087-a20 -->
- Progression risk depends on cause, GFR and albuminuria. A one-off loss after nephrectomy without albuminuria has the best prognosis. Albuminuria and a family history of ESRD (end-stage renal disease) predict decline. <!-- c087-a18 c087-r14 c087-r22 -->
- eGFR equations (Cockcroft-Gault, MDRD, CKD-EPI) are inaccurate with changing function, extremes of age or size, obesity, low muscle mass or advanced illness. Cystatin C may be better. Tubular creatinine secretion overestimates GFR at low levels; cimetidine and trimethoprim block it. <!-- c087-r15 c087-r16 c087-r18 -->
- Renal biopsy is needed for a definitive diagnosis when function is abnormal. <!-- c087-a19 -->
- ACE inhibitors and ARBs slow progression by haemodynamic and non-haemodynamic effects. They can reduce function in bilateral renal artery stenosis or ADPKD with cysts over 10 cm. <!-- c087-a16 c087-a21 c087-r19 c087-r28 -->

### ESRD
- Preemptive transplantation is the best treatment. <!-- c087-a22 -->
- In haemodialysis patients, access catheter infection is the commonest reason for admission. Sepsis mortality is one to several hundred times that of the general population. Remaining lifetime is 15%–25% of normal. <!-- c087-a23 c087-r17 c087-r24 -->
- Predictors of admission: low albumin and poor nutrition, low activity, diabetic ESRD, PVD, white race, age, CHF and inflammation (CRP). <!-- c087-a25 -->

## Ch 117 Overactive bladder

- Terms: "detrusor hyperreflexia" is now NDO (neurogenic detrusor overactivity); "detrusor instability" is not recommended. <!-- c117-a01 -->
- OAB (overactive bladder) is a symptom diagnosis: urgency, with or without urgency incontinence, usually with frequency and nocturia, without infection or other pathology. DO (detrusor overactivity) is urodynamic. The book says urgency plus one other symptom is essential. **Update:** in the ICS definition, urgency itself is the core symptom. <!-- c117-r01 c117-r03 -->
- Urgency is of rapid onset and may, but need not, lead to incontinence; urgency incontinence affects many OAB patients. Mixed incontinence is stress plus urgency incontinence. <!-- c117-a02 c117-a04 c117-a05 -->
- Prevalence is 10%–20% (16% in North American and European surveys, about 12% in EPIC). Over 50% have at least one LUTS. <!-- c117-a03 -->
- Contributors: ageing, neurological disease, female sex, BOO (bladder outlet obstruction) and the metabolic syndrome. <!-- c117-r08 -->
- DO causes may be neurogenic or myogenic. Neurogenic: reduced suprapontine inhibition, re-emerged spinal reflexes, new C-fibre reflexes (plasticity) and sensitised afferent terminals. Myogenic: spontaneous excitation from upregulated membrane receptors. <!-- c117-r04 -->
- DO means involuntary contractions during filling, of any size. Patients cannot suppress them voluntarily. <!-- c117-a06 c117-a07 c117-a08 -->
- The two main urodynamic findings are DO and increased filling sensation. Voided volumes are erratic. <!-- c117-r02 c117-r06 -->
- A voiding diary is essential. The ICIQ (International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire) is a modular, formally validated system. <!-- c117-a09 c117-r05 -->
- Treatment runs from conservative and lifestyle measures, to drugs, to nerve stimulation or bladder surgery for refractory cases. **Update:** AUA 2024 advises discussing dementia risk with antimuscarinics. <!-- c117-r07 -->

## Ch 124 Pelvic organ prolapse surgery

### Anatomy and principles
- The iliococcygeus fuses in the midline to the coccyx to form the levator plate, which supports the upper vagina and cervix. The book names coccygeus; DeLancey describes iliococcygeus plus the posterior pubococcygeus fibres. <!-- c124-r01 -->
- The urethra is fused to the anterior vaginal wall for most of its length. Endopelvic connective tissue supports the vagina. <!-- c124-r02 c124-r03 -->
- DeLancey levels: level I, cardinal and uterosacral ligaments (uterus and upper vagina); level II, endopelvic and pubocervical fascia to the arcus tendineus (mid vagina); level III, levator ani and perineal body (distal vagina). <!-- c124-r04 -->
- Prolapse is mainly a quality-of-life issue. Repair only symptomatic prolapse (e.g. Ba 0 with a bulge). Anterior defects are central, lateral or both. <!-- c124-a09 c124-r05 c124-r06 -->
- Screen all advanced anterior prolapse for occult SUI (stress urinary incontinence). <!-- c124-r16 -->
- Operative order: apex, anterior, sling, posterior. Do the sling before the posterior repair so a rectal injury can be fixed with the mesh uninvolved. <!-- c124-a05 -->
- If the bladder or rectum is injured, do not use mesh. <!-- c124-r09 -->

### Anterior and posterior repair
- Anterior colporrhaphy restores level II support but does not suspend the apex. Neither it nor paravaginal repair treats SUI. <!-- c124-a07 c124-a08 c124-r07 -->
- Stage 3 anterior prolapse with apical descent needs vault suspension plus anterior repair (anterior repair alone fails too often). Add no sling without demonstrated SUI, and no posterior repair if that compartment is intact. <!-- c124-a02 -->
- After anterior colporrhaphy, cystoscopy with indigo carmine or methylene blue is recommended routinely. If there is no efflux, catheterise or take down plication sutures. <!-- c124-a01 c124-r15 -->
- Traditional posterior colporrhaphy is preferred. Mesh and biological grafts add complications without better success. Levator plication causes de novo dyspareunia and is largely abandoned. <!-- c124-a03 c124-a06 -->
- Repair improves bulge, pressure, splinting and dyspareunia but not constipation (a dysmotility problem). <!-- c124-a04 -->

### Apical and uterine-sparing procedures
- Sacrospinous fixation corrects the apex but displaces it posteriorly, raising the risk of anterior prolapse. Gluteal or posterior-leg neuropathic pain occurs in 15%. <!-- c124-r08 c124-r12 c124-r17 -->
- Sacrospinous suture: the book says 1 cm lateral and slightly superior to the ischial spine. The pudendal nerve and vessels lie just behind the spine; the inferior gluteal vessels and hypogastric plexus lie above. **Update:** standard teaching places the suture 1.5–2 cm medial to the spine. <!-- c124-a21 -->
- Abdominal sacrocolpopexy maximises functional vaginal length without distorting the vaginal axis. A supracervical hysterectomy with sacrocervicopexy reduces mesh extrusion in a sexually active woman. <!-- c124-a18 c124-r13 -->
- Sacral osteomyelitis is a rare complication of sacrocolpopexy: back pain, fever, lethargy and discharge. <!-- c124-a20 -->
- Uterine sparing:
  - Abdominal sacrohysteropexy with mesh: for a woman wanting fertility and the most durable repair; also for connective tissue disorders and exstrophy (mesh from the lower uterus or cervix to S1–S2).
  - Manchester-Fothergill: for an elongated cervix with a well-supported corpus.
  - Sacrospinous hysteropexy: when abdominal access is hard (adhesions, previous hernia mesh).
  - Uterosacral hysteropexy (vaginal or laparoscopic): avoids mesh when the woman has had mesh problems.
  <!-- c124-a10 c124-a11 c124-a13 c124-a14 c124-a15 -->
- Colpocleisis obliterates part of the vagina and precludes intercourse. It has the lowest recurrence and reoperation rates. Le Fort (partial) colpocleisis can be done under local anaesthesia in frail women. <!-- c124-a12 c124-a22 c124-r14 -->

### Complications
- Synthetic mesh erodes in about 10%. Mesh raises reoperation rates compared with native tissue. After failed prolapse and incontinence surgery, 29% need reoperation. <!-- c124-a22 c124-r10 c124-r11 -->
- Posterior colporrhaphy has the highest dyspareunia rate (about 18%, range 0%–46%). Causes: poor lateral dissection, aggressive plication or skin trimming, a narrow introitus, scarring and atrophy. Sexual function usually improves after prolapse repair. Uterosacral suspension can entrap sacral nerves (1%–2%). <!-- c124-a16 -->
- Uterosacral suspension carries the highest ureteric injury risk (0%–11%). <!-- c124-a19 -->
- Positioning: sciatic injury from hip hyperflexion and external rotation with the knee extended (foot drop, calf and lateral foot numbness). Femoral injury from hip flexion, abduction and external rotation (weak quadriceps, anterior thigh numbness). Risks are BMI under 20, diabetes and each extra hour in lithotomy. <!-- c124-a17 -->

## Ch 138 Surgical management of bladder cancer

### TURBT
- Image the upper tracts before endoscopic treatment. <!-- c138-r01 -->
- Give routine antibiotic prophylaxis 30–60 minutes before TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour). <!-- c138-r12 -->
- Initial resection should include muscle, with a 2-cm visibly clear surface margin. <!-- c138-r02 -->
- Lateral wall tumours trigger the obturator reflex and perforation. Reduce it with low distension, bipolar current and paralysis under general anaesthesia. <!-- c138-r13 -->
- A ureteric orifice resected with cutting current does not routinely need a stent. <!-- c138-r14 -->
- A single immediate instillation of epirubicin or mitomycin C modestly reduces recurrence, not progression. BCG must never be instilled immediately after resection. <!-- c138-r03 c138-r04 -->

### Radical cystectomy
- Neoadjuvant MVAC (Grossman 2003, SWOG 8710) improved median survival from 46 to 77 months. **Update:** NIAGARA (2024) added perioperative durvalumab to cisplatin-gemcitabine. <!-- c138-a01 -->
- Staging upper tract urothelial cancer needs parenchymal and luminal imaging. PET/CT is limited inside the urinary tract (excreted tracer). <!-- c138-a02 -->
- Gadolinium (book): caution at GFR 30–60 and absolutely contraindicated below 30 because of NSF (the book calls it "sclerosis"). **Update:** the ACR allows group II agents when needed. <!-- c138-a03 -->
- Preparation: the stoma site is marked by a stoma nurse with the patient awake. Bowel preparation is controversial (the evidence is from general surgery, where bowel is not opened into the peritoneum). Give IV antibiotics 30–60 minutes before incision. In women, prep the vagina into the field. <!-- c138-r05 c138-r15 c138-r16 -->
- Standard node template: genitofemoral nerve laterally, internal iliac artery medially, Cooper ligament caudally, ureter crossing the common iliac cranially. Herr: more than 10 nodes gave about 15% (15 months) better survival. **Update:** LEA and SWOG S1011 showed no gain from extended dissection. <!-- c138-a04 c138-r10 -->
- Urethral recurrence is about 8% at 5 years. A positive frozen-section urethral margin contraindicates orthotopic diversion. <!-- c138-a05 -->
- Natural potency is lower than after prostatectomy. Prostate- or capsule-sparing improves function, but rule out occult urothelial or prostate cancer first (local recurrence risk). Capsule-sparing and nerve-sparing give similar function. <!-- c138-a06 c138-r06 -->
- Female anterior exenteration removes the whole urethra including the meatus but keeps the introitus. Vaginal capacity can be kept with either approach; no routine colpocleisis. **Update:** the urethra can be spared in selected women having orthotopic diversion. <!-- c138-a07 c138-r07 -->
- Sealing devices near the rectum can injure it by radiated heat. <!-- c138-r17 -->
- Partial cystectomy: a solitary lesion (e.g. 1-cm T2 at the dome) without CIS, a 2-cm margin, away from the orifices and neck. Results then approach radical cystectomy. <!-- c138-a08 c138-r08 -->
- Robotic vs open: RAZOR (2018) showed non-inferior 2-year progression-free survival. Robotic surgery reduced blood loss in RAZOR and the MSKCC trial (diversion done open). <!-- c138-a11 c138-a12 -->

### Recovery and complications
- Bowel recovery: early feeding, neostigmine and alvimopan help; routine nasogastric suction does not (use only with poor airway protection). <!-- c138-a09 c138-r18 -->
- Extended VTE (venous thromboembolism) prophylaxis for 4 weeks cut events from 4.6% to 0.8% (Kakkar 2010), in addition to pre-incision prophylaxis. <!-- c138-a10 -->
- 64% have a complication within 3 months (13% high grade), mostly gastrointestinal. 90-day mortality is about 3%. <!-- c138-r09 c138-r11 -->

## Ch 157 Radiation therapy for prostate cancer

### Risk and outcome markers
- Risk groups (D'Amico):

| Group | Stage | PSA (ng/mL) | Gleason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | T1c–T2a | under 10 (book "under 11") | 6 or less |
| Intermediate | T2b | 10–20 | 7 |
| High | T2c | over 20 | 8–10 |
<!-- c157-r14 -->
- Unfavourable intermediate risk (Zumsteg) includes primary Gleason pattern 4 (4+3), e.g. 4+3, PSA 12.8, cT2a. Percentage of positive cores and PSA velocity also stratify risk. <!-- c157-a02 c157-r15 -->
- Pretreatment PSA velocity over 2 ng/mL/yr predicts biochemical failure. High-risk disease treated with RT alone has about 33% 5-year PSA failure-free survival (D'Amico 1998). <!-- c157-a01 c157-r02 -->
- Failure definitions: ASTRO 1997 was three consecutive rises after nadir, 3–4 months apart. Phoenix (now standard) is nadir plus 2 ng/mL. <!-- c157-r03 -->
- PSA nadir is the strongest predictor; a nadir under 0.5 ng/mL means success. <!-- c157-r04 c157-r05 -->

### Techniques and doses
- CRT (conformal RT) shapes the dose to the prostate by computer. IMRT varies beam intensity across the field and spares rectum, bladder and bowel better. IMRT dose is 75–79 Gy. <!-- c157-r07 c157-r08 c157-r09 c157-r13 -->
- Over time came CT planning, then IMRT, then image guidance. Four RCTs showed dose escalation improves control. <!-- c157-a06 -->
- Protons and neutrons show the Bragg peak (sharp fall-off beyond range), but there is no proven advantage over IMRT. <!-- c157-r10 c157-r17 -->
- Brachytherapy has a rapid dose fall-off within millimetres. I-125 half-life is about 60 days (dose 140–160 Gy); Pd-103 is 17 days (110–125 Gy). <!-- c157-r01 c157-r11 c157-r13 -->
- Hypofractionation trial (Fox Chase, Pollack): 70.2 Gy in 26 fractions vs 76 Gy in 38 did not improve 5-year biochemical failure. Late urinary toxicity was higher in men with poor baseline function. **Update:** CHHiP and PACE-B support moderate and extreme hypofractionation. <!-- c157-a07 -->
- Adding RT to ADT (androgen deprivation therapy) for locally advanced disease improves overall survival. NCIC/MRC: 7-year OS 74% vs 66%. SPCG-7: 10-year mortality 29.6% vs 39.4%. <!-- c157-a05 -->
- Radium-223 (alpha emitter) prolongs survival in castration-resistant prostate cancer with bone metastases (ALSYMPCA). It spares the marrow more than strontium-89. <!-- c157-a08 c157-r18 -->

### Follow-up and complications
- PSA bounce after brachytherapy: classic at 12–30 months, in 65% of men under 55 (more if sexually active), and can mimic failure. Review the dosimetry, check PSA every 3 months and biopsy if no decline by 30 months. <!-- c157-a04 -->
- Early, fast PSA rise after IMRT plus ADT suggests distant failure. Stage systemically (with or without mpMRI) before any salvage or ADT. <!-- c157-a03 -->
- Radiation distorts histology; biopsies are interpretable 30 months after treatment. <!-- c157-r16 c157-r19 -->
- After conformal EBRT, about 50% of potent men are impotent at 5 years and 10% have rectal bleeding. Less often: urinary and faecal incontinence, haemorrhagic cystitis and stricture. <!-- c157-r06 -->
- After brachytherapy, contrast filling the rectum from the prostatic urethra means a urethrorectal fistula. <!-- c157-a09 -->
- Impending cord compression: immediate androgen ablation (orchiectomy is fastest), steroids and RT; occasionally emergency laminectomy. **Update:** avoid LHRH agonist flare (use orchiectomy or an antagonist). <!-- c157-r12 -->
