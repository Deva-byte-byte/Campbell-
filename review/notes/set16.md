## Ch 45 Hypospadias

### Definition and embryology
- Hypospadias is an ectopic urethral meatus below the centre of the glans. Most boys have no anomalies outside the genitourinary tract. <!-- c045-a01 -->
- About 10% are proximal; a bifid scrotum is typical of proximal hypospadias. <!-- c045-a01 -->
- Ventral curvature with a normally placed meatus is "chordee without hypospadias"; it can usually be corrected without urethral repair. <!-- c045-a01 -->
- The urethral groove forms between the urethral folds and becomes the urethra. The preputial folds fuse as the urethra closes, which explains the typical hypospadiac prepuce. <!-- c045-a03 -->
- DHT (dihydrotestosterone) at 8–12 weeks' gestation is the key mediator of penile development. <!-- c045-r01 -->

### Aetiology and risk
- The cause is multifactorial. Placental disruption and androgen disruption, partly from environmental endocrine-disrupting chemicals, may explain a rising incidence. <!-- c045-r03 c045-a04 -->
- A shorter AGD (anogenital distance) reflects disruption of this androgen cascade. <!-- c045-r03 c045-a04 -->
- Risk is higher after assisted reproduction (IVF, in-vitro fertilisation) and in monozygotic twins, partly from hormone disruption. <!-- c045-r02 c045-a04 -->
- TDS (testicular dysgenesis syndrome) links poor sperm quality, cryptorchidism and hypospadias to fetal androgen disruption. <!-- c045-a04 -->
- A brother of a boy with hypospadias has up to 15% risk, higher in twins. The overall risk stays small, so another son most likely will not be affected. <!-- c045-a31 -->

### Evaluation
- Examination may show a ventrally deficient prepuce, downward glans tilt, a deviated median raphe, ventral curvature, scrotal encroachment onto the shaft, a scrotal cleft and penoscrotal transposition. <!-- c045-r06 -->
- Grade the severity after degloving, using meatal position plus degree of curvature. Glans separation is common and does not correlate with severity. <!-- c045-a02 -->
- Hypospadias with an undescended testis may indicate a DSD (difference of sex development). This is commoner with a proximal meatus and a non-palpable gonad, but obtain a karyotype in any boy with hypospadias plus cryptorchidism. <!-- c045-a05 c045-r10 -->
- Isolated hypospadias, even proximal, needs neither karyotype nor urinary tract imaging; renal anomalies are rare in non-syndromic hypospadias. A karyotype can be offered for proximal cases. Proceed with surgery: delay does not increase penile growth. <!-- c045-a07 c045-a12 -->
- Hypospadias with aniridia suggests WAGR (Wilms tumour, aniridia, genital anomalies, retardation): do a renal ultrasound to screen for Wilms tumour. <!-- c045-a26 -->
- A glanular meatus with no stenosis and no curvature needs no surgery. Any curvature, even with a mildly displaced meatus, warrants repair. <!-- c045-a25 -->
- Counsel parents on early anaesthesia exposure versus the benefits of surgery in infancy. <!-- c045-r04 -->
- Curvature with penoscrotal webbing suggests a severely hypoplastic urethra that may need a two-stage repair. Even distal repairs have about a 10% complication rate, not 5%. <!-- c045-a12 -->

### Operative principles
- Preoperative androgen stimulation transiently increases penile length and glans width (a small glans raises complications). Its effect on complication rates is controversial. <!-- c045-r05 c045-a22 -->
- Side effects of androgen stimulation (more erections, thin pubic hair, aggression) are transient. It does not reduce adult penile size. <!-- c045-a22 -->
- An enlarged utricle, commoner in severe hypospadias, is the usual cause of difficult catheterisation. Do not excise it at primary repair. <!-- c045-a06 c045-a07 -->
- Dorsal penile nerves lie at 11 and 1 o'clock; a midline dorsal plication avoids them. A perineal block suits older ambulatory boys who would not tolerate leg weakness after a caudal block. <!-- c045-a08 -->
- Measure curvature by artificial erection after degloving. <!-- c045-r11 -->
- Curvature under 30° after degloving: a single midline dorsal plication, without clinically apparent shortening. Even 15° should be corrected, or a second operation becomes likely. <!-- c045-a10 c045-r11 -->
- Curvature over 30° after degloving and dartos dissection: urethral plate transection and/or ventral corporal lengthening (grafting). <!-- c045-a10 c045-r11 -->
- Chordee without hypospadias usually comes from short ventral skin and dartos; it corrects with degloving and ventral dartos dissection. Avoid multiple plications. <!-- c045-a11 -->
- Severe curvature (e.g. 50°) after plate division needs a two-stage repair with corporal lengthening (dermal graft, corporotomies or tunica vaginalis flap). Dorsal plication alone risks recurrence. <!-- c045-a29 -->
- MIP (megameatus intact prepuce) is a hypospadias variant, not a circumcision injury. Tubularisation of local skin flaps usually suffices. <!-- c045-a13 -->
- After foreskin reconstruction, do not retract the foreskin for 6 weeks. Complication rates match circumcision. Do not compromise the repair to preserve the prepuce. <!-- c045-a14 -->
- Suture type does not change outcome. Subepithelial neourethral suturing and extra vascularised layers (barrier flaps, tunica vaginalis) do help. <!-- c045-a15 c045-a14 -->
- Risk factors for urethroplasty complications: proximal meatus, reoperation, glans width under 14 mm, no barrier flap. <!-- c045-r08 -->

### Complications and reoperation
- Urethrocutaneous fistula is the commonest complication. Fistulas rarely, if ever, close with catheter drainage. <!-- c045-r14 c045-a30 -->
- Fistula with supple tissue and good glans: primary closure plus a ventral dartos barrier flap. Thin glans tissue, a coronal or distal fistula, or a fistula close to the meatus: redo distal urethroplasty and glansplasty. <!-- c045-a18 c045-r14 -->
- Always look for distal obstruction (meatal stenosis) with a fistula; treat it by reoperation, not dilatation. <!-- c045-a18 -->
- Meatal stenosis after TIP (tubularised incised plate) repair is usually from suturing the plate too far distally into the glans. <!-- c045-a23 -->
- After Thiersch-Duplay repair the meatus may look small. Calibrate it: an 8–10 Fr sound passes if it is not stenotic. Examine under anaesthesia only if symptomatic (UTI, straining, prolonged stream). <!-- c045-a24 -->
- Urethral diverticulum follows Byars or preputial onlay flaps: prolonged stream and post-void dribbling, ventral ballooning. Calibrate the meatus. Repair with de-epithelialised redundant tissue as extra layers. It does not cause erectile dysfunction. <!-- c045-a16 c045-a30 -->
- Distal narrowing with slow stream and high PVR (post-void residual): dorsal inlay graft (buccal or skin). DVIU (direct vision internal urethrotomy) succeeds under 10% even with CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation); if the inlay fails, do a staged buccal repair. <!-- c045-a20 -->
- BXO (balanitis xerotica obliterans, lichen sclerosus) after repair (white meatus, stenosis): meatotomy and skin flaps fail. Do cystoscopy to find the proximal extent, excise all affected tissue and do a staged buccal graft repair. <!-- c045-a17 c045-r12 -->
- Office biopsy for BXO is not advised. Topical steroids help only temporarily. Failure rate is up to 40%, so follow closely. <!-- c045-a17 c045-r12 -->
- Scarred tissue is a relative contraindication to TIP or flip-flap redo. With webbing and skin shortage: excise the scar, do a two-stage buccal graft, and recheck curvature. <!-- c045-a19 -->
- Use lip mucosa in the glans (thinner, fewer complications) and cheek mucosa on the shaft. Excise flat glans skin to make a deep groove. <!-- c045-a21 c045-r13 -->
- Haematoma under adjacent skin or scrotum after a buccal graft: observe with bed rest. Quilting and tie-over dressings prevent haematoma under the graft. <!-- c045-a28 -->
- Late penile deviation toward a tunica vaginalis flap means it was not mobilised to the external ring. Transect it through a small scrotal incision. <!-- c045-a27 -->
- Recurrent UTI after repair: do a VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) for stricture, new reflux or poor emptying; then cystoscopy if abnormal. <!-- c045-a09 -->
- Recurrent curvature: diagnose and treat aggressively; plication alone often fails. <!-- c045-a30 -->

### Long-term outcome
- 80% of complications appear within 1 year, but stricture and curvature can appear late, so follow boys (especially proximal) into puberty. <!-- c045-r07 -->
- Adults after repair report more ejaculatory problems, less satisfaction with sexual function and with penile appearance. Patient-reported outcomes need more study. <!-- c045-r09 -->

## Ch 49 Surgery for DSD, cloacal and anorectal malformations

### Urogenital sinus and CAH
- UGS (urogenital sinus) anomalies are commonest in DSD, mainly CAH (congenital adrenal hyperplasia); mild non-classic CAH can be as frequent as 1 in 500. <!-- c049-a02 -->
- The vagina usually joins the urinary tract at the mid to distal urethra. <!-- c049-r01 -->
- In CAH the level of the vaginal–urethral confluence is the critical determinant of surgery. <!-- c049-r02 -->
- Hydrometrocolpos (urine draining into a poorly drained vagina) is often the first sign of a UGS anomaly. It is a cystic pelvic mass between bladder and rectum. <!-- c049-r03 c049-a03 -->
- Persistent clitoral hypertrophy can occur in premature infants without DSD. <!-- c049-r04 -->
- A cervical impression at the vaginal dome on genitography means normal female internal organs. <!-- c049-r05 -->
- Neonatal gonadal biopsy should be deep, because the ovarian part of an ovotestis may cover the testicular part. <!-- c049-r06 -->
- Adult women with CAH are less satisfied with their genitalia than controls. <!-- c049-r08 -->

### Feminising genitoplasty
- Reconstruction addresses clitoroplasty, labiaplasty and vaginoplasty. <!-- c049-r09 -->
- Clitoroplasty preserves the glans, tunics and neurovascular bundles; do not mobilise the bundles. <!-- c049-r10 -->
- Timing is controversial. A single-stage repair (vaginoplasty with clitoroplasty and labiaplasty) uses redundant phallic skin, and maternal oestrogen thickens and vascularises vaginal tissue. Less scarring is claimed; less stenosis is not proven. <!-- c049-r11 c049-a05 -->
- Neonatal vaginoplasty usually needs a secondary procedure after puberty. <!-- c049-r14 -->

| Confluence/vagina | Procedure |
|---|---|
| Simple labial fusion | Cut-back (rarely used) |
| Low confluence | Posterior perineal flap vaginoplasty |
| High confluence | Pull-through vaginoplasty |
| Absent or rudimentary vagina | Vaginal replacement |
<!-- c049-r12 c049-a06 -->

- The flap in a flap vaginoplasty must reach normal-calibre vagina, above the narrowed distal segment. <!-- c049-r13 -->
- McIndoe (skin graft) vaginoplasty has more stenosis and needs dilatation; bowel vagina has less stenosis. <!-- c049-a01 -->
- Transverse vaginal septum: determine whether there is a cervix and exactly where it lies. <!-- c049-r19 -->
- Vaginal atresia, unlike vaginal agenesis and testicular feminisation, spares Müllerian structures: uterus, cervix and upper vagina are normal. <!-- c049-r20 -->

### Cloaca
- Hendren's 154 cloacas: 66 one vagina, 68 two vaginas, 20 absent vagina. The uterus usually mirrors the vagina (two vaginas, two uteri). <!-- c049-a04 -->
- Renal and spinal cord anomalies are common. <!-- c049-r07 c049-r17 -->
- Steps: decompress the gut (usually colostomy); decompress the urinary tract; correct nephron-destroying or lethal urinary anomalies; later, single-stage definitive repair of rectum, vagina and urethra. Vaginostomy is not a standard step. <!-- c049-a07 c049-r15 -->
- Intermittent catheterisation of the vagina can decompress the genitourinary tract. <!-- c049-r16 -->
- After repair, many have a neuropathic component to urinary and faecal incontinence; faecal continence depends on neurological status. <!-- c049-r18 c049-a08 -->

## Ch 86 Renal physiology and renovascular hypertension

### GFR
- GFR (glomerular filtration rate) reflects total renal function and is approximated by creatinine clearance. <!-- c086-r01 c086-r02 -->
- Formulas using age, weight and creatinine estimate GFR best. **Update:** CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) is now the standard equation. <!-- c086-r03 -->
- Plasma creatinine is not sensitive to early GFR loss. Inulin clearance is accurate but impractical; 24-hour creatinine clearance overestimates GFR by 10%–20%; the MDRD (Modification of Diet in Renal Disease) formula improves accuracy. <!-- c086-a11 -->
- GFR is regulated by afferent arteriolar tone, distal tubular chloride (tubuloglomerular feedback), angiotensin II and nitric oxide, not serum osmolality. <!-- c086-a10 -->
- Renal vascular tone: the RAS (renin-angiotensin system) is one of several mediators. Endothelin is the most potent vasoconstrictor; nitric oxide and carbon monoxide are potent vasodilators. <!-- c086-r20 c086-r21 c086-r22 -->

### Tubule
- Each nephron segment has a distinct homeostatic role. <!-- c086-r04 -->
- The PCT (proximal convoluted tubule) reabsorbs about 65% of sodium (the most of any segment) and most bicarbonate and other ions. <!-- c086-a12 c086-r05 c086-a06 -->
- The thin ascending limb reabsorbs NaCl passively and is water-impermeable. The book adds urea, but urea enters the thin limbs from the medulla (urea recycling). <!-- c086-a13 -->
- Loop of Henle architecture builds a hypertonic interstitium, essential for maximal concentration. <!-- c086-r06 -->

### Water and sodium
- Water movement sets solute concentration, especially sodium. It depends on ADH (antidiuretic hormone), which inserts aquaporin channels. <!-- c086-r13 c086-r14 c086-r15 -->
- Judge sodium disorders by volume status; history, volume status and urine sodium best show the cause. <!-- c086-r07 c086-r08 -->
- Normal compensation for hyponatraemia is lower ADH secretion and suppressed thirst. <!-- c086-a02 -->
- Correct severe sodium deficit or excess slowly. <!-- c086-r09 -->
- Desmopressin treats hypernatraemia due to diabetes insipidus. <!-- c086-a03 -->

### Potassium
- Potassium is mainly intracellular. Serum K reflects total body K and the intra/extracellular balance. <!-- c086-r10 c086-r11 -->
- ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors cause hyperkalaemia, not hypokalaemia. <!-- c086-a04 -->
- Symptomatic hyperkalaemia needs urgent treatment to protect the heart. Mild, chronic hyperkalaemia (e.g. K above 5.5 without ECG changes) does not need emergency therapy. <!-- c086-r12 c086-a05 -->

### Acid–base
- Body chemistry needs a narrow pH range. Acid is excreted by lungs and kidneys. <!-- c086-r16 c086-r17 -->
- First decide whether kidney (HCO3−) or lung (pCO2) is primary, then whether compensation is appropriate. <!-- c086-r19 -->
- Type 1 (distal) RTA (renal tubular acidosis) is the only type with stones. Type 4 RTA causes hyperkalaemia (aldosterone deficiency or resistance lowers distal K secretion). <!-- c086-r18 c086-a07 -->

### Hormones and bone
- Low oxygen tension increases erythropoiesis. <!-- c086-a01 -->
- Kidney hormones influence bone. Skin cholecalciferol is hydroxylated at the 25-position (liver) and 1-position (kidney) to calcitriol, 100 times more potent, which maintains calcium and phosphate. <!-- c086-r23 c086-a08 c086-r24 -->
- PTH (parathyroid hormone) increases renal calcium reabsorption and increases phosphate excretion. <!-- c086-r25 c086-a09 -->

### Renovascular hypertension
- Renal artery stenosis is the commonest cause; atherosclerosis causes about two-thirds. <!-- c086-r26 c086-r27 -->
- FMD (fibromuscular dysplasia) has four types; medial fibroplasia is commonest and least likely to progress. <!-- c086-r28 -->
- Suspect it with severe or abrupt-onset hypertension without risk factors. <!-- c086-r29 -->
- Goldblatt dog models: two-kidney one-clip (angiotensin-dependent, because the contralateral kidney allows natriuresis) and one-kidney one-clip (volume-dependent). <!-- c086-r30 c086-a15 -->
- Screen non-invasively with MR angiography, CT angiography or duplex ultrasound. <!-- c086-r31 -->
- Medical therapy is the mainstay. CORAL found stenting no better than medical therapy. <!-- c086-r32 c086-a16 -->
- **Update:** CORAL's medical arm used candesartan (an angiotensin receptor blocker) ± thiazide, amlodipine, atorvastatin and antiplatelet; the book says ACE inhibitor. <!-- c086-a14 -->
- Stable CKD on RAS blockade and diuretic: no further imaging or angiography. Gadolinium in significant renal failure risks NSF (nephrogenic systemic fibrosis). <!-- c086-a14 -->
- Percutaneous intervention may work, but evidence questions long-term BP or renal benefit. Surgery is no longer first line but suits selected patients. <!-- c086-r33 c086-r34 -->
- Surgical revascularisation does better when collaterals fill the distal renal artery. Kidneys under 8 cm do poorly. Most patients have vascular disease elsewhere. Aortic aneurysm repair need not be simultaneous. Ostial lesions may be stented. <!-- c086-a17 -->
- Ischaemic nephropathy is driven by proinflammatory cytokines and/or angiotensin II, not by failed autoregulation alone. Flow may be preserved, collaterals are common, and it can reverse. <!-- c086-a18 -->

## Ch 106 Adrenal disorders

### Anatomy and physiology
- The right adrenal is triangular, the left crescentic. <!-- c106-r01 -->
- The neonatal gland has fetal and adult components, weighs twice the adult gland and atrophies after birth. The zona reticularis develops last, by 3 years. <!-- c106-a01 -->
- With renal agenesis the adrenal is usually in its normal place; adrenal agenesis is very rare. <!-- c106-a03 -->
- Testicular adrenal rests occur in CAH; remember them to avoid needless orchidectomy. <!-- c106-a02 -->
- Zona glomerulosa makes mineralocorticoids (its sole source of aldosterone); fasciculata makes glucocorticoids; reticularis makes sex steroids. <!-- c106-r02 c106-a05 -->
- Output per day: adrenal androgens over 20 mg (most abundant), cortisol 10–20 mg, aldosterone 100–150 µg. Adrenal androgens are ACTH-controlled. <!-- c106-a04 c106-r04 -->
- Aldosterone is regulated mainly by angiotensin II and potassium; ACTH is a weak stimulus, so the glomerulosa does not atrophy in pituitary failure. <!-- c106-a06 c106-r21 -->
- Cortisol is circadian: peak early morning, nadir 11 pm. <!-- c106-r03 -->
- Renin is released with low renal perfusion pressure, renal sympathetic activity and low sodium. <!-- c106-r06 -->
- Mineralocorticoid excess first retains sodium and expands volume; the kidney then "escapes". <!-- c106-r07 -->
- PNMT (phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase) converts noradrenaline to adrenaline. It is essentially confined to the adrenal medulla (also brain and organ of Zuckerkandl), so an adrenaline-dominant tumour suggests adrenal origin. <!-- c106-a07 c106-r22 -->
- COMT (catechol-O-methyltransferase) makes metanephrine (from adrenaline) and normetanephrine (from noradrenaline), together "metanephrines". "Free" means not sulphate-conjugated; "total" includes conjugated; "fractionated" means reported separately. <!-- c106-a08 c106-a09 -->

### Cushing syndrome
- Cushing disease (pituitary ACTH, adrenocorticotropic hormone) is about 70% of endogenous Cushing syndrome. Ectopic ACTH almost always comes from malignant tissue. <!-- c106-a10 c106-r23 c106-r05 -->
- Stones occur in up to 50%; investigate cushingoid stone formers. Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism also occurs. <!-- c106-a11 c106-r24 -->
- Overnight 1-mg dexamethasone test: 1 mg at 11 pm, cortisol next morning; book cut-off 5 µg/dL or more. **Update:** current cut-off is above 1.8 µg/dL. <!-- c106-a12 -->
- Oral contraceptives raise cortisol-binding globulin and total cortisol, giving up to 50% false positives. <!-- c106-a34 c106-a12 -->
- Transsphenoidal surgery cures 60%–80%, with about 25% relapse. Bilateral adrenalectomy for refractory cases needs an MDT decision: Nelson syndrome (enlarging pituitary adenoma, raised pressure, chiasm compression) in up to 30%. <!-- c106-a13 c106-r25 -->

### Primary aldosteronism
- Only 9%–37% are hypokalaemic at diagnosis. <!-- c106-a14 c106-r26 -->
- Familial hyperaldosteronism type I: a chimeric 11β-hydroxylase promoter/aldosterone synthase gene makes aldosterone ACTH-driven. <!-- c106-a15 -->
- 50%–70% with a positive screen are confirmed. Idiopathic hyperplasia is about 60%. <!-- c106-a16 c106-a17 -->
- Stop spironolactone and eplerenone for at least 6 weeks before testing. <!-- c106-a19 -->
- Sodium loading suppresses renin and aldosterone unless secretion is autonomous. <!-- c106-r08 -->
- Lateralisation is the main determinant of surgical success. AVS (adrenal vein sampling): cortisol gradients confirm placement; book example ratio 2.5:1 lateralises left, so counsel left adrenalectomy. **Update:** a lateralisation index of 4:1 (with cosyntropin) is the usual cut-off. <!-- c106-a18 c106-a38 -->
- Persistent hypertension after adrenalectomy is predicted by age over 50, more than two drugs, a hypertensive first-degree relative, long duration and renal insufficiency. <!-- c106-r09 -->

### Phaeochromocytoma
- About 5% of incidentalomas are phaeochromocytomas; incidental lesions are 10%–25% of all phaeos. <!-- c106-a20 -->

| Syndrome | Phaeo frequency | Malignant if phaeo |
|---|---|---|
| MEN 2A/2B (RET) | about 50% | about 3% |
| VHL | 10%–20% | 5% |
| NF1 | 1% | over 10% |
| SDHB (PGL4) | about 20% | 30%–50% (highest) |
| SDHD (PGL1) | about 20% | under 3% |
<!-- c106-a22 -->

- VHL types: 1 no phaeo; 2A phaeo with low RCC risk; 2B phaeo with high RCC risk; 2C phaeo only, no other VHL stigmata. The book wrongly says 2B has no RCC. <!-- c106-a21 -->
- Test plasma free or fractionated urinary metanephrines: conversion is continuous within the tumour, so it is more sensitive than paroxysmal catecholamines, and fairly specific. Serum chromogranin A has been used to confirm. <!-- c106-a23 c106-r27 c106-r11 -->
- Book: PET is the preferred imaging. **Update:** CT or MRI first; functional imaging (Ga-68 DOTATATE PET, MIBG) for staging or metastases. <!-- c106-r18 -->
- Preparation: restoring intravascular volume is most important. α-blockade first; β-blockade only after, for reflex tachycardia or arrhythmia (β-blockade alone leaves α1 vasoconstriction unopposed). <!-- c106-r12 c106-a24 -->
- Up to 25% of apparently sporadic phaeos carry germline mutations; book: test under 50. **Update:** offer genetic testing to all. <!-- c106-a39 -->
- Recheck metanephrines about 2 weeks after resection, again at 6 months, then yearly for life. Recurrence is up to 16% (over 15% within 10 years, some after 15 years), and 50% of recurrences are malignant. <!-- c106-a39 c106-r19 -->

### Adrenal insufficiency
- Commonest cause: autoimmune adrenalitis in the USA, tuberculosis in developing countries. <!-- c106-r13 -->
- Crisis mimics an acute abdomen; children may have hypoglycaemic seizures; priapism is not a feature. <!-- c106-a25 -->

### Other masses
- All lesions except an aldosteronoma can be extra-adrenal (myelolipoma, ganglioneuroma, phaeo, oncocytoma). <!-- c106-a26 -->
- Ganglioneuroma: rare, often calcified, may secrete VIP (vasoactive intestinal polypeptide) causing profound diarrhoea; most are silent. <!-- c106-a27 c106-r28 -->
- Myelolipoma (fat with haematopoietic elements) is benign: no treatment. <!-- c106-a40 -->
- Adrenal cysts: pseudocyst, endothelial, epithelial, parasitic; 7% have associated malignancy. <!-- c106-r17 c106-a28 -->
- CAH carries a high risk of benign adrenocortical adenomas. Metastases to the adrenal are common. <!-- c106-r14 c106-r20 -->
- Every adrenal mass needs testing for cortisol and catecholamine excess (and aldosterone if hypertensive): late-night salivary cortisol, plasma aldosterone and renin activity, plasma free metanephrines. <!-- c106-r29 c106-a36 -->
- Even a likely RCC metastasis to the adrenal needs functional testing; a phaeo may be lurking. <!-- c106-a37 -->
- Under 10 HU on unenhanced CT strongly suggests adenoma. <!-- c106-a42 c106-r16 -->

### Adrenocortical carcinoma
- Up to 74% of functional tumours secrete cortisol. <!-- c106-a30 -->
- Weiss criteria: 3 or more suggest malignancy; use with caution in children and oncocytic tumours. Ki-67 is perhaps the best marker. <!-- c106-r15 c106-a31 c106-r10 -->
- Marked atypia, mitoses and Ki-67 positivity mean carcinoma: no more pathology needed; follow with short-interval imaging and give mitotane if metastases appear. <!-- c106-a41 -->
- Children survive better: 5-year 54% vs 20%–47% in adults. <!-- c106-a29 -->
- Complete resection gives the best chance of cure. Adjuvant RT lowers local recurrence without OS gain; functional status does not consistently affect survival. <!-- c106-a33 -->
- Book: adjuvant mitotane improves RFS and OS. **Update:** ADIUVO (2023) showed no benefit in low-to-intermediate risk. <!-- c106-a33 -->
- Metastatic: mitotane alone or with cytotoxics. **Update:** FIRM-ACT showed EDP (etoposide, doxorubicin, cisplatin) plus mitotane beat streptozotocin plus mitotane for PFS, without clear OS gain. <!-- c106-a32 -->
- Radical nephrectomy need not include adrenalectomy, except for large (T2) upper-pole tumours, an abnormal gland on imaging, or thrombus at adrenal level. <!-- c106-a35 -->

## Ch 114 Urodynamics

### Indications
- Level-1 indications are lacking. Use UDS (urodynamic studies) when there is a clear question and the answer will guide treatment; it matters most in neuropathic dysfunction and may be unnecessary in pure SUI (stress urinary incontinence) seen clinically. <!-- c114-a01 -->
- Male obstruction nomograms exist, but indications in men are no clearer than in women. <!-- c114-a01 -->
- The AUA/SUFU guideline helps select tests after evaluation; it is mostly based on lower-level evidence and expert opinion. <!-- c114-a02 -->
- In pure or stress-predominant SUI with normal emptying, UDS has not beaten office evaluation in RCTs or predicted surgical outcome. It is considered useful with urgency incontinence or emptying problems before surgery. <!-- c114-a13 -->
- UDS is done in an unnatural setting and may not reflect daily activity. <!-- c114-r01 -->

### Flow, filling and compliance
- Normal uroflow is bell-shaped. <!-- c114-r02 -->
- CMG (cystometrogram) measures bladder pressure during filling. Compliance = change in volume over change in pressure; mean 40–120 mL/cmH2O. <!-- c114-a03 c114-r05 -->
- Pdet (detrusor pressure) = Pves − Pabd and stays near zero during filling. Rectal or vaginal catheters measure Pabd and best show abdominal straining. <!-- c114-a04 c114-a12 -->
- Compliance is hard to interpret, so filling pressures are used more to predict outcome. Sustained Pdet over 40 cmH2O damages the upper tracts. <!-- c114-r06 c114-r08 -->
- VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) acts as a pop-off and makes compliance look better than it is. Outlet obstruction, radiation and TB (tuberculosis) truly reduce compliance. Fast filling and involuntary contractions make it look worse. Catheter type does not matter. <!-- c114-a10 -->
- DO (detrusor overactivity) is a urodynamic observation of involuntary contractions; it occurs in asymptomatic people. OAB (overactive bladder) is a symptom diagnosis. Poor compliance, not DO, threatens the kidneys. <!-- c114-a05 -->

### Urethral function and leak points
- ALPP (abdominal leak point pressure): intravesical pressure at leakage from raised abdominal pressure. DLPP (detrusor leak point pressure): detrusor pressure at leakage without contraction or straining, used in poor compliance or lower motor neuron disease. <!-- c114-r07 -->
- MUCP (maximum urethral closure pressure) = peak urethral minus intravesical pressure, normally 40–60 cmH2O. <!-- c114-r09 -->
- ALPP and MUCP do not reliably define ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency) or predict outcome, and are not used alone to grade severity (ICS, International Continence Society). If UDS is done before SUI surgery, AUA/SUFU advises measuring one. <!-- c114-a09 -->
- Functional classification: SUI is failure to store due to an underactive outlet. <!-- c114-a11 -->
- Occult (latent) SUI appears only when prolapse is reduced. <!-- c114-r14 -->

### Voiding and obstruction
- Obstruction = high-pressure, low-flow voiding, with or without residual; impaired contractility may follow. <!-- c114-a06 -->
- BOOI (bladder outlet obstruction index) = PdetQmax − 2Qmax: over 40 obstructed, under 20 unobstructed. <!-- c114-r10 -->
- Women: Qmax under 12 mL/s with Pdet over 25 cmH2O suggests obstruction. <!-- c114-r11 -->

### Sphincter and EMG
- The external sphincter contracts progressively with filling and during involuntary contractions (guarding reflex). It relaxes first, before the detrusor contracts. <!-- c114-a07 c114-r17 -->
- DESD (detrusor external sphincter dyssynergia) needs a neurological lesion above the sacral micturition centre; a learned version is dysfunctional voiding (intermittent flow in a neurologically normal person). <!-- c114-a07 c114-r12 -->
- Patch EMG (electromyography) electrodes assume perineal muscle reflects the sphincter; needle electrodes are needed for the sphincter itself. <!-- c114-r03 c114-r04 -->

### Video-urodynamics
- VUDS (video-urodynamics) is the procedure of choice for bladder neck dysfunction. Internal sphincter dyssynergia needs VUDS and a cord lesion above sympathetic outflow (T10–L1). <!-- c114-a08 c114-r16 c114-r13 c114-r15 -->
- VUDS helps in complex or neuropathic dysfunction, unexplained retention in women, after radical pelvic surgery, diversion, transplant or pelvic radiation. It is not needed to diagnose DO. <!-- c114-a08 -->

## Ch 134 Urology in transgender individuals

- The urologist has an important role in treatment and follow-up. <!-- c134-r01 -->
- "Gender dysphoria" is the ICD/DSM term (the book's ICD sense). **Update:** DSM-5 uses gender dysphoria; ICD-11 uses "gender incongruence"; "gender identity disorder" is obsolete. <!-- c134-a09 -->
- It can be diagnosed in childhood, is commoner in birth-assigned males, and is not due to hormonal imbalance. Psychotherapy as "treatment" is no longer recommended. <!-- c134-a11 -->

### Transwomen
- Urinary incontinence is the commonest complication. <!-- c134-r02 -->
- The prostate remains: be alert to prostate disease. The neovagina lies between rectum and prostate, so examine the prostate transvaginally. <!-- c134-r03 c134-a10 -->

### Transmen
- Pre-op assessment: urological history, urinary tract ultrasound and uroflowmetry. <!-- c134-a05 -->
- Discuss whether to perform vaginectomy; rectal injury occurs in about 0.9%. <!-- c134-r04 c134-a03 -->
- Scrotum is made from the labia majora (embryological homologue). <!-- c134-a04 -->
- Overall complication rate of phalloplasty is about 40%. Urethral fistula is the commonest urological complication; fistulas and strictures occur mostly at the anastomosis between the fixed and phallic urethra. <!-- c134-a07 c134-a01 c134-a02 c134-r05 -->
- Catheterisation is hard because of the sharp curve between the native urethra and the pars fixa. <!-- c134-a06 -->
- Metoidioplasty is an alternative for those unwilling to accept phalloplasty complications. <!-- c134-r06 -->
- Erectile prosthesis: implant about 1 year after phalloplasty, when nerve anastomosis gives sensation. Complications are higher than in native males. <!-- c134-a08 c134-r07 -->

## Ch 161 Hormonal therapy for prostate cancer

### Mechanisms
- All ADT (androgen deprivation therapy) lowers androgen or blocks its binding; the AR (androgen receptor) itself is not directly targeted, so CRPC (castration-resistant prostate cancer) is thought to be AR reactivation. <!-- c161-a03 -->
- The AR stays responsive in CRPC, so continue ADT. <!-- c161-r10 -->
- Oestrogen acts mainly by negative feedback on LH (luteinising hormone); oestradiol is 1000 times more potent than testosterone at suppressing LH and FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone). <!-- c161-a01 c161-r14 -->
- Aminoglutethimide blocks cholesterol to pregnenolone. <!-- c161-a01 -->
- Antiandrogens bind the AR competitively and are steroidal or non-steroidal. <!-- c161-r01 -->
- Steroidal (cyproterone acetate) also suppress LH centrally (progestational). It causes fluid retention and thromboembolism. <!-- c161-r02 c161-a04 -->
- Non-steroidal agents block central ARs too, so LH, testosterone and oestrogen all rise, causing gynaecomastia and mastodynia. <!-- c161-a02 c161-r15 c161-r02 -->
- Antiandrogen monotherapy aims to preserve potency, but long-term sexual function is only about 20%, no better than castration. <!-- c161-a04 -->
- All antiandrogens can act as agonists; withdrawal can lower PSA and cause responses. <!-- c161-a04 c161-r16 -->
- Flutamide causes diarrhoea most. All non-steroidals can cause hepatitis up to fulminant failure: monitor liver tests. <!-- c161-a04 -->
- Nilutamide: delayed dark adaptation in about a quarter; interstitial pneumonitis in about 1%. <!-- c161-a05 -->
- LHRH (luteinising hormone-releasing hormone) agonists desensitise pituitary receptors. Early agents needed daily dosing; analogues are longer acting. All are given IM or SC. <!-- c161-a06 c161-r17 -->
- The testosterone flare lasts 10–20 days. Start an antiandrogen together with the agonist and continue it about 3 weeks (21–28 days). <!-- c161-a06 c161-r04 -->
- Survival with LHRH agonists equals orchidectomy. The antagonist abarelix caused severe allergic reactions. **Update:** relugolix is an oral GnRH antagonist. <!-- c161-a06 -->
- Bilateral orchidectomy lowers testosterone by 90% within 24 hours. Double-ligate each cord, one with a transfixion suture. <!-- c161-r09 c161-r03 -->
- Ketoconazole, aminoglutethimide and abiraterone need glucocorticoid replacement. Aminoglutethimide also needs mineralocorticoid. Abiraterone raises mineralocorticoids, causing hypokalaemia and hypertension. <!-- c161-r11 -->
- Cost: DES (diethylstilbestrol) 1–3 mg/day without breast irradiation is cheapest; LHRH agonists beat orchidectomy on cost only if survival is a few months; CAB (combined androgen blockade) is most expensive. <!-- c161-a13 -->

### Response
- Not reaching an undetectable PSA gives 15-fold higher odds of castration-resistant progression at 24 months. <!-- c161-a07 -->
- Size and speed of PSA fall, pre-ADT PSA doubling time and baseline testosterone also predict response. Each unit rise in Gleason score raises the hazard of CRPC by nearly 70%. <!-- c161-a07 -->

### Side effects
- ADT causes bone loss, sexual dysfunction, hot flushes, cognitive decline, muscle loss, fat gain, anaemia, gynaecomastia, diabetes and higher cardiovascular mortality. <!-- c161-r07 -->
- Up to 20% keep some sexual activity, but only about 5% (1 in 20) keep high libido. <!-- c161-a08 -->
- Over half meet osteopenia or osteoporosis criteria; the average man is osteopenic by 4 years. <!-- c161-a08 c161-r05 -->
- Hot flushes affect 50%–80%; treat only if bothersome (megestrol, cyproterone, DES, oestradiol, medroxyprogesterone, gabapentin). <!-- c161-a08 c161-r06 -->
- Prophylactic breast RT (10 Gy) prevents gynaecomastia but does not help once present; mastectomy may then be needed. <!-- c161-a08 c161-r18 -->

### Indications and timing
- ADT is indicated for symptomatic metastatic disease in hormone-intact men. It has no role in PIN (prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia). <!-- c161-a15 -->
- **Update:** metastatic hormone-sensitive disease now gets ADT plus docetaxel and/or an AR pathway inhibitor. <!-- c161-a15 -->
- Neoadjuvant ADT before prostatectomy cuts positive margins (about 50% to 15%) but not PSA progression in four RCTs; 3 months of any form does not lower biochemical failure. <!-- c161-a09 c161-a15 -->
- Book: ADT with EBRT (external beam radiotherapy) benefits only locally advanced and/or high-grade disease. **Update:** short-course ADT also helps intermediate risk (RTOG 9408). <!-- c161-a09 c161-r21 -->
- CAB targets adrenal androgens. With cyproterone it is slightly worse (more non-cancer deaths). Non-steroidal CAB shows a slight survival gain. Adding an antiandrogen at PSA rise is secondary hormonal manipulation. <!-- c161-a10 -->
- Orchidectomy plus flutamide showed no benefit over orchidectomy alone in minimal metastatic disease (INT-0105). <!-- c161-a10 c161-r19 -->
- Early ADT does not prevent CRPC and in most studies does not improve OS; in localised low-risk disease it increases overall deaths. <!-- c161-a11 c161-r20 c161-a15 -->
- After PSA failure post-prostatectomy the course is protracted: median time to metastasis about 8 years (Pound 1999; book says 10). Many men never need ADT. <!-- c161-a11 c161-r13 -->
- Immediate ADT for nodes found at prostatectomy (prostatectomy completed) improved OS (Messing). EORTC (no prostatectomy) showed no benefit. <!-- c161-a12 -->
- 20% of men with metastatic prostate cancer die of other causes. <!-- c161-r08 -->

### Intermittent ADT
- Book: not superior and possibly worse. **Update:** NCIC PR.7 showed non-inferior OS after radiotherapy; SWOG 9346 could not prove non-inferiority in metastatic disease. <!-- c161-r12 -->
- Off-treatment intervals shorten: median 20.1, 13.2, 9.1 months, then 4–5 months. Attrition was 5% in the first interval and 68% by the third. <!-- c161-a14 -->
- Prostate cancer deaths: 120 intermittent vs 94 continuous; other deaths 148 vs 162. Quality of life was better with intermittent ADT (hot flushes, sexual desire, urinary symptoms). <!-- c161-a14 -->
