## Ch 36 Management of Defecation Disorders

### Diagnosis of functional constipation
- Functional constipation is a diagnosis of exclusion. Rome IV criteria: symptoms for at least 1 month, judged for developmental age, with no organic cause; criteria include painful defecation, large-diameter stools that may block the toilet and 2 or fewer defecations a week. <!-- c036-a01 c036-r02 -->
- Constipation can cause significant voiding dysfunction; suspect it in every child with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). Treat it and dysfunctional voiding before any surgical correction; a paediatric urologist or nurse practitioner can do this with the primary care doctor. <!-- c036-r01 c036-a19 -->
- Drugs for LUTS, especially antimuscarinics such as oxybutynin, commonly cause or worsen constipation; so do diet changes such as stopping breastfeeding. <!-- c036-a02 -->

### History and examination
- Age of onset is the easiest and most important history item: onset in infancy, before toilet training, suggests organic disease. <!-- c036-a03 c036-r08 -->
- Also ask about: failed toilet training, pain or bleeding with defecation, abdominal pain, faecal incontinence, holding behaviour, vomiting, weight loss, growth, developmental delay. <!-- c036-a03 -->
- Warning signs of an organic cause (not functional constipation): alternating constipation and diarrhoea, bowel obstruction, bilious vomiting, ribbon-like stools. <!-- c036-a14 -->
- Record stool form with the Bristol scale (consistency, shape, difficulty of passage; odour is not part of it). <!-- c036-a15 -->
- Examine weight, height, perineum, genital and perianal regions (anal position, soiling, trauma, fissures, sensation). Digital rectal examination is not routine in children; reserve it for difficult cases, by experienced staff, to look for anal stenosis, a large faecal mass or an empty rectum. <!-- c036-a04 -->

### Organic causes
- Organic causes: cystic fibrosis, hypothyroidism, coeliac disease, food allergy, Hirschsprung disease, anal stenosis, trisomy 21. In older children consider mental health problems, eating disorders, sexual abuse and irritable bowel syndrome. <!-- c036-r03 -->
- Endocrine and metabolic causes: chronic dehydration (diabetes insipidus), hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, hypothyroidism, hypervitaminosis D. Precocious puberty does not cause constipation. <!-- c036-a05 c036-r09 -->
- Suspected Hirschsprung disease: deep suction rectal biopsy (including submucosa), transanal, 2–3 cm above the dentate line. Diagnostic features: absent ganglion cells, hypertrophied nerve fibres, raised acetylcholinesterase in lamina propria and muscularis mucosae. <!-- c036-a16 -->

### Investigations
- Plain abdominal X-ray is of modest value but widely used. It shows faecal load and distribution and impaction, checks response to washout, convinces parents, and may show spinal dysraphism or sacral agenesis. <!-- c036-a08 c036-r10 c036-a18 -->
- The amount and distribution of stool on one film do not predict response to treatment or recurrence. <!-- c036-a18 -->
- Colonic transit studies: only for difficult or unresponsive cases. Contrast enema: for suspected Hirschsprung disease or repaired anorectal malformation. <!-- c036-a08 -->
- Neuropathic concern: spinal ultrasound in the first 3–6 months (before vertebral ossification), lumbosacral MRI later. <!-- c036-a08 -->
- Anorectal manometry: only for suspected Hirschsprung disease or internal sphincter achalasia (rectoanal relaxation reflex absent); adds little in functional constipation. <!-- c036-a08 -->

### Faecal impaction
- Suspect impaction with a lower abdominal or left lower quadrant mass, or a dilated stool-filled rectum on ultrasound or X-ray. About 30% of children with functional constipation present impacted. <!-- c036-r06 c036-a20 -->
- First reduce the faecal load (disimpaction): enemas or suppositories, high-dose polyethylene glycol (PEG) with or without sodium chloride, phosphate or mineral oil enemas; sometimes digital disimpaction under anaesthesia. <!-- c036-a20 -->
- Do not add fibre during initial treatment of impaction; it increases faecal load and worsens the problem. <!-- c036-a20 c036-r07 -->

### Medical management
- First line: behaviour change (regular toileting, activity), more fluid and fibre, stool softeners and laxatives, and careful use of enemas. <!-- c036-r04 -->
- PEG is the preferred agent: nearly tasteless, dissolves fast, better tolerated than lactulose, mineral oil and milk of magnesia. <!-- c036-a13 c036-r11 -->
- Prucalopride is an oral, selective, high-affinity 5-HT4 prokinetic for resistant constipation; it does not change stool consistency or bulk and is not antispasmodic. **Update:** it is a 5-HT4 receptor agonist (the book wrongly says antagonist). <!-- c036-a12 -->
- Outcome: about 50% recover and stop medicines within 6–12 months; up to 80% are controlled; up to 50% relapse within 5 years. <!-- c036-a14 -->

### Antegrade continence enemas (MACE and caecostomy)
- Principle: regular, complete colonic washout gives faecal continence, and antegrade emptying is feasible. <!-- c036-a07 -->
- MACE (Malone antegrade continence enema) channels and caecostomy (C) tubes give more independence than retrograde enemas. Open and laparoscopic results are similar. <!-- c036-a06 -->
- Best results in motivated children aged 5–12 years, and in neuropathic bowel or anorectal malformation rather than idiopathic constipation; failures are commoner in younger children. The child may sit on the toilet up to an hour. <!-- c036-a06 c036-r05 -->
- Access can be caecal or left colonic; a left-colon conduit shortens the washed segment in severe constipation with a redundant colon. <!-- c036-a06 c036-r05 -->
- If the appendix is long enough with good blood supply it can be split to make both a MACE and a Mitrofanoff channel. Previous surgery is not an absolute contraindication to laparoscopy. <!-- c036-a09 -->
- Caecostomy tubes suit: absent appendix, refusal of catheterisation, MACE stomal stenosis, a trial of antegrade enemas, choosing right vs left colon, or a non-operative permanent option. Drawback: unsightly granulation at the tube site. <!-- c036-a10 -->
- Regimen: success is not immediate; reaching a reliable routine can take 6 months of trial and error. Start with daily washouts of 20 mL/kg, then try alternate days; usually given in the evening; tap or salt water with additives such as glycerin; fluid need not be sterile. <!-- c036-a11 -->
- Commonest problem: pain or discomfort during instillation, usually settling within 3 months. Always exclude distal faecal impaction (a large rectal and descending colon load) as the cause. Perforation is very rare; retrograde ileal flow is not a usual cause of new pain. <!-- c036-a17 c036-r12 -->

## Ch 64 Male Reproductive Physiology

### Hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis
- Normal testosterone and sperm production need pulsatile hypothalamic GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) and pituitary LH (luteinising hormone) and FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone). The axis is regulated mainly by negative feedback. <!-- c064-r01 c064-r02 -->
- Testosterone feeds back on LH; inhibin and activin regulate FSH. <!-- c064-a10 -->
- Exogenous testosterone or anabolic steroids suppress LH and FSH and cause azoospermia in most men (depends on dose, frequency, duration). <!-- c064-a06 -->
- Hyperprolactinaemia inhibits GnRH and LH pulses, lowering testosterone. <!-- c064-a08 -->
- Testosterone: made by Leydig cells, mostly bound in plasma, regulated mainly by LH, converted to DHT (dihydrotestosterone) in target tissue by 5α-reductase. <!-- c064-a21 -->
- Testosterone peaks: first trimester of fetal life, about 2 months after birth (mini-puberty), and at puberty. <!-- c064-a14 -->
- Ageing: lower testosterone, blunted feedback, irregular pulsatility. With paternal age, sperm structural chromosomal abnormalities, autosomal dominant mutations and epigenetic changes increase, causing disease in offspring. <!-- c064-r04 c064-r08 -->

### Development
- SRY on the Y chromosome determines maleness; WNT4 and DAX1 are anti-testis genes that can drive ovarian development. <!-- c064-r03 -->
- The Wolffian ducts form epididymis, vas and seminal vesicles; Müllerian ducts regress; the gubernaculum guides the testis into the scrotum. <!-- c064-a01 -->

### Testis structure
- Normal adult testis volume is 18–22 mL (about 20 mL). Seminiferous tubules are 70%–80% of volume and interstitium 20%–30%. <!-- c064-a11 c064-a02 -->
- Young adult testis: about 250 m of seminiferous tubules and 700 million Leydig cells. <!-- c064-r05 -->
- Arterial supply of testis and epididymis: internal spermatic, deferential and cremasteric (external spermatic) arteries; not the pudendal artery. <!-- c064-a05 -->
- Sertoli cells are regulated mainly by FSH and testosterone; they make androgen-binding protein (ABP). <!-- c064-a03 -->
- Sertoli–Sertoli tight junctions form the blood–testis barrier, separating spermatogonia (basal) from meiotic and post-meiotic cells (adluminal). <!-- c064-a07 -->

### Spermatogenesis
- Sequence: dark type A (Ad), pale type A (Ap), type B spermatogonia; preleptotene, leptotene, zygotene, pachytene primary spermatocytes; secondary spermatocytes; spermatids Sa, Sb1, Sb2, Sc, Sd1, Sd2. <!-- c064-a09 -->
- Type A spermatogonia are the only true stem cells (self-renew or differentiate). <!-- c064-a18 -->
- Meiosis differs from mitosis by crossing-over (recombination), pairing of homologous chromosomes in meiosis I, and haploid daughter cells. <!-- c064-a13 -->
- Spermiogenesis: round spermatids become mature sperm with no cell division, in the adluminal compartment; changes are nuclear compaction, cytoplasm loss, acrosome and flagellum formation, organelle migration. <!-- c064-a16 c064-a22 -->
- Spermatogenesis is organised in stages, cycles and waves for constant output. Genes on both X and Y govern it and contribute to infertility. <!-- c064-r06 c064-r07 -->
- Timing (book): 45–60 days in the testis, 2–12 days in the epididymis, plus days to weeks of storage in the cauda. **Update:** the full human spermatogenic cycle is usually quoted as about 64–74 days. <!-- c064-a19 -->

### Epididymis and sperm maturation
- Regions: caput, corpus, cauda, each with histological zones. <!-- c064-a12 -->
- Cells: principal (absorptive and secretory), basal (said to derive from macrophages) and contractile cells for transport. <!-- c064-r09 -->
- In transit sperm gain progressive motility and ability to bind and penetrate the zona pellucida; metabolic changes include increased glycolytic capacity and changes in pH, calcium, adenylate cyclase and phospholipids. <!-- c064-r10 c064-a17 -->
- In obstruction (congenital absence of vas, post-vasectomy) the best motility is in proximal epididymal sperm. <!-- c064-a15 -->
- Epididymal function depends on temperature and androgens (mainly DHT): relevant to cryptorchidism, varicocele and 5α-reductase inhibitors. <!-- c064-r11 -->

### Vas, seminal vesicles and ejaculate
- The vas is Wolffian (mesonephric) and carries sperm from cauda to ejaculatory duct during emission. After stimulation, vasal contents are pushed back toward the epididymis because the distal vas contracts more strongly. <!-- c064-r12 c064-a23 -->
- Seminal vesicles give 65%–70% or more of ejaculate volume; the rest is vasal fluid with sperm, prostatic and periurethral secretions. The seminal vesicle–ejaculatory duct unit behaves like bladder and urethra, with physical or functional obstruction causing infertility. <!-- c064-a04 c064-r13 -->

### Sperm structure and motility
- Sperm are ciliated cells with a 9 + 2 axoneme; the midpiece has helical mitochondria around outer dense fibres. <!-- c064-r14 c064-a20 -->
- 200–300 genes regulate motility. Motility defects (ciliary dyskinesias) are common: correctable (non-specific flagellar anomalies) or genetic (dysplasia of the fibrous sheath). <!-- c064-r15 c064-r16 -->
- Sperm mitochondrial DNA is circular, histone- and intron-free, encodes respiratory chain proteins and mutates easily. <!-- c064-r17 -->

## Ch 83 Surgery of the Scrotum and Seminal Vesicles

### General scrotal surgery
- Scrotal surgery is clean, not sterile: give prophylactic antibiotics; remove hair just before surgery. A drain does not reduce complications or haematoma. <!-- c083-r01 c083-r08 -->
- Fournier gangrene: necrotising fasciitis of skin and subcutaneous tissue, limited by dartos (penis), Colles (perineum) and Scarpa (abdomen) fascia. Treat with fluids, broad antibiotics (third-generation cephalosporin, aminoglycoside, metronidazole), debridement once stable, and daily theatre debridement until all dead tissue is gone. <!-- c083-r02 -->

### Vasectomy
- The vas may get branches from several arteries but not from the superior vesical artery. <!-- c083-a01 -->
- No-scalpel vasectomy: no incision with a scalpel, smaller wound, fewer haematomas and infections, less pain. <!-- c083-a02 c083-a03 -->
- Occlusion: clips on both ends fail in under 1%; fascial (dartos) interposition between the ends brings recanalisation near zero. No method is 100% effective. <!-- c083-a04 c083-a05 c083-r04 -->
- To ease later reversal: do not excise a long segment; divide the straight vas about 3 cm above the cauda epididymis; low-voltage cautery plus fascial interposition. <!-- c083-a06 c083-r04 -->
- Open-ended vasectomy: sperm granulomas in 97%. <!-- c083-r03 -->
- Over 80% are azoospermic at 3 months. Book: repeat vasectomy if motile sperm persist at 3 months. **Update:** AUA 2012 advises semen analysis at 8–16 weeks and repeat vasectomy if motile sperm persist beyond 6 months. <!-- c083-r04 c083-a09 -->
- Vasectomy is not linked to prostate cancer, dementia, cardiovascular disease or atherosclerosis. Book: chronic scrotal pain in 10%. **Update:** AUA quotes 1%–2% with pain severe enough to seek care. <!-- c083-a07 c083-r13 -->
- Vasectomy breaches the blood–testis barrier: antisperm antibodies in 60%–80%. Pressure damage falls mainly on epididymis and efferent ductules. <!-- c083-a08 c083-r14 c083-a10 -->
- Any surgical manipulation of the epididymis causes azoospermia on that side. <!-- c083-r07 -->

### Chronic orchialgia
- Palpable varicocele with pain: varicocelectomy relieves pain in 50%. <!-- c083-a18 -->
- Microsurgical denervation of the cord helps up to two-thirds in small series; consider only if a cord block works. <!-- c083-a11 c083-r10 -->
- Orchiectomy: no level 1 evidence; if done, the inguinal route relieves pain better than the scrotal route. <!-- c083-r05 c083-r06 -->
- Epididymectomy for chronic epididymitis works best with a palpable epididymal abnormality; worse with ultrasound changes only; no benefit with neither. <!-- c083-a14 -->
- Up to 75% of epididymo-orchitis has no bacterial UTI, yet empirical antibiotics rose from 75% to 95% (1965–2005) and are not indicated without infection. <!-- c083-a17 -->

### Hydrocele and other scrotal surgery
- Jaboulay (bottleneck) repair sews the sac edges behind the cord and lowers recurrence. Excise long-standing, thick-walled or loculated sacs. <!-- c083-a12 c083-r15 c083-a16 -->
- Large hydroceles splay the epididymis and vessels; identify them to avoid injury. <!-- c083-r09 -->
- Most solid, non-transilluminating epididymal masses are benign adenomatoid tumours. <!-- c083-a13 -->
- Retractile testis needing fixation: dartos pouch orchidopexy prevents retraction. <!-- c083-a15 -->

### Seminal vesicles
- Develop at about 12 weeks as a dorsolateral bud of the distal mesonephric duct; supply from the vesiculodeferential artery (branch of superior vesical). Give 60%–80% of ejaculate. <!-- c083-a19 c083-a21 c083-a20 -->
- Unilateral agenesis (0.6%–1%) reflects an insult to the mesonephric duct before 7 weeks; may come with absent vas and ipsilateral renal anomalies. <!-- c083-a23 -->
- Bilateral absence of vas or seminal vesicles: 70%–80% carry a CFTR (cystic fibrosis) mutation; 80%–95% of men with cystic fibrosis have it. <!-- c083-a24 c083-r16 -->
- Cysts (congenital or acquired) result from ejaculatory duct obstruction; two-thirds have ipsilateral renal agenesis or dysplasia; linked to polycystic kidney disease. <!-- c083-a25 c083-r11 -->
- Infections include tuberculosis and Schistosoma haematobium. Vesiculitis on MRI: low T1, T2 brighter than fat and normal vesicle. <!-- c083-r12 c083-a22 -->
- Primary tumours are very rare; secondary spread from bladder, prostate, rectum or lymphoma is commoner. <!-- c083-a26 c083-r17 -->
- Transrectal ultrasound is the first test (easy, allows biopsy); biopsy a mass. <!-- c083-a27 c083-a28 -->
- Surgery: laparoscopic excision gives good view, low morbidity, shorter stay and allows dealing with the ipsilateral kidney. Transcoccygeal route suits men with previous suprapubic or perineal surgery. <!-- c083-a29 c083-a30 -->
- Abscess near the prostate: deep transurethral unroofing at 5 or 7 o'clock just distal to the bladder neck; CT-guided aspiration and drainage is now the least traumatic preferred option. <!-- c083-a31 -->

## Ch 97 Malignant Renal Tumors

### Epidemiology and risk factors
- Tobacco is the most accepted environmental risk factor (relative risk 1.4–2.3, dose-related); obesity and hypertension are also established. Acquired renal cystic disease raises risk. <!-- c097-a19 c097-r15 -->
- Bilateral RCC (renal cell carcinoma), synchronous or metachronous: 2%–4%. Evidence of impaired immune surveillance is ample. <!-- c097-r06 c097-r05 -->

### Imaging and biopsy
- Triple-phase renal CT (computed tomography) is the key test; an enhancing mass (more than 15 HU, Hounsfield units) is RCC until proved otherwise. <!-- c097-a01 c097-r01 -->
- About 20% of enhancing masses under 4 cm and 40% under 2 cm are benign. <!-- c097-r02 -->
- Bosniak II: minimally complicated, benign; hyperdense cysts (old blood) are small (<3 cm), round, sharp, non-enhancing; 3 cm or more is IIF. Bosniak II risk of malignancy under 10%. <!-- c097-a02 c097-r03 c097-a41 -->
- Bosniak IV: enhancing mural nodules (with dystrophic calcification) mean cystic RCC. <!-- c097-a72 -->
- Fat under −20 HU on unenhanced CT is diagnostic of angiomyolipoma (AML). Fat with calcification is almost always RCC (reactive fat with necrosis); AML almost never calcifies. <!-- c097-a39 c097-a41 -->
- Renal lymphoma has 5 radiological patterns, including a solitary mass that mimics RCC. <!-- c097-a38 -->
- Tumour thrombus: CT and MRI both show presence and upper extent; multiplanar CT is enough at many centres. <!-- c097-a10 -->
- FDG-PET-CT is not useful for staging RCC. Sestamibi uptake suggests oncocytoma or hybrid oncocytic tumours; girentuximab (carbonic anhydrase IX, clear cell) and PSMA imaging are in development. <!-- c097-a66 -->
- Classic biopsy indications: suspected abscess or infected cyst (e.g. fever, UTI and a solid/cystic mass), or to separate RCC from metastasis or lymphoma; now used more widely when several options exist. <!-- c097-a03 c097-a35 -->
- Biopsy limitation: oncocytoma vs eosinophilic RCC variants. Non-diagnostic in 10%–15%; complications low; tract seeding rare. Biopsy before or during thermal ablation. <!-- c097-a40 c097-a43 -->
- Bone scan: not needed if ECOG performance status 0, no bone pain and no extraosseous metastases; in others bone metastases exceed 15%. <!-- c097-a53 -->
- Perirenal nodes under 2 cm may be inflammatory; over 2 cm usually metastatic. <!-- c097-r12 -->

### Pathology and genetics
- Subtypes: clear cell 70%–80%, papillary 10%–15%, chromophobe 3%–5%, collecting duct under 1%, medullary rare. <!-- c097-r07 c097-a31 -->
- Clear cell arises from the proximal tubule; oncocytoma and chromophobe from the distal tubule or cortical collecting duct. <!-- c097-r04 c097-a31 c097-a50 -->
- Clear cell: chromosome 3 alterations; VHL inactivated in over 75% of sporadic cases. <!-- c097-a29 c097-r08 c097-r35 -->
- Type 1 papillary: trisomy 7 and 17; papillary RCC tends to be multifocal. **Update:** WHO 2022 no longer splits papillary into types 1 and 2. <!-- c097-a30 c097-r09 -->
- Medullary carcinoma: sickle cell trait, young Black patients (third decade), usually metastatic at diagnosis, death within months. <!-- c097-a32 c097-r36 -->
- Collecting duct carcinoma: central, infiltrative, aggressive, Ulex europaeus lectin positive, chemoresistant; usually metastatic, so follow closely (cisplatin role undefined). <!-- c097-a51 c097-r37 c097-a70 -->
- Sarcomatoid change: commonest with clear cell and chromophobe; can be grossly infiltrative (most RCCs have a pseudocapsule). <!-- c097-a52 c097-a28 -->
- TFE3 fusion (Xp11 translocation) RCC is common in children: t(X;17) often indolent, t(X;1) may recur late in nodes. <!-- c097-a42 -->
- WHO/ISUP grade 1–4, based on nuclear features. <!-- c097-r14 -->
- P-glycoprotein (170 kDa efflux pump) in 80%–90% of RCCs pumps out cytotoxic drugs. <!-- c097-a26 -->

### Hereditary syndromes
- VHL disease: autosomal dominant tumour suppressor loss. Lost VHL stabilises HIF (hypoxia-inducible factor), upregulating VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor), so tumours are hypervascular. <!-- c097-a23 c097-r33 c097-a46 -->
- VHL tumours: clear cell RCC, phaeochromocytoma, retinal angiomas, haemangioblastomas (brainstem, cerebellum, cord), plus inner ear, epididymis and pancreas; penetrance incomplete. RCC is now the leading cause of death. <!-- c097-a20 c097-a21 c097-r32 c097-a22 c097-a47 -->
- VHL: treat all tumours in a kidney when the largest reaches 3 cm. **Update:** belzutifan (HIF-2α inhibitor) is approved for VHL-associated RCC. <!-- c097-r27 -->
- Hereditary papillary RCC: autosomal dominant, activating MET mutation at 7q31 (receptor for hepatocyte growth factor); few non-renal tumours. <!-- c097-a24 c097-a25 c097-r34 c097-a47 -->
- HLRCC (hereditary leiomyomatosis and RCC; fumarate hydratase): cutaneous and uterine leiomyomas; aggressive tumours, so operate early and widely. <!-- c097-a21 c097-a48 -->
- BHD (Birt-Hogg-Dubé): facial fibrofolliculomas, lung cysts, pneumothorax, chromophobe RCC and oncocytoma. <!-- c097-a21 c097-a49 c097-a50 c097-a15 -->
- Genetic counselling: all patients 46 years or younger, suspected SDH (succinate dehydrogenase) deficiency, suggestive history, relatives of known cases; not usually an elderly patient with multifocal discordant histologies. <!-- c097-a15 -->

### Paraneoplastic syndromes
- Hypercalcaemia (up to 13%; mainly PTH-related peptide, also vitamin D and prostaglandins, or bone lysis): hydrate, furosemide, bisphosphonates, steroids, calcitonin. <!-- c097-a33 -->
- Others: hypertension, polycythaemia, Stauffer syndrome (non-metastatic liver dysfunction; if it persists after nephrectomy, disease persists). <!-- c097-r10 c097-r11 -->

### Staging and prognosis (TNM 8th edition)
- Renal vein, sinus or perinephric fat and collecting system invasion: T3a. Contiguous ipsilateral adrenal invasion: T4 (beyond Gerota). Non-contiguous or contralateral adrenal: M1. Any nodes: N1. <!-- c097-a27 c097-a45 c097-a57 c097-r38 -->
- Medial (sinus fat) invasion is worse than lateral perinephric fat invasion; adrenal involvement is worse still. <!-- c097-a54 -->
- Stage is the strongest single predictor; integrated nomograms (size, stage, grade, performance status, subtype) predict best. Age and comorbidity affect overall but not cancer-specific survival. <!-- c097-a55 c097-a56 c097-a37 -->
- Worst signs: T4, nodes (N1) and metastases (M1); node involvement carries a dismal prognosis. <!-- c097-r26 c097-a11 -->
- IVC (inferior vena cava) thrombus confined to the kidney: 31%–50% 5-year survival with surgery. Good if nodes negative and wall not invaded; very poor with wall invasion or nodal disease. <!-- c097-a36 c097-r16 c097-r17 c097-r18 -->
- Small RCCs under 3.5 cm grow under 0.5 cm a year (some up to 1 cm). <!-- c097-r22 -->

### Surgery for localised disease
- Classic partial nephrectomy criteria: solitary tumour under 5 cm with a normal contralateral kidney. <!-- c097-a05 -->
- Young patient with a peripheral exophytic lower-pole clear cell tumour: partial nephrectomy. <!-- c097-a69 -->
- Margin width does not matter if the margin is negative. <!-- c097-r21 -->
- Renal function after partial nephrectomy depends mainly on preoperative function and preserved vascularised parenchyma; prolonged warm ischaemia adds harm. <!-- c097-a63 -->
- Renal vein thrombus up to the IVC junction in a large tumour: radical nephrectomy. <!-- c097-a71 -->
- Ipsilateral adrenalectomy only for CT involvement, contiguous spread or large adjacent upper-pole tumours. <!-- c097-r19 -->
- Lymphadenectomy gives staging, not proven therapeutic benefit; EORTC 30881 showed no survival gain. Extended dissection (interaortocaval and ipsilateral great-vessel nodes, crus to common iliac) is not better for most; still offered for bulky suspicious nodes. <!-- c097-a67 c097-r13 c097-a34 -->
- Cryoablation needs rapid freezing, slow thawing and a double freeze–thaw cycle. <!-- c097-a09 c097-r29 -->
- Thermal ablation: more local recurrence than partial nephrectomy, but similar control after repeat ablation; best under 3 cm; active surveillance is often better in the elderly. <!-- c097-a43 -->
- Oncocytoma on biopsy in an elderly patient: observe with imaging every 6–12 months. <!-- c097-a13 -->

### Renal function after surgery
- Identify CKD (chronic kidney disease) by cause, eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate; MDRD or CKD-EPI) and albuminuria. Creatinine alone underestimates disease in thin elderly women. Measured GFR is costly; no urine test beats eGFR for screening. <!-- c097-a08 -->
- After major nephron loss, check urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio yearly (proteinuria is the first sign of hyperfiltration injury). <!-- c097-a07 c097-r28 -->
- CKD from medical causes raises cardiovascular events, hospitalisation and death; surgical CKD is more stable. <!-- c097-a64 -->
- AUA 2017 nephrology referral: preoperative GFR under 45, confirmed proteinuria, diabetics with GFR under 60, or expected GFR under 30 after surgery. A patient with GFR 70 and equal split function having radical nephrectomy (new GFR about 40–42) need not be referred. <!-- c097-a68 -->

### Follow-up
- Recurrence risk is highest in the first 3 years. Low risk (pT1N0) after radical nephrectomy: chest X-ray yearly for 3 years. <!-- c097-r20 c097-a04 -->
- Intermediate–high risk after partial nephrectomy: baseline CT or MRI at 3–6 months, then imaging every 6 months for at least 3 years and yearly to 5 years. <!-- c097-a06 -->

### Metastatic and adjuvant treatment
- Solitary metastasis (1.6%–3.2%): nephrectomy (partial if needed to avoid dialysis) with metastasectomy, possibly in one minimally invasive session. <!-- c097-a12 -->
- Debulking alone is rarely the sole treatment. <!-- c097-r23 -->
- Targeted drugs act on VEGF (sunitinib, sorafenib, axitinib, pazopanib, lenvatinib, cabozantinib) and mTOR (temsirolimus, everolimus); responses mainly in clear cell; non-clear cell care is undefined. <!-- c097-a62 c097-a61 c097-a16 -->
- Pazopanib and axitinib are VEGF receptor TKIs (tyrosine kinase inhibitors). Nivolumab and pembrolizumab block PD-1 (activating immunity), atezolizumab blocks PD-L1, ipilimumab CTLA-4. <!-- c097-a16 c097-a44 -->
- Adjuvant sunitinib (S-TRAC, 615 patients): longer disease-free survival (6.8 vs 5.6 years), more toxicity, no overall survival gain; other adjuvant agents failed. **Update:** adjuvant pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-564) improved disease-free and overall survival and is now standard; first-line metastatic therapy is now immunotherapy-based combinations. <!-- c097-a14 -->

### Benign and other renal tumours
- Tuberous sclerosis: about 20% of AMLs; autosomal dominant; epilepsy, learning disability, adenoma sebaceum. Bleeding AML with TSC, bilateral disease or poor function: selective embolisation. <!-- c097-r30 c097-a17 -->
- Cystic nephroma: benign, middle-aged women, Bosniak II–III without solid parts (related to MEST, mixed epithelial and stromal tumour, which looks solid or Bosniak IV). <!-- c097-a18 c097-r31 -->
- Renal sarcoma: leiomyosarcoma commonest (50%–60%); in the retroperitoneum liposarcoma. Prognosis depends on margin and grade; pseudocapsule is not a safe plane; wide excision is essential. <!-- c097-a58 c097-a59 c097-r24 -->
- Renal carcinoid has a good prognosis, unlike other rare renal tumours. <!-- c097-a60 -->
- Metastases to the kidney occur in 12% of those dying of cancer: lung, breast, GI, melanoma, haematological. <!-- c097-r25 -->
- An adrenal lesion bright on T2 MRI suggests phaeochromocytoma and should be removed with careful preparation. <!-- c097-a65 -->

## Ch 115 Urinary Incontinence and Pelvic Prolapse: Epidemiology and Pathophysiology

### Definitions and epidemiology
- Urgency incontinence: leakage with urgency; DO (detrusor overactivity) need not be shown. Mixed incontinence: symptoms of both stress and urgency incontinence. <!-- c115-a01 -->
- Incontinence affects 25%–40% of women, 10% weekly. Faecal incontinence in 17% of women with prolapse. <!-- c115-r02 -->
- SUI (stress urinary incontinence) is commonest in young women; urgency and mixed become commoner with age. Continuous leakage is rare (fistula). <!-- c115-a02 c115-r11 -->
- Age is strongly linked to all types; white women are at higher risk than Black and Asian women. OAB (overactive bladder) rises with age. 12% of men report terminal dribbling. <!-- c115-r12 c115-r07 c115-r06 -->
- Risk factors include parity, obesity and others; caffeine is linked to urgency but mostly not to incontinence. Younger age at first pregnancy predicts later SUI. <!-- c115-a03 c115-a04 -->
- Oral oestrogen is associated with incontinence; topical oestrogen is not linked to SUI. <!-- c115-r08 -->
- Prolapse may mask incontinence. Only a vaginal bulge is consistently linked to prolapse; pressure and pain have other causes. <!-- c115-r01 c115-a05 -->

### Continence mechanism
- The female bladder neck is weaker than the male and often incompetent; most of the urethra is active sphincter, but the mid-urethra matters most. <!-- c115-r03 c115-r04 -->
- Urethral mucosa and wall properties help continence. The anterior vaginal wall backs the urethra so it can be compressed; urethropelvic ligaments attach it laterally to the arcus tendineus. <!-- c115-r09 c115-r10 -->
- VLPP (Valsalva leak point pressure) under 60 cmH2O suggests but does not confirm intrinsic sphincter deficiency. <!-- c115-r05 -->
- Voiding: parasympathetic pelvic nerve active; sympathetic hypogastric and pudendal (Onuf nucleus) suppressed. A-delta afferents act during storage, closing the bladder neck reflexly. <!-- c115-a09 -->

### POP-Q
- Points are measured in cm from the hymen. Aa and Ap lie 3 cm inside the hymen, so they range only from −3 to +3 (Ap +4 is impossible). Ba and Bp mark the leading edge and can exceed +3. C is cervix or vault. <!-- c115-a06 c115-r13 -->
- Stage by the leading edge: stage 1 more than 1 cm above the hymen; stage 2 within 1 cm above or below; stage 3 more than 1 cm beyond but not total; stage 4 complete eversion. <!-- c115-a07 c115-r14 -->
- Posterior points beyond the hymen with C at the hymen: needs posterior repair plus apical support (e.g. posterior colporrhaphy and sacrospinous fixation). <!-- c115-a08 -->

## Ch 156 Laparoscopic and Robotic-Assisted Radical Prostatectomy and Pelvic Lymphadenectomy

### Patient selection and set-up
- Contraindications: uncorrectable bleeding disorder, or unfit for general anaesthesia (severe cardiopulmonary disease). <!-- c156-a15 -->
- Avoid complex cases early in experience (large gland, large median lobe, morbid obesity, previous pelvic surgery, radiation, TURP); these are not absolute contraindications and are best done in high-volume centres. <!-- c156-r01 c156-a16 -->
- A skilled bedside assistant who knows the robot greatly helps. <!-- c156-r02 -->
- Pneumoperitoneum with steep Trendelenburg: hypercarbia, acidosis, oliguria, corneal oedema, raised intraocular pressure, neurapraxia; pad pressure points. <!-- c156-a13 c156-r03 c156-r14 -->

### Approach and technique
- Transperitoneal vs extraperitoneal: surgeon preference; margins similar. Extraperitoneal avoids bowel handling, useful after major abdominal surgery. <!-- c156-a02 c156-r04 c156-a05 -->
- The robot facilitates suturing and dissection, especially for surgeons without advanced laparoscopic skills. <!-- c156-a09 c156-r05 -->
- Find the bladder neck–prostate junction with physical manoeuvres and visual cues; after dividing the anterior neck, check for a median lobe before the posterior neck. <!-- c156-r06 c156-r07 -->
- Fully develop the plane between rectum and prostate (incise the posterior Denonvilliers layer) to avoid rectal injury and define pedicles and NVBs (neurovascular bundles). <!-- c156-a07 c156-r08 -->
- The NVB lies between prostatic fascia and levator fascia; use interfascial dissection for nerve sparing in organ-confined cancer; minimise thermal energy near the nerves. <!-- c156-a10 c156-r10 c156-r09 -->
- Antegrade approach divides pedicles early and the dorsal venous complex late, so less bleeding; retrograde does the reverse. <!-- c156-a11 -->
- Watch for an anterior or posterior apical lip protruding under the urethra, to avoid an iatrogenic positive apical margin. <!-- c156-a14 c156-r11 -->
- Bladder neck reconstruction may be needed for size mismatch (after TURP, median lobe, large gland). <!-- c156-r12 -->
- Vesicourethral anastomosis: interrupted or running; a running suture with a single knot is most efficient. <!-- c156-a01 c156-r13 -->

### Outcomes and complications
- Blood loss is low because pneumoperitoneum tamponades venous sinuses; usually a few hundred mL, transfusion under 2%. <!-- c156-a03 c156-a08 c156-r14 -->
- Positive margins fall with experience. Costs are driven by operative time, stay and disposables. <!-- c156-a04 c156-a12 -->
- Book list: rectal injury rare (higher in salvage surgery), repair when seen; urine leak needs longer catheter; bladder neck stricture under 5%; machine failure under 0.5%. <!-- c156-r14 -->
- Book: oncological outcomes equal to open, better margins in high-volume centres; potency better with robot; at least 20% not pad-free and 5% severely incontinent; discharge in 24 hours, normal activity in 3 weeks. **Update:** the randomised trial of robotic vs open prostatectomy (Yaxley, Lancet 2016) found similar urinary and sexual function at 12 weeks and 24 months. <!-- c156-r14 -->

### Pelvic lymph node dissection
- PLND (pelvic lymph node dissection) is advised for intermediate- or high-risk disease or nodal risk over 5% on a nomogram; laparoscopic yield equals open. <!-- c156-r15 c156-a06 -->
- Extended PLND gives more nodes, but its benefit is debated against extra time and complications. <!-- c156-r16 -->
- Lymphocele: clip visible lymphatics; transperitoneal approach does not prevent it; drain symptomatic ones (venous or bladder compression) percutaneously or laparoscopically. <!-- c156-r17 c156-r18 c156-r19 -->
