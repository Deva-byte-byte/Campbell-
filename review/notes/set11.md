## Ch 34 Neuromuscular dysfunction of the lower urinary tract in children

### Causes and goals
- Most causes are congenital (spina bifida, sacral agenesis, anorectal malformation); cerebral palsy is acquired. <!-- c034-a01 -->
- The primary goal is preservation of renal function. Secondary goals: urinary and faecal continence, no UTI (urinary tract infection), sexual function and fertility. <!-- c034-a02 c034-r07 -->
- Renal function is preserved by keeping bladder filling pressures low, actively managing VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) and avoiding UTI. <!-- c034-r08 -->

### Newborn with myelodysplasia: evaluation
- Renal ultrasound and residual urine as soon as possible after birth; they are the baseline for spotting later deterioration or progressive denervation. <!-- c034-r01 -->
- Identify infants at risk early (poorly compliant or overactive detrusor, DSD (detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia)) so prophylactic treatment starts before the upper tract deteriorates. <!-- c034-r02 -->
- Three urodynamic patterns: synergic 26%, dyssynergic with or without poor compliance 37%, complete denervation 36%. <!-- c034-r03 -->
- DSD is the pattern that compromises emptying and is most dangerous: over 70% deteriorate within 3 years, versus under a quarter with synergy or denervation. Low capacity and detrusor overactivity alone are not linked to upper tract deterioration. <!-- c034-a05 c034-a16 -->
- Risk factors for renal decline: DSD, high detrusor pressure, detrusor overactivity, febrile UTI, VUR; manage each actively. <!-- c034-r06 -->
- Renal function: nuclear renography is the gold standard for GFR (glomerular filtration rate); cystatin C is better than creatinine (low muscle mass). <!-- c034-a17 c034-r05 -->

### Follow-up
- ICCS (International Children's Continence Society) follow-up is based on developmental stage and the risk of secondary tethering. <!-- c034-r09 -->
- Tethering is commonest during the fastest growth: newborn to toddler and adolescence to adulthood. <!-- c034-a03 -->
- Signs of a significant change in bladder function: new UTI, hydronephrosis, worsening continence, change in leg function (improved continence is not one). <!-- c034-a04 -->
- A CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention)-endorsed protocol covers management from birth to 5 years. <!-- c034-r10 -->

### Management
- Least invasive first; antimuscarinics plus CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) are the mainstay. <!-- c034-r11 -->
- Early CIC and antimuscarinics improve urodynamics and reduce UTI, VUR, upper tract deterioration and ESRD (end-stage renal disease). <!-- c034-a06 c034-r04 -->
- Antimuscarinics increase capacity and volume to first contraction, and reduce contractions, incontinence and catheterisations. <!-- c034-a07 -->
- Start antimuscarinics for poor compliance with or without hydronephrosis. <!-- c034-r17 -->
- Keep filling pressures below 30 cmH2O where possible. <!-- c034-r12 -->
- Indications for CIC include poor emptying and high-grade reflux; a residual of 3 mL after Credé is normal and needs no CIC. <!-- c034-a15 -->
- Intravesical botulinum toxin is well tolerated and avoids bigger surgery in many; pre-existing poor compliance predicts poor response (Kask 2013). <!-- c034-a08 c034-r13 -->
- Enterocystoplasty protects the upper tract but has many complications. Ileum is the preferred segment: autoaugmentation fails, stomach causes haematuria-dysuria syndrome and metabolic problems, ileocaecal segment worsens bowel function in neural tube defects, tissue-engineered bladder failed in phase II. <!-- c034-a11 c034-r14 -->
- Stones after augmentation are more likely with ileum and a stoma; urethral catheterisation, mobility and routine irrigation reduce them; antimuscarinics have no effect. <!-- c034-a09 -->
- Cancer after augmentation presents at a younger age than usual bladder cancer, with atypical symptoms and advanced disease, usually at least 10 years after surgery. <!-- c034-a10 -->
- Isolated bladder neck procedures (AUS (artificial urinary sphincter), slings, bladder neck repair) worsen bladder function in up to 30%, especially with pre-existing overactivity or poor compliance; follow closely. <!-- c034-a12 c034-r15 -->

### Reflux
- Low-grade VUR with good emptying and no outlet resistance: antibiotic prophylaxis alone; high-grade VUR or poor emptying: add CIC. <!-- c034-r16 -->
- The Credé manoeuvre is contraindicated with reflux and a reactive external sphincter (reflex sphincter contraction worsens reflux). <!-- c034-a13 c034-r18 -->
- Antireflux surgery indications and success match non-neurogenic children if emptying is addressed. <!-- c034-r19 -->
- Prenatal closure of myelomeningocele (MOMS trial) reduces shunting and improves motor function, but increases preterm labour and does not improve bladder function. <!-- c034-a14 -->
- Sexual function in men with spina bifida is best with a sacral lesion; worse with higher lesions, hydrocephalus, incontinence or living with parents. <!-- c034-a18 -->

### Occult spinal dysraphism and other conditions
- Occult neural tube defects present after age 3–4 with incontinence or constipation, or with new symptoms after a growth spurt (tethering). <!-- c034-r20 -->
- 90% have a skin marker over the lower spine: dimple, hair tuft, skin tag, lipoma, vascular malformation, asymmetric gluteal cleft. <!-- c034-a19 -->
- Imaging: spinal radiograph, MRI and renal ultrasound; spinal ultrasound only before 3 months (before ossification), MRI after. <!-- c034-a20 c034-r21 -->
- Do urodynamics before and after detethering; early detethering does better. <!-- c034-r22 c034-r23 -->
- Sacral agenesis: perineal sensation is usually preserved. <!-- c034-a21 -->
- Anorectal malformation: spinal cord anomalies (tethered cord, thick or fatty filum, lipoma) in 18%–50%. Urodynamics if spinal anomaly, hydronephrosis, VUR or incontinence; a low fistula alone is not an indication. <!-- c034-a22 c034-r24 -->
- Cerebral palsy: LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) in 16%–94%, commonest is incontinence; daytime dryness comes first but later than peers; symptoms increase with age; recurrent UTI or DSD symptoms (retention, interrupted stream, hesitancy) signal upper tract risk and warrant ultrasound and urodynamics. <!-- c034-a23 c034-r25 -->
- Transverse myelitis: retention in 95% in the acute phase. <!-- c034-a24 -->
- Spinal cord injury: spinal shock first (acontractile bladder, non-reactive sphincter), then usually overactive detrusor with DSD. Lesions above T6 cause autonomic dysreflexia during filling and contractions. <!-- c034-r26 c034-r27 -->

## Ch 35 Functional disorders of the lower urinary tract in children

### Normal development
- In the first 2 years, immature detrusor-sphincter coordination (hypercontractility, interrupted voiding) causes functional outflow obstruction. <!-- c035-r01 -->
- Even newborns do not void during sleep, showing higher-centre control. <!-- c035-r02 -->

### Associations
- BBD (bladder bowel dysfunction): constipation with urological problems; bowel problems are common in children with voiding dysfunction and with acquired sphincter dysfunction. <!-- c035-r03 c035-r05 -->
- LUTD (lower urinary tract dysfunction) is linked to bowel dysfunction, UTI, VUR and psychiatric diagnoses: stasis causes UTI, detrusor hypertrophy may distort the ureterovesical junction, BBD lowers reflux resolution, and treating bowels alone can improve bladder function. <!-- c035-a04 -->
- Bladder dysfunction is linked to non-resolution of high-grade VUR. <!-- c035-r06 -->
- Behavioural and emotional disorders occur in 20%–30%. <!-- c035-r10 -->

### Specific conditions
- OAB (overactive bladder) is the commonest LUT disorder in children, peaking at 5–7 years. <!-- c035-r09 -->
- Giggle incontinence (enuresis risoria): school-age girls, large-volume or complete emptying triggered by laughing; diagnosis of exclusion; treat with biofeedback or methylphenidate. <!-- c035-a01 c035-r04 -->
- Vaginal reflux (vaginal voiding): prepubertal girls wet 10–15 minutes after a normal void; may cause labial adhesions; treat with reassurance and posture to empty the vagina. <!-- c035-a02 c035-r11 -->
- Pollakiuria: daytime-only frequency, up to 50 times a day; age 4–6, both sexes; often after a family death or stress; self-limited over about 6 months. The child does not wake to void. <!-- c035-a03 c035-r12 -->
- Dysfunctional voiding: staccato flow with incomplete emptying; biofeedback coordinates pelvic floor and abdominal muscles. <!-- c035-a06 -->
- Botulinum toxin A in the external sphincter blocks presynaptic acetylcholine release, paralysing striated muscle; onset 5–7 days, peak 4–6 weeks, lasts 3–12 months, reversible. <!-- c035-a05 -->
- All children with LUTD get behavioural therapy: demystification, education, counselling, timed voiding, relaxation, stool monitoring and bladder-bowel diaries. <!-- c035-r13 -->

### Enuresis
- Three systems: bladder (low nocturnal capacity), kidney (nocturnal polyuria) and brain (arousal); maturational lag in one or more. <!-- c035-a07 -->
- Nocturnal urine output often exceeds sleep bladder capacity; many have reduced functional capacity and detrusor overactivity. <!-- c035-r07 c035-r08 -->
- Alarm: motivated families, no polyuria, low voided volume. Desmopressin: nocturnal polyuria with normal capacity, infrequent wetting, or alarm failed or refused. <!-- c035-a08 c035-r14 -->

## Ch 71 Disorders of male orgasm and ejaculation

### Physiology
- Many transmitters are involved; dopamine (D2) promotes ejaculation and serotonin inhibits it. <!-- c071-a01 -->
- Three phases: emission, ejection, orgasm. Emission: seminal vesicle and prostate contraction into the posterior urethra, sympathetic and parasympathetic (T10–L2). Ejection: somatic (S2–S4, pudendal) rhythmic bulbospongiosus and pelvic floor contraction, a spinal reflex with little voluntary control. The bladder neck and internal sphincter contract to prevent retrograde flow; the external sphincter contracts intermittently. <!-- c071-a02 -->

### Premature ejaculation (PE): definition and epidemiology
- PE is common; reported prevalence 4%–39%, higher with self-report than with the strict ISSM (International Society for Sexual Medicine) definition. <!-- c071-r01 c071-a07 -->
- Stopwatch IELT (intravaginal ejaculatory latency time) is positively skewed, median 5.4 minutes (0.55–44.1), falls with age, varies by country; under 1 minute occurs in only about 2.5% of men. <!-- c071-a03 -->
- Lifelong PE: about 90% of treatment seekers ejaculate within 1 minute and 10% within 1–2 minutes (77% within 30 seconds); a 1-minute cut-off captures 80%–90%. <!-- c071-a04 c071-a06 -->
- ISSM lifelong PE (2007): ejaculation always or nearly always before or within about 1 minute of vaginal penetration, inability to delay on nearly all penetrations, and negative personal consequences. ISSM 2013 added acquired PE: a bothersome fall in latency to about 3 minutes or less. It applies only to vaginal intercourse. <!-- c071-a05 -->
- The ISSM definition should be the basis of office diagnosis. DSM-5 (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual) is based on it (latency, control, bother); earlier DSM versions had no latency. <!-- c071-r03 c071-a04 -->
- Subtypes: lifelong, acquired, natural variable and subjective PE (normal or long IELT but a complaint). <!-- c071-a04 -->
- Self-estimated IELT by patient and partner is acceptable in practice. Voluntary delay acts before or early in emission. <!-- c071-a06 -->
- PE causes distress, bother, frustration and avoidance; it harms quality of life, relationships, self-esteem and confidence, and deters single men from new relationships. <!-- c071-a06 c071-r02 -->

### Causes
- Lifelong PE may have a genetic and neurobiological basis (5-HT2C hyposensitivity or 5-HT1A hypersensitivity). Acquired PE is usually from performance anxiety, psychological or relationship problems or ED (erectile dysfunction), and sometimes prostatitis, hyperthyroidism or drug withdrawal. <!-- c071-r04 c071-a07 -->
- Thyroid: hyperthyroidism (not hypothyroidism) is linked to PE; PE fell from 50% to 15% after treating hyperthyroidism. Routine TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) screening is not indicated. <!-- c071-a07 -->
- Up to half of men with ED also have PE: they rush intercourse or need more stimulation, and performance anxiety often worsens it. <!-- c071-a08 -->

### Evaluation
- Full medical and sexual history, examination (mandatory in acquired PE), erectile function inventory. Lifelong PE is diagnosed on history alone. <!-- c071-a09 -->
- IIEF (International Index of Erectile Function) is not validated in PE: 33.3% of potent men with PE get a false-positive ED score. The IPE (Index of Premature Ejaculation) was not designed as a screening tool. <!-- c071-a09 -->

### Treatment
- Everyone gets basic psychosexual education. CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy) is limited first-line but useful with drugs, especially in acquired PE with anxiety; behavioural methods work short term but relapse long term. <!-- c071-a10 c071-r06 -->
- Oral SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) and topical anaesthetics (lidocaine, prilocaine, benzocaine) are effective and safe. SSRIs block presynaptic serotonin reuptake, not a postsynaptic transporter. Daily dosing delays more than on-demand dosing 3–6 hours before sex. <!-- c071-a11 c071-r05 -->
- Dapoxetine, a short-acting on-demand SSRI, is approved in over 50 countries but not by the FDA. Other options: clomipramine, tramadol, PDE5 (phosphodiesterase type 5) inhibitors, α-blockers. <!-- c071-a11 -->
- PDE5 inhibitors are not recommended for lifelong PE with normal erections, but are recommended with comorbid ED. Treat acquired PE causes (ED, hyperthyroidism, infection, prostatodynia, CPPS (chronic pelvic pain syndrome)) alone or with an SSRI. <!-- c071-a08 c071-r07 -->

### Delayed ejaculation (DE) and anejaculation
- Causes are many. Most men ejaculate 4–10 minutes after intromission; DE is suspected beyond 25–30 minutes with distress. Lifelong 25%, acquired 75%; global or situational. <!-- c071-a12 c071-r08 c071-r09 -->
- Psychogenic DE comes from performance anxiety or idiosyncratic vigorous masturbation that intercourse cannot replicate. <!-- c071-a12 -->
- Common causes: psychogenic, ageing penile afferents and Pacinian corpuscles, hypogonadism, diabetic neuropathy, multiple sclerosis, SSRIs and major tranquillisers, prostatectomy, pelvic surgery or radiotherapy. <!-- c071-a13 -->
- Treatment is cause-specific and addresses fertility; drugs have limited success. <!-- c071-r10 c071-r11 -->
- Spinal cord injury: ejaculation improves with lower lesions (erection does not). Under 5% with complete upper motor neuron lesions ejaculate; about 15% with lower motor neuron lesions; 22% with incomplete upper and almost all with incomplete lower lesions. Vibratory stimulation works in up to 70%; electroejaculation is safe. Both risk autonomic dysreflexia; pre-treat with nifedipine (not tamsulosin). <!-- c071-a14 -->

### Retrograde ejaculation
- Orgasm without antegrade semen; confirm with sperm (and fructose) in first-void post-masturbation urine; this separates it from failure of emission. <!-- c071-a15 c071-r13 -->
- Commonest causes: TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate, 42%–100%) and diabetic neuropathy (about 30% of diabetics, unrelated to duration or HbA1c). TUIP (transurethral incision) causes it in 5%–45%. <!-- c071-a15 c071-r12 -->
- Drugs (variable success): pseudoephedrine, ephedrine, midodrine, phenylpropanolamine, and imipramine 25 mg twice daily. <!-- c071-a15 c071-r14 -->

### Post-orgasmic illness syndrome (POIS)
- Flu-like state after ejaculation: myalgia, fatigue, nasal congestion, itchy eyes; starts within 30 minutes in 87% and peaks on day 2. <!-- c071-r15 c071-r16 -->
- Proposed type 1 hypersensitivity to own semen; hyposensitisation with subcutaneous autologous semen has been tried. <!-- c071-r17 c071-r18 -->

## Ch 104 Treatment of advanced renal cell carcinoma

### Biology
- VHL (von Hippel-Lindau) inactivation by mutation or methylation in 70%–90% of clear cell RCC (renal cell carcinoma), upregulating HIF (hypoxia-inducible factor) targets such as VEGF; Raf-1 is not one of them. <!-- c104-a14 c104-a13 -->
- Sirolimus and its analogues target mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin). <!-- c104-a07 -->

### Prognosis
- MSKCC (Memorial Sloan Kettering) factors include Karnofsky status under 80%. **Update:** the IMDC (International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium) model is now standard. <!-- c104-a02 -->

### Cytoreductive nephrectomy
- Two randomised trials in the cytokine era showed survival benefit for nephrectomy before IFN-α (interferon alpha). <!-- c104-r01 -->
- Poor performance status, comorbidity, rapid progression or brain metastases predict no benefit. <!-- c104-r02 -->
- CARMENA: in intermediate and poor-risk patients, sunitinib alone was not inferior to nephrectomy then sunitinib; SURTIME (deferred nephrectomy) closed early. So poor-risk patients get systemic therapy first. <!-- c104-a03 c104-r03 -->
- Nephrectomy is still offered to well-selected patients (low metastatic burden, favourable features). <!-- c104-r04 -->
- It can palliate pain, haematuria or paraneoplastic effects, but non-surgical palliation usually works, so purely palliative nephrectomy is uncommon. <!-- c104-r09 c104-r10 -->

### Metastasectomy
- Resection of isolated metastases is appropriate in selected patients; median survival rates of 35%–50% are reported. <!-- c104-r05 c104-r06 -->
- Better outcome: complete resection, solitary lesion, age under 60, small size, lung site, metachronous disease. A late solitary lung nodule after nephrectomy can be resected. <!-- c104-r07 c104-a06 -->
- No randomised trials; benefit may reflect selection bias or tumour biology. <!-- c104-r08 -->
- Metastases may also be resected (often with radiotherapy or systemic therapy) to relieve or prevent disabling symptoms. <!-- c104-r11 -->

### Systemic therapy
- High-dose IL-2 (interleukin-2): complete response in 7%–9% of clear cell RCC, most lasting over 15 years; no randomised survival benefit; reserved for selected clear cell patients. <!-- c104-a01 -->
- IL-2 plus interferon gives higher response than either alone but no survival gain; adding LAK (lymphokine-activated killer) cells does not help. Cytokines work mainly in clear cell RCC. <!-- c104-a04 c104-a05 -->
- Nivolumab plus ipilimumab beats sunitinib in untreated intermediate/poor-risk clear cell RCC (objective response 42%, better survival); sunitinib is no longer standard. <!-- c104-a08 c104-a09 -->
- Axitinib plus pembrolizumab or plus avelumab beat sunitinib; cabozantinib beat sunitinib in response and PFS (progression-free survival) in intermediate/poor risk (phase 2). **Update:** also cabozantinib plus nivolumab and lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab; avelumab-axitinib improved PFS only; adjuvant pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-564) for high-risk resected disease. <!-- c104-a11 -->
- Axitinib beat sorafenib in PFS after first-line VEGF or cytokine therapy. <!-- c104-a10 -->
- Temsirolimus beat interferon in poor-risk patients (3 or more adverse factors): median survival 10.9 vs 7.3 months. <!-- c104-a12 -->
- Papillary RCC: no proven benefit from clear cell agents; MET (c-met) mutations make MET inhibitor trials reasonable. **Update:** cabozantinib beat sunitinib in papillary RCC (PAPMET). <!-- c104-a15 -->

## Ch 118 The underactive detrusor and nocturia

### Detrusor underactivity (DUA)
- ICS (International Continence Society) 2002: a contraction of reduced strength and/or duration, causing prolonged or incomplete emptying in a normal time span. Acontractile detrusor: no contraction on urodynamics. <!-- c118-a01 c118-r01 -->
- The book says there is no recognised definition of UAB (underactive bladder); its symptoms overlap with OAB. **Update:** ICS 2018 proposed a symptom definition (slow stream, hesitancy, straining, with or without incomplete emptying). <!-- c118-a02 -->
- Urodynamic criteria mostly use contraction strength from detrusor pressure at maximum flow. The Watt factor measures bladder power, depends little on volume and is unaffected by outlet resistance, but has no validated cut-off. <!-- c118-a04 c118-a05 -->
- Elbadawi's 'degeneration' pattern (disrupted myocytes, axonal degeneration) is linked to DUA. <!-- c118-a03 -->

### Causes
- DUA often coexists with other dysfunctions in the elderly; ageing reduces autonomic innervation and sensation. <!-- c118-r02 c118-r03 -->
- Diabetes harms detrusor function by myogenic and autonomic damage. Lumbosacral cord or disc disease and pelvic surgery injure the pelvic plexus. <!-- c118-r04 c118-r05 -->
- Multisystem atrophy (can mimic Parkinson disease) causes DUA in at least half through parasympathetic atrophy. <!-- c118-a09 -->
- Afferent nerves are central to starting and sustaining a contraction; intact sensation predicts better treatment response (sphincter botulinum toxin, muscarinic agonists). <!-- c118-r06 c118-a08 -->

### Management and natural history
- Timed and double voiding, pelvic floor physiotherapy and intermittent self-catheterisation. <!-- c118-r07 -->
- Muscarinic agonists are not used: little efficacy and severe cardiac depression including arrest. <!-- c118-a06 -->
- Intravesical electrotherapy stimulates mechanosensitive myelinated Aδ afferents, restoring sensation and possibly voiding. <!-- c118-a07 c118-r08 -->
- Untreated DUA in men showed no significant symptomatic or urodynamic deterioration over 10 years (Thomas). <!-- c118-a10 -->

### Nocturia: definitions and epidemiology
- Two or more voids a night cause bother and are clinically significant (one void is not). <!-- c118-a12 c118-r13 -->
- 20% of people aged 20–40 void twice or more nightly versus 60% over 70. Younger people tend to have low nocturnal capacity; older people overproduce urine. <!-- c118-r12 c118-r09 -->
- NUV (nocturnal urine volume) is all urine passed during the night plus the first morning void. The diary must record bedtime and waking time. <!-- c118-a20 c118-a13 -->
- Nocturnal polyuria: NPi (nocturnal polyuria index = NUV/24-hour volume) over 0.20 in young adults and over 0.33 over 65, or NUV over 0.9 mL/min (54 mL/h) or 1.5 mL/min (90 mL/h); no universal definition. Global polyuria: over 40 mL/kg in 24 hours. Example: NUV 1000 of 2000 mL gives NPi 0.5, nocturnal polyuria. <!-- c118-a11 c118-a15 -->

### Causes
- Third-spaced fluid returning on lying down, higher supine blood pressure and loss of the normal night-time rise in ADH (antidiuretic hormone). <!-- c118-r10 c118-r11 -->
- ADH is inhibited (diuresis) by PGE2, ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide), hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, lithium and tetracyclines (not hyperkalaemia). <!-- c118-a14 -->
- Obstructive sleep apnoea is a common cause. <!-- c118-r14 -->

### Treatment
- Desmopressin acts on renal V2 receptors: more water permeability and reabsorption, diluted extracellular fluid, concentrated urine. It suits nocturnal polyuria. <!-- c118-a17 c118-a18 -->
- Desmopressin can cause hyponatraemia; women are more sensitive. Over 65: monitor sodium closely and avoid with baseline hyponatraemia. <!-- c118-r15 c118-a21 -->
- After 6–12 months nocturia fell 7% with watchful waiting, 17% with α-blockers, 32% with TUMT (transurethral microwave therapy) and 75% with TURP. α-Blockers, 5-ARIs (5α-reductase inhibitors) and antimuscarinics give statistically significant but clinically minimal reductions. <!-- c118-a16 c118-a19 -->

## Ch 135 Tumours of the bladder

### Epidemiology
- Mean age at diagnosis in the USA is 73; incidence peaks in the seventh decade. Cancers in adolescents and young adults are usually well differentiated and non-invasive. <!-- c135-a01 c135-r20 c135-r03 -->
- 63% of cases are in developed countries (55% in North America and Europe); highest rates in Southern and Eastern Europe, parts of Africa, the Middle East and North America. <!-- c135-a03 -->
- Mortality has fallen 5% since 1990 (less smoking, fewer carcinogens). Lifetime risk for a white man is 3.7%, about 3 times white women or African American men. <!-- c135-a04 c135-a05 -->
- Third commonest cancer death in men over 80; about 31% of bladder cancer deaths were judged avoidable, more in non-invasive disease. <!-- c135-a08 -->
- Egypt: mortality 3 times Europe and 8 times North America because of squamous cancer; Schistosoma haematobium causes squamous cancer in endemic areas. <!-- c135-a07 c135-r17 -->
- Histology: 90% urothelial, 5% squamous, under 2% adenocarcinoma and others; urothelial cancer is the second commonest cause of genitourinary cancer death. <!-- c135-a06 c135-r22 -->

### Risk factors
- Smoking causes 30%–50% of male cases (2- to 6-fold risk); risk rises linearly with intensity and duration with no plateau. After stopping, relative risk is 2.6 at 1–3 years and 1.1 after 15 years. <!-- c135-a32 c135-r04 c135-a18 -->
- β-Naphthylamine (dye and rubber workers) was among the first chemicals implicated; NAT-2 and GSTM1 polymorphisms alter susceptibility. <!-- c135-a17 -->
- Cyclophosphamide is the only chemotherapy proven to cause bladder cancer (dose-related; metabolite phosphoramide mustard). <!-- c135-a12 c135-r24 -->
- High-dose radiation (over 500 mSv) doubles risk (relative risk 1.63 men, 1.74 women), latency 15–30 years, not age-related; nuclear and radiology workers are not at increased risk. <!-- c135-a19 c135-r26 -->
- First-degree relatives have twofold risk, probably via low-penetrance genes; higher in women and non-smokers, not from passive smoking. <!-- c135-a13 c135-a14 c135-r25 -->
- Healthy diet lowers risk. No convincing link with fluid intake, alcohol, sweeteners or analgesics; chronic irritation, infection and radiation are linked. <!-- c135-r05 c135-r06 -->
- Spinal cord injury: older series 2.5%–10% squamous cancer after a mean 17 years; recent risk only 0.38% with better catheter care. <!-- c135-a37 -->
- BCG plus oral vitamins A, B6, C, E and zinc may reduce recurrence (some evidence). <!-- c135-r21 -->

### Benign lesions
- Inverted papilloma: benign (about 1% recurrence), linked to chronic inflammation; sometimes coexists with urothelial cancer, more often in the upper tract; FISH (fluorescence in situ hybridisation) separates it from cancer with an inverted growth pattern. An unusual biopsy should be reviewed with the pathologist (can be mistaken for adenocarcinoma). <!-- c135-a02 c135-r01 c135-a40 -->
- Cystitis glandularis may accompany pelvic lipomatosis. Ureteral pseudodiverticulosis is associated with bladder cancer in 30%, so watch the bladder. <!-- c135-r02 c135-a42 -->

### Pathology and genetics
- Normal urothelium is under 7 cell layers; papillary lesions are thicker. At presentation 80% are non-muscle-invasive (CIS, low- or high-grade papillary, sessile). <!-- c135-r19 c135-a15 -->
- WHO grading: PUNLMP (papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential), low grade and high grade replace grades 1–3. PUNLMP: minimal atypia, often solitary on the trigone, no invasion or metastasis but frequent recurrence, so perioperative mitomycin C is given. <!-- c135-r08 c135-a16 -->
- Low-grade tumours: chromosome 9 loss (80% of PUNLMP), FGFR3 (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3), HRAS and PI3K changes; cytokeratin-20 and CD44 positive. <!-- c135-a11 c135-a21 c135-r07 c135-r13 -->
- High-grade and invasive: TP53, RB and PTEN loss (poor prognosis) and chromosome 17 loss; high-grade NMIBC has TP53 and RB deletions. All CIS is high grade with RB, TP53 and PTEN changes. <!-- c135-a09 c135-a10 c135-a25 c135-r12 c135-r23 -->
- Tumour suppressor genes are inactivated by loss of one allele then a point mutation in the other (book says 'activated'); oncogenes are activated by point mutation, amplification or translocation. <!-- c135-a27 -->
- MIBC (muscle-invasive bladder cancer) subtypes: basal tumours benefit most from neoadjuvant chemotherapy; DNA repair gene mutations (ERCC2, FANCC, ATM) predict response (SWOG S1314). <!-- c135-a26 -->
- Natural history: low-grade papillary tumours recur in 60% but progress in under 10%; high-grade NMIBC recurs in 80% and high-grade T1 progresses in up to 50%. Angiolymphatic invasion is a poor sign. <!-- c135-r10 c135-r11 -->

### Staging
- AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer) 2017: stage 0 non-invasive, I lamina propria, II muscle-invasive organ-confined, III locally advanced or node-positive without distant spread (nodes were stage IV before), IV metastatic. <!-- c135-a20 -->
- Muscularis propria visible on biopsy means at least T2. <!-- c135-a41 -->
- Repeat TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour) for T1 tumours even if muscle was present, because of understaging. <!-- c135-a22 c135-r27 -->

### Diagnosis
- Painless gross haematuria in 85%; investigate any episode even if later urine is clear. 50% have a cause, 20% a urological cancer and 12% bladder cancer. <!-- c135-a28 -->
- After a full negative work-up, cancer risk with recurrent haematuria is under 5% (near zero for 6 years). <!-- c135-a28 c135-r29 -->
- Microhaematuria: age under 40 is not a risk factor (smoking, pelvic radiation, UTI and urological surgery are); the book advises 6-monthly urinalysis, cytology and blood pressure for high-risk patients. **Update:** AUA/SUFU 2020 uses low/intermediate/high risk groups with imaging by risk; cytology is not routine. <!-- c135-a29 -->
- Urine FISH (UroVysion): aneuploidy of chromosomes 3, 7, 17 and 9p21 loss; sensitivity 79%, specificity 70%. Microsatellite analysis PCR-amplifies polymorphic repeats to detect loss of heterozygosity (sensitivity 72%–97%, specificity 80%–100%). No marker replaces cystoscopy. <!-- c135-a30 c135-a31 c135-r15 -->
- Blue-light (porphyrin) and narrow-band imaging increase cystoscopic sensitivity. <!-- c135-r14 -->
- CT urography with bladder masses and a ureteric filling defect needs cystoscopy and ureteroscopy (synchronous ureteral tumour). <!-- c135-a43 -->

### Prostatic urethral involvement
- 90% of prostatic urethral cancers occur with bladder cancer (mainly CIS, multifocal). Only 3% at first presentation; 15% at 5 years and 30% at 15 years after bladder cancer (with intravesical therapy); 40% at cystectomy. <!-- c135-a38 -->
- Risk factors: CIS of trigone, bladder neck or distal ureters, recurrent tumours, intravesical therapy; low-grade tumours rarely involve it. <!-- c135-a39 -->
- Non-invasive mucosal or ductal disease: complete TURP plus BCG induction and maintenance. Prognosis: good if mucosal, worse with stromal invasion, poor with direct invasion from the bladder. <!-- c135-a23 c135-r09 -->

### Variants and non-urothelial tumours
- Micropapillary and nested patterns carry poor prognosis. <!-- c135-r18 -->
- Micropapillary: surgery is the best treatment; TUR and BCG fail unless completely resected; neoadjuvant chemotherapy seems ineffective (book). <!-- c135-a33 c135-r30 -->
- Nested variant: male:female 6:1, mimics von Brunn nests, cystitis cystica and inverted papilloma; 70% die within 3 years. <!-- c135-a34 -->
- Small cell carcinoma (under 1%): treat as metastatic; very chemosensitive; chemoradiation or chemotherapy then cystectomy. <!-- c135-a24 c135-r28 -->
- Signet ring cell carcinoma: under 1%, may be urachal, presents high-grade and advanced, often peritoneal studding; CEA may rise; mean survival under 20 months despite cystectomy. <!-- c135-a36 -->
- Sarcomas, most to least common: leiomyosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, angiosarcoma, osteosarcoma, carcinosarcoma; male:female 2:1, sixth decade, linked to radiation and chemotherapy, not smoking. <!-- c135-a35 c135-r16 -->

## Ch 148 Epidemiology, aetiology and prevention of prostate cancer

### Incidence and mortality
- Commonest visceral cancer in US men and third leading cause of cancer death. <!-- c148-r01 -->
- SEER (Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results): incidence peaked in 1992 after PSA (prostate-specific antigen) arrived, fell until 1995, then fluctuated; falling since 2008. <!-- c148-a01 -->
- Mortality has fallen since 1991; for white men it is now below pre-PSA levels. PSA caused downward migration of age and stage. <!-- c148-r02 c148-r04 -->
- Incidence is highest in high-income countries with most screening; highest in African American and Jamaican men. African Americans: 186.8 per 100,000, 1.75 times white incidence and 2.2 times mortality; causes are multifactorial with a biological basis. <!-- c148-a03 c148-r03 c148-a02 -->

### Screening
- PSA screening reduces prostate cancer mortality, but the absolute benefit is small given the numbers needed to screen and treat. <!-- c148-r05 -->
- PLCO (Prostate, Lung, Colorectal and Ovarian) was criticised: 44% prescreened, poor biopsy compliance, 52% contamination of controls; analysed by actual screening, it matches ERSPC (European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer). <!-- c148-a04 -->

### Genetics
- Genetics and environment both matter. Family history raises risk 2–3 times, more with more and younger and closer relatives. <!-- c148-r06 c148-a05 -->
- GWAS (genome-wide association studies) found over 100 risk SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphisms); single loci predict little, so multi-SNP models are needed; none predict aggressiveness. <!-- c148-a05 c148-r07 c148-r08 -->
- HOXB13 and BRCA greatly raise risk; BRCA tumours are more aggressive. <!-- c148-r09 -->
- TMPRSS2-ERG fusion (ERG at 21q22.3) in about 50% of localised cancers puts ETS oncogenes under androgen control; it is highly cancer-specific. <!-- c148-a07 -->
- Seven molecular subtypes: four fusions (ERG, ETV1, ETV4, FLI1) and three mutations (SPOP, FOXA1, IDH1); methylation profiles further separate them. <!-- c148-r22 c148-r23 -->
- SPOP mutation in about 10%; a SPINK1-overexpressing subgroup is commoner in African Americans with higher Gleason scores. Genomics differ between African American and European American men. <!-- c148-a09 c148-r25 -->
- Epigenetic changes (methylation, histone modification and others) alter expression without changing DNA sequence. <!-- c148-a10 -->
- Stem cells with multilineage potential are the origin of clonal expansion. <!-- c148-r21 -->

### Inflammation, androgens and lifestyle
- Chronic inflammation (from diet, infection, oestrogens) causes regenerative proliferation; weak oxidant defences and PIA (proliferative inflammatory atrophy) are key steps. <!-- c148-r10 c148-r11 c148-r12 c148-r13 -->
- DHT (dihydrotestosterone) from type II 5α-reductase is the main prostatic androgen; androgen exposure before or at puberty is needed for cancer; intraprostatic levels are locally regulated, not set by serum levels; hypogonadal men can still get cancer. <!-- c148-a08 c148-r20 -->
- Androgens matter but no dose-response with serum levels exists; long-term absence protects. AR (androgen receptor) and androgen-metabolism gene polymorphisms may modify risk; AR drives development and progression. <!-- c148-r14 c148-r15 c148-r16 c148-r24 -->
- Vitamin D and its receptor modulate risk and aggressiveness. <!-- c148-r17 -->
- Smoking increases recurrence and prostate cancer death. <!-- c148-r18 -->
- Obesity: lower PSA (lower androgens, haemodilution) and bigger prostates, so fewer biopsies and more sampling error; more oxidative stress, higher free IGF-1, more high-grade disease, more treatment failure and death. <!-- c148-a06 c148-r19 -->

### Chemoprevention
- Primary chemoprevention aims to lower incidence, treatment harm and death, with non-toxic agents acting on specific steps; prostate cancer is a good target. <!-- c148-r26 c148-r27 c148-r28 -->
- PCPT (finasteride) and REDUCE (dutasteride) cut diagnosis by about 25% and were well tolerated with BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) benefit; extra high-grade cancers did not change mortality, and there was no effect on overall mortality. **Update:** the FDA declined a prevention indication because of the high-grade signal. <!-- c148-a12 c148-r29 -->
- SELECT: neither vitamin E nor selenium prevents prostate cancer (HR 1.13 vitamin E, 1.04 selenium, 1.05 both); longer follow-up showed vitamin E increases risk (HR 1.17). The book prints the vitamin E CI as 0.95–1.13; the correct interval is 0.95–1.35. <!-- c148-a11 c148-r30 -->
