## Ch 39 Renal Dysgenesis and Cystic Disease of the Kidney

### Dysgenesis and hypoplasia
- Potter facies: hypertelorism, prominent inner canthal folds, recessive chin. <!-- c039-r01 -->
- Dysplasia is histological: embryonic immature mesenchyme and primitive renal elements; often linked to ureteric bud abnormality and/or obstruction. <!-- c039-r02 -->
- Hypoplasia: fewer than normal calyces and nephrons, with no dysplasia. Oligomeganephronia: fewer nephrons with hypertrophy of the rest; many reach renal failure. <!-- c039-r03 c039-r04 -->
- Ask-Upmark kidney (segmental hypoplasia): mainly young women and girls; often with reflux; hallmark is severe hypertension (headache, encephalopathy, retinopathy); no renal artery intimal disease proven. <!-- c039-a02 c039-r05 -->

### Polycystic kidney disease
| | ADPKD (autosomal dominant PKD) | ARPKD (autosomal recessive PKD) |
|---|---|---|
| Genes | PKD1 chromosome 16p (short arm), PKD2 chromosome 4; PKD3 unmapped | Chromosome 6 |
| Liver | Cysts common; hepatic fibrosis rare | Congenital hepatic fibrosis in all, with variable biliary ectasia and periportal fibrosis |
| Onset | Clinically in 4th–5th decades; cysts usually on US by age 20; glomerular cysts in some newborns | Most severe form appears earliest, in newborns |
<!-- c039-a06 c039-a26 c039-a27 c039-r14 c039-a04 c039-r06 -->
- ARPKD ultrasound (fetus and newborn): bilateral very large, diffusely echogenic kidneys (brighter than liver), homogeneous, pyramids blend in. Echogenicity comes from innumerable microcysts (tightly packed dilated collecting ducts) creating many interfaces. <!-- c039-a29 c039-a04 -->
- Cyst formation needs (1) tubular epithelial proliferation, (2) fluid accumulation and (3) disturbed extracellular matrix. Net fluid gain comes from an imbalance of secretion and absorption: beyond the loop of Henle, cAMP (cyclic adenosine monophosphate) drives NaCl and fluid secretion, which wins when ENaC (epithelial sodium channel) reabsorption falls. <!-- c039-a05 -->
- ADPKD extrarenal features: cysts of liver, pancreas, spleen and lungs; berry aneurysms; colonic diverticula; aortic aneurysms; mitral valve prolapse. Cerebellar haemangioblastoma belongs to VHL, not ADPKD. RCC risk is not higher than in the general population. <!-- c039-r06 c039-a07 c039-a06 -->
- ADPKD pain (commonest adult symptom): mass effect, bleeding into a cyst (most typical), infection, stones. <!-- c039-a09 -->
- Infected cysts need lipid-soluble antibiotics: TMP-SMX (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole), chloramphenicol (best results), fluoroquinolones. Failure with a non-lipid-soluble drug suggests a non-communicating infected cyst. <!-- c039-a20 -->
- Pain management: if conservative care fails, consider US/CT-guided aspiration (diagnostic and therapeutic) or laparoscopic/open unroofing of large or multiple cysts. Surgery relieves symptoms but neither speeds nor slows renal decline. **Update:** tolvaptan (vasopressin V2 antagonist) slows eGFR decline in rapidly progressive ADPKD (TEMPO 3:4, REPRISE). <!-- c039-a21 -->

### Multicystic dysplastic kidney (MCDK)
- No identifiable normal parenchyma; commoner on the left at any age; unilateral MCDK commoner in males (2.4:1). <!-- c039-a08 c039-r12 -->
- Large series: no increased risk of hypertension or malignancy. <!-- c039-a14 c039-r12 -->
- Contralateral VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) 18%–43%, commoner than contralateral UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) obstruction. <!-- c039-a22 -->
- US versus hydronephrosis: MCDK cysts are haphazard, varied in size, with small cysts between large ones, no large central/medial cyst and no communications. UPJ obstruction: calyces arranged peripherally, connecting to a central pelvis, no small interposed cysts; an identifiable renal sinus favours hydronephrosis. <!-- c039-a24 -->

### Multilocular cyst, simple cysts and sinus cysts
- Benign multilocular cyst (some prefer "cystic nephroma", implying benign neoplasm). Spectrum: benign multilocular cyst, then multilocular cyst with Wilms nodules, then cystic Wilms (malignant). Adults also have multilocular cystic RCC, so remove multilocular cystic lesions. <!-- c039-a25 c039-r07 c039-r08 -->
- Age: presents before 4 years or after 30 years (only 5% between); under 4 it is 2× likelier male, over 30 8× likelier female. <!-- c039-a11 -->
- Simple cysts increase with age: about 20% by 40 years and about 33% after 60 (CT). Fetal renal cysts usually resolve before birth (25 of 28; one persisting was early MCDK). <!-- c039-a19 c039-a18 -->
- Simple cyst on US: no internal echoes; sharp thin smooth wall; good through-transmission with posterior acoustic enhancement; spherical/ovoid. CT: thin smooth wall, round/ovoid, homogeneous. <!-- c039-a30 -->
- Renal sinus cysts are mostly lymphatic in origin. <!-- c039-a17 -->

### Bosniak classification (book)
| Class | Features | Malignancy | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Simple, imperceptible wall | ~0% | None |
| II | Few thin septa or fine calcification; non-enhancing hyperdense lesion <3 cm | ~0% | None |
| IIF | More septa, minimal thickening, nodular/thick calcification, hyperdense >3 cm intrarenal | ~5% | Imaging follow-up (about 6 months) |

- **Update:** Bosniak 2019 adds MRI and defines wall/septal thickness and enhancement more precisely; contemporary malignancy rates for III are lower (about 50%) and many III lesions may be surveyed.

### Genetic syndromes
- TSC (tuberous sclerosis complex): chromosomes 9 (TSC1) and 16 (TSC2). Angiomyolipoma in 40%–80%; RCC about 2%. Diagnosis: 2 major features (e.g. renal angiomyolipoma plus cardiac rhabdomyoma; others: facial angiofibroma, ungual fibroma, ≥3 hypomelanotic macules, shagreen patch, retinal hamartomas, cortical tuber, subependymal nodule or giant cell astrocytoma, lymphangioleiomyomatosis) or 1 major plus 2 minor (multiple renal cysts, non-renal hamartoma, rectal polyps, retinal achromic patch, white-matter migration lines, bone cysts, gingival fibromas, confetti skin, enamel pits). <!-- c039-a01 c039-a13 c039-a15 c039-a27 -->
- VHL (von Hippel-Lindau): autosomal dominant, chromosome 3; cerebellar and retinal haemangioblastomas; cysts of pancreas, kidney and epididymis; epididymal cystadenoma; phaeochromocytoma; clear cell RCC (35%–38% in book). Renal cysts (76%), not RCC, are the commonest and earliest renal finding. VHL mutation is present in about 50% of sporadic clear cell RCC. <!-- c039-a10 c039-a13 c039-a16 c039-r11 -->
- VHL tumours are multiple, bilateral and recurrent: aim is control and nephron preservation, not cure. Nephron-sparing surgery is standard for low-grade tumours (e.g. bilateral upper pole tumours: bilateral partial nephrectomy); recurrence 75%–85% but 10-year disease-specific survival 81%–94% (versus about 50% historically after nephrectomy); high-grade disease may need bilateral nephrectomy; ablation under study. **Update:** belzutifan (HIF-2α inhibitor) is FDA-approved (2021) for VHL-associated RCC not needing immediate surgery. <!-- c039-a31 -->

### Medullary disorders and acquired cystic disease
- Juvenile NPH (nephronophthisis): usually autosomal recessive (chromosome 2), ESRD at mean 13 years, almost always before 25; 20% of families have extrarenal features (e.g. retinitis pigmentosa). MCKD (medullary cystic kidney disease): usually autosomal dominant, milder, renal only, ESRD in 3rd–4th decade, present in all by 50. Both cause polyuria and polydipsia in over 80% (less than diabetes insipidus). Shared histology triad: tubular basement membrane thickening and disintegration; tubular atrophy with cysts; interstitial inflammation and fibrosis. <!-- c039-a12 c039-r15 c039-a27 -->
- A child with this histology (interstitial nephritis-fibrosis, atrophic tubules, glomerular microcysts) has NPH: follow for hypertension and declining renal function. <!-- c039-a32 -->
- Medullary sponge kidney: dilated collecting ducts; hypercalciuria (one third), hypocitraturia and stones. <!-- c039-r09 c039-r13 -->
- ARCD (acquired renal cystic disease): related to duration of renal failure (a feature of ESRD), as common on peritoneal dialysis and even without dialysis. Presents with loin pain and/or haematuria; bleeding in up to 50%. <!-- c039-a03 c039-a23 -->
- Bilateral congenital absence of the vas: cystic fibrosis. Unilateral absence: may have ipsilateral renal agenesis. <!-- c039-r10 -->

## Ch 74 Sexual Function and Dysfunction in the Female

### Physiology and concepts
- Sexual health includes absence of dysfunction, mental well-being and human development and maturation; optimal health needs physical, emotional and mental well-being. <!-- c074-a01 c074-r07 -->
- Acetylcholine has only a minor role in female genital response (VIP and nitric oxide matter more). <!-- c074-a02 -->
- Vaginal pH 4–5, from lactic acid–producing flora. <!-- c074-r01 -->
- Testosterone comes from ovaries and adrenals and declines gradually through life (oestrogen and progesterone fall abruptly at menopause). <!-- c074-r02 -->
- Oestrogen keeps genital tissue thick and intact; deficiency impairs function through less vasocongestion and lubrication and more atrophy, not directly through desire. <!-- c074-r05 -->
- Sexual neutrality (receptive rather than initiating) is normal; women may follow a circular or linear response model. <!-- c074-r03 c074-a05 -->
- G-spot: anterior vaginal wall at the midurethra (Grafenberg described it as urethral); its anatomy remains controversial. <!-- c074-a16 -->

### Evaluation
- History is critical; assess the intimate relationship. Women with spinal cord injury can have orgasm. Up to half of women who have sex with women do not identify as lesbian or bisexual. <!-- c074-a05 -->
- Laboratory tests are rarely needed unless a specific medical cause is suspected. Random testosterone is useless (no female normal range; levels do not correlate with libido). If a hormonal cause is suspected, screen prolactin, thyroid and adrenal function; DHEA-S if adrenal insufficiency is suspected. <!-- c074-a17 -->
- Biothesiometry is rarely needed; history and examination give the same information. Vaginal photoplethysmography (vaginal pulse amplitude) is the most studied and validated physiological test. <!-- c074-a04 c074-a19 -->
- FSFI (Female Sexual Function Index): 19 items, 6 domains (desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, pain), past 4 weeks, 10–15 minutes; range 2–36; under 26.55 means at risk of dysfunction; validated in research, cancer and pelvic pain; a 6-item version screens pre- and postmenopausal women. It does not measure distress. <!-- c074-a03 c074-a18 -->

### DSM-5 classification and prevalence
- DSM-5 merged hypoactive desire and arousal disorders into female sexual interest/arousal disorder, and dyspareunia plus vaginismus into genitopelvic pain/penetration disorder; FOD (female orgasmic disorder) carried over. <!-- c074-a10 -->
- Criteria (except genitopelvic pain disorder): present 6 months, on at least 75% of occasions, causing clinically significant distress, not due to another mental disorder, relationship distress, stressors, drugs or illness. <!-- c074-a20 -->
- FOD no longer requires a "normal excitement phase"; it often coexists with other problems (arousal 31%, lubrication 18%, desire 14%, pain 12%, vaginismus 0.9%); questionnaires alone cannot diagnose it but track response. <!-- c074-a23 -->
- PRESIDE study (31,531 respondents): low desire 37.7%; distressing low desire (HSDD) 8.9% at 18–44, 12.3% at 45–64, 7.4% over 65. The book keys arousal disorder as most prevalent; **Update:** its own PRESIDE data show low desire is the most prevalent problem. <!-- c074-a21 -->
- PGAD (persistent genital arousal disorder): spontaneous, intrusive, unwanted genital arousal without desire, lasting hours to days, not relieved by orgasm, triggered by sexual, non-sexual or no stimulus, distressing. <!-- c074-a24 -->
- PVD (provoked vestibulodynia): primary (pain from first penetration attempt; early onset, longer duration, family history, enuresis, dysmenorrhoea; more nerve fibres and thicker vestibule; less inflammation; commoner in Hispanic women) versus secondary (acquired after pain-free period; larger area, worst at clitoral hood; commoner, 65% vs 35%). Pelvic floor hypertonicity is common. <!-- c074-a25 -->

### Causes
- Low desire: antidepressants, life stressors, hypoestrogenism, relationship problems. Depression is the one unambiguously linked condition. <!-- c074-a08 c074-a13 -->
- Hormonal contraception, prolactin, surgical menopause and raised SHBG (sex hormone–binding globulin) lower bioavailable testosterone; this is the main proposed mechanism for contraceptive-related problems. <!-- c074-a11 c074-a15 c074-r08 -->
- Incontinent women are up to 3 times more likely to have low arousal, infrequent orgasm and dyspareunia. <!-- c074-r04 -->
- SSRIs inhibit desire, arousal and orgasm (30%–70% report dysfunction). <!-- c074-r06 c074-a23 -->

### Treatment
- Treat only bothersome problems (e.g. distressing loss of desire); not climaxing with penetration alone is normal; lubricant safely manages dryness. <!-- c074-a09 -->
- Antidepressant-associated dysfunction: evidence for adjunctive antidepressant, cessation, drug holiday and sildenafil; reassurance has least evidence. <!-- c074-a14 -->
- Muscle relaxants help sexual pain but not arousal. <!-- c074-a12 -->
- Testosterone may help some women; low testosterone is not clearly linked to worse function in all women. Adverse effects: hirsutism, acne, lower HDL, vaginal bleeding; cardiovascular and cancer risks uncertain. Systemic testosterone (± oestrogen) and tibolone improve desire, arousal and orgasm in postmenopausal women with low hormone levels. <!-- c074-a06 c074-a07 c074-a23 -->
- Flibanserin 100 mg at bedtime: postsynaptic 5-HT1A agonist and 5-HT2A antagonist, lowers serotonin and raises dopamine and noradrenaline; for premenopausal acquired generalised HSDD. **Update:** bremelanotide (melanocortin agonist, on-demand injection) was also FDA-approved in 2019, so flibanserin is no longer the only approved drug. <!-- c074-a22 -->

## Ch 77 Surgery of Testicular Tumors

### Orchiectomy and staging
- Radical inguinal orchiectomy: the ilioinguinal nerve lies on the cord immediately under the external oblique (injury: numb medial thigh and scrotum); mobilise the cord 1–2 cm above the internal ring and ligate vas and vessels separately with silk so the stump can be found at RPLND (retroperitoneal lymph node dissection). <!-- c077-a01 c077-r01 -->
- After scrotal orchiectomy: low-stage seminoma, extend radiation fields to groin and scrotum; low-stage NSGCT (non-seminomatous germ cell tumour), excise scrotal scar with cord remnant; after full platinum chemotherapy, remove only the cord stump at RPLND. <!-- c077-r02 -->
- Partial orchiectomy suits a suspected benign tumour (also solitary testis or bilateral tumours); not for a large tumour with a normal contralateral testis. <!-- c077-a02 -->
- Staging: orchiectomy (T), chest X-ray or CT, CT/MRI abdomen-pelvis (N, M), post-orchiectomy markers (S). PET has no role in initial staging; it is used for post-chemotherapy seminoma residual masses over 3 cm. <!-- c077-a03 -->
- Lymphatics: right testis to interaortocaval, then paracaval/precaval nodes; left to para-aortic and preaortic nodes. Crossover is commoner from right-sided tumours. <!-- c077-r03 c077-r04 -->

### Clinical stage I and primary RPLND
- Clinical stage I: 20%–30% have positive nodes; about 25% relapse on surveillance, mostly within 2 years, rarely after 5. Risk factors: LVI (lymphovascular invasion), higher T stage, cord/capsule/scrotal involvement, high embryonal carcinoma percentage. Absence of teratoma in the primary does not exclude it in the retroperitoneum. <!-- c077-r05 c077-r13 -->
- Persistently raised markers after orchiectomy need chemotherapy, not RPLND. Best RPLND candidates: stage IIA and low-volume IIB under 3 cm. <!-- c077-r14 c077-r15 -->
- Suprahilar metastases are rare in low-stage NSGCT; 3%–23% of node-positive patients have extra-template disease; commonest residual suprahilar site is retrocrural. <!-- c077-r06 -->
- Bilateral template is standard for pathological stage II and for documented or suspected metastases; nerve fibres may be preserved selectively. <!-- c077-r08 c077-r17 -->
- After primary RPLND: reliable pN1 patients can be observed; pN2 is debated (2 cycles adjuvant or observation). No other histological variable reliably predicts relapse, except teratoma-only nodes, which have very low relapse and are observed. Observed pathological IIA–B patients relapse in 30%–50%. Adjuvant BEP×2 or EP×2 nearly eliminates relapse but does not improve cancer-specific or overall survival (about 100% either way); relapse needs full BEP×3 or EP×4. <!-- c077-a14 c077-a17 -->

### Surgical anatomy and technique
- Ureters mark the lateral borders of the para-aortic and paracaval packets; find the ureter at the lower pole, passing behind the gonadal vein; it crosses anterior to the common iliac artery. <!-- c077-a06 c077-r29 -->
- Three paired lumbar arteries lie between renal hilum and aortic bifurcation in nearly all patients (sometimes a short common trunk); lumbar veins and the 4 postganglionic sympathetic roots per side vary; node counts vary widely. <!-- c077-a07 c077-r30 -->
- Splitting over the aorta first lets you see accessory right lower pole renal arteries, but risks sympathetic fibres; stop at the IMA (inferior mesenteric artery) and identify efferents first. The split over the IVC is unlikely to harm nerves. <!-- c077-a05 c077-r09 -->
- Antegrade ejaculation (emission and bladder neck closure) needs L1–L4 postganglionic sympathetic fibres; identify them before dissecting on the aorta. Erection is parasympathetic and not needed for ejaculation. <!-- c077-a22 c077-r10 -->
- Cisterna chyli lies posteromedial to the aorta just above the right renal artery: clip or tie lymphatics there, at the top of the packets and in retrocrural dissection, to prevent chylous ascites. <!-- c077-a08 c077-r07 -->
- If the aortic wall is stripped of adventitia, replace with a graft (delayed rupture). With complete or near-complete IVC occlusion, routine caval reconstruction is not needed. <!-- c077-r23 c077-r27 -->

### Post-chemotherapy RPLND (PC-RPLND)
- Indicated for NSGCT residual masses over 1 cm; may be omitted if the retroperitoneum was never involved. Resect with a complete bilateral dissection. SMA, coeliac or porta hepatis involvement usually precludes resection. <!-- c077-r21 c077-r24 -->
- Residual mass histology: after first-line chemotherapy necrosis/fibrosis 40%, teratoma 45%, viable GCT 15%; after salvage chemotherapy viable 50%, teratoma 40%, fibrosis 10%. Teratoma is unpredictable, so resect completely. <!-- c077-r20 -->
- Rising AFP/hCG after first-line chemotherapy usually means unresectable viable tumour: salvage chemotherapy (standard or high-dose), not surgery. <!-- c077-r19 c077-a18 -->
- Desperation PC-RPLND (declining or plateaued AFP, slowly rising AFP after complete serological response, all chemotherapy options exhausted): 53.9% cancer-specific survival at 6 years. <!-- c077-a18 -->
- Modified unilateral template candidates: well-defined mass ≤5 cm in the primary landing zone before and after chemotherapy, normal markers, IGCCCG (International Germ Cell Cancer Collaborative Group) good or intermediate risk. Results: in-field recurrence 0%–1%, cancer-specific survival 98%–100%, antegrade ejaculation in over 85%. <!-- c077-a13 -->
- Auxiliary procedures in 23%–45%, most often nephrectomy then vascular. Nephrectomy (5%–31%) is likelier with salvage, desperation, reoperative or late-relapse surgery, large masses and left primaries. <!-- c077-a09 c077-a10 -->
- Residual liver lesions: about 75% necrosis only; 94% concordance with retroperitoneal necrosis, so observe them if retroperitoneal necrosis would mean morbid hepatic resection. Retroperitoneal necrosis predicts necrotic chest lesions 89% of the time. <!-- c077-a11 c077-r28 -->
- Pelvic disease is rare except after groin surgery, repeat RPLND or late relapse (Indiana: 134 of 2722; 14% prior groin surgery, 98% prior chemotherapy, 19.4% prior RPLND, 24% late relapse). <!-- c077-a12 -->
- Higher post-surgery recurrence: somatic-type malignancy and mediastinal primary. Immature teratoma has no worse outcome than mature. Viable GCT at PC-RPLND: survival is predicted by completeness of resection, proportion viable and IGCCCG risk (Fizazi); prior salvage chemotherapy is worst. <!-- c077-a15 c077-a16 -->
- No benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy after salvage chemotherapy with viable GCT, teratoma only or somatic transformation. <!-- c077-r31 -->
- Seminoma residual masses rarely contain teratoma and are hard to remove: observe under 3 cm; PET if over 3 cm; viable seminoma means more chemotherapy or RPLND. <!-- c077-r25 -->

### Reoperation and late relapse
- Retroperitoneal recurrence after RPLND usually reflects technical failure (landing-zone recurrence, unligated lumbars, unresected gonadal vein, disease behind great vessels). Pelvic relapse does not, as pelvic nodes are not in the template. <!-- c077-a19 -->
- Late relapse (2%–4%, over half beyond 10 years) needs long follow-up. After prior chemotherapy it is often yolk sac tumour and chemorefractory, so resect resectable disease; chemotherapy-naive late relapse responds better to chemotherapy. <!-- c077-r22 c077-a20 c077-r32 -->

### Complications and fertility
- Bleomycin causes restrictive pulmonary fibrosis and fluid sensitivity; postoperative ARDS (acute respiratory distress syndrome) is rare, likelier after 4 cycles and with pulmonary disease; risks are high FiO2 and high-volume fluids/blood (Trendelenburg is not a factor). <!-- c077-a04 c077-r18 -->
- Chylous ascites is usually transient: drain, MCT (medium-chain triglyceride) diet, then TPN, ± octreotide; peritoneovenous shunt last. Preoperative fat restriction does not prevent it. <!-- c077-a23 -->
- Spinal cord ischaemia is very rare; risk if more than 3 sequential lumbar artery pairs are divided (bulky para-aortic, retrocrural and mediastinal disease). <!-- c077-a24 -->
- Platinum chemotherapy brings a sevenfold rise in cardiovascular complications. <!-- c077-r11 -->
- Infertility or subfertility is present at diagnosis in 20%–60%; 60% have subnormal semen; offer sperm banking before RPLND. 65% on surveillance father children after orchiectomy. Spermatogenesis may take 3 years to recover after chemotherapy. <!-- c077-a21 c077-r12 c077-r16 c077-r26 -->

## Ch 90 Upper Urinary Tract Trauma

### Renal trauma: evaluation
- Suspect blunt renal injury with a significant flank blow, lower rib fractures, flank ecchymosis or a fall from height. Rapid deceleration injures fixed points: UPJ and renal hilum (renal artery intimal tear). <!-- c090-a01 c090-r03 -->
- Gross haematuria is the best indicator of significant injury, but its absence does not exclude it, and degree of haematuria does not track severity. <!-- c090-r01 c090-r04 -->
- Children: higher risk of renal injury, often stay normotensive despite major blood loss, more renal anomalies. <!-- c090-r02 -->
- Image: all penetrating trauma; rapid deceleration; blunt trauma with gross haematuria; blunt with microhaematuria and hypotension; children with haematuria. Adults with microhaematuria and no shock can be observed. <!-- c090-r05 c090-r06 -->
- CT with IV contrast plus delayed images at 10–15 minutes (to show collecting system and ureter). Deep laceration in a stable patient: get delayed images; no surgery or embolisation needed. <!-- c090-a07 c090-a09 -->
- CT signs of major injury: medial laceration, large perinephric haematoma, intravascular contrast extravasation, medial urinary extravasation, devitalised fragments. Absent enhancement of the whole kidney means main renal artery occlusion (intimal injury). <!-- c090-r07 c090-a10 -->

### Renal trauma: management
- Non-operative management is preferred in stable patients, especially grades I–III. **Update:** AAST (American Association for the Surgery of Trauma) 2018 grading adds vascular injury and active bleeding; most grade IV–V injuries in stable patients are also managed non-operatively or with angioembolisation. <!-- c090-r08 -->
- Low-velocity renal gunshot wounds: may be observed if stable, contained haematoma, isolated parenchymal injury and no intra-abdominal injury. <!-- c090-r09 -->
- Absolute indications for exploration: haemodynamic instability with shock, expanding or pulsatile haematoma, suspected pedicle avulsion, uncontained retroperitoneal haematoma. Relative: large perinephric haematoma, intravascular extravasation, devitalised fragments, arterial thrombosis, collecting system extravasation. <!-- c090-r10 c090-r11 -->
- Damage control in unstable patients: control bleeding and faecal/urinary leak only; for the ureter, ligate plus nephrostomy, externalise or stent; repair later. <!-- c090-r12 -->
- Post-injury hypertension is usually short-lived; also from vascular injury or Page kidney (subcapsular haematoma compression). <!-- c090-r13 -->

### Ureteral injury
- Causes: persisting with stone basketing after a ureteral tear (stop and stent); gynaecological laparoscopy, especially electrosurgical or laser lysis of endometriosis; aortic grafts near the ureter (periureteral inflammation, stenosis). <!-- c090-a04 c090-a08 c090-r15 -->
- Ureteroscopic injury: stent; if not possible or inadequate, nephrostomy. Delayed injury in a stable patient: retrograde urogram is the best test. <!-- c090-r21 c090-r18 -->
- Repair principles: debride to bleeding edges; spatulated, tension-free, stented, watertight anastomosis; closed suction drain; isolate the repair with omentum or peritoneum (from colon or vascular graft). <!-- c090-a03 c090-r14 c090-r20 c090-r22 -->
- Timing: repair within a week or defer 6 weeks or more. <!-- c090-r19 -->
- Upper and mid ureter: ureteroureterostomy (up to 32% of a large series; success up to 90%); gap bridged depends on mobilisation and elasticity. Distal: reimplant (psoas hitch or Boari flap for long gaps; ureteroureterostomy in selected cases). <!-- c090-a02 c090-r16 c090-r17 -->
- High-velocity gunshot contusion: excise and do ureteroureterostomy (blast injury causes necrosis or stricture). Low-velocity contusion: stent. <!-- c090-a06 -->
- Transureteroureterostomy is contraindicated with a history of stones (a distal stone would endanger both kidneys). <!-- c090-a05 -->

## Ch 119 Nocturia

### Definitions
- Nocturia: voiding preceded and followed by sleep; prevalence rises with age; impairs sleep (efficiency, latency, slow-wave sleep), linked to falls and mortality. Two or more voids a night cause bother and reduced QoL (quality of life). <!-- c119-a02 c119-r05 -->
- 20% of people aged 20–40 void twice or more at night versus 60% of those 70 or older. Younger people more often have reduced nocturnal bladder capacity; older people overproduce urine. <!-- c119-r04 c119-r01 -->
- NUV (nocturnal urine volume): all urine passed during the night plus the first morning void. <!-- c119-a10 -->
- Nocturnal polyuria: NPi (nocturnal polyuria index = NUV ÷ 24-hour volume) over 0.20 in young adults and over 0.33 over 65 years, with normal 24-hour output; other definitions NUV over 0.9 mL/min (54 mL/h) or over 1.5 mL/min (90 mL/h); no universal definition. Global polyuria: over 40 mL/kg per 24 hours. <!-- c119-a01 c119-a05 -->
- Frequency-volume chart: record bedtime and waking time (and volumes) to count nocturnal voids and NUV; fluid intake is helpful but not essential. <!-- c119-a03 -->

### Pathophysiology
- Fluid third-spacing returning to circulation when lying down; higher supine arterial pressure; loss of the normal nocturnal rise in ADH (antidiuretic hormone). Obstructive sleep apnoea is a common cause. <!-- c119-r02 c119-r03 c119-r06 -->
- ADH is inhibited (causing diuresis) by PGE2, ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide), hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, lithium and tetracyclines (not hyperkalaemia). <!-- c119-a04 -->

### Treatment
- Desmopressin acts on V2 receptors in collecting ducts: raises water permeability and reabsorption, dilutes extracellular fluid, concentrates urine. Best for nocturnal polyuria. <!-- c119-a07 c119-a08 -->
- Hyponatraemia risk: women are more sensitive (lower doses); over 65, monitor sodium closely and avoid with baseline hyponatraemia. <!-- c119-r07 c119-a11 -->
- At 6–12 months nocturia fell 7% with watchful waiting, 17% with α-blockers, 32% with TUMT and 75% with TURP; QoL gains followed nocturia reduction. α-Blockers, 5-ARIs, antimuscarinics and combinations reduce episodes significantly but with minimal clinical value. <!-- c119-a06 c119-a09 -->

## Ch 125 Slings: Autologous, Biologic, Synthetic and Midurethral

### Theory and evaluation
- Integral theory: pubourethral ligaments, suburethral vaginal hammock and pubococcygeus preserve continence; injury from surgery, childbirth, ageing or hormone loss impairs midurethral function. <!-- c125-a01 -->
- Female urethra has four layers: the middle muscular layer keeps resting closure and the outer seromuscular layer augments it; fast-twitch sphincter fibres give sudden protection and slow-twitch fibres passive guarding. Levator ani, urethropelvic ligament and pubocervical fascia support the bladder neck; the round ligament supports the uterus. Maximum urethral closure pressure is at the midurethra. <!-- c125-a06 c125-r06 -->
- ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency) is considered the primary cause of SUI (stress urinary incontinence); hypermobility is secondary (many hypermobile women are dry). <!-- c125-a12 -->
- AUA: urinalysis and PVR (post-void residual) in all; imaging not routine; do a focused neurological examination. Index patient options (book: all strongly recommended): synthetic MUS (midurethral sling), autologous PVS (pubovaginal sling), Burch, bulking agents. <!-- c125-a02 c125-a03 -->
- Obese women should first be offered weight loss; trocar bladder injury is commoner in non-obese women. <!-- c125-a13 c125-r16 -->

### Pubovaginal (bladder neck) slings
- Slings are the procedure of choice for female SUI and are particularly useful for ISD. PVS is placed at the bladder neck. <!-- c125-r01 c125-r02 c125-r03 -->
- PVS indications: ISD with hypermobility, SUI with cystocele, with diverticulum, after MUS urethral damage, with urethral defects needing reconstruction (e.g. fistula), neurogenic SUI. Avoid PVS when low outlet resistance coexists with poor compliance: treat compliance first (risk to upper tracts). <!-- c125-a05 -->
- Materials: ideal graft is biocompatible with fibroblast ingrowth and neovascularisation; autologous tissue has the lowest erosion and infection; synthetic materials degrade least and show most ingrowth; freeze-dried fascia lata has lower stiffness and load to failure than autologous, solvent-dehydrated and dermal grafts; porcine SIS is weaker than cadaveric fascia; allograft HIV risk 1 in 1,667,600 and CJD 1 in 3.5 million. Synthetic material is no longer used for bladder neck slings (perforation 15 times and vaginal exposure 14 times commoner; urethral perforation presents at mean 9 months with retention, urgency, mixed leakage). <!-- c125-a04 c125-a07 c125-a10 c125-r12 c125-r14 -->
- Tension the sling after removing the weighted speculum; close the abdomen after tensioning. <!-- c125-a08 -->
- Cure 24%–97%; no factor predicts outcome. SISTEr: PVS cured more than Burch but caused more UTI, voiding difficulty and urge incontinence. <!-- c125-a09 -->
- Voiding dysfunction after continence surgery 2.5%–35%; urgency/urge leakage (8%–25%) is commoner than retention and is the commonest cause of dissatisfaction. Transient retention usually resolves within 10 days; wait about 3 months before surgery after autologous PVS (shorter after MUS). First conservative: catheter, CIC, timed or double voiding, biofeedback, pelvic floor training, anticholinergics. Urodynamics do not predict outcome but help diagnose obstruction; recurrent SUI after urethrolysis 0%–18%. <!-- c125-a11 c125-a21 c125-r05 c125-r13 -->
- Obstruction may present with retention, urgency, irritative symptoms, recurrent UTI, suprapubic pain or painful voiding. Subclinical impaired contractility, dysfunctional voiding and habitual straining raise risk; most needing CIC after PVS had a neurogenic bladder. <!-- c125-a15 c125-a18 c125-r04 -->

### Midurethral slings
- TVT (tension-free vaginal tape): retropubic trocars (suprapubic or vaginal approach), type 1 macroporous monofilament mesh, loose tension, no suture fixation, cystoscopy mandatory. MUS works by stabilising the posterior urethral wall (anterior/anteroinferior motion and lumen narrowing). <!-- c125-a14 c125-a30 c125-r10 -->
- Success needs urethral mobility; lower with fixed urethra or low leak-point pressure. Durable: 5-, 7- and over 10-year results match 1-year results; better than laparoscopic colposuspension (open and laparoscopic Burch similar); less voiding dysfunction than colposuspension or PVS; mixed incontinence results acceptable but lower. <!-- c125-a17 c125-r07 c125-r08 -->
- Elderly: more postoperative urgency, but similar cure, satisfaction, mixed incontinence resolution and retention. Salvage MUS: efficacy similar to primary, possibly more bladder perforation, less success with less mobility. With prolapse surgery: safe and effective, but tensioning for occult SUI is hard. <!-- c125-a20 c125-a23 c125-a22 -->
- Recurrent SUI: meta-analysis of 10 RCTs found no difference in subjective cure between retropubic and transobturator MUS. Recurrent SUI after MUS: evidence poor (no trial met Cochrane criteria); urodynamics and cystoscopy help; bulking is inferior to repeat MUS; tightening unsupported. Periurethral bulking has limited success. <!-- c125-a28 c125-a43 c125-r11 -->
- Transobturator sling: passes obturator internus, obturator membrane and obturator externus (not rectus fascia); stays above the perineal membrane, outside the true pelvis, not through levator ani; adductor longus tendon passage causes pain; gracilis and adductor brevis may be traversed; obturator vessels lie superolateral; dorsal clitoral nerve 1–2 cm away. Outside-in or inside-out both acceptable; cystoscopy still needed (bladder, urethra, even ureter can be injured). Outcomes similar to retropubic, also with ISD; less hypermobility lowers success. More thigh/groin pain, which lasts longer (most resolves by day 2). <!-- c125-a16 c125-a19 c125-a37 c125-a38 c125-a39 c125-a40 -->
- Dorsal clitoral nerves cross under the pubis about 1.4 cm from midline: keep retropubic trocars at least 2 cm lateral. <!-- c125-a27 -->

### MUS complications
- Bladder perforation up to about 5%; voiding dysfunction 4%–20%; de novo urgency up to 12%; wound problems about 1%. Trocar injury is commoner with retropubic slings (case reports with transobturator too). Bowel and vascular injury can be lethal; perivesical vessel bleeding. <!-- c125-a24 c125-a31 c125-a34 -->
- Haematoma over 5 cm in 12.9% (Tseng); nearly all resolve; bleeding mostly with retropubic slings. <!-- c125-a36 -->
- Wound infection: vaginal 2% (Ward) and 0.7% (Richter); necrotising fasciitis linked to obesity (88%), hypertension (65%) and diabetes (47%). <!-- c125-a41 -->
- De novo dyspareunia 3%–14.5%; sexual function may improve (less coital leakage) or worsen; sling removal can relieve dyspareunia. <!-- c125-a35 -->
- MUS obstruction is usually transient; manage conservatively for days to a month, then release; midline incision of the sling is enough. Release resolves symptoms with continence kept in most. <!-- c125-a32 c125-a33 c125-r09 c125-r17 -->
- Exposure and perforation are reduced by macroporous monofilament mesh (rates under 5%–10%); tension may still matter; they lower satisfaction. Remove or cover all exposed material (endoscopic removal possible if complete; small vaginal exposures may heal with oestrogen or closure); mesh in urethra or bladder must be removed. For urethral perforation use an inverted-U vaginal incision (exposure plus non-overlapping flap), not midline. <!-- c125-a26 c125-a29 c125-a42 c125-r15 -->
- IUGA/ICS terms: synthetic mesh is a prosthesis, biological material a graft; mesh in the urinary tract is perforation; through skin or vagina is exposure. <!-- c125-a25 -->
- FDA: 2011 panel deemed MUS safe and effective; 2012 ordered 522 post-market studies for prolapse mesh and single-incision slings; 2016 reclassified transvaginal prolapse mesh to class III (PMA required); full-length MUS and sacrocolpopexy mesh remain class II. **Update:** in 2019 the FDA ordered sale of transvaginal prolapse mesh to stop. <!-- c125-a44 c125-a45 -->

## Ch 162 Treatment of Castration-Resistant Prostate Cancer

### Concepts and monitoring
- "Castration-resistant" replaced "hormone-resistant" because ADT is first-line, the AR (androgen receptor) still drives growth at castrate testosterone (under 50 ng/dL), further AR-targeted therapy still works, and castration resistance is not hormone resistance. <!-- c162-a01 -->
- Castration resistance is defined by PSA or disease progression on primary and secondary hormonal therapy, found by careful clinical, radiological and PSA monitoring, not by Gleason score or extent. Most men on hormones eventually progress and need chemotherapy. <!-- c162-a02 -->
- Androgen ablation induces apoptosis. The AR can be activated by oestrogens, progestins, growth factors and cytokines; AR signalling is upregulated in CRPC. These patients are not androgen-independent: continue ADT. <!-- c162-r01 c162-r02 c162-r03 c162-r14 c162-a05 -->
- PSADT (PSA doubling time) predicts bone scan progression and survival; under 3 months means a rapid course. Progression-free survival is a better endpoint than response rate; PSA may not reflect drug efficacy (circulating tumour cells may be better). <!-- c162-r04 c162-r05 c162-r06 -->

### Chemotherapy
- Docetaxel every 3 weeks (up to 10 cycles) with prednisone is first-line chemotherapy: longer survival and better QoL than mitoxantrone, regardless of age, performance status or pain; fit patients without visceral disease and with normal haemoglobin and LDH often survive over 2 years. Toxicity: fatigue, myelosuppression, neuropathy, lacrimation, nail changes. Do not stop for a PSA rise in the first 3–4 cycles (early flare). <!-- c162-a03 c162-r07 -->
- Cabazitaxel (active in docetaxel-resistant models): TROPIC showed longer survival than mitoxantrone after docetaxel, including early progressors; survival over 12 months possible; less neuropathy, fatigue, lacrimation and nail change but more neutropenic fever and diarrhoea than docetaxel. <!-- c162-a04 -->

### AR-targeted therapy
- Abiraterone (CYP17 inhibitor) blocks adrenal and intratumoural androgen synthesis; benefits before and after docetaxel (more so before). Causes mineralocorticoid excess (hypertension, hypokalaemia), so given with prednisone (occasionally a mineralocorticoid antagonist); also fatigue, abnormal liver tests, possible cardiac toxicity, drug interactions. <!-- c162-a05 c162-r08 -->
- Enzalutamide: greater AR affinity than flutamide, bicalutamide, nilutamide and blocks nuclear translocation and DNA binding; superior to placebo. <!-- c162-a05 -->
- M0 CRPC: apalutamide and enzalutamide approved, not abiraterone (M1 only); enzalutamide also for M1 CRPC. **Update:** darolutamide is also approved for M0 CRPC (ARAMIS); PARP inhibitors (e.g. olaparib, PROfound) for HRR-mutated mCRPC and Lu-177-PSMA-617 (VISION) after ARPI and taxane are now standard options. <!-- c162-a10 c162-r16 -->

### Immunotherapy and radiopharmaceuticals
- Sipuleucel-T (autologous vaccine): for asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic metastatic CRPC; very safe; no symptom relief, PSA fall or delay in progression, but prolongs survival versus placebo. <!-- c162-a09 c162-r15 -->
- Radium-223 (α-emitter): survival benefit over best supportive care; α particles are about 7000 times heavier than β, need 1–2 hits to kill, travel under 100 µm, so marrow toxicity is low. For bone metastases with haemoglobin over 10 g/dL (book writes g/L) and nodes under 3 cm; approved with or without prior docetaxel. β-emitters strontium-89 and samarium-153 palliate pain but can cause severe myelotoxicity. <!-- c162-a08 c162-r12 -->

### Complications of advanced disease
- Back pain with bone metastases: assess for cord or root compression; give high-dose dexamethasone, get early MRI, then radiotherapy or decompression. Do not assume stability from PSA or bone scan; systemic therapy does not treat compression. <!-- c162-a06 -->
- Cord compression in a man not yet castrated: immediate orchiectomy, or aminoglutethimide or ketoconazole with high-dose steroids; consider laminectomy and radiotherapy. **Update:** a GnRH antagonist (degarelix) gives rapid castration without flare; aminoglutethimide is obsolete. <!-- c162-r10 -->
- Bone metastases are usually blastic; hypercalcaemia is rare. Bisphosphonates or denosumab reduce skeletal events, with calcium and vitamin D. <!-- c162-r09 c162-r11 -->
- Neuroendocrine/anaplastic transformation: rapid visceral and brain spread, low or no PSA (PSA stain and AR often negative), hormone-resistant; biopsy changes management. Small-cell tumours get platinum chemotherapy; non-small-cell anaplastic tumours taxanes. <!-- c162-a07 c162-r13 -->
