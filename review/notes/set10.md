## Ch 32 Prune-Belly Syndrome

### Antenatal picture
- Antenatal ultrasound: dilated urinary tract, large full bladder, abnormal abdominal circumference; penis normal for age but scrotum empty (abdominal testes). Prune-belly syndrome (PBS) is not associated with ambiguous genitalia. <!-- c032-a01 -->
- Bilateral hydronephrosis with a distended bladder is usually well tolerated and is not proof of obstruction; urinary ascites or pulmonary hypoplasia alone do not justify fetal drainage. Only progressive oligohydramnios (suggesting an atretic urethra with functioning kidneys) justifies fetal bladder decompression. <!-- c032-a02 -->

### Urinary tract
- Kidneys: variable dysplasia (one or both), not proportional to abdominal flaccidity, dilatation or reflux; bilateral dysplasia compromises survival. <!-- c032-a03 -->
- Hydroureteronephrosis is often severe but calyces may be well preserved; it rarely affects initial renal function. <!-- c032-r01 c032-a03 -->
- Dilatation is not proportional to abdominal wall laxity; the lower ureter is more dilated than the upper, with or without reflux; the proximal ureter has more normal muscle. Collagen-thickened pelvis or ureteral folds can mimic UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) stenosis. <!-- c032-a07 c032-r02 -->
- Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) in two-thirds. Urachal diverticulum (pseudodiverticulum) is common; rarely a patent urachus drains the bladder when the urethra is atretic. <!-- c032-a03 c032-r03 -->
- Bladder: large, thick-walled (more collagen, fewer smooth muscle cells), high capacity and compliance, poor sensation and contraction, low voiding pressure; emptying relies on abdominal straining, so large residuals are common. <!-- c032-a08 -->
- Bladder neck is wide open (not elevated or hypertrophied); posterior urethra dilated from lack of prostatic support even with a normal distal urethra. Absent bladder neck hypertrophy distinguishes PBS from posterior urethral valves. <!-- c032-a03 c032-a09 c032-r03 -->
- Megalourethra is commoner in PBS than any other syndrome, from segmental lack of spongiosum (not distal stenosis): scaphoid type (ventral urethra balloons on voiding) or fusiform type (whole phallus dilates). <!-- c032-a09 c032-r04 -->

### Non-urinary features
- Abdominal flaccidity is the hallmark: uneven deficiency (usually medial and lower), thin muscle interwoven with aponeurosis; impairs breathing and causes pectus carinatum or excavatum; lack of abdominal pressure contributes to cryptorchidism. Intestinal obstruction is not a primary feature (only after intraperitoneal surgery). <!-- c032-a04 -->
- Flaccidity does not correlate with urological severity and does not shorten survival, but with orthopaedic problems it lowers quality of life in adolescence. <!-- c032-r07 -->
- Pulmonary hypoplasia (from oligohydramnios due to dysplasia or severe outlet obstruction, worsened by poor abdominal support) is the main threat to early survival and can cause newborn death; cardiac, orthopaedic and endocrine problems rarely threaten early life. <!-- c032-a05 c032-r06 -->
- Testes lie high in the abdomen on the dilated ureters and never descend spontaneously. <!-- c032-a06 -->
- Infertility: testicular histological defects, ductal and prostatic abnormalities, retrograde ejaculation. Sperm can be found in ejaculate or post-masturbation urine, so assisted reproduction is possible. <!-- c032-r05 c032-a10 -->

### Evaluation
- Team: neonatology, nephrology, urology, cardiology and others. <!-- c032-r08 -->
- Chest X-ray for pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum and pulmonary hypoplasia; baseline renal and bladder ultrasound, BUN (blood urea nitrogen), creatinine and electrolytes. <!-- c032-r10 c032-r11 -->
- Neonatal VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) only if renal insufficiency or outlet obstruction, and only under antibiotic prophylaxis. <!-- c032-r09 -->

### Management
- Expectant: dilatation improves spontaneously with time (basis of care, especially in milder/grade III cases, as in megaureter and reflux); antibiotic prophylaxis for the vulnerable; timed voiding and/or CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) for high residuals. No role for antimuscarinics or α-blockers (no overactivity, no hypertrophic neck); upper tract diversion rarely needed. <!-- c032-a11 -->
- Early intervention for outlet obstruction, preferably a percutaneous suprapubic tube. <!-- c032-r13 -->
- Upper tract reconstruction is controversial but indicated for falling renal function with recurrent upper tract infection or progressive dilatation. It removes the dilated distal ureter and reimplants it, with excision of the urachal diverticulum and part of the dome; residuals may persist or recur even after reduction cystoplasty. It can be combined with abdominoplasty, orchidopexy and circumcision, with acceptable risk even in infants. <!-- c032-r14 c032-a12 c032-a08 -->
- Circumcision is advisable if the penis is structurally normal. <!-- c032-r12 -->
- Orchidopexy at 6–18 months, as in other cryptorchidism (timing adjusted to other major surgery); before age 2 Fowler-Stephens is usually unnecessary; early surgery favours a single stage and germ cell preservation; endocrine function is preserved, so hormone replacement is not needed. <!-- c032-a10 c032-r15 -->
- Abdominal wall reconstruction improves bladder emptying, cough and defecation; long-term cosmetic benefit is debated. <!-- c032-r16 -->

## Ch 33 Posterior Urethral Valves

### Definition and epidemiology
- Type 1 valves (commonest): leaflets from the verumontanum fusing anteriorly just proximal to the external sphincter. Type 3: a congenital obstructing membrane, likely perforated at the first postnatal catheterisation. <!-- c033-r01 c033-r02 -->
- Incidence 1.6–2.1 per 10,000 births; up to 50% are diagnosed antenatally. Emerging genetics: chromosome 11 and copy number variants. <!-- c033-r03 c033-r10 c033-r04 -->

### Pathophysiology
- Renal impairment comes from renal dysplasia plus obstructive uropathy: sustained high bladder storage pressures are transmitted to ureter, pelvis and glomeruli. Renal dysfunction, VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) and voiding dysfunction are all mediated by the dysfunctional bladder. <!-- c033-a03 c033-r05 c033-r06 -->
- VURD (vesicoureteral reflux and dysplasia) syndrome does not protect long-term renal function; the "good" kidney often has dysplasia too, so follow closely. <!-- c033-a04 c033-r07 -->
- High pressure can rupture a calyceal fornix (perirenal urinoma) or rarely cause ascites; bladder perforation is not typical, because the bladder wall hypertrophies. <!-- c033-a01 -->
- Pulmonary hypoplasia is the commonest cause of perinatal and early death. <!-- c033-a06 c033-r15 -->

### Antenatal diagnosis and intervention
- Thick dilated bladder plus bilateral upper tract dilatation with oligohydramnios is highly sensitive for valves (ultrasound, sometimes fetal MRI); genital anomalies are not part of the picture. <!-- c033-r07 c033-a05 c033-a02 -->
- Severity: amniotic fluid volume, degree of dysplasia, fetal urinary markers. <!-- c033-r11 -->
- Intervention criteria: favourable urine chemistry, singleton, normal karyotype, no significant dysplasia; options are vesicoamniotic shunting or fetal cystoscopic ablation (early, varied experience; highly selected cases only). <!-- c033-a02 -->
- Vesicoamniotic shunting increases live births and may improve neonatal lung function but does not reduce renal failure. **Update:** the PLUTO randomised trial (2013) found the same: possible survival benefit, poor renal outcomes. <!-- c033-a02 c033-a08 c033-r12 c033-r24 -->

### Postnatal diagnosis
- Antenatal thick bladder with bilateral ureterectasis needs early postnatal ultrasound and VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram); contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography is an emerging alternative. <!-- c033-r08 c033-r13 -->
- VCUG: irregular, hypertrophied bladder; elevated bladder neck; dilated elongated posterior urethra. <!-- c033-r14 -->
- Late presentation after 6 months is not rare: recurrent UTIs, overflow incontinence, haematuria, renal dysfunction, rarely ejaculatory dysfunction; investigate with VCUG. <!-- c033-a07 -->
- Electrolytes and creatinine; nadir creatinine predicts long-term renal function; a nadir under 0.8 mg/dL at 1 year means a much lower ESRD (end-stage renal disease) risk. <!-- c033-r09 c033-r25 -->

### Treatment
- Cystoscopic valve ablation is the preferred first treatment. <!-- c033-r16 -->
- Vesicostomy when the urethra is too small (e.g. low-birth-weight infant with renal impairment): decompresses, keeps bladder cycling, managed in nappies; does not impair contractility. High (upper tract) diversion gives no renal protection, needs bilateral incisions and a complex reversal. <!-- c033-a09 c033-r17 c033-r18 c033-a12 -->
- Circumcision should be encouraged, especially after any UTI. <!-- c033-r19 -->
- VUR is secondary to high bladder pressure: treat the bladder (timed and double voiding, anticholinergics); reimplantation only if UTIs persist despite maximal bladder therapy. <!-- c033-a10 c033-r20 -->

### Valve bladder and prognosis
| Phase | Bladder pattern |
|---|---|
| Infancy, early childhood | Detrusor hyperreflexia, high pressure |
| Childhood | Lower pressures, better compliance |
| Adolescence | Large capacity, hypocontractility, atony |
<!-- c033-a11 c033-r21 -->
- Valve bladder syndrome: growth and polyuria (acquired nephrogenic diabetes insipidus) fill a bladder that empties poorly; constant stretch triggers gene-expression changes that further weaken contraction. <!-- c033-a12 -->
- Videourodynamics when the picture changes (worse dilatation, recurrent UTI); overnight bladder drainage for rising residuals, UTIs, worsening hydronephrosis or renal function. <!-- c033-r22 c033-r23 -->
- Prognostic factors: age at diagnosis, dysplasia with or without VUR, nadir creatinine in year 1, recurrent UTIs, bladder dysfunction. <!-- c033-a13 -->
- Transplant into a valve bladder: more ureteral obstruction (thick wall) but no increase in graft loss or death. <!-- c033-a14 c033-r26 -->

### Other urethral conditions
- Anterior urethral valves and urethral atresia are rarer causes of LUTO (lower urinary tract obstruction); outcomes similar to PUV if the neonate survives. <!-- c033-r27 -->
- Urethral duplication: commoner in boys (rare in girls); most are continent (duplication distal to sphincter); UTI uncommon; blind-ending or orthotopic-ending duplications need no surgery; only functional problems need reconstruction. <!-- c033-a15 -->
- Urethrorrhagia: terminal bleeding or spotting without pain, no UTI link; clinical diagnosis after excluding other haematuria causes; no imaging test; linked to dysfunctional voiding (turbulent flow); treat voiding habits conservatively; cystoscopy only for refractory cases or marked straining. <!-- c033-a16 c033-r28 c033-r29 -->
- Anorectal malformation: renal ultrasound and VCUG (high rate of genitourinary anomalies). <!-- c033-r30 -->

## Ch 82 Surgery for Benign Disorders of the Penis and Urethra

### Grafts and flaps
- A graft is excised tissue that revascularises in the host bed ("take", about 96 hours): imbibition (~48 hours, nutrients absorbed, graft cooler than core) then inosculation (~48 hours, true microcirculation, graft reaches core temperature). Anything harming host bed vascularity harms take. <!-- c082-a01 -->
- Layers: superficial (intradermal) plexus near the epidermis; deep (subdermal) plexus at the junction of deep dermis and underlying tissue; deep (reticular) dermis carries most lymphatics and collagen and gives the physical characteristics. Extragenital full-thickness grafts are bulkier and take less reliably than genital skin. <!-- c082-a02 -->
- Bladder epithelium: superficial and deep plexuses with many perforators. Buccal (oral) mucosa has a panlaminar plexus, so it can be thinned and has optimal vascular characteristics. <!-- c082-a03 -->
- Split-thickness grafts (STSG) take more easily but contract and become brittle; full-thickness grafts take less easily but contract less and are durable. STSGs carry few lymphatics; full-thickness grafts carry a full complement. <!-- c082-r03 c082-r02 -->
- Meshed STSG on the genitalia: do not expand it; the slits let collections escape. <!-- c082-r01 -->
- Tunica vaginalis grafts become aneurysmal over large defects. <!-- c082-r04 -->
- Urethra: flaps suit distal reconstruction, grafts proximal. Onlay grafts or flaps succeed more than tubularised grafts. <!-- c082-r12 c082-r16 -->
- Genital skin island flaps (on penile dartos or scrotal tunica dartos) are one concept with many applications (Quartey's microinjection studies); skin must be non-hirsute; key factors are tissue nature, vasculature and mechanics of transfer; usable even after circumcision and for the fossa navicularis. <!-- c082-a21 -->

### Anatomy
- Corpora cavernosa form one space through an incompetent septum; erectile tissue is separated from the tunica by the space of Smith; Buck fascia abuts the tunica and envelops spongiosum and dorsal neurovascular bundle; dartos is loose areolar tissue under the skin carrying superficial external pudendal and posterior scrotal vessels. <!-- c082-a04 c082-r19 c082-r06 -->
- Urethra: fossa navicularis in the glans; bulbar urethra has the thickest spongiosum and lies dorsally, covered by bulbospongiosus; the membranous urethra is the only segment not invested by another structure (delicate transitional epithelium). <!-- c082-a05 -->
- Arteries: internal pudendal becomes the common penile artery after perineal and posterior scrotal branches; it ends as bulbourethral, dorsal (within Buck fascia) and cavernosal arteries; circumflex branches are inconsistent. <!-- c082-a06 -->
- Veins: superficial dorsal vein to the (usually left) saphenous; deep dorsal and circumflex veins (beneath Buck fascia) to the periprostatic plexus. <!-- c082-r05 -->
- Nerves: dorsal nerve is the first branch of the pudendal nerve in Alcock canal (sensory to tunica and skin); cavernosal nerves are autonomic (parasympathetic and visceral afferent); penile skin also from genitofemoral branches. <!-- c082-a07 -->

### Lichen sclerosus, meatus, fistula, circumcision
- LS (lichen sclerosus): cause unclear; infection has no proven link (Koebner, autoimmune and genetic theories). It is a skin disease, so genital skin should not be used for repair (recurrence); oral mucosa is best. It causes meatal stenosis, often with proximal strictures (full work-up), and may be premalignant: watch for squamous cell carcinoma. <!-- c082-a08 c082-a09 c082-r07 c082-r08 -->
- Childhood meatal stenosis follows circumcision (ammoniacal meatitis causes ventral fusion); meatitis settles with a short course of dilation plus steroid cream; established stenosis: divide the fusion membrane (slit meatus). <!-- c082-a11 c082-r20 -->
- Urethrocutaneous fistula causes: hypospadias or urethral surgery (haematoma, infection, tension), distal obstruction with high-pressure voiding, infected extravasation and abscess; not genital herpes. A spontaneous fistula or unexplained periurethral abscess may herald urethral carcinoma. <!-- c082-a10 c082-r09 -->
- Circumcision: never in a newborn with any hypospadias (keep the foreskin); reduces heterosexual HIV acquisition by 50%–60% in endemic areas, plus HSV-2, HPV and genital ulcer disease; reduces infant UTI; most skin dehiscence heals conservatively. Paraphimosis recurs, so plan dorsal slit or circumcision. <!-- c082-a13 c082-r10 c082-r15 -->

### Trauma, lymphoedema, curvature
- Penile amputation (often self-inflicted in psychosis; replant unless the patient adamantly refuses, even via court order with two surgeons): microreplantation preferred (tunica, spatulated two-layer urethra, epineural nerve, dorsal vein and arteries; cavernosal arteries cannot be joined); McRoberts macroreplantation works well when micro is impossible but leaves distal numbness. <!-- c082-a12 -->
- Genital lymphoedema (including from radiation): excise and cover with STSG; fix testes in the midline; meshed STSG on testes if the scrotum cannot close; excise the parietal tunica (hydroceles common) and graft onto the visceral layer. Lymphoedema recurs in full-thickness or thick grafts; spared posterior and lateral scrotal skin may allow closure; lymphangiectasia can mimic papilloma; cellulitis is a recurring problem. <!-- c082-a14 c082-r11 -->
- Congenital curvature or chordee without hypospadias: usually ventral, with or without torsion, often with ventral skin abnormality; erect penis is large in congenital curvature; patients may need sex therapy; the limiting factor is usually inelastic ventral corpora, not the spongiosum; correct with dorsal plication or excision, mobilising the dorsal bundle with Buck fascia. <!-- c082-a23 -->
- Acquired non-Peyronie curvature (subclinical fracture after "minimal" buckling trauma): lateral scar and indentation; usually no global CVOD (cavernosal veno-occlusive dysfunction) and no shortening, unlike Peyronie disease. <!-- c082-a24 -->
- Peyronie curvature needing repair: plication rather than grafting (book). <!-- c082-r18 -->

### Urethral stricture
- Stricture (anterior urethra) involves epithelium and spongiosum (spongiofibrosis); scar contraction narrows the lumen; squamous metaplasia proximal to tight strictures. Posterior narrowing is a contracture or stenosis; pelvic fracture lesions are PFUI (pelvic fracture urethral injury), a distraction fibrosis, not a true stricture. <!-- c082-a15 c082-a16 c082-r13 -->
- Planning: location, length, depth and density by contrast studies, urethroscopy, ultrasound and bougie à boule calibration; MRI is not useful for anterior strictures (except carcinoma) but helps distorted PFUI anatomy. <!-- c082-a17 -->
- A suprapubic tube for 6–8 weeks (urethral rest) reveals hidden strictures; assess the whole urethra endoscopically and with bougies during surgery; coned-down proximal segments are involved; sonourethrography gives length but not spongiofibrosis depth. <!-- c082-a18 c082-r21 -->
- Internal urethrotomy: cure 29%–30% overall; 75%–78% for bulbar strictures under 1.5 cm without dense spongiofibrosis; poor elsewhere. Commonest complication is recurrence; deep 12 o'clock distal incisions can enter the corpora and cause ED (erectile dysfunction); also bleeding and extravasation. <!-- c082-a19 c082-r22 -->
- Excision and primary anastomosis (EPA) is the most dependable repair: excise all fibrosis, wide spatulation, tension-free, epithelial apposition; intracrural development and bulbospongiosus detachment allow long segments, more so near the membranous urethra. <!-- c082-a20 c082-r23 -->
- Vessel-sparing EPA (Jordan 2007, Mundy 2010) keeps bulbar arteries; no proven benefit in outcome or de novo ED; most logical after a failed transecting repair or with prior hypospadias (poor distal supply). After hypospadias, avoid transecting repair of a bulbar stricture; use onlay or vessel-sparing. <!-- c082-a29 c082-a30 c082-a31 -->

### Pelvic fracture urethral injury
- Distraction is commonest at the bulbomembranous junction (not prostate apex), but can involve any part of the membranous urethra. <!-- c082-a25 c082-r24 -->
- An aligning catheter at worst helps later repair, at best leaves an endoscopically manageable urethra. <!-- c082-r14 -->
- Repair: mobilise spongiosum cautiously (retrograde blood supply may be tenuous); detach Buck fascia, intracrural development, infrapubectomy and spongiosum rerouting shorten the path; do not mobilise beyond the penoscrotal junction (chordee). <!-- c082-a26 c082-r25 -->
- Continence is judged after urethral continuity is restored; bladder neck appearance does not predict it. <!-- c082-a22 -->
- ED after PFUI: one patent internal pudendal artery: often potent, reconstruct directly; reconstituted vessels only: rarely potent but repairable; bilateral occlusion without reconstitution: risk of ischaemic stenosis, so revascularise first. No ED means normal haemodynamics. <!-- c082-a27 -->
- Rectourethral or vesicourethrorectal fistula: interpose healthy tissue, ideally a gracilis flap. <!-- c082-r17 -->
- Neophallus: insensate at transfer, sensation from 3–4 months; implant a prosthesis only after 1 year with protective sensation and a durable patent urethra. <!-- c082-a28 -->

## Ch 88 Urological Complications of Renal Transplantation

### Haematuria and stents
- Most postoperative haematuria settles; persistent or severe haematuria after graft biopsy needs duplex ultrasound for AV (arteriovenous) fistula. <!-- c088-r01 c088-r02 -->
- Stents reduce stricture and urine leak; removal can be individualised after 3 weeks; early removal (before day 15) lowers UTI without more leak or stenosis; 27% of stents colonise whatever the dwell time. <!-- c088-r03 c088-r04 c088-a03 -->

### Ureteral obstruction, leak, reflux
- The graft is denervated, so recipients rarely have classic colic: they present with falling function, low urine output, recurrent UTI or a vague ache. <!-- c088-a01 -->
- After 3 months, strictures dominate obstruction; main cause is ischaemia; suspect with hydronephrosis plus falling function. Open repair gives the best long-term results (endourology is an option; only very frail patients are relative exceptions). <!-- c088-r05 c088-r06 c088-r07 c088-r08 c088-a11 -->
- Urine leak: divert with Foley catheter and nephrostomy (or retrograde stent); drain percutaneously for large urinoma, obstruction or infection; fluid restriction plays no part. <!-- c088-a10 -->
- VUR comes from technical error, outlet obstruction or high-pressure storage (overactivity, poor compliance), and develops with any anastomotic technique. <!-- c088-a04 -->
- Lymphocele: from dissection of recipient iliac or donor hilar lymphatics; also obesity, age, dialysis duration, warm ischaemia, LMWH (low-molecular-weight heparin) prophylaxis, delayed function, rejection, redo transplant, mTOR inhibitors (not atherosclerosis). Success: aspiration 41%, sclerotherapy 69%, drain 50%, laparoscopic marsupialisation 92%, open 84%. <!-- c088-a09 c088-a05 -->

### Stones, BPH and cancer
- Stones: calcium-based 67% (oxalate and phosphate), struvite 20%, uric acid 13%. <!-- c088-a02 -->
- BPH: medical therapy is fine; TURP or HoLEP are safe after 3 weeks; avoid TURP in the first 2 weeks or with a stent (sepsis, death). <!-- c088-a07 -->
- Renal tumours mostly arise in native kidneys and are low grade; graft tumours are mostly indolent (papillary 56%; 65% of non-papillary are Fuhrman 1–2). <!-- c088-r09 c088-a06 -->
- Urothelial carcinoma risk is threefold and more aggressive (recurrence, progression, metastasis): offer early cystectomy for high-grade NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer); BCG in selected cases; MIBC by chemoradiation or cystectomy. <!-- c088-r11 c088-a08 -->
- Prostate cancer: active surveillance, radiation or surgery are all reasonable. <!-- c088-r10 -->

## Ch 131 Surgical Procedures for Sphincteric Incontinence in the Male

### Pathophysiology and evaluation
- Male SUI (stress urinary incontinence) needs both internal and external sphincter impairment. Internal: radical prostatectomy, bladder neck injury, sympathetic neuropathy, congenital. External: prostatectomy (commonest), pelvic fracture, myelopathy, dysraphism, sacral agenesis, exstrophy-epispadias. Spinal cord injury causes dyssynergia and retention, not SUI. <!-- c131-a01 -->
- Nerve sparing speeds return of continence but does not change final continence. <!-- c131-a03 -->
- Initial evaluation: history, voiding diary, examination, urinalysis (culture if needed), uroflowmetry and PVR (post-void residual); pressure-flow studies are not routine, only for suspected obstruction, overactivity or poor contractility. <!-- c131-a04 c131-r01 c131-r02 c131-r03 c131-r05 -->
- Cystoscopy if bladder neck, anastomotic or urethral pathology is suspected (e.g. high PVR after prostatectomy); urethrography is weaker for posterior strictures. <!-- c131-a05 c131-r04 -->

### Choice of treatment
- Surgery for ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency) SUI after failed conservative care; choice depends on severity, prior surgery, bladder function, cystoscopy, dexterity, cognition, long-term risks and preference. <!-- c131-r06 c131-r07 -->
- Bulking agents are not part of the post-prostatectomy algorithm (maybe a trial in neurogenic male SUI). AUS (artificial urinary sphincter) and slings are first-line. <!-- c131-a08 c131-a09 -->
- Slings: mild to moderate UI (24-hour pad weight under 150 g mild, under 400 g moderate), intact contractility, non-radiated well-vascularised urethra; use very selectively after radiation, erosion or severe gravitational leak (poor tissue elasticity). <!-- c131-a08 c131-r08 c131-r15 -->
- Transobturator slings (Advance) reposition and lengthen the membranous urethra without compression (higher abdominal leak point pressure, no obstruction on urodynamics; MRI shows longer membranous urethra, elevated bladder neck and rhabdosphincter); they need an intact external sphincter and are durable. Quadratic fixation (Virtue) has limited data. <!-- c131-a02 c131-r16 c131-r17 c131-r23 -->
- Severe UI with poor dexterity: Advance sling rather than AUS. AUS is avoided with poor dexterity, cognitive disability, poor urethral tissue or severely low compliance. <!-- c131-a09 c131-r09 -->
- If an Advance sling loosens in the first 6 weeks (continent, then leaks after exertion): re-explore early, cut and remove the sling, place another. <!-- c131-a07 -->

### AUS technique and complications
- Bulbar cuff around spongiosum proximal to the corporal convergence; 61–70 cm H2O pressure-regulating balloon via abdominal, scrotal or perineal route; keep deactivated 4–6 weeks. <!-- c131-r10 c131-r11 c131-r12 -->
- Retention after AUS: failed trial at 48 hours, place a suprapubic tube (image-guided to protect the balloon); avoid prolonged urethral catheter or CIC (erosion even if deactivated); retention beyond several weeks means an undersized cuff. <!-- c131-a11 c131-r13 -->
- Infection: usually skin Staphylococcus (epidermidis, aureus); total explant usually, salvage washout and reimplant in selected cases. Erosion: total explant and catheter drainage. Erosion risk: radiation, catheterisation, prior revision, urethroplasty (not a prior Advance sling). <!-- c131-a12 c131-r18 c131-r19 c131-a13 -->
- Recurrent leak needs systematic work-up; at 4 years the commonest non-mechanical cause is subcuff atrophy (mechanical failure grows with time). Atrophy: downsize, reposition, tandem cuff or transcorporal cuff. <!-- c131-a06 c131-r20 c131-a14 c131-r21 c131-r14 -->
- Tandem cuffs erode more (distal cuff); 3.5-cm cuffs erode more after radiation; in irradiated or atrophic urethras use a transcorporal cuff at a new site. <!-- c131-a10 -->
- Anastomotic stricture with an AUS: surgically uncouple the cuff, then endoscopic incision or dilation. <!-- c131-a15 -->
- AUS remains reliable for all severities, first-time and complex reoperative cases. <!-- c131-r22 -->

## Ch 140 Cutaneous Continent Urinary Diversion

### Patient selection
- Must be able and willing to self-catheterise; poor candidates: multiple sclerosis, quadriplegia, frailty, mental impairment. Always consent for a possible ileal conduit. <!-- c140-r01 c140-a33 c140-r02 -->
- Renal function: creatinine clearance at least 60 mL/min (creatinine above 1.8 mg/dL suggests insufficient function). Even if creatinine returns to 1.8 mg/dL, clearance under 60 means no continent diversion (replace the conduit instead). With hydronephrosis, drain the upper tracts (stents or nephrostomies) and reassess. **Update:** many guidelines accept eGFR above about 50 mL/min. <!-- c140-a01 c140-a19 c140-a02 c140-r03 c140-r08 -->
- Normal hepatic function is mandatory; otherwise, cystoprostatectomy with ileal conduit. <!-- c140-a07 c140-r08 -->
- Weight loss of 20 lb: look for metastases; if malnourished, give preoperative hyperalimentation. <!-- c140-a20 -->
- Any pouch using colon needs preoperative colonic evaluation (colonoscopy). <!-- c140-a11 -->
- Mainz II and other anal-continence procedures need a sphincter test (oatmeal enema); contraindicated with dilated ureters, pelvic irradiation or lax anal tone. <!-- c140-a21 c140-r11 c140-a40 -->
- Loss of the ileocaecal valve in neurological or bowel disorders causes debilitating diarrhoea; after repeated pipestem conduits in myelomeningocele with short bowel, revise the conduit rather than build a pouch. <!-- c140-a06 c140-r10 -->

### Pouch and valve types
- T pouch and Kock pouch (ileal) preserve the ileocaecal valve; right colon pouches sacrifice it. <!-- c140-a04 -->
- Indiana, Le Bag and Mainz I remove the appendix (nidus of infection or abscess). Penn pouch was the first to use the Mitrofanoff appendix principle. <!-- c140-a24 c140-a44 -->
- Short ureters (positive margins high up): right colon pouch with ureters into the terminal ileum plus a Mitrofanoff; the ileocaecal valve and isoperistaltic ileum reduce reflux. <!-- c140-a03 c140-a15 -->
- Mitrofanoff problems (calibre, short or absent appendix, stenosis) all have surgical solutions; catheterise the appendix with a 14 Fr coudé (straight is harder); appendiceal channels drain mucus least well. <!-- c140-a09 c140-a26 c140-a14 -->
- Existing conduit: incorporate it into the new pouch when possible (fewer metabolic effects and complications). <!-- c140-a46 -->
- Gastric pouch: less electrolyte reabsorption, no loss of absorptive bowel, acid urine may limit colonisation; but low pH causes haematuria and skin erosion. <!-- c140-a45 c140-a10 -->
- Benchekroun hydraulic ileal valve: continence depends on pouch pressure; worst long-term outcome of all mechanisms. <!-- c140-a29 c140-a30 -->
- Initial capacity is lowest in ileal (T, Kock) pouches; small bowel pouches start much smaller than right colon pouches. <!-- c140-a28 c140-r14 -->

### Technique
- Single-J stents out through separate stab wounds plus a Malecot in the reservoir; fix the reservoir to the abdominal wall at the Malecot exit. <!-- c140-r04 c140-r07 -->
- Antireflux ureteral anastomoses have uncertain benefit and more strictures. <!-- c140-r05 -->
- Absorbable sutures only (never silk). Absorbable staples: faster, reliable, best in large bowel pouches (no ischaemia); never overlap them (they will not seat); evert the bowel to apply the second row. Overlapping staple lines in absorbable-stapled ileal pouches cause ischaemic contraction. <!-- c140-a34 c140-a31 c140-a32 c140-a47 c140-r15 c140-a12 -->
- Fix the nipple valve to the side wall so filling lengthens it. Catheterise the channel intraoperatively to confirm easy passage. <!-- c140-a18 c140-a41 -->

### Complications
- Nipple valves: highest reoperation rate; most retention (catheter misdirected if the chimney is deep); atrophy needs a new valve from additional bowel; Kock pouch forms the most stones (staples). <!-- c140-a08 c140-a27 c140-a05 c140-a25 -->
- Pouchitis: urine culture first, then antibiotics. Pyelonephritis with a nipple pouch: pouchogram for proximal valve failure and reflux. Asymptomatic bacteriuria needs no treatment. <!-- c140-a16 c140-a17 c140-a42 -->
- Rupture: decompress, image and explore with repair plus broad antibiotics; small leaks without peritonitis may be managed with catheter and antibiotics under close watch. <!-- c140-a43 -->
- Nocturnal emptying may be needed for any pouch; it is mandatory after ureterosigmoidostomy (faecal incontinence, acidosis). Rectal bladders cause hyperchloraemic acidosis, potassium depletion and rectal cancer. <!-- c140-a23 c140-r13 c140-r09 -->
- Anastomotic urothelial cancer: distal ureterectomy and reimplantation (an extra ileal limb can bridge). <!-- c140-a13 -->
- Cancer can arise in any bowel exposed to urine, highest when urothelium, urine and faeces meet (ureterosigmoidostomy): cytology and colonoscopy with faecal admixture; annual pouchoscopy and cytology for all large bowel pouches. <!-- c140-a35 c140-a36 c140-a22 c140-r12 c140-r16 -->

### Outcomes
- Continent diversion may improve psychosocial adjustment, but no randomised data show better quality of life for any specific type; most patients are satisfied with whatever diversion they have; the ileal conduit is the gold standard. <!-- c140-a37 c140-a38 c140-r06 c140-a39 -->

## Ch 152 Diagnosis and Staging of Prostate Cancer

### PSA biology and modifiers
- 65%–90% of serum PSA is bound to α1-antichymotrypsin. Without cancer, PSA varies with age, race and volume (higher in African Americans). <!-- c152-a01 c152-a02 c152-r08 -->
- Disease (cancer, BPH, prostatitis) is the main driver; manipulation (massage, biopsy, cystoscopy) also raises PSA; ejaculation within 24 hours may raise it; DRE does not change it significantly. Not all prostate disease raises PSA, and rises are not cancer-specific. <!-- c152-a03 c152-r01 c152-r02 c152-a04 -->
- PSA is lower with hypogonadism, statins and BPH surgery. <!-- c152-r01 c152-r03 -->
- Finasteride 5 mg halves PSA by 12 months: multiply by 2 (by 2.3 at 2 years, 2.5 at 7 years); get a baseline; any rise on finasteride suggests occult cancer. <!-- c152-a06 -->
- Abnormal PSA: repeat after observation; AUA does not recommend empirical antibiotics. <!-- c152-a04 -->

### Improving specificity and screening
- PSA has the highest PPV (positive predictive value) of any single test. Refinements: PSA density, transition zone density, velocity, percent free PSA. <!-- c152-a07 c152-a05 -->
- PSA 4–10 ng/mL: velocity over 0.75 ng/mL/year and PSAD are concerning; low free PSA predicts cancer (over 25% free is reassuring). **Update:** velocity is no longer recommended as a screening trigger. <!-- c152-r04 c152-r05 -->
- PSA under 1 ng/mL at 40–45 years can set screening intervals. <!-- c152-r07 -->
- Screening: one trial (ERSPC) reduced advanced disease and cancer death, another (PLCO) did not; harms are overdiagnosis and unnecessary biopsies. In 2017 USPSTF, AUA, ACS and EAU all advised shared decision-making. <!-- c152-r06 c152-a12 -->

### Grading and pathological stage
- Grade groups: GG1 ≤6, GG2 3+4, GG3 4+3, GG4 8 (including 4+4), GG5 9–10. <!-- c152-a14 -->
- A small higher-grade focus is the tertiary pattern; the Gleason sum is primary plus highest grade. <!-- c152-r09 -->
- PSA vs stage: under 4 ng/mL, 80% organ-confined; 4–10 ng/mL, about two-thirds; over 10, over 50% extraprostatic; over 20, nodes in nearly 20%; over 50, nodes in 75%. <!-- c152-a13 c152-r13 -->
- Partin tables: clinical stage, PSA and Gleason sum give percentage probabilities of pathological stage (dashes mean insufficient data); used for counselling. <!-- c152-a15 c152-r14 -->
- Pathological staging beats clinical staging (tumour volume, margins, capsular penetration, seminal vesicles, nodes; not PSA). Seminal vesicle invasion or node involvement predicts occult metastasis. <!-- c152-a10 c152-a11 -->

### Clinical staging
- Staging aims to predict prognosis and choose therapy; no imaging detects all nodal metastases. Tools: DRE, markers, grade, imaging, lymphadenectomy. <!-- c152-a08 c152-a09 -->
- Bone scan only if PSA over 20, T3–T4 or bone symptoms (book). **Update:** PSMA PET is now more accurate than CT plus bone scan (proPSMA) for high-risk staging. <!-- c152-r10 -->
- Multiparametric MRI (diffusion and dynamic contrast) improves specificity; a T2 low-signal peripheral zone lesion with restricted diffusion and early enhancement needs targeted biopsy. **Update:** MRI before biopsy is now standard (PRECISION). <!-- c152-r11 c152-a16 -->
- Pelvic lymphadenectomy criteria are not firm; at least for Gleason over 8, enlarged nodes, T3, or PSA over 20 (book). **Update:** EAU uses a nomogram risk above about 5%–7% for extended node dissection. <!-- c152-r12 -->
