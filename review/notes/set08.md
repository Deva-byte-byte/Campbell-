## Ch 28 Evaluation of lower urinary tract dysfunction in children

### History, diaries and questionnaires
- First decide from history and examination whether the problem is mainly storage or emptying; tailor work-up to that. <!-- c028-r01 -->
- Full history: symptoms, voiding frequency, bowel function, prior UTIs (urinary tract infections), family history, maternal prenatal history and scans, birth history, milestones including toilet training, neuropsychiatric conditions, medical and surgical history, social history, diet, systems review. <!-- c028-r02 -->
- A 7-day bowel and bladder diary or 48-hour frequency-volume chart is invaluable and comes before invasive tests; it reveals low functional capacity, infrequent voiding, nocturia and polydipsia. <!-- c028-a12 c028-r03 -->
- Constipation, neuropsychiatric problems, UTI and vesicoureteral reflux commonly accompany lower urinary tract dysfunction. <!-- c028-a13 -->
- Validated tools: symptoms (DVSS, dysfunctional voiding scoring system; DVISS; ISI-P, incontinence symptom index-paediatric); quality of life (PinQ, Pediatric Incontinence Questionnaire); behaviour (CBCL, Child Behavior Checklist; SSIPPE screening for psychological problems in enuresis). <!-- c028-r04 -->

### Examination and occult spinal dysraphism
- Inspect the lower back for dimples, gluteal cleft deviation, hair tufts, dermal sinuses and vascular lesions; palpate for the sacrum and any mass. <!-- c028-r05 -->
- Risk of occult dysraphism, lowest to highest: simple dimple, symmetric bifid gluteal cleft, hair tuft, dermal sinus, haemangioma. <!-- c028-a09 -->
- MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) is the most sensitive and specific test; spinal ultrasound works up to 6 months of age but is less sensitive; CT, PET and videourodynamics have no role. <!-- c028-a10 -->
- For low-risk findings (for example a deviated gluteal cleft in a 6-month-old) ultrasound is reasonable, with MRI if it is positive or inconclusive. <!-- c028-a11 -->

### Urine and blood tests
- Every child with LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) needs urinalysis: specific gravity, protein, glucose, white cells, red cells, bacteria (AUA, American Urological Association). Other tests are not routine at first visit. <!-- c028-a08 c028-r06 -->
- Malodorous urine alone has poor sensitivity and specificity for a positive culture. <!-- c028-r07 -->
- Blood tests only when upper tract disease is suspected. <!-- c028-r08 -->

### Non-invasive urodynamics
- Non-invasive tests: uroflowmetry, patch EMG (electromyography), pelvic ultrasound. <!-- c028-r09 -->
- Uroflow gives curve shape; Qmax is the key number. Valid if voided volume is over 50 mL (adults over 150 mL) and ideally under 115% of expected capacity. <!-- c028-a01 c028-r10 -->
- ICCS (International Children's Continence Society, 2014) curve shapes: bell, tower, staccato, interrupted (intermittent), plateau. <!-- c028-a02 -->
- EMG lag time (start of sphincter relaxation to start of flow) is normally 2–6 s with a bell curve; under 2 s suggests overactive bladder with a tower curve; over 6 s suggests primary bladder neck dysfunction with a plateau curve. EMG also shows synergic versus dyssynergic voiding. <!-- c028-a03 c028-a04 c028-a05 c028-r12 -->
- Functional bladder capacity = voided volume plus PVR (post-void residual), or the largest voided volume on the diary. (Age + 2) × 30 mL is expected capacity, not functional capacity. **Update:** the book's explanation says "smallest voided volume", but the correct answer is the largest (ICCS excludes the first morning void). <!-- c028-a06 -->
- Ultrasound gives pre- and post-void volumes (PVR), bladder wall thickness, rectal diameter and debris; debris means a higher chance of a positive culture, but interpret it cautiously if there are no symptoms. <!-- c028-a07 c028-r11 -->

### Invasive urodynamics
- Urodynamics is the gold standard for diagnosing lower urinary tract dysfunction. <!-- c028-r13 -->
- Fill at 5%–10% of expected capacity per minute; a fixed 20 mL/min is too fast for a neonate. <!-- c028-a14 -->
- Sedation or anaesthesia (for catheter placement or the study) abolishes detrusor overactivity; compliance, detrusor leak point pressure and capacity are unaffected. <!-- c028-a15 c028-r15 -->
- Post-urodynamics UTI is 1.4%; routine prophylactic antibiotics or pre-study cultures do not prevent it. <!-- c028-r14 -->

## Ch 29 Vesicoureteral reflux

### Epidemiology and genetics
- VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) prevalence: about 30% of children with UTI and about 17% without infection. <!-- c029-a01 -->
- Antenatally detected reflux: male predominance, usually high grade and bilateral in boys, often with congenital dysplasia and renal impairment at birth. <!-- c029-a02 -->
- VUR is about 10 times less common in girls of African descent. <!-- c029-a03 c029-r16 -->
- About one third of siblings have reflux; inheritance is thought autosomal dominant. Once sibling reflux is found, treatment indications are the same as for any child; renal imaging, age and UTI history guide whether to screen. <!-- c029-a04 c029-r01 c029-r02 -->

### Pathophysiology
- Primary reflux: congenital deficiency of the longitudinal muscle of the intravesical ureter, so the flap-valve fails. Normal tunnel length to ureteric diameter ratio is 5:1 (Paquin). <!-- c029-a05 c029-a06 -->
- Embryology (Mackie and Stevens): a bud arising lateral (cranial) to normal gives a refluxing, laterally placed orifice; a caudal bud tends to obstruction. <!-- c029-a26 -->
- Secondary causes: posterior urethral valves (commonest anatomic obstruction in boys; reflux in 48%–70%, often resolving after valve ablation), prolapsing ureterocele (commonest in girls), neuropathic bladder. Treat the primary cause first and look for occult dysraphism. <!-- c029-a08 -->
- Non-neurogenic neurogenic bladder (Hinman): voluntary sphincter contraction during voiding (learned dyssynergia), incomplete emptying, rising residual and eventual myogenic failure. <!-- c029-a07 -->
- Poor compliance, detrusor decompensation and incomplete emptying damage the ureterovesical junction (UVJ); subureteric bulking injection does not. <!-- c029-a09 -->
- Intravesical pressure over 40 cm H2O is strongly linked to reflux and upper tract damage in neuropathic bladders (damage can also occur at lower pressures). <!-- c029-a13 -->
- Cystitis can cause reflux by reducing compliance, raising pressure and weakening the UVJ. <!-- c029-a14 c029-r19 -->
- Bladder and bowel dysfunction (BBD) and constipation/encopresis are commonly associated with reflux and UTI. Signs: dribbling, urgency, incontinence, Vincent curtsy in girls, penile squeezing in boys. Treat it medically first (lower detrusor overactivity and pressure); urethral dilatation has no role. <!-- c029-a10 c029-a11 c029-a12 c029-r03 -->
- BBD does not lower the success of Deflux (dextranomer/hyaluronic acid) injection or open surgery, but it increases UTIs after surgery. <!-- c029-a30 -->

### Grading and imaging
- Grading uses the International Reflux Study classification (1981), a merger of the Heikel-Parkkulainen and Dwoskin-Perlmutter systems, based on VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) appearance. Grading is impossible with coexisting ipsilateral obstruction. <!-- c029-a15 c029-a16 -->
- Fever suggests upper tract involvement and raises the yield of VCUG, but it is unreliable; it can occur with under 100,000 colonies and pyelonephritis does not always scar. <!-- c029-a17 -->
- VCUG plus ultrasound is reasonable for recurrent culture-proven UTIs with a DMSA scar; question the diagnosis first if the sample was a bag specimen or another fever source exists. Do not do immediate VCUG for UTI in children over 5. <!-- c029-a18 -->
- Older children with asymptomatic bacteriuria or afebrile cystitis need no screening imaging; focus on voiding, bowel habits and fluids; cystography only for abnormal upper tracts or recalcitrant infection. <!-- c029-a19 -->
- After a first febrile UTI with a properly collected urine: renal and bladder ultrasound; VCUG only if ultrasound is abnormal or a second infection occurs. <!-- c029-a45 -->
- Cystography pitfalls: overhydration can mask low-grade reflux; some reflux shows only during infection; during infection endotoxins paralyse ureteric muscle and overstate the grade. <!-- c029-a20 -->
- Nuclear cystography is accurate for detecting and following reflux but lacks anatomic detail; modern digital VCUG has narrowed the radiation difference. Ultrasound cannot detect reflux. Cystoscopy is not routine. Uroflowmetry is valuable since reflux is dynamic. <!-- c029-a21 c029-a25 c029-r20 -->
- Ultrasound is the first study of the upper tracts, but kidney appearance does not correlate with presence or grade of reflux. <!-- c029-a22 -->
- DMSA (Tc-99m dimercaptosuccinic acid) is best for acute pyelonephritis and scarring. Photopenic areas may be scars or congenital dysplasia; neither DMSA nor ultrasound can tell them apart. <!-- c029-a23 c029-a27 c029-r21 -->
- Urodynamics is indicated when a secondary cause is suspected (valves, neuropathic or non-neurogenic neurogenic bladder, voiding dysfunction); sedation can alter results. <!-- c029-a24 -->

### Natural history and consequences
- Spontaneous resolution: almost 80% of low-grade and about half of grade III reflux; paraureteral diverticulum reflux resolves at a similar rate. <!-- c029-r08 c029-r15 -->
- Sterile reflux with normal bladder function is benign and unlikely to matter clinically. <!-- c029-r09 c029-r13 -->
- Scarring risk after infection is greatest in the first year of life. <!-- c029-r14 -->
- High-grade reflux is associated with renal maldevelopment; cardinal associated anomalies are multicystic dysplastic kidney and renal agenesis. <!-- c029-r05 c029-r06 -->
- Renal growth is affected by associated congenital dysmorphism (30%), number and type of infections, the contralateral kidney and reflux grade. <!-- c029-a29 -->
- Reflux nephropathy is the commonest cause of severe hypertension in children and young adults. <!-- c029-a28 c029-r22 -->

### Pregnancy
- In pregnancy bladder tone falls from oedema and hyperaemia (predisposing to bacteriuria) and upper tract volume rises with physiological dilatation. Reflux, past scarring, UTI tendency, hypertension and renal impairment all increase risk. Women reimplanted in childhood still need monitoring for UTI in pregnancy. <!-- c029-a33 c029-a34 c029-r07 -->

### Antibiotic prophylaxis and RIVUR
- RIVUR (Randomized Intervention for Children with Vesicoureteral Reflux, NEJM 2014): adverse drug reactions were about 2% in both prophylaxis and placebo groups (not 0.5% vs 2%). Prophylaxis helped most in grade III–IV reflux, febrile index infection and baseline BBD. <!-- c029-a35 c029-a44 -->
- After stopping CAP (continuous antibiotic prophylaxis) in toilet-trained children, recurrence is likelier with higher-grade reflux and BBD; uncircumcised boys over 1 year are not at higher risk. <!-- c029-a43 -->

### Surgery
- All reimplants create a valvular mechanism: a submucosal tunnel with good muscle backing, tunnel length 5 times ureteric diameter. <!-- c029-a36 c029-r18 -->
- Cohen cross-trigonal reimplant suits small and thick-walled bladders. <!-- c029-r10 -->
- About 10% of antireflux operations involve duplication; reimplant both ureters with one mucosal cuff (common sheath) because they share blood supply. Weigert-Meyer rule: the upper pole ureter enters distal and medial, the lower pole ureter proximal and lateral. <!-- c029-a31 c029-a37 c029-r23 -->
- If both UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) and UVJ need repair, fix the UPJ first. <!-- c029-r04 -->
- Contralateral reflux appears in 10%–15% after unilateral repair, but prophylactic bilateral reimplant is not indicated. <!-- c029-r11 c029-r12 -->
- Early postoperative obstruction (oedema, subtrigonal bleeding, spasm, mucus, clots) is usually mild and resolves; symptomatic cases present 1–2 weeks after surgery with pain, nausea and vomiting; observe, and stent or nephrostomy only for unabating symptoms. <!-- c029-a38 c029-a39 -->
- Recurrent reflux after correction, apart from technical error, usually means a missed secondary cause: neuropathic bladder or severe voiding dysfunction. <!-- c029-a40 c029-r24 -->
- Endoscopic injection: less invasive but less durable than open repair; same indications; needle entry point and placement matter. Volume injected, surgeon experience, volcano mound without hydrodistention, double HIT technique and negative intraoperative cystogram have not consistently predicted success. <!-- c029-a32 c029-a41 c029-r17 -->
- Laparoscopic reimplant: smaller incisions, less pain, shorter stay and convalescence; but learning curve, two surgeons, extraperitoneal repair becomes intraperitoneal, longer operating time and higher cost. <!-- c029-a42 -->

## Ch 30 Bladder anomalies in children

### Embryology and prenatal findings
- The urorectal septum divides the cloaca at 4–6 weeks; the allantois and ventral cloaca involute by 4–5 months; the urachus becomes the median umbilical ligament. Definitive nephrons make urine before 12 weeks, and lower tract anomalies may show from about 10 weeks. <!-- c030-a01 -->
- The fetal bladder empties every 15–20 minutes, so watch long enough before calling it non-visualised. <!-- c030-a02 -->
- Megacystis: first trimester, bladder over 6 mm (normal 6 mm or less); later, a bladder that does not empty in 45 minutes. It has a wide differential, not only obstruction. <!-- c030-a04 -->
- Most megacystis has no genetic diagnosis; when found, trisomy 13 and 18 are commonest, above rates expected for maternal age and nuchal translucency. Aneuploidy predicts poor outcome. <!-- c030-a05 c030-a06 -->
- Persistent megacystis with oligohydramnios suggests LUTO (lower urinary tract obstruction); megacystis that resolves before birth may have no sequelae. <!-- c030-r01 -->
- MMIH (megacystis-microcolon-intestinal hypoperistalsis syndrome): rare, usually detected by the bladder findings, may show only prenatal megacystis; thought due to actin smooth-muscle dysfunction; morbidity mainly enteric; survival beyond the neonatal period now documented. <!-- c030-a07 c030-r02 -->
- Non-dilated anomalies (for example bladder exstrophy) are the most severe malformations, usually with normal amniotic fluid, and most are diagnosed after birth. <!-- c030-r03 -->

### Agenesis, duplication and diverticula
- Bladder agenesis: about 1 in 600,000, commoner in females, compatible with life only if the ureters drain ectopically. <!-- c030-a03 c030-r04 -->
- Bladder duplication: most have genital anomalies, over 25% gastrointestinal anomalies; urethra may or may not be duplicated; continence hard to predict. <!-- c030-a08 -->
- Congenital diverticula (incidence 1.7%): mucosa herniating through a hiatus in the wall; diagnosed by cystogram, usually after symptoms; most need no resection. <!-- c030-a09 -->
- Connective tissue disorders: multiple diverticula with smooth muscle and mucosa in the wall; treat if symptomatic (stasis, infection, stones); recurrence expected. <!-- c030-a10 c030-r05 -->
- Acquired diverticula can grow large enough to obstruct or cause stasis and recurrent UTI. <!-- c030-r10 -->

### Urachal anomalies
- Usually found after birth from umbilical drainage; a patent omphalomesenteric duct can mimic a patent urachus. <!-- c030-a11 c030-r06 c030-r08 -->
- Most patent urachus is not due to outlet obstruction. Treat infected remnants with antibiotics, then excise; open or minimally invasive surgery both work. <!-- c030-a11 c030-r07 -->
- Adenocarcinoma arises in adults (rare in children); discuss excising incidental remnants because of adult malignant potential. <!-- c030-a11 c030-r09 -->

### Bladder masses and cystitis
- Haemangiomas: usually single, linked to Klippel-Trenaunay and Sturge-Weber; simple resection cures. Fibroepithelial polyps: rare, endoscopic resection. <!-- c030-a12 -->
- Nephrogenic adenoma: benign reaction to infection, stones or injury; commoner in adults; recurs in up to 80% (latency about 4 years) after cystoscopic resection, so do surveillance cystoscopy. <!-- c030-a13 c030-r11 -->
- Eosinophilic cystitis: commoner in boys, often self-limiting in young children, no typical appearance (biopsy needed: eosinophils in all layers); treat with steroids, antihistamines and antibiotics. <!-- c030-a14 c030-r13 -->
- Cystitis cystica: commoner in girls, can mimic tumour, benign; long-term antibiotic prophylaxis. <!-- c030-a14 c030-r12 -->
- Cyclophosphamide haemorrhagic cystitis: give mesna simultaneously; oral pentosan polysulfate shows promise. <!-- c030-r14 -->

## Ch 65 Integrated men's health

### Life expectancy and risk
- Men live shorter and sicker lives than women everywhere; the gap persists despite rising life expectancy and is widest in Eastern Europe (about 7 years). <!-- c065-a01 c065-r01 c065-r02 c065-r03 -->
- Causes are social, environmental and physiological: workplace hazards, risk-taking and masculine health norms. Coordinated programmes targeting men are needed. <!-- c065-a02 c065-r04 c065-r05 -->

### Metabolic syndrome
- Definitions (WHO, AACE American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists, EGIR European Group for the Study of Insulin Resistance, ATP III Adult Treatment Panel III, IDF International Diabetes Federation) share hypertension, abdominal obesity, dyslipidaemia and insulin resistance or hyperglycaemia. LUTS are linked epidemiologically but are not part of the definition. Prevalence is rising worldwide. <!-- c065-a03 c065-r06 -->
- Insulin resistance causes hyperinsulinaemia, which drives sympathetic overactivity and hypertension. <!-- c065-a04 -->
- Linked urological conditions: renal insufficiency, stones, kidney, bladder and prostate cancer, overactive bladder, LUTS, low testosterone and ED (erectile dysfunction), besides cardiovascular disease, diabetes and stroke. Priapism is not linked. <!-- c065-a05 c065-r07 -->
- Exercise raises insulin-mediated glucose uptake and lowers inflammation and IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1); fitness affects incidence and outcome of urological disease. Trials are testing statins and metformin. <!-- c065-a06 c065-r08 -->
- Exercise improves quality of life in advanced prostate cancer; diet, weight loss and exercise can improve erectile function. Men's health needs individualised, precision programmes. <!-- c065-r09 c065-r10 c065-r11 -->

### Testosterone therapy
- Prescriptions rose sharply (UK and USA), although benefit and safety for age-related decline were not established. <!-- c065-a07 c065-r12 -->
- Large randomised trials (TTrials) showed modest gains in sexual and physical function and vitality. <!-- c065-r13 -->
- The book says limited evidence suggests more cardiovascular events. **Update:** TRAVERSE (NEJM 2023) found testosterone non-inferior to placebo for major cardiac events in hypogonadal men at high cardiovascular risk, but with more atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury and pulmonary embolism. <!-- c065-r14 -->
- Endocrine Society 2018: diagnose deficiency only in symptomatic men with unequivocally low testosterone; do not treat men planning fertility soon; discuss cardiovascular risks; keep up with guidelines. <!-- c065-a08 c065-r15 -->
- Contraindications: breast or prostate cancer, near-term fertility plans, high prostate cancer risk, raised haematocrit, untreated severe sleep apnoea, severe LUTS, uncontrolled heart failure, MI (myocardial infarction) or stroke within 6 months, thrombophilia. <!-- c065-a09 -->

### Erectile dysfunction and cardiovascular disease
- Shared risk factors (dyslipidaemia, smoking, hypertension, type 2 diabetes) and mechanisms (inflammation, atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction). <!-- c065-a10 c065-r16 -->
- ED and CVD (cardiovascular disease) are independent risk factors for each other, but their timing relationship is not settled. ED precedes cardiac symptoms by about 2–3 years; about 50% of sudden cardiac events have no prior symptoms, so ED may be the only warning. MESA (Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) showed subclinical CVD predicts ED. <!-- c065-a11 c065-r17 -->
- CAC (coronary artery calcium) score is a surrogate for accelerated atherosclerosis, including penile arteries. <!-- c065-r18 -->

### Mental health and opioids
- Anxiety, depression and suicide are rising and underdiagnosed in men; over 6 million US men have depression (fatigue, irritability, loss of interest). Men's suicide rate is about 4 times women's; peak age 45–54. <!-- c065-a12 c065-r19 c065-r20 c065-r21 -->
- Opioid crisis: 33,000 overdose deaths in 2015 (15,000 prescription), over 115 deaths a day, across all groups. Prescribe the lowest effective dose and reassess. Chronic opioids cause symptomatic hypogonadism. <!-- c065-a13 c065-r22 c065-r23 c065-r24 -->
- A men's health centre should focus on conditions with higher impact on men, in partnership with cardiology, endocrinology, psychiatry, orthopaedics and dermatology. <!-- c065-a14 -->

## Ch 96 Benign renal tumours

### Renal cysts
- Renal cysts are the commonest benign renal lesion; most need no follow-up. Acquired cystic kidney disease carries a significant malignancy risk and needs surveillance. <!-- c096-r01 c096-r02 c096-r03 -->
- Bosniak classification estimates malignancy risk and directs therapy; enhancement of solid components separates Bosniak III/IV from I–IIF (septa, thin calcification and similar features occur in II/IIF). **Update:** Bosniak 2019 revision adds MRI and defines wall/septal thickness and enhancement more precisely. <!-- c096-a02 c096-r04 -->
- ADPKD (autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease) diagnosis in at-risk families (Ravine): 2 cysts under age 30; 2 cysts in each kidney at 30–59; 4 in each kidney at 60 or over. **Update:** unified Pei criteria (2009): 3 or more cysts at 15–39, 2 or more in each kidney at 40–59, 4 or more in each kidney at 60 or over. <!-- c096-a01 -->

### Oncocytoma
- Commonest benign enhancing renal mass; histologically similar to chromophobe RCC (renal cell carcinoma). ISUP (International Society of Urological Pathology) recommends CK7: rarely positive in oncocytoma, diffusely positive in chromophobe. <!-- c096-a03 c096-r05 c096-r06 -->
- Associated with Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome (folliculin gene, chromosome 17): lung cysts, spontaneous pneumothorax, fibrofolliculomas. <!-- c096-a04 c096-r07 -->
- Core biopsy with immunostaining can give the diagnosis; active surveillance is reasonable and uses growth kinetics. <!-- c096-r08 c096-r09 -->
- Chromosome clues: 4 and 16 in ADPKD, 7 in papillary adenoma/papillary RCC/metanephric adenoma, 9 (hamartin) and 16 (tuberin) in tuberous sclerosis. <!-- c096-a04 -->

### Angiomyolipoma
- AML (angiomyolipoma): benign tumour of dysmorphic vessels, smooth muscle and fat; macroscopic fat on imaging is diagnostic. In- and opposed-phase MRI shows a hypointense (India ink) border, not a hyperintense one. HMB-45 and Melan-A positive. <!-- c096-a03 c096-a06 c096-r10 -->
- Mostly asymptomatic, but AML is the commonest cause of spontaneous retroperitoneal haemorrhage (Wunderlich syndrome); pregnancy hormones can make it grow. <!-- c096-a12 c096-r11 -->
- Tuberous sclerosis (TSC): multifocal bilateral AMLs from hamartin (TSC1) or tuberin mutations that activate mTOR. <!-- c096-a05 c096-r12 -->
- Treatment depends on size, symptoms and pregnancy, aiming to preserve renal function; selective angioembolisation for acute bleeding. <!-- c096-r13 c096-r14 -->
- Everolimus (EXIST-2) for large multifocal AMLs in TSC and lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM): 42% had 50% or more shrinkage, 80% had 30% or more, 100% stable or responding; at 4 years 92% responded and 14% progressed. <!-- c096-a07 c096-r15 -->

### Other benign tumours
- Papillary adenoma: 5 mm or less, so not seen on imaging; shares staining and genetics (chromosome 7) with papillary RCC. <!-- c096-r16 c096-r17 -->
- Metanephric adenoma: rare, benign, enhancing; symptoms in about 50%, female predominance; polycythaemia (erythropoietin, IL-6, IL-8, G-CSF, GM-CSF); WT1 and AMACR staining; on a continuum with Wilms tumour and papillary RCC. <!-- c096-a08 c096-r18 c096-r19 -->
- Cystic nephroma and MEST (mixed epithelial and stromal tumour) are related; oestrogen and progesterone receptor positive, female predominance. Absence of blastemal elements separates cystic nephroma from Wilms tumour. Partial nephrectomy is preferred. <!-- c096-a09 c096-r20 c096-r21 c096-r22 -->
- Lymphangioma: cystic mass compressing the kidney from outside (abnormal perirenal lymphatics). <!-- c096-a10 -->
- Leiomyoma: anywhere in the urinary tract, mostly the renal capsule; imaging cannot confirm it; nephron sparing when possible. <!-- c096-r23 c096-r24 c096-r25 -->
- Many rare benign tumours exist, and imaging cannot reliably separate them from cancer. <!-- c096-r26 c096-r27 -->
- Reninoma (juxtaglomerular cell tumour): renin excess causes secondary hyperaldosteronism, hypertension and hypokalaemia; SIADH is not a feature. <!-- c096-a11 c096-r28 -->
- Smoking points to a malignant rather than benign renal mass. <!-- c096-a13 -->

## Ch 107 Surgery of the adrenal glands

### Anatomy
- Right adrenal: upper pole and medial border of the right kidney, more cephalad than the left, close to the diaphragm behind; arteries from the inferior phrenic (superior), aorta (middle) and renal artery (inferior). The medulla is innervated by preganglionic sympathetic fibres. <!-- c107-a01 -->
- The right adrenal vein enters the IVC (inferior vena cava) posterolaterally; if torn, rotate the cava to suture it. <!-- c107-a01 c107-r01 -->
- The pancreatic tail can lie against the left adrenal and upper pole of the left kidney. <!-- c107-r03 -->
- Avoid ligating an upper-pole renal artery branch with large masses: it causes segmental infarction, not bleeding. <!-- c107-a10 c107-r06 -->

### Indications and preparation
- Malignancy risk by size: under 4 cm 2%, 4–6 cm 6% (consider resection), over 6 cm 25% (resect). <!-- c107-a02 c107-r09 -->
- Commonest primaries metastasising to the adrenal: lung, breast, kidney, melanoma. Adrenocortical carcinoma recurs locoregionally in 60%. <!-- c107-r02 c107-r07 -->
- Phaeochromocytoma: α-blockade for at least 2 weeks restores volume and controls blood pressure and glucose. Phenoxybenzamine is non-selective and irreversible, causing tachycardia, postoperative hypotension and somnolence. Start β-blockade only after full α-blockade. Ligate the adrenal vein early; give fluids after removal; use short-acting nitroprusside, phentolamine, nitroglycerine or nicardipine for intraoperative hypertension. **Update:** PRESCRIPT (2020) found doxazosin as effective as phenoxybenzamine. <!-- c107-a04 c107-r10 -->
- Aldosteronoma: consider preoperative spironolactone and correct hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia. <!-- c107-r08 -->
- Addisonian crisis is commonest after removing a cortisol-secreting tumour (the other gland is suppressed); after bilateral adrenalectomy start steroids as soon as both glands are out. Crisis features: pain, vomiting, diarrhoea, hypotension, fever, hypoglycaemia, hyperkalaemia. <!-- c107-a06 c107-r05 -->

### Approaches
- Laparoscopy: large size is no longer an absolute contraindication (more conversion), but invasive ACC with IVC thrombus is. <!-- c107-a03 -->
- Posterior (prone) open or retroperitoneoscopic: direct, no muscle division, both glands without repositioning; but limited exposure, poor vein and vessel access and harder ventilation; ideal for bilateral hyperplasia and small benign tumours, not for large tumours or ACC. <!-- c107-a05 c107-a11 -->
- Retroperitoneal approach avoids bowel injury, pneumoperitoneum effects and adhesions; drawbacks are small working space, few landmarks and much fat. The lateral approach converts easily to transperitoneal. <!-- c107-a11 -->
- Thoracoabdominal approach for large invasive tumours involving the retrohepatic IVC, especially on the right; incise the diaphragm circumferentially, not radially (phrenic nerve injury). <!-- c107-a07 c107-r04 -->
- Several approaches are acceptable for most lesions. <!-- c107-a08 -->
- Partial adrenalectomy: expose without mobilising; arterial plexus can be divided; main vein can be ligated if the remnant stays in situ (preserve it when safe); useful in MEN 2A. A local recurrence after partial adrenalectomy is an absolute contraindication to repeat partial adrenalectomy. <!-- c107-a09 -->

### Complications
- Repair any diaphragmatic injury (herniation risk). Superior mesenteric vein ligation can be fatal: revascularise and resect bowel if needed. After splenectomy give pneumococcal, Hib and meningococcal vaccines once recovered, usually before discharge, not intraoperatively. <!-- c107-a12 -->

## Ch 129 Urinary tract fistulae

### Vesicovaginal fistula: causes and diagnosis
- VVF (vesicovaginal fistula) is the commonest urinary fistula. Industrialised world: hysterectomy (also cancer, prolapse, incontinence surgery, radiotherapy). Developing world, especially sub-Saharan Africa: obstructed labour in young primigravidas with narrow pelvis, causing large distal ischaemic fistulas involving trigone and bladder neck, often with rectovaginal fistula (obstructed labour injury complex), hard to repair. <!-- c129-a01 c129-a02 c129-a03 c129-a05 -->
- Bladder laceration is the commonest injury at hysterectomy; ureterovaginal fistula is less common than VVF. No blue dye in the pelvis does not exclude injury; recognising and repairing bladder injury lowers but does not remove the risk. <!-- c129-a03 c129-a04 -->
- Post-hysterectomy VVF usually lies on the anterior vaginal wall at the cuff. About 10%–12% have an associated ureteric injury. <!-- c129-a08 c129-r13 -->
- Radiation VVF may appear years later. Work-up: VCUG, cystoscopy and biopsy (exclude recurrence), CT, urodynamics for compliance; ureteroscopy is not needed. Always exclude recurrent malignancy after radiotherapy. <!-- c129-a06 c129-r01 c129-r12 -->
- Clear vaginal fluid is not always urine: peritoneovaginal, lymphatic fistula, vaginitis, tubal fluid. A fluid creatinine similar to serum excludes urine; wet but unstained pads on phenazopyridine suggest peritoneovaginal fistula. <!-- c129-a07 c129-r02 -->
- Three-swab/tampon test: orange (phenazopyridine) staining proximally means ureterovaginal; blue distally means stress leakage; VVF tends to give green staining in the middle. <!-- c129-a20 -->

### Vesicovaginal fistula: repair
- Radiation VVF: wait 5–6 months for demarcation; vaginal repair with flap is possible; success is lower than for non-radiation fistulas. <!-- c129-a09 -->
- Choose approach by anatomy, circumstances and surgeon; success similar. Vaginal: shorter stay, less morbidity, but possible vaginal shortening (Latzko). Abdominal: allows omental interposition; it does not reduce ureteric injury. <!-- c129-a10 c129-a16 -->
- Vaginal repair: 3–4 layer absorbable closure; Martius or peritoneal flaps preferred over gracilis; not contraindicated by obstetric fistula, a nearby ureter, or a high narrow cuff in nulliparous women. <!-- c129-a11 c129-a14 -->
- Excising the fistula tract is not always required. No evidence supports routine oestrogen, IV antibiotics or a particular drainage method, but unobstructed drainage is critical. <!-- c129-a12 c129-a13 -->
- Principles: multiple layers, no overlapping suture lines; abdominal repair needs bladder mobilisation caudal to the fistula; the book says cholinergic agents are used liberally after repair. **Update:** it is anticholinergics that are given, to prevent bladder spasms. Tissue interposition for failed repairs, very large and radiation fistulas; gracilis, rectus abdominis and Martius flaps are excellent. <!-- c129-r04 c129-r06 c129-r14 c129-r15 -->
- Martius flap supplied by internal and external pudendal and obturator arteries; obturator branches are sacrificed and either end can be divided. Peritoneal flap is advanced without opening the peritoneum. Omentum (right and left gastroepiploic arteries) often reaches the pelvis without mobilisation. <!-- c129-a18 c129-a19 c129-r07 c129-r08 -->
- Postoperative cystogram at 2–3 weeks must include voiding or post-void images. <!-- c129-a17 -->
- New SUI after repair occurs mainly with bladder neck or proximal urethral (obstetric) fistulas. Long-term: vaginal shortening and stenosis. Non-healing: poor nutrition, fungal infection, malignancy, TB, distal obstruction or foreign body. <!-- c129-a15 c129-r03 c129-r05 -->

### Ureterovaginal, vesicouterine and urethrovaginal fistula
- Ureterovaginal fistula: distal third of the ureter, mostly after benign hysterectomy of any route; voiding is normal because the other kidney fills the bladder; VCUG excludes coexisting VVF. Decompress the upper tract after ureteric injury. <!-- c129-a21 c129-a37 c129-r09 -->
- Vesicouterine fistula: mostly after low-segment caesarean section (rarely uterine rupture); urine leaks through the cervix but incontinence is not always present; VCUG confirms. Fertility-preserving options exist; hysterectomy if fertility is not wanted. It is unlikely after vaginal surgery. <!-- c129-a22 c129-a23 c129-a24 c129-a25 c129-r10 -->
- Distal urethrovaginal fistula is often asymptomatic (beyond the sphincter); vaginal flap repair; soft tissue flaps are important. <!-- c129-a26 c129-r11 c129-r17 -->

### Enteric and vascular fistulas
- Colovesical fistula: diverticulitis, then colon cancer, then Crohn disease. CT triad: air in bladder, bladder wall thickening next to thickened bowel, colonic diverticula. Cystoscopy abnormal in 80%–100% (bullous oedema is suggestive); biopsy to exclude cancer. Air in the bladder also follows instrumentation or gas-forming infection. <!-- c129-a27 c129-a28 c129-a29 c129-a30 c129-r18 -->
- Ureterocolic fistula: mostly right-sided, in Crohn disease. <!-- c129-a31 -->
- Rectourethral fistula: rectal injury in 1%–1.5% of radical prostatectomies (similar laparoscopically), rarely becoming a fistula if repaired; presents with faecaluria; biopsy if cancer was treated. Small uninfected fistula: trial of catheter; then single-stage repair (York-Mason has low anal incontinence) without colostomy. <!-- c129-a32 c129-a33 c129-a34 -->
- Pyelovascular fistula: mostly after percutaneous procedures; tamponade, then embolisation. <!-- c129-a35 -->
- Ureteroarterial fistula (long-term stent, prior pelvic surgery or radiation): angiography diagnoses and treats (embolisation or endovascular stent graft, usually iliac); do not pull the stent. <!-- c129-a36 c129-r16 -->

## Ch 147 Simple prostatectomy

- Versus TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate): more complete removal under direct vision, lower retreatment, no TUR syndrome. <!-- c147-a01 -->
- Only absolute indication: renal damage from prostatic obstruction. Relative: retention, recurrent UTI, refractory symptoms, prostatic haematuria, bladder stones. <!-- c147-r01 -->
- Do not operate on glands under 75 g (high complications). Consider it when lithotomy is impossible. Contraindications: small fibrous gland, significant cancer (for example multiple Gleason 8 cores), prior prostatectomy or pelvic surgery. **Update:** AUA and EAU now offer HoLEP and other size-independent endoscopic options for large glands as alternatives. <!-- c147-a05 c147-r02 c147-r03 -->
- Estimate size preoperatively (cystoscopy may help); cystoscopy is indicated for haematuria, stricture, known stone or diverticulum. <!-- c147-r04 -->
- Suprapubic (transvesical) approach: sees bladder neck and mucosa; best for a large median lobe, a diverticulum needing repair (do diverticulectomy at the same time to avoid stasis and infection) and large stones; good in obese men. The dorsal vein complex need not be controlled. Both suprapubic and retropubic approaches work in the space of Retzius. <!-- c147-a02 c147-a03 c147-a04 c147-a06 c147-r06 -->
- Risks: incontinence, ED, retrograde ejaculation, UTI, bladder neck contracture, urethral stricture, transfusion. <!-- c147-r05 -->
- Robot-assisted simple prostatectomy: smaller incisions, shorter stay, less bleeding and transfusion, less continuous irrigation. <!-- c147-a07 c147-r07 -->
