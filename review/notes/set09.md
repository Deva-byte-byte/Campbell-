## Ch 31 Exstrophy-Epispadias Complex

### Epidemiology and genetics
- Classic bladder exstrophy: 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 50,000 live births; male:female 2.3:1. <!-- c031-a01 c031-r01 -->
- Family risk is raised: offspring of an affected parent have a 1 in 70 risk, about 500 times the general population (Shapiro). <!-- c031-a02 c031-r02 -->
- Cloacal exstrophy: 1 in 200,000 to 400,000 births. Male epispadias: 1 in 117,000 males. <!-- c031-a29 c031-a35 -->

### Embryology
- Basic defect (Marshall and Muecke): abnormal overdevelopment of the cloacal membrane, which blocks medial migration of mesenchyme and proper lower abdominal wall formation. <!-- c031-a03 c031-r19 -->
- Superior vesical fissure variant: same muscle and bone defects as classic exstrophy, but the cloacal membrane ruptures only at its top, leaving a small vesicostomy-like fistula above a normal umbilicus; bladder extrusion is minimal. <!-- c031-a38 -->

### Anatomy of the defect
- Pelvis (Sponseller): posterior pelvis externally rotated 12° each side, acetabular retroversion, anterior pelvis externally rotated 18°, pubic rami 30% shorter. <!-- c031-a04 -->
- Inguinal hernia in about 82% of boys and 10% of girls; reflux in 100%. <!-- c031-a05 c031-r06 -->
- Penis: anterior corporal length almost 50% shorter than in controls (MRI). Prostate volume, weight and cross-sectional area are normal. <!-- c031-a06 c031-r20 c031-a07 -->
- Vagina: short (barely over 6 cm) but of normal calibre. <!-- c031-a08 -->
- Bladder: muscarinic receptor density and affinity are normal, so the bladder's neural make-up is not grossly altered. 80% of bladders are compliant and stable before bladder neck reconstruction. <!-- c031-a09 c031-a10 -->
- Autonomic nerves are displaced laterally. An isolated ectopic bowel segment may lie in the lower abdominal wall. <!-- c031-r05 c031-r07 -->
- Rectal prolapse is common in untreated exstrophy with wide symphyseal diastasis and disappears after closure; prolapse after closure suggests bladder outlet obstruction. <!-- c031-r03 c031-r04 -->

### Prenatal and newborn assessment
- Prenatal ultrasound signs: no bladder filling, low-set umbilicus, widened pubic rami, small genitalia, and a lower abdominal mass that grows with gestation. <!-- c031-a11 -->
- At birth: tie the cord with silk (no clamp) and cover the bladder with non-adherent film to prevent trauma and drying. <!-- c031-r15 -->
- A small bladder may still be adequate if it bulges when the baby cries or indents easily under anaesthesia. If it is too small, wait 4 to 6 months for growth rather than risk a closure that dehisces. <!-- c031-a12 c031-a14 -->

### Staged reconstruction (modern staged repair)
- Key changes: early closure of bladder, posterior urethra and abdominal wall (usually with osteotomy); early epispadias repair; later continent bladder neck reconstruction with ureteric reimplantation; strict selection criteria. Bladder neck repair comes after, not before, epispadias repair. <!-- c031-a13 -->
- Osteotomy is rarely needed in newborns unless diastasis exceeds 4 cm. Combined anterior osteotomy: done supine with the urological repair (no turning), allows external fixator and pins under vision, and gives a better cosmetic result than the posterior iliac approach. <!-- c031-r08 c031-a15 -->
- Immobilisation with external fixator plus modified Buck traction gave 97% successful closure. <!-- c031-a16 -->
- Closure reshapes the pelvis, redistributes the levators and smooths the pelvic floor. Control bladder spasms after closure. <!-- c031-r14 c031-r16 -->
- After closure: calibrate the outlet before removing the suprapubic tube at 4 weeks, image the kidneys, and give antibiotic prophylaxis (all have reflux); check residuals and cultures. <!-- c031-a19 -->
- Recurrent UTI or a distended bladder after closure: cystoscopy to look for erosion of the intrapubic stitch into the posterior urethra or bladder. <!-- c031-a18 c031-r22 -->
- Epispadias repair goals: correct dorsal (not ventral) chordee, reconstruct the urethra and glans, and close penile skin. <!-- c031-a21 -->
- Bladder neck reconstruction at 4 to 5 years, when capacity is adequate and, above all, the child can join a voiding programme. Most become dry by day within 1 year. <!-- c031-r21 c031-a23 -->
- Erlangen approach: all reconstruction in one stage at 8 to 10 weeks of age. <!-- c031-a17 -->

### Continence and failure
- Best predictors of continence: bladder template size at birth, successful primary closure, and bladder capacity at bladder neck reconstruction (also true in epispadias). <!-- c031-r09 c031-a22 c031-a37 -->
- About 75% are continent after repair (continence = 3 hours dry). A successful first closure gives earlier continence and higher rates. <!-- c031-r10 c031-a25 -->
- Dehiscence (from poor pelvic floor mobilisation, poor immobilisation, infection, distension or tube problems) needs 6 months before re-closure. After two closures only 60% reach adequate capacity for bladder neck reconstruction. <!-- c031-a24 c031-a20 -->
- Most failed bladder necks need augmentation or continent diversion: bladder neck transection, augmentation and a continent (Mitrofanoff) stoma; an artificial sphincter suits only those with good capacity. <!-- c031-a27 c031-r23 -->
- Ureterosigmoidostomy needs normal anal continence; risks are pyelonephritis, hyperchloraemic acidosis, rectal incontinence, ureteric obstruction and late malignancy. <!-- c031-a28 -->

### Complete primary repair (Mitchell)
- Principle: return bladder neck and urethra to a normal posterior pelvic position for pelvic floor support. Dissection limit is the pelvic floor, which is split. Reflux is usually not corrected at the same time. <!-- c031-a43 c031-a46 c031-a42 -->
- Penile disassembly works because each corporal body and the urethral wedge have independent proximal blood supply, which must be preserved. <!-- c031-a45 -->
- No prolonged nil by mouth; decompress any ileus because distension strains the repair. <!-- c031-a44 -->
- Commonest complication: ventral urethrocutaneous fistula; others are corporal devascularisation, hydronephrosis and hypospadias. Dehiscence may cause loss of glans, corpora and urethral plate. <!-- c031-a48 c031-a26 -->

### Cloacal exstrophy
- Features: bladder exstrophy, complete phallic separation, wide diastasis, exstrophied terminal ileum between the bladder halves, rudimentary hindgut, imperforate anus, omphalocele (88% to 95% or more) and often spinal defects (rare in classic exstrophy). <!-- c031-r11 c031-a32 -->
- Spinal defects are thought to come from disrupted dorsal mesenchyme, not failed neural tube closure. Neurological deficit causes most long-term disability. <!-- c031-a30 c031-r18 -->
- Müllerian anomalies in up to 95%, mostly partial uterine duplication (bicornuate). <!-- c031-a31 -->
- Use the hindgut remnant to add bowel length for fluid absorption. <!-- c031-a33 -->
- Single-stage repair is risky with a large omphalocele, wide diastasis, myelomeningocele or small bladder plate. <!-- c031-a47 -->
- Gender: assign according to karyotype where possible; neonatal testis histology is normal and a functional phallus can now be built. <!-- c031-a34 c031-r24 -->

### Epispadias
- Reflux in 30% to 40% (deficient ureterovesical junction); reflux and hernia are less common than in exstrophy. <!-- c031-a36 c031-r17 -->

### Long-term outcomes
- Sexual function and libido are normal in both sexes. Men worry about penile length, appearance and deviation; women about genital appearance, vaginal opening and uterine prolapse. <!-- c031-a39 c031-r12 -->
- Women can deliver children; cervical and uterine prolapse after pregnancy is common. <!-- c031-a40 c031-r13 -->
- Children with exstrophy do not have clinical psychopathology. <!-- c031-a41 -->

## Ch 72 Surgery for Erectile Dysfunction

### Counselling and choice
- Consent points: erect penis will likely be shorter and thinner (the main cause of disappointment); the implant only gives rigidity for intercourse; sensation and ejaculation may decrease; pain varies; reoperation may be needed for mechanical failure or infection. <!-- c072-r01 c072-r02 c072-r03 c072-a05 c072-r04 c072-r05 c072-r06 c072-r10 -->
- Alternatives: oral drugs, intraurethral inserts, injections and vacuum devices. Types: three-piece and two-piece inflatables and semirigid rods. <!-- c072-r07 c072-r08 -->
- Three-piece inflatables give the best rigidity and flaccidity and are most popular. Poor manual dexterity: choose a semirigid rod. <!-- c072-r09 c072-r11 -->
- Outcomes: satisfaction 80% to 90% (patients and partners); revision about 15% at 5 years; infection 1% to 3%; rod erosion 20% to 50% in spinal cord injury. Coloplast has the lock-out valve on the reservoir, Boston Scientific on the pump. <!-- c072-a03 -->

### Technique
- Penoscrotal approach: subdartos pump, cosmetic incision, spares the dorsal nerves, allows two-piece devices; semirigid rods are easier through a subcoronal incision. <!-- c072-a06 -->
- Keep scissors and dilators pointing dorsolaterally and parallel to the shaft to avoid crural crossover and urethral injury. Crossover causes difficult insertion, asymmetry around a catheter, a lopsided erection and unequal lengths, but the erection is usually still straight. <!-- c072-r14 c072-a08 -->
- Urethral perforation during dilation: do not place both cylinders. Options are abandoning, placing only the contralateral cylinder and returning later, or creating a hypospadias, repairing securely and placing both. Close any corporal-to-skin defect securely before leaving a cylinder. <!-- c072-a02 c072-r12 -->
- After a T-shunt for priapism avoid AMS 700 LGX, the only cylinder that expands in length, as it may push through the distal defect. <!-- c072-a01 -->
- Peyronie disease: a non-length-expanding cylinder usually straightens the curve; LGX tends to exaggerate curvature. <!-- c072-r13 c072-a07 -->
- Reservoir in a capacious space to avoid bladder or bowel injury. Not in a scrotal cavity after orchidectomy: the lockout valve can be squeezed and cause autoinflation. <!-- c072-r17 c072-a09 -->
- Poor penile perfusion: distal necrosis risk, so limit or stage aggressive reconstruction. <!-- c072-r16 -->

### Infection
- Remove all foreign material in an established infection. Salvage is less likely with cellulitis, pus, onset within 2 months, or aggressive organisms (MRSA, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus; Pseudomonas); diabetes raises infection risk but not salvage failure. <!-- c072-r15 c072-a04 -->

## Ch 75 Anatomy of the Retroperitoneum

### Boundaries and contents
- Contents: kidneys, ureters, adrenals, pancreas, 2nd and 3rd parts of duodenum, ascending and descending colon, mesentery, aorta, IVC (inferior vena cava), lymphatics, sympathetic trunk, lumbosacral plexus. Transverse colon is intraperitoneal. <!-- c075-a01 c075-r01 -->
- Bounded by posterior peritoneum in front, posterior abdominal wall behind, diaphragm above and extraperitoneal pelvis below. <!-- c075-r02 c075-r03 -->

### Body wall
- Psoas major (anterior rami L1 to L3) and iliacus both flex the hip. <!-- c075-a02 -->
- 11th and 12th ribs are floating; bony landmarks are reliable for incisions, the umbilicus is not. Intercostal nerves and vessels run between internal and innermost intercostals in the costal groove on the lower border of the upper rib. Lower rib fractures should raise suspicion of retroperitoneal injury. <!-- c075-a03 c075-r04 -->
- Lumbodorsal fascia has three layers and merges anterolaterally with transversus abdominis. Transversalis fascia lies deep to transversus abdominis and superficial to preperitoneal fat. <!-- c075-r05 c075-a04 -->

### Fasciae and spaces
- Embryology: outer stratum forms transversalis fascia; intermediate stratum surrounds urinary organs; inner stratum the gut. <!-- c075-a07 -->
- Perirenal space (between laminae of Gerota fascia) is cone-shaped and open caudally, so haematoma or fluid spreads downwards. <!-- c075-a05 c075-r07 c075-r08 -->
- Gerota laminae fuse laterally as the lateroconal fascia, separating anterior and posterior pararenal spaces. Posterior pararenal space: posterior Gerota lamina in front, transversalis fascia behind and laterally. <!-- c075-a06 c075-r06 -->
- White line of Toldt: lateral edge of colonic mesentery fusion with posterior peritoneum. The mesentery is continuous, with gut, immune, endocrine and vascular roles; small mesenteric defects carry more internal hernia risk and should be closed. <!-- c075-r09 c075-a08 -->

### Vessels
- Adrenal arteries: superior (inferior phrenic), middle (aorta), inferior (renal artery). <!-- c075-a09 c075-r10 -->
- Renal hila at L1; renal veins lie anterior to arteries. Gonadal arteries arise anterolaterally from the aorta below the renal arteries. <!-- c075-r11 c075-r12 -->
- SMA (superior mesenteric artery) ligation causes catastrophic bowel ischaemia; the IMA (inferior mesenteric artery) can be sacrificed thanks to the marginal artery of Drummond and haemorrhoidal collaterals. <!-- c075-a10 c075-r13 -->
- Ureteric blood supply: medial for the proximal ureter (renal artery, aorta), lateral for the distal ureter (internal iliac, vesical arteries). <!-- c075-a13 c075-r14 -->
- Right gonadal vein drains to the IVC; left gonadal (testicular or ovarian) vein enters the left renal vein at a right angle, hence left varicoceles. A new right-sided varicocele needs retroperitoneal imaging. <!-- c075-a12 c075-r15 -->
- On right colon mobilisation find the ureter lateral to the gonadal vein; going medial risks IVC injury and gonadal avulsion. <!-- c075-a11 -->
- Splenic vein tributaries: inferior mesenteric, short gastric, left gastroepiploic, pancreatic; right gastroepiploic drains to the superior mesenteric vein. <!-- c075-a14 -->

### Lymphatics
- Testes are embryologically retroperitoneal, with retroperitoneal blood supply and lymph drainage. <!-- c075-r16 -->
- Right testis: interaortocaval mainly, some right paracaval and a little left para-aortic. Left testis: left para-aortic, some interaortocaval. Flow is right to left. <!-- c075-a15 c075-r17 c075-r18 c075-r19 -->

### Nerves
- Parasympathetic: craniosacral (cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X and S2 to S4); postganglionic fibres lie in the organ walls. Sympathetic preganglionic fibres leave T1 to L2 and synapse in the trunk or plexuses. Somatic supply via the lumbosacral plexus. <!-- c075-a16 c075-r20 c075-r21 c075-r22 -->
- Genitofemoral nerve arises from L1 to L2 (not L3 to L4). <!-- c075-a17 -->
- Superior hypogastric plexus (aortic bifurcation to front of L5) injury causes loss of emission or failed bladder neck closure with retrograde ejaculation. <!-- c075-a18 -->
- Obturator nerve supplies the medial thigh adductors (gracilis, adductor longus, brevis, magnus, obturator externus): injury weakens hip adduction. <!-- c075-a19 -->

## Ch 102 Laparoscopic and Robotic Surgery of the Kidney

### General principles
- Minimally invasive renal surgery gives less pain, shorter recovery and better cosmesis with equal efficacy; indications match open surgery; follow oncological principles (negative margins, maximal parenchymal preservation); know landmarks and pitfalls. <!-- c102-r01 c102-r02 c102-r03 c102-r04 -->
- Obesity distorts anatomy and raises complication and conversion rates but is not a contraindication; obese patients benefit. Absolute contraindications: uncorrected coagulopathy, untreated infection, hypovolaemic shock. Large masses, elderly patients, renal vein thrombus and prior surgery are feasible. <!-- c102-a01 c102-a10 -->
- Retroperitoneal approach suits posterior tumours, transperitoneal anterior ones. LESS (laparoendoscopic single-site surgery) can use standard instruments; articulating tools help but are not required for LESS or NOTES (natural orifice surgery). <!-- c102-a02 c102-a03 -->

### Benign procedures
- Cyst surgery for pain, obstruction, suspected malignancy or infection, not for ADPKD (autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease) alone. Multiple or bilateral cysts can be treated together (over 90% improve). <!-- c102-a04 c102-a05 -->
- Laparoscopic renal biopsy is preferred after failed percutaneous biopsy, with anatomical anomalies, bleeding tendency, morbid obesity, multiple cysts or a solitary kidney. <!-- c102-a06 -->
- Nephropexy: 90% to 100% pain relief, 80% to 95% resolution of the 5-cm descent, 90% better renal function on scan. Nephrolysis (hilar skeletonisation) treats refractory chyluria. Calyceal diverticulectomy rarely needs hilar clamping. <!-- c102-a07 c102-a08 -->

### Radical and partial nephrectomy
- Laparoscopic radical nephrectomy: better cosmesis, shorter stay, less analgesia and recovery; oncological outcomes equal to open, not better. <!-- c102-a09 -->
- Hand-assisted: similar recovery, cost and survival to pure laparoscopy, but slightly more wound infection and hernia. <!-- c102-a12 -->
- Safe: en bloc hilar stapling, cytoreductive laparoscopic nephrectomy. Mandatory: retrieval bag when morcellating. Avoid: clips alone on the renal artery (fatal clip failures), blunt hand dissection of the adrenal. <!-- c102-a11 -->
- Partial nephrectomy: central, hilar, solitary-kidney and multiple tumours are feasible in experienced hands; redo surgery bleeds more; drain after collecting system repair; suture renorrhaphy is the most reliable haemostasis. <!-- c102-a13 -->
- Hilar control: bulldogs or Satinsky; artery-only clamping is safe; parenchymal compression suits peripheral tumours; off-clamp for small exophytic tumours; early unclamping before renorrhaphy is acceptable (shorter warm ischaemia, similar blood loss). <!-- c102-a14 -->
- Thermal ablation: less blood loss and fewer complications than partial nephrectomy; laparoscopic ablation allows tumour visualisation and organ retraction; keep the iceball off collecting system, ureter, vessels and bowel; RFA (radiofrequency ablation) heats tissue to 60°C to 100°C, monitored by temperature or impedance; avoid over 105°C (vaporisation). <!-- c102-a15 -->

### Complications
- Risk factors: obesity, comorbidity, prior surgery, larger and complex tumours; age alone is not one. <!-- c102-a16 -->
- Urine leak (large, hilar tumours, collecting system repair, long ischaemia): most settle with percutaneous drainage alone; stent if that fails or there is distal obstruction. <!-- c102-a17 -->
- Gallbladder injury: cholecystectomy. Unrecognised bowel injury is mostly thermal (about 50%) and presents late. <!-- c102-a18 c102-a19 -->
- Gross haematuria after partial nephrectomy suggests pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula: go straight to angiography and selective embolisation. <!-- c102-a20 -->

## Ch 120 Pharmacology of Storage and Emptying Failure

### Muscarinic receptors
- Bladder contraction is driven by acetylcholine on postganglionic parasympathetic muscarinic receptors. Detrusor and urothelium contain M2 and M3; M2 outnumber M3 at least 3:1, but M3 mediates contraction (via a rise in intracellular calcium from influx and stores). <!-- c120-r01 c120-a02 c120-a03 c120-r12 c120-a32 c120-a35 -->
- Receptor function can change in non-neurogenic bladder disorders. Atropine resistance is mainly due to ATP (adenosine triphosphate), not noradrenaline (released by sympathetic hypogastric nerves). <!-- c120-r02 c120-a31 -->
- Antimuscarinics raise volume at first involuntary contraction, lower its amplitude and raise capacity, without changing outlet resistance. At OAB (overactive bladder) doses they act in the storage phase, mainly by reducing C-fibre and Aδ afferent activity, micromotions and urothelial excitation, not by reducing the voiding contraction. <!-- c120-a01 c120-a04 c120-a33 -->
- Side effects by subtype: M3 for dry mouth, constipation and blurred vision; M1 for cognition; M2 for heart rate (tachycardia when blocked); M4 and M5 none. QT prolongation (hERG channel) and reduced, not increased, sweating are not class effects. <!-- c120-a06 c120-a08 c120-a30 c120-a33 -->
- Brain entry is favoured by high lipophilicity, small size and low charge. Tertiary amines enter the CNS; quaternary amines (trospium) are poorly absorbed and barely enter, so trospium is safest for cognition. Antimuscarinics may harm cognition in the elderly and may have cardiac effects. **Update:** AUA 2024 advises counselling on dementia risk with antimuscarinics. <!-- c120-a04 c120-r09 c120-a34 c120-r08 c120-r10 -->

### Antimuscarinic drugs
- Oxybutynin: antimuscarinic, direct relaxant and local anaesthetic; orally it acts mainly as an antimuscarinic (500 times weaker as a relaxant). Transdermal oxybutynin cuts the side-effect-causing metabolite; main problem is skin reaction (pruritus 14%, erythema 8.3%). Intravesical oxybutynin works in OAB and augmented bladders. <!-- c120-a08 c120-a09 c120-r07 -->
- Darifenacin: relatively M3-selective. Trospium is renally excreted by proximal tubules; darifenacin, oxybutynin, solifenacin and tolterodine are metabolised by hepatic cytochrome P450. <!-- c120-a10 c120-a11 -->
- ICI 2013 (Oxford grading): level 1, grade A for tolterodine, fesoterodine, darifenacin, solifenacin, propiverine (mixed action, not in USA), oxybutynin and trospium; flavoxate is level 2, grade D. <!-- c120-a05 c120-a35 -->
- Always combine drugs with behavioural therapy. Calcium channel blockers and current potassium channel openers do not work for OAB. <!-- c120-r03 c120-r04 c120-r05 -->

### β3 agonists, PDE5 inhibitors, botulinum toxin, vanilloids
- Three β receptors in detrusor; β3 predominates and relaxes it. Mirabegron, the first β3 agonist, activates β3 and also inhibits filling-induced Aδ and C-fibre afferent activity. **Update:** vibegron is a second approved β3 agonist. <!-- c120-a07 c120-a10 c120-r13 c120-a37 -->
- PDE5 (phosphodiesterase 5) inhibitors improve IIEF and IPSS scores but not peak flow or detrusor pressure; tadalafil is approved for LUTS from BPO. <!-- c120-a11 c120-r14 -->
- Antimuscarinic plus α-blocker beats α-blocker alone in men with BPO and OAB without worsening emptying. <!-- c120-a13 -->
- Botulinum toxin A cleaves SNAP-25, inactivating the SNARE complex; blocks acetylcholine release in striated and smooth muscle and reduces afferent firing; works in neurogenic and idiopathic DO and OAB without DO. Seven serotypes; A and B used. Intrasphincteric injection treats sphincter dyssynergia but causes, not treats, SUI. <!-- c120-a13 c120-a29 -->
- Neurogenic DO failing or intolerant of antimuscarinics: intradetrusor botulinum toxin rather than more antimuscarinic. Repeat every 3 to 12 months; improves quality of life; efficacy does not fall with repeats; can cause retention; local anaesthesia suffices. <!-- c120-a36 -->
- Vanilloids desensitise C fibres (perhaps Aδ too) after initial excitation; resiniferatoxin is 1000 times more potent than capsaicin for desensitisation, a few hundred times for excitation. DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide) 50% is for bladder pain syndrome, not DO. <!-- c120-a35 -->

### Oestrogens, desmopressin, tricyclics
- Oral oestrogen (± progestogen) worsens incontinence and causes new incontinence (HERS, WHI, Nurses' Health Study); no evidence supports oestrogen for incontinence. Vaginal oestrogen treats urogenital atrophy (dryness, pruritus, dyspareunia), better than oral. <!-- c120-a14 c120-a18 c120-a36 c120-r06 -->
- Desmopressin: risk of hyponatraemia (confusion in the elderly); check sodium before and a few days after starting. <!-- c120-a19 -->
- Imipramine side effects: antimuscarinic effects, weakness, fatigue, tremor, sedation, postural hypotension, sweating, delayed orgasm, arrhythmias; not priapism. <!-- c120-a12 -->

### Outlet drugs
- α-agonists cause hypertension, anxiety, insomnia, headache, tremor, palpitations and arrhythmias, not somnolence; caution in hypertension, heart disease or hyperthyroidism. Phenylpropanolamine raised haemorrhagic stroke risk in women under 50 and was withdrawn in the USA. <!-- c120-a15 c120-a16 -->
- Propranolol might unmask α-tone and raise outlet resistance in a hypertensive patient with SUI (unproven). <!-- c120-a17 -->
- Duloxetine (serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor) raises sphincter activity; it is hepatically metabolised, licensed in the EU for moderate to severe SUI (15 or more episodes a week) but not in the USA. <!-- c120-a18 -->
- Lower tract has more α2 than α1 receptors, but contraction is mediated by α1 (α1A; α1D in detrusor). α-blockers: phenoxybenzamine and phentolamine are non-selective; prazosin was the first selective; terazosin, doxazosin, alfuzosin and tamsulosin are α1-selective. Tamsulosin gives more retrograde ejaculation and rhinitis; terazosin and doxazosin more dizziness and asthenia. α-blockers do not work in women and can worsen SUI. <!-- c120-a23 c120-a37 c120-r15 c120-a24 c120-a25 c120-r11 c120-a35 -->
- Bethanechol is muscarinic-selective and cholinesterase-resistant, but no evidence shows it helps bladder emptying; oral 200 mg equals 5 mg subcutaneously. <!-- c120-a20 c120-a21 -->
- Prostanoids potentiate acetylcholine release, not ATP release. <!-- c120-a22 -->
- No drug selectively relaxes the pelvic floor striated muscle. GABA is the main CNS inhibitory transmitter. Benzodiazepines act on GABA-A; baclofen on GABA-B (spinal cord); dantrolene blocks calcium release in skeletal muscle. <!-- c120-a26 c120-a27 c120-a28 -->

## Ch 142 Minimally Invasive Urinary Diversion

### Background and selection
- Robotic cystectomy gives less pain and blood loss and faster bowel recovery. Intracorporeal diversion is rising about 11% a year; ileal conduit is the commonest robotic diversion. <!-- c142-r01 c142-r02 c142-r03 -->
- Selection has widened (high BMI, locally advanced disease, older age, prior surgery); Xi system and AirSeal allow less steep Trendelenburg; neobladder contraindications are as in open surgery. <!-- c142-a02 -->

### Perioperative care
- Avoid mechanical bowel preparation and oral antibiotics; IV broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour before incision. <!-- c142-r04 c142-r06 -->
- VTE: haematological and vascular complications about 10%; mean diagnosis 15.2 days, 55% after discharge; give LMWH (low-molecular-weight heparin) for 4 weeks. <!-- c142-a01 c142-r05 -->
- ERAS (enhanced recovery after surgery; over 20 elements: alvimopan, goal-directed fluids, early diet, non-opioid analgesia) reduces stay, complications and readmissions. Avoid overhydration in these older patients. <!-- c142-a03 c142-r07 c142-r16 -->

### Technique
- Six to seven ports placed cephalad; an extra robotic arm can go through a 15-mm port; a 0° lens throughout (30° down sometimes for a deep pelvis). <!-- c142-r08 c142-r09 c142-a04 c142-r10 -->
- Marionette stitch is held, not tied, so the bowel can move. Leave enough ileum near the ileocaecal valve. Remove the guidewire only after the anchoring stitch (no tactile feedback). <!-- c142-a05 c142-r12 c142-r13 c142-r14 -->
- Uretero-ileal anastomosis: cold-scissor button-hole enterotomy, no cautery (stricture), and retroperitonealise with a peritoneal flap. <!-- c142-a06 c142-r15 -->
- Neobladder-urethral tension: reduce Trendelenburg or undock, do the anastomosis early, incise peritoneum over the mesentery, mobilise the ileocaecal area, move the sigmoid out of the pelvis, apply perineal pressure, use barbed sutures, detubularise, and use temporary traction (Penrose). <!-- c142-a07 c142-a11 c142-r11 -->
- Nerve sparing only with safe tumour location, low burden and good function. Nerves are at risk at the common iliacs, presacral area and pelvic plexus near Denonvilliers fascia; spare the lower seminal vesicles. 3D reconstruction maps the pelvic nerves; the dorsal venous complex overlaps a third of the sphincter. <!-- c142-a10 c142-a09 -->

### Outcomes
- Operating time: no difference between intracorporeal and extracorporeal diversion (IRCC, 2134 patients); high-volume centres are faster. <!-- c142-a08 -->
- 90-day complications 41% intracorporeal vs 49% extracorporeal (P = .05); gastrointestinal complications and sepsis dominate; sepsis was commonest with intracorporeal diversion. <!-- c142-a12 -->
- Drain the pelvis and stent the ureters; irrigate a neobladder every 8 hours to prevent mucus blockage. <!-- c142-r17 c142-r18 -->

## Ch 150 Prostate Biopsy

### TRUS anatomy and ultrasound
- Five divisions: anterior fibromuscular stroma, transition, central, periurethral and peripheral zones. <!-- c150-r01 -->
- Corpora amylacea and calcifications lie along the surgical capsule (transition–peripheral zone junction); small diffuse calcifications are age-related; large symptomatic calculi suggest infection or inflammation. <!-- c150-a01 c150-a02 c150-r02 -->
- Seminal vesicle: 4.5 to 5.5 cm long, 2 cm wide, symmetric. Cystic masses are benign; solid ones are occasionally malignant, and schistosomiasis is a cause in endemic regions. <!-- c150-a04 c150-a03 c150-r03 -->
- Adult prostate is 20 to 25 g, stable until about age 50. <!-- c150-a05 -->
- Higher probe frequency gives better resolution, lower frequency deeper penetration; remove any air interface. <!-- c150-r04 -->
- Volume needs three dimensions (ellipsoid, sphere or prolate formula); planimetry is most accurate. **Update:** measure width and AP height on the mid-gland transverse view and length on the sagittal view; volume = length × width × height × 0.52. <!-- c150-r05 -->
- Echogenicity: 39% of cancers are isoechoic, 1% hyperechoic; a hypoechoic lesion is cancer about 20% of the time and does not predict Gleason grade. Biopsy hypoechoic lesions; mimics are granulomatous prostatitis, infarct and lymphoma; haematological malignancies are often isoechoic. <!-- c150-a06 c150-a16 c150-r06 c150-r07 -->
- MRI image case: a well-demarcated low-signal focus in the peripheral zone with intact capsule means organ-confined cancer. <!-- c150-a17 -->

### Technique
- No PSA level rules out cancer. Initial detection with PSA 4 to 10 ng/mL is 22% (book misprints μg/mL); repeat biopsies find 10%, 5% and 4%, with cancers of lower grade and stage. <!-- c150-r08 c150-r17 c150-a13 -->
- Left lateral decubitus is commonest (lithotomy possible); self-administered enema usual. Best needle-path view is sagittal. <!-- c150-a08 c150-r16 c150-r11 -->
- Analgesia: periprostatic block with 5 mL lidocaine each side at the seminal vesicle–base junction via a 22-gauge spinal needle through the biopsy channel. <!-- c150-a07 c150-r10 -->
- At least 12 systematic cores (AUA); 18 to 20 cores add little. Isolated transition zone cancers are under 5%. After repeated negative biopsies target the anterior zone and apex (template or fusion). <!-- c150-a09 c150-r15 c150-r18 c150-r19 -->
- Four seminal vesicle cores in a high-risk man means a misdirected biopsy: repeat it. <!-- c150-a18 -->
- No rectum: transperineal US-guided or CT-guided biopsy. Transperineal sampling covers the apex better and may cause fewer infections. **Update:** EAU now prefers transperineal biopsy for its lower infection risk. <!-- c150-r13 c150-r14 -->
- Brachytherapy: seeds evenly distributed with urethral sparing. <!-- c150-a10 -->

### Imaging-targeted biopsy
- MRI-TRUS fusion coregisters a prebiopsy multiparametric MRI with real-time ultrasound using software; the MRI must show targets. Other options: Doppler, elastography, endorectal MRI with dynamic contrast and diffusion, spectroscopy. **Update:** prebiopsy MRI with targeted biopsy is now standard (PRECISION 2018). <!-- c150-a15 c150-r21 c150-r22 -->

### Complications and prophylaxis
- Complications: febrile UTI, bacteraemia, prostatitis, bleeding, haematospermia (up to 4 to 6 weeks, harmless), retention and sepsis. <!-- c150-r20 c150-a12 -->
- Infection risk factors: non-white race, comorbidities, diabetes, large prostate, foreign travel and recent antibiotics. <!-- c150-a14 c150-r12 -->
- Prophylaxis reduces but does not eliminate febrile UTI. Book: fluoroquinolone 2 hours before and for 48 hours after; with endocarditis risk or prostheses, IV ampicillin (vancomycin if allergic) plus gentamicin, then fluoroquinolone. **Update:** fluoroquinolone restrictions (EMA 2019) and resistance favour targeted prophylaxis or the transperineal route. <!-- c150-a11 c150-r09 -->
