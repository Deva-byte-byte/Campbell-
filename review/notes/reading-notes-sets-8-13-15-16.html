Ch 28 Evaluation of lower urinary tract dysfunction in children

History, diaries and questionnaires

  • First decide from history and examination whether the problem is mainly storage or emptying; tailor work-up to that.
  • Full history: symptoms, voiding frequency, bowel function, prior UTIs (urinary tract infections), family history, maternal prenatal history and scans, birth history, milestones including toilet training, neuropsychiatric conditions, medical and surgical history, social history, diet, systems review.
  • A 7-day bowel and bladder diary or 48-hour frequency-volume chart is invaluable and comes before invasive tests; it reveals low functional capacity, infrequent voiding, nocturia and polydipsia.
  • Constipation, neuropsychiatric problems, UTI and vesicoureteral reflux commonly accompany lower urinary tract dysfunction.
  • Validated tools: symptoms (DVSS, dysfunctional voiding scoring system; DVISS; ISI-P, incontinence symptom index-paediatric); quality of life (PinQ, Pediatric Incontinence Questionnaire); behaviour (CBCL, Child Behavior Checklist; SSIPPE screening for psychological problems in enuresis).

Examination and occult spinal dysraphism

  • Inspect the lower back for dimples, gluteal cleft deviation, hair tufts, dermal sinuses and vascular lesions; palpate for the sacrum and any mass.
  • Risk of occult dysraphism, lowest to highest: simple dimple, symmetric bifid gluteal cleft, hair tuft, dermal sinus, haemangioma.
  • MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) is the most sensitive and specific test; spinal ultrasound works up to 6 months of age but is less sensitive; CT, PET and videourodynamics have no role.
  • For low-risk findings (for example a deviated gluteal cleft in a 6-month-old) ultrasound is reasonable, with MRI if it is positive or inconclusive.

Urine and blood tests

  • Every child with LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) needs urinalysis: specific gravity, protein, glucose, white cells, red cells, bacteria (AUA, American Urological Association). Other tests are not routine at first visit.
  • Malodorous urine alone has poor sensitivity and specificity for a positive culture.
  • Blood tests only when upper tract disease is suspected.

Non-invasive urodynamics

  • Non-invasive tests: uroflowmetry, patch EMG (electromyography), pelvic ultrasound.
  • Uroflow gives curve shape; Qmax is the key number. Valid if voided volume is over 50 mL (adults over 150 mL) and ideally under 115% of expected capacity.
  • ICCS (International Children's Continence Society, 2014) curve shapes: bell, tower, staccato, interrupted (intermittent), plateau.
  • EMG lag time (start of sphincter relaxation to start of flow) is normally 2–6 s with a bell curve; under 2 s suggests overactive bladder with a tower curve; over 6 s suggests primary bladder neck dysfunction with a plateau curve. EMG also shows synergic versus dyssynergic voiding.
  • Functional bladder capacity = voided volume plus PVR (post-void residual), or the largest voided volume on the diary. (Age + 2) × 30 mL is expected capacity, not functional capacity. Update: the book's explanation says "smallest voided volume", but the correct answer is the largest (ICCS excludes the first morning void).
  • Ultrasound gives pre- and post-void volumes (PVR), bladder wall thickness, rectal diameter and debris; debris means a higher chance of a positive culture, but interpret it cautiously if there are no symptoms.

Invasive urodynamics

  • Urodynamics is the gold standard for diagnosing lower urinary tract dysfunction.
  • Fill at 5%–10% of expected capacity per minute; a fixed 20 mL/min is too fast for a neonate.
  • Sedation or anaesthesia (for catheter placement or the study) abolishes detrusor overactivity; compliance, detrusor leak point pressure and capacity are unaffected.
  • Post-urodynamics UTI is 1.4%; routine prophylactic antibiotics or pre-study cultures do not prevent it.

Ch 29 Vesicoureteral reflux

Epidemiology and genetics

  • VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) prevalence: about 30% of children with UTI and about 17% without infection.
  • Antenatally detected reflux: male predominance, usually high grade and bilateral in boys, often with congenital dysplasia and renal impairment at birth.
  • VUR is about 10 times less common in girls of African descent.
  • About one third of siblings have reflux; inheritance is thought autosomal dominant. Once sibling reflux is found, treatment indications are the same as for any child; renal imaging, age and UTI history guide whether to screen.

Pathophysiology

  • Primary reflux: congenital deficiency of the longitudinal muscle of the intravesical ureter, so the flap-valve fails. Normal tunnel length to ureteric diameter ratio is 5:1 (Paquin).
  • Embryology (Mackie and Stevens): a bud arising lateral (cranial) to normal gives a refluxing, laterally placed orifice; a caudal bud tends to obstruction.
  • Secondary causes: posterior urethral valves (commonest anatomic obstruction in boys; reflux in 48%–70%, often resolving after valve ablation), prolapsing ureterocele (commonest in girls), neuropathic bladder. Treat the primary cause first and look for occult dysraphism.
  • Non-neurogenic neurogenic bladder (Hinman): voluntary sphincter contraction during voiding (learned dyssynergia), incomplete emptying, rising residual and eventual myogenic failure.
  • Poor compliance, detrusor decompensation and incomplete emptying damage the ureterovesical junction (UVJ); subureteric bulking injection does not.
  • Intravesical pressure over 40 cm H2O is strongly linked to reflux and upper tract damage in neuropathic bladders (damage can also occur at lower pressures).
  • Cystitis can cause reflux by reducing compliance, raising pressure and weakening the UVJ.
  • Bladder and bowel dysfunction (BBD) and constipation/encopresis are commonly associated with reflux and UTI. Signs: dribbling, urgency, incontinence, Vincent curtsy in girls, penile squeezing in boys. Treat it medically first (lower detrusor overactivity and pressure); urethral dilatation has no role.
  • BBD does not lower the success of Deflux (dextranomer/hyaluronic acid) injection or open surgery, but it increases UTIs after surgery.

Grading and imaging

  • Grading uses the International Reflux Study classification (1981), a merger of the Heikel-Parkkulainen and Dwoskin-Perlmutter systems, based on VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) appearance. Grading is impossible with coexisting ipsilateral obstruction.
  • Fever suggests upper tract involvement and raises the yield of VCUG, but it is unreliable; it can occur with under 100,000 colonies and pyelonephritis does not always scar.
  • VCUG plus ultrasound is reasonable for recurrent culture-proven UTIs with a DMSA scar; question the diagnosis first if the sample was a bag specimen or another fever source exists. Do not do immediate VCUG for UTI in children over 5.
  • Older children with asymptomatic bacteriuria or afebrile cystitis need no screening imaging; focus on voiding, bowel habits and fluids; cystography only for abnormal upper tracts or recalcitrant infection.
  • After a first febrile UTI with a properly collected urine: renal and bladder ultrasound; VCUG only if ultrasound is abnormal or a second infection occurs.
  • Cystography pitfalls: overhydration can mask low-grade reflux; some reflux shows only during infection; during infection endotoxins paralyse ureteric muscle and overstate the grade.
  • Nuclear cystography is accurate for detecting and following reflux but lacks anatomic detail; modern digital VCUG has narrowed the radiation difference. Ultrasound cannot detect reflux. Cystoscopy is not routine. Uroflowmetry is valuable since reflux is dynamic.
  • Ultrasound is the first study of the upper tracts, but kidney appearance does not correlate with presence or grade of reflux.
  • DMSA (Tc-99m dimercaptosuccinic acid) is best for acute pyelonephritis and scarring. Photopenic areas may be scars or congenital dysplasia; neither DMSA nor ultrasound can tell them apart.
  • Urodynamics is indicated when a secondary cause is suspected (valves, neuropathic or non-neurogenic neurogenic bladder, voiding dysfunction); sedation can alter results.

Natural history and consequences

  • Spontaneous resolution: almost 80% of low-grade and about half of grade III reflux; paraureteral diverticulum reflux resolves at a similar rate.
  • Sterile reflux with normal bladder function is benign and unlikely to matter clinically.
  • Scarring risk after infection is greatest in the first year of life.
  • High-grade reflux is associated with renal maldevelopment; cardinal associated anomalies are multicystic dysplastic kidney and renal agenesis.
  • Renal growth is affected by associated congenital dysmorphism (30%), number and type of infections, the contralateral kidney and reflux grade.
  • Reflux nephropathy is the commonest cause of severe hypertension in children and young adults.

Pregnancy

  • In pregnancy bladder tone falls from oedema and hyperaemia (predisposing to bacteriuria) and upper tract volume rises with physiological dilatation. Reflux, past scarring, UTI tendency, hypertension and renal impairment all increase risk. Women reimplanted in childhood still need monitoring for UTI in pregnancy.

Antibiotic prophylaxis and RIVUR

  • RIVUR (Randomized Intervention for Children with Vesicoureteral Reflux, NEJM 2014): adverse drug reactions were about 2% in both prophylaxis and placebo groups (not 0.5% vs 2%). Prophylaxis helped most in grade III–IV reflux, febrile index infection and baseline BBD.
  • After stopping CAP (continuous antibiotic prophylaxis) in toilet-trained children, recurrence is likelier with higher-grade reflux and BBD; uncircumcised boys over 1 year are not at higher risk.

Surgery

  • All reimplants create a valvular mechanism: a submucosal tunnel with good muscle backing, tunnel length 5 times ureteric diameter.
  • Cohen cross-trigonal reimplant suits small and thick-walled bladders.
  • About 10% of antireflux operations involve duplication; reimplant both ureters with one mucosal cuff (common sheath) because they share blood supply. Weigert-Meyer rule: the upper pole ureter enters distal and medial, the lower pole ureter proximal and lateral.
  • If both UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) and UVJ need repair, fix the UPJ first.
  • Contralateral reflux appears in 10%–15% after unilateral repair, but prophylactic bilateral reimplant is not indicated.
  • Early postoperative obstruction (oedema, subtrigonal bleeding, spasm, mucus, clots) is usually mild and resolves; symptomatic cases present 1–2 weeks after surgery with pain, nausea and vomiting; observe, and stent or nephrostomy only for unabating symptoms.
  • Recurrent reflux after correction, apart from technical error, usually means a missed secondary cause: neuropathic bladder or severe voiding dysfunction.
  • Endoscopic injection: less invasive but less durable than open repair; same indications; needle entry point and placement matter. Volume injected, surgeon experience, volcano mound without hydrodistention, double HIT technique and negative intraoperative cystogram have not consistently predicted success.
  • Laparoscopic reimplant: smaller incisions, less pain, shorter stay and convalescence; but learning curve, two surgeons, extraperitoneal repair becomes intraperitoneal, longer operating time and higher cost.

Ch 30 Bladder anomalies in children

Embryology and prenatal findings

  • The urorectal septum divides the cloaca at 4–6 weeks; the allantois and ventral cloaca involute by 4–5 months; the urachus becomes the median umbilical ligament. Definitive nephrons make urine before 12 weeks, and lower tract anomalies may show from about 10 weeks.
  • The fetal bladder empties every 15–20 minutes, so watch long enough before calling it non-visualised.
  • Megacystis: first trimester, bladder over 6 mm (normal 6 mm or less); later, a bladder that does not empty in 45 minutes. It has a wide differential, not only obstruction.
  • Most megacystis has no genetic diagnosis; when found, trisomy 13 and 18 are commonest, above rates expected for maternal age and nuchal translucency. Aneuploidy predicts poor outcome.
  • Persistent megacystis with oligohydramnios suggests LUTO (lower urinary tract obstruction); megacystis that resolves before birth may have no sequelae.
  • MMIH (megacystis-microcolon-intestinal hypoperistalsis syndrome): rare, usually detected by the bladder findings, may show only prenatal megacystis; thought due to actin smooth-muscle dysfunction; morbidity mainly enteric; survival beyond the neonatal period now documented.
  • Non-dilated anomalies (for example bladder exstrophy) are the most severe malformations, usually with normal amniotic fluid, and most are diagnosed after birth.

Agenesis, duplication and diverticula

  • Bladder agenesis: about 1 in 600,000, commoner in females, compatible with life only if the ureters drain ectopically.
  • Bladder duplication: most have genital anomalies, over 25% gastrointestinal anomalies; urethra may or may not be duplicated; continence hard to predict.
  • Congenital diverticula (incidence 1.7%): mucosa herniating through a hiatus in the wall; diagnosed by cystogram, usually after symptoms; most need no resection.
  • Connective tissue disorders: multiple diverticula with smooth muscle and mucosa in the wall; treat if symptomatic (stasis, infection, stones); recurrence expected.
  • Acquired diverticula can grow large enough to obstruct or cause stasis and recurrent UTI.

Urachal anomalies

  • Usually found after birth from umbilical drainage; a patent omphalomesenteric duct can mimic a patent urachus.
  • Most patent urachus is not due to outlet obstruction. Treat infected remnants with antibiotics, then excise; open or minimally invasive surgery both work.
  • Adenocarcinoma arises in adults (rare in children); discuss excising incidental remnants because of adult malignant potential.

Bladder masses and cystitis

  • Haemangiomas: usually single, linked to Klippel-Trenaunay and Sturge-Weber; simple resection cures. Fibroepithelial polyps: rare, endoscopic resection.
  • Nephrogenic adenoma: benign reaction to infection, stones or injury; commoner in adults; recurs in up to 80% (latency about 4 years) after cystoscopic resection, so do surveillance cystoscopy.
  • Eosinophilic cystitis: commoner in boys, often self-limiting in young children, no typical appearance (biopsy needed: eosinophils in all layers); treat with steroids, antihistamines and antibiotics.
  • Cystitis cystica: commoner in girls, can mimic tumour, benign; long-term antibiotic prophylaxis.
  • Cyclophosphamide haemorrhagic cystitis: give mesna simultaneously; oral pentosan polysulfate shows promise.

Ch 65 Integrated men's health

Life expectancy and risk

  • Men live shorter and sicker lives than women everywhere; the gap persists despite rising life expectancy and is widest in Eastern Europe (about 7 years).
  • Causes are social, environmental and physiological: workplace hazards, risk-taking and masculine health norms. Coordinated programmes targeting men are needed.

Metabolic syndrome

  • Definitions (WHO, AACE American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists, EGIR European Group for the Study of Insulin Resistance, ATP III Adult Treatment Panel III, IDF International Diabetes Federation) share hypertension, abdominal obesity, dyslipidaemia and insulin resistance or hyperglycaemia. LUTS are linked epidemiologically but are not part of the definition. Prevalence is rising worldwide.
  • Insulin resistance causes hyperinsulinaemia, which drives sympathetic overactivity and hypertension.
  • Linked urological conditions: renal insufficiency, stones, kidney, bladder and prostate cancer, overactive bladder, LUTS, low testosterone and ED (erectile dysfunction), besides cardiovascular disease, diabetes and stroke. Priapism is not linked.
  • Exercise raises insulin-mediated glucose uptake and lowers inflammation and IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1); fitness affects incidence and outcome of urological disease. Trials are testing statins and metformin.
  • Exercise improves quality of life in advanced prostate cancer; diet, weight loss and exercise can improve erectile function. Men's health needs individualised, precision programmes.

Testosterone therapy

  • Prescriptions rose sharply (UK and USA), although benefit and safety for age-related decline were not established.
  • Large randomised trials (TTrials) showed modest gains in sexual and physical function and vitality.
  • The book says limited evidence suggests more cardiovascular events. Update: TRAVERSE (NEJM 2023) found testosterone non-inferior to placebo for major cardiac events in hypogonadal men at high cardiovascular risk, but with more atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury and pulmonary embolism.
  • Endocrine Society 2018: diagnose deficiency only in symptomatic men with unequivocally low testosterone; do not treat men planning fertility soon; discuss cardiovascular risks; keep up with guidelines.
  • Contraindications: breast or prostate cancer, near-term fertility plans, high prostate cancer risk, raised haematocrit, untreated severe sleep apnoea, severe LUTS, uncontrolled heart failure, MI (myocardial infarction) or stroke within 6 months, thrombophilia.

Erectile dysfunction and cardiovascular disease

  • Shared risk factors (dyslipidaemia, smoking, hypertension, type 2 diabetes) and mechanisms (inflammation, atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction).
  • ED and CVD (cardiovascular disease) are independent risk factors for each other, but their timing relationship is not settled. ED precedes cardiac symptoms by about 2–3 years; about 50% of sudden cardiac events have no prior symptoms, so ED may be the only warning. MESA (Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis) showed subclinical CVD predicts ED.
  • CAC (coronary artery calcium) score is a surrogate for accelerated atherosclerosis, including penile arteries.

Mental health and opioids

  • Anxiety, depression and suicide are rising and underdiagnosed in men; over 6 million US men have depression (fatigue, irritability, loss of interest). Men's suicide rate is about 4 times women's; peak age 45–54.
  • Opioid crisis: 33,000 overdose deaths in 2015 (15,000 prescription), over 115 deaths a day, across all groups. Prescribe the lowest effective dose and reassess. Chronic opioids cause symptomatic hypogonadism.
  • A men's health centre should focus on conditions with higher impact on men, in partnership with cardiology, endocrinology, psychiatry, orthopaedics and dermatology.

Ch 96 Benign renal tumours

Renal cysts

  • Renal cysts are the commonest benign renal lesion; most need no follow-up. Acquired cystic kidney disease carries a significant malignancy risk and needs surveillance.
  • Bosniak classification estimates malignancy risk and directs therapy; enhancement of solid components separates Bosniak III/IV from I–IIF (septa, thin calcification and similar features occur in II/IIF). Update: Bosniak 2019 revision adds MRI and defines wall/septal thickness and enhancement more precisely.
  • ADPKD (autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease) diagnosis in at-risk families (Ravine): 2 cysts under age 30; 2 cysts in each kidney at 30–59; 4 in each kidney at 60 or over. Update: unified Pei criteria (2009): 3 or more cysts at 15–39, 2 or more in each kidney at 40–59, 4 or more in each kidney at 60 or over.

Oncocytoma

  • Commonest benign enhancing renal mass; histologically similar to chromophobe RCC (renal cell carcinoma). ISUP (International Society of Urological Pathology) recommends CK7: rarely positive in oncocytoma, diffusely positive in chromophobe.
  • Associated with Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome (folliculin gene, chromosome 17): lung cysts, spontaneous pneumothorax, fibrofolliculomas.
  • Core biopsy with immunostaining can give the diagnosis; active surveillance is reasonable and uses growth kinetics.
  • Chromosome clues: 4 and 16 in ADPKD, 7 in papillary adenoma/papillary RCC/metanephric adenoma, 9 (hamartin) and 16 (tuberin) in tuberous sclerosis.

Angiomyolipoma

  • AML (angiomyolipoma): benign tumour of dysmorphic vessels, smooth muscle and fat; macroscopic fat on imaging is diagnostic. In- and opposed-phase MRI shows a hypointense (India ink) border, not a hyperintense one. HMB-45 and Melan-A positive.
  • Mostly asymptomatic, but AML is the commonest cause of spontaneous retroperitoneal haemorrhage (Wunderlich syndrome); pregnancy hormones can make it grow.
  • Tuberous sclerosis (TSC): multifocal bilateral AMLs from hamartin (TSC1) or tuberin mutations that activate mTOR.
  • Treatment depends on size, symptoms and pregnancy, aiming to preserve renal function; selective angioembolisation for acute bleeding.
  • Everolimus (EXIST-2) for large multifocal AMLs in TSC and lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM): 42% had 50% or more shrinkage, 80% had 30% or more, 100% stable or responding; at 4 years 92% responded and 14% progressed.

Other benign tumours

  • Papillary adenoma: 5 mm or less, so not seen on imaging; shares staining and genetics (chromosome 7) with papillary RCC.
  • Metanephric adenoma: rare, benign, enhancing; symptoms in about 50%, female predominance; polycythaemia (erythropoietin, IL-6, IL-8, G-CSF, GM-CSF); WT1 and AMACR staining; on a continuum with Wilms tumour and papillary RCC.
  • Cystic nephroma and MEST (mixed epithelial and stromal tumour) are related; oestrogen and progesterone receptor positive, female predominance. Absence of blastemal elements separates cystic nephroma from Wilms tumour. Partial nephrectomy is preferred.
  • Lymphangioma: cystic mass compressing the kidney from outside (abnormal perirenal lymphatics).
  • Leiomyoma: anywhere in the urinary tract, mostly the renal capsule; imaging cannot confirm it; nephron sparing when possible.
  • Many rare benign tumours exist, and imaging cannot reliably separate them from cancer.
  • Reninoma (juxtaglomerular cell tumour): renin excess causes secondary hyperaldosteronism, hypertension and hypokalaemia; SIADH is not a feature.
  • Smoking points to a malignant rather than benign renal mass.

Ch 107 Surgery of the adrenal glands

Anatomy

  • Right adrenal: upper pole and medial border of the right kidney, more cephalad than the left, close to the diaphragm behind; arteries from the inferior phrenic (superior), aorta (middle) and renal artery (inferior). The medulla is innervated by preganglionic sympathetic fibres.
  • The right adrenal vein enters the IVC (inferior vena cava) posterolaterally; if torn, rotate the cava to suture it.
  • The pancreatic tail can lie against the left adrenal and upper pole of the left kidney.
  • Avoid ligating an upper-pole renal artery branch with large masses: it causes segmental infarction, not bleeding.

Indications and preparation

  • Malignancy risk by size: under 4 cm 2%, 4–6 cm 6% (consider resection), over 6 cm 25% (resect).
  • Commonest primaries metastasising to the adrenal: lung, breast, kidney, melanoma. Adrenocortical carcinoma recurs locoregionally in 60%.
  • Phaeochromocytoma: α-blockade for at least 2 weeks restores volume and controls blood pressure and glucose. Phenoxybenzamine is non-selective and irreversible, causing tachycardia, postoperative hypotension and somnolence. Start β-blockade only after full α-blockade. Ligate the adrenal vein early; give fluids after removal; use short-acting nitroprusside, phentolamine, nitroglycerine or nicardipine for intraoperative hypertension. Update: PRESCRIPT (2020) found doxazosin as effective as phenoxybenzamine.
  • Aldosteronoma: consider preoperative spironolactone and correct hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia.
  • Addisonian crisis is commonest after removing a cortisol-secreting tumour (the other gland is suppressed); after bilateral adrenalectomy start steroids as soon as both glands are out. Crisis features: pain, vomiting, diarrhoea, hypotension, fever, hypoglycaemia, hyperkalaemia.

Approaches

  • Laparoscopy: large size is no longer an absolute contraindication (more conversion), but invasive ACC with IVC thrombus is.
  • Posterior (prone) open or retroperitoneoscopic: direct, no muscle division, both glands without repositioning; but limited exposure, poor vein and vessel access and harder ventilation; ideal for bilateral hyperplasia and small benign tumours, not for large tumours or ACC.
  • Retroperitoneal approach avoids bowel injury, pneumoperitoneum effects and adhesions; drawbacks are small working space, few landmarks and much fat. The lateral approach converts easily to transperitoneal.
  • Thoracoabdominal approach for large invasive tumours involving the retrohepatic IVC, especially on the right; incise the diaphragm circumferentially, not radially (phrenic nerve injury).
  • Several approaches are acceptable for most lesions.
  • Partial adrenalectomy: expose without mobilising; arterial plexus can be divided; main vein can be ligated if the remnant stays in situ (preserve it when safe); useful in MEN 2A. A local recurrence after partial adrenalectomy is an absolute contraindication to repeat partial adrenalectomy.

Complications

  • Repair any diaphragmatic injury (herniation risk). Superior mesenteric vein ligation can be fatal: revascularise and resect bowel if needed. After splenectomy give pneumococcal, Hib and meningococcal vaccines once recovered, usually before discharge, not intraoperatively.

Ch 129 Urinary tract fistulae

Vesicovaginal fistula: causes and diagnosis

  • VVF (vesicovaginal fistula) is the commonest urinary fistula. Industrialised world: hysterectomy (also cancer, prolapse, incontinence surgery, radiotherapy). Developing world, especially sub-Saharan Africa: obstructed labour in young primigravidas with narrow pelvis, causing large distal ischaemic fistulas involving trigone and bladder neck, often with rectovaginal fistula (obstructed labour injury complex), hard to repair.
  • Bladder laceration is the commonest injury at hysterectomy; ureterovaginal fistula is less common than VVF. No blue dye in the pelvis does not exclude injury; recognising and repairing bladder injury lowers but does not remove the risk.
  • Post-hysterectomy VVF usually lies on the anterior vaginal wall at the cuff. About 10%–12% have an associated ureteric injury.
  • Radiation VVF may appear years later. Work-up: VCUG, cystoscopy and biopsy (exclude recurrence), CT, urodynamics for compliance; ureteroscopy is not needed. Always exclude recurrent malignancy after radiotherapy.
  • Clear vaginal fluid is not always urine: peritoneovaginal, lymphatic fistula, vaginitis, tubal fluid. A fluid creatinine similar to serum excludes urine; wet but unstained pads on phenazopyridine suggest peritoneovaginal fistula.
  • Three-swab/tampon test: orange (phenazopyridine) staining proximally means ureterovaginal; blue distally means stress leakage; VVF tends to give green staining in the middle.

Vesicovaginal fistula: repair

  • Radiation VVF: wait 5–6 months for demarcation; vaginal repair with flap is possible; success is lower than for non-radiation fistulas.
  • Choose approach by anatomy, circumstances and surgeon; success similar. Vaginal: shorter stay, less morbidity, but possible vaginal shortening (Latzko). Abdominal: allows omental interposition; it does not reduce ureteric injury.
  • Vaginal repair: 3–4 layer absorbable closure; Martius or peritoneal flaps preferred over gracilis; not contraindicated by obstetric fistula, a nearby ureter, or a high narrow cuff in nulliparous women.
  • Excising the fistula tract is not always required. No evidence supports routine oestrogen, IV antibiotics or a particular drainage method, but unobstructed drainage is critical.
  • Principles: multiple layers, no overlapping suture lines; abdominal repair needs bladder mobilisation caudal to the fistula; the book says cholinergic agents are used liberally after repair. Update: it is anticholinergics that are given, to prevent bladder spasms. Tissue interposition for failed repairs, very large and radiation fistulas; gracilis, rectus abdominis and Martius flaps are excellent.
  • Martius flap supplied by internal and external pudendal and obturator arteries; obturator branches are sacrificed and either end can be divided. Peritoneal flap is advanced without opening the peritoneum. Omentum (right and left gastroepiploic arteries) often reaches the pelvis without mobilisation.
  • Postoperative cystogram at 2–3 weeks must include voiding or post-void images.
  • New SUI after repair occurs mainly with bladder neck or proximal urethral (obstetric) fistulas. Long-term: vaginal shortening and stenosis. Non-healing: poor nutrition, fungal infection, malignancy, TB, distal obstruction or foreign body.

Ureterovaginal, vesicouterine and urethrovaginal fistula

  • Ureterovaginal fistula: distal third of the ureter, mostly after benign hysterectomy of any route; voiding is normal because the other kidney fills the bladder; VCUG excludes coexisting VVF. Decompress the upper tract after ureteric injury.
  • Vesicouterine fistula: mostly after low-segment caesarean section (rarely uterine rupture); urine leaks through the cervix but incontinence is not always present; VCUG confirms. Fertility-preserving options exist; hysterectomy if fertility is not wanted. It is unlikely after vaginal surgery.
  • Distal urethrovaginal fistula is often asymptomatic (beyond the sphincter); vaginal flap repair; soft tissue flaps are important.

Enteric and vascular fistulas

  • Colovesical fistula: diverticulitis, then colon cancer, then Crohn disease. CT triad: air in bladder, bladder wall thickening next to thickened bowel, colonic diverticula. Cystoscopy abnormal in 80%–100% (bullous oedema is suggestive); biopsy to exclude cancer. Air in the bladder also follows instrumentation or gas-forming infection.
  • Ureterocolic fistula: mostly right-sided, in Crohn disease.
  • Rectourethral fistula: rectal injury in 1%–1.5% of radical prostatectomies (similar laparoscopically), rarely becoming a fistula if repaired; presents with faecaluria; biopsy if cancer was treated. Small uninfected fistula: trial of catheter; then single-stage repair (York-Mason has low anal incontinence) without colostomy.
  • Pyelovascular fistula: mostly after percutaneous procedures; tamponade, then embolisation.
  • Ureteroarterial fistula (long-term stent, prior pelvic surgery or radiation): angiography diagnoses and treats (embolisation or endovascular stent graft, usually iliac); do not pull the stent.

Ch 147 Simple prostatectomy

  • Versus TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate): more complete removal under direct vision, lower retreatment, no TUR syndrome.
  • Only absolute indication: renal damage from prostatic obstruction. Relative: retention, recurrent UTI, refractory symptoms, prostatic haematuria, bladder stones.
  • Do not operate on glands under 75 g (high complications). Consider it when lithotomy is impossible. Contraindications: small fibrous gland, significant cancer (for example multiple Gleason 8 cores), prior prostatectomy or pelvic surgery. Update: AUA and EAU now offer HoLEP and other size-independent endoscopic options for large glands as alternatives.
  • Estimate size preoperatively (cystoscopy may help); cystoscopy is indicated for haematuria, stricture, known stone or diverticulum.
  • Suprapubic (transvesical) approach: sees bladder neck and mucosa; best for a large median lobe, a diverticulum needing repair (do diverticulectomy at the same time to avoid stasis and infection) and large stones; good in obese men. The dorsal vein complex need not be controlled. Both suprapubic and retropubic approaches work in the space of Retzius.
  • Risks: incontinence, ED, retrograde ejaculation, UTI, bladder neck contracture, urethral stricture, transfusion.
  • Robot-assisted simple prostatectomy: smaller incisions, shorter stay, less bleeding and transfusion, less continuous irrigation.

Ch 31 Exstrophy-Epispadias Complex

Epidemiology and genetics

  • Classic bladder exstrophy: 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 50,000 live births; male:female 2.3:1.
  • Family risk is raised: offspring of an affected parent have a 1 in 70 risk, about 500 times the general population (Shapiro).
  • Cloacal exstrophy: 1 in 200,000 to 400,000 births. Male epispadias: 1 in 117,000 males.

Embryology

  • Basic defect (Marshall and Muecke): abnormal overdevelopment of the cloacal membrane, which blocks medial migration of mesenchyme and proper lower abdominal wall formation.
  • Superior vesical fissure variant: same muscle and bone defects as classic exstrophy, but the cloacal membrane ruptures only at its top, leaving a small vesicostomy-like fistula above a normal umbilicus; bladder extrusion is minimal.

Anatomy of the defect

  • Pelvis (Sponseller): posterior pelvis externally rotated 12° each side, acetabular retroversion, anterior pelvis externally rotated 18°, pubic rami 30% shorter.
  • Inguinal hernia in about 82% of boys and 10% of girls; reflux in 100%.
  • Penis: anterior corporal length almost 50% shorter than in controls (MRI). Prostate volume, weight and cross-sectional area are normal.
  • Vagina: short (barely over 6 cm) but of normal calibre.
  • Bladder: muscarinic receptor density and affinity are normal, so the bladder's neural make-up is not grossly altered. 80% of bladders are compliant and stable before bladder neck reconstruction.
  • Autonomic nerves are displaced laterally. An isolated ectopic bowel segment may lie in the lower abdominal wall.
  • Rectal prolapse is common in untreated exstrophy with wide symphyseal diastasis and disappears after closure; prolapse after closure suggests bladder outlet obstruction.

Prenatal and newborn assessment

  • Prenatal ultrasound signs: no bladder filling, low-set umbilicus, widened pubic rami, small genitalia, and a lower abdominal mass that grows with gestation.
  • At birth: tie the cord with silk (no clamp) and cover the bladder with non-adherent film to prevent trauma and drying.
  • A small bladder may still be adequate if it bulges when the baby cries or indents easily under anaesthesia. If it is too small, wait 4 to 6 months for growth rather than risk a closure that dehisces.

Staged reconstruction (modern staged repair)

  • Key changes: early closure of bladder, posterior urethra and abdominal wall (usually with osteotomy); early epispadias repair; later continent bladder neck reconstruction with ureteric reimplantation; strict selection criteria. Bladder neck repair comes after, not before, epispadias repair.
  • Osteotomy is rarely needed in newborns unless diastasis exceeds 4 cm. Combined anterior osteotomy: done supine with the urological repair (no turning), allows external fixator and pins under vision, and gives a better cosmetic result than the posterior iliac approach.
  • Immobilisation with external fixator plus modified Buck traction gave 97% successful closure.
  • Closure reshapes the pelvis, redistributes the levators and smooths the pelvic floor. Control bladder spasms after closure.
  • After closure: calibrate the outlet before removing the suprapubic tube at 4 weeks, image the kidneys, and give antibiotic prophylaxis (all have reflux); check residuals and cultures.
  • Recurrent UTI or a distended bladder after closure: cystoscopy to look for erosion of the intrapubic stitch into the posterior urethra or bladder.
  • Epispadias repair goals: correct dorsal (not ventral) chordee, reconstruct the urethra and glans, and close penile skin.
  • Bladder neck reconstruction at 4 to 5 years, when capacity is adequate and, above all, the child can join a voiding programme. Most become dry by day within 1 year.
  • Erlangen approach: all reconstruction in one stage at 8 to 10 weeks of age.

Continence and failure

  • Best predictors of continence: bladder template size at birth, successful primary closure, and bladder capacity at bladder neck reconstruction (also true in epispadias).
  • About 75% are continent after repair (continence = 3 hours dry). A successful first closure gives earlier continence and higher rates.
  • Dehiscence (from poor pelvic floor mobilisation, poor immobilisation, infection, distension or tube problems) needs 6 months before re-closure. After two closures only 60% reach adequate capacity for bladder neck reconstruction.
  • Most failed bladder necks need augmentation or continent diversion: bladder neck transection, augmentation and a continent (Mitrofanoff) stoma; an artificial sphincter suits only those with good capacity.
  • Ureterosigmoidostomy needs normal anal continence; risks are pyelonephritis, hyperchloraemic acidosis, rectal incontinence, ureteric obstruction and late malignancy.

Complete primary repair (Mitchell)

  • Principle: return bladder neck and urethra to a normal posterior pelvic position for pelvic floor support. Dissection limit is the pelvic floor, which is split. Reflux is usually not corrected at the same time.
  • Penile disassembly works because each corporal body and the urethral wedge have independent proximal blood supply, which must be preserved.
  • No prolonged nil by mouth; decompress any ileus because distension strains the repair.
  • Commonest complication: ventral urethrocutaneous fistula; others are corporal devascularisation, hydronephrosis and hypospadias. Dehiscence may cause loss of glans, corpora and urethral plate.

Cloacal exstrophy

  • Features: bladder exstrophy, complete phallic separation, wide diastasis, exstrophied terminal ileum between the bladder halves, rudimentary hindgut, imperforate anus, omphalocele (88% to 95% or more) and often spinal defects (rare in classic exstrophy).
  • Spinal defects are thought to come from disrupted dorsal mesenchyme, not failed neural tube closure. Neurological deficit causes most long-term disability.
  • Müllerian anomalies in up to 95%, mostly partial uterine duplication (bicornuate).
  • Use the hindgut remnant to add bowel length for fluid absorption.
  • Single-stage repair is risky with a large omphalocele, wide diastasis, myelomeningocele or small bladder plate.
  • Gender: assign according to karyotype where possible; neonatal testis histology is normal and a functional phallus can now be built.

Epispadias

  • Reflux in 30% to 40% (deficient ureterovesical junction); reflux and hernia are less common than in exstrophy.

Long-term outcomes

  • Sexual function and libido are normal in both sexes. Men worry about penile length, appearance and deviation; women about genital appearance, vaginal opening and uterine prolapse.
  • Women can deliver children; cervical and uterine prolapse after pregnancy is common.
  • Children with exstrophy do not have clinical psychopathology.

Ch 72 Surgery for Erectile Dysfunction

Counselling and choice

  • Consent points: erect penis will likely be shorter and thinner (the main cause of disappointment); the implant only gives rigidity for intercourse; sensation and ejaculation may decrease; pain varies; reoperation may be needed for mechanical failure or infection.
  • Alternatives: oral drugs, intraurethral inserts, injections and vacuum devices. Types: three-piece and two-piece inflatables and semirigid rods.
  • Three-piece inflatables give the best rigidity and flaccidity and are most popular. Poor manual dexterity: choose a semirigid rod.
  • Outcomes: satisfaction 80% to 90% (patients and partners); revision about 15% at 5 years; infection 1% to 3%; rod erosion 20% to 50% in spinal cord injury. Coloplast has the lock-out valve on the reservoir, Boston Scientific on the pump.

Technique

  • Penoscrotal approach: subdartos pump, cosmetic incision, spares the dorsal nerves, allows two-piece devices; semirigid rods are easier through a subcoronal incision.
  • Keep scissors and dilators pointing dorsolaterally and parallel to the shaft to avoid crural crossover and urethral injury. Crossover causes difficult insertion, asymmetry around a catheter, a lopsided erection and unequal lengths, but the erection is usually still straight.
  • Urethral perforation during dilation: do not place both cylinders. Options are abandoning, placing only the contralateral cylinder and returning later, or creating a hypospadias, repairing securely and placing both. Close any corporal-to-skin defect securely before leaving a cylinder.
  • After a T-shunt for priapism avoid AMS 700 LGX, the only cylinder that expands in length, as it may push through the distal defect.
  • Peyronie disease: a non-length-expanding cylinder usually straightens the curve; LGX tends to exaggerate curvature.
  • Reservoir in a capacious space to avoid bladder or bowel injury. Not in a scrotal cavity after orchidectomy: the lockout valve can be squeezed and cause autoinflation.
  • Poor penile perfusion: distal necrosis risk, so limit or stage aggressive reconstruction.

Infection

  • Remove all foreign material in an established infection. Salvage is less likely with cellulitis, pus, onset within 2 months, or aggressive organisms (MRSA, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus; Pseudomonas); diabetes raises infection risk but not salvage failure.

Ch 75 Anatomy of the Retroperitoneum

Boundaries and contents

  • Contents: kidneys, ureters, adrenals, pancreas, 2nd and 3rd parts of duodenum, ascending and descending colon, mesentery, aorta, IVC (inferior vena cava), lymphatics, sympathetic trunk, lumbosacral plexus. Transverse colon is intraperitoneal.
  • Bounded by posterior peritoneum in front, posterior abdominal wall behind, diaphragm above and extraperitoneal pelvis below.

Body wall

  • Psoas major (anterior rami L1 to L3) and iliacus both flex the hip.
  • 11th and 12th ribs are floating; bony landmarks are reliable for incisions, the umbilicus is not. Intercostal nerves and vessels run between internal and innermost intercostals in the costal groove on the lower border of the upper rib. Lower rib fractures should raise suspicion of retroperitoneal injury.
  • Lumbodorsal fascia has three layers and merges anterolaterally with transversus abdominis. Transversalis fascia lies deep to transversus abdominis and superficial to preperitoneal fat.

Fasciae and spaces

  • Embryology: outer stratum forms transversalis fascia; intermediate stratum surrounds urinary organs; inner stratum the gut.
  • Perirenal space (between laminae of Gerota fascia) is cone-shaped and open caudally, so haematoma or fluid spreads downwards.
  • Gerota laminae fuse laterally as the lateroconal fascia, separating anterior and posterior pararenal spaces. Posterior pararenal space: posterior Gerota lamina in front, transversalis fascia behind and laterally.
  • White line of Toldt: lateral edge of colonic mesentery fusion with posterior peritoneum. The mesentery is continuous, with gut, immune, endocrine and vascular roles; small mesenteric defects carry more internal hernia risk and should be closed.

Vessels

  • Adrenal arteries: superior (inferior phrenic), middle (aorta), inferior (renal artery).
  • Renal hila at L1; renal veins lie anterior to arteries. Gonadal arteries arise anterolaterally from the aorta below the renal arteries.
  • SMA (superior mesenteric artery) ligation causes catastrophic bowel ischaemia; the IMA (inferior mesenteric artery) can be sacrificed thanks to the marginal artery of Drummond and haemorrhoidal collaterals.
  • Ureteric blood supply: medial for the proximal ureter (renal artery, aorta), lateral for the distal ureter (internal iliac, vesical arteries).
  • Right gonadal vein drains to the IVC; left gonadal (testicular or ovarian) vein enters the left renal vein at a right angle, hence left varicoceles. A new right-sided varicocele needs retroperitoneal imaging.
  • On right colon mobilisation find the ureter lateral to the gonadal vein; going medial risks IVC injury and gonadal avulsion.
  • Splenic vein tributaries: inferior mesenteric, short gastric, left gastroepiploic, pancreatic; right gastroepiploic drains to the superior mesenteric vein.

Lymphatics

  • Testes are embryologically retroperitoneal, with retroperitoneal blood supply and lymph drainage.
  • Right testis: interaortocaval mainly, some right paracaval and a little left para-aortic. Left testis: left para-aortic, some interaortocaval. Flow is right to left.

Nerves

  • Parasympathetic: craniosacral (cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X and S2 to S4); postganglionic fibres lie in the organ walls. Sympathetic preganglionic fibres leave T1 to L2 and synapse in the trunk or plexuses. Somatic supply via the lumbosacral plexus.
  • Genitofemoral nerve arises from L1 to L2 (not L3 to L4).
  • Superior hypogastric plexus (aortic bifurcation to front of L5) injury causes loss of emission or failed bladder neck closure with retrograde ejaculation.
  • Obturator nerve supplies the medial thigh adductors (gracilis, adductor longus, brevis, magnus, obturator externus): injury weakens hip adduction.

Ch 102 Laparoscopic and Robotic Surgery of the Kidney

General principles

  • Minimally invasive renal surgery gives less pain, shorter recovery and better cosmesis with equal efficacy; indications match open surgery; follow oncological principles (negative margins, maximal parenchymal preservation); know landmarks and pitfalls.
  • Obesity distorts anatomy and raises complication and conversion rates but is not a contraindication; obese patients benefit. Absolute contraindications: uncorrected coagulopathy, untreated infection, hypovolaemic shock. Large masses, elderly patients, renal vein thrombus and prior surgery are feasible.
  • Retroperitoneal approach suits posterior tumours, transperitoneal anterior ones. LESS (laparoendoscopic single-site surgery) can use standard instruments; articulating tools help but are not required for LESS or NOTES (natural orifice surgery).

Benign procedures

  • Cyst surgery for pain, obstruction, suspected malignancy or infection, not for ADPKD (autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease) alone. Multiple or bilateral cysts can be treated together (over 90% improve).
  • Laparoscopic renal biopsy is preferred after failed percutaneous biopsy, with anatomical anomalies, bleeding tendency, morbid obesity, multiple cysts or a solitary kidney.
  • Nephropexy: 90% to 100% pain relief, 80% to 95% resolution of the 5-cm descent, 90% better renal function on scan. Nephrolysis (hilar skeletonisation) treats refractory chyluria. Calyceal diverticulectomy rarely needs hilar clamping.

Radical and partial nephrectomy

  • Laparoscopic radical nephrectomy: better cosmesis, shorter stay, less analgesia and recovery; oncological outcomes equal to open, not better.
  • Hand-assisted: similar recovery, cost and survival to pure laparoscopy, but slightly more wound infection and hernia.
  • Safe: en bloc hilar stapling, cytoreductive laparoscopic nephrectomy. Mandatory: retrieval bag when morcellating. Avoid: clips alone on the renal artery (fatal clip failures), blunt hand dissection of the adrenal.
  • Partial nephrectomy: central, hilar, solitary-kidney and multiple tumours are feasible in experienced hands; redo surgery bleeds more; drain after collecting system repair; suture renorrhaphy is the most reliable haemostasis.
  • Hilar control: bulldogs or Satinsky; artery-only clamping is safe; parenchymal compression suits peripheral tumours; off-clamp for small exophytic tumours; early unclamping before renorrhaphy is acceptable (shorter warm ischaemia, similar blood loss).
  • Thermal ablation: less blood loss and fewer complications than partial nephrectomy; laparoscopic ablation allows tumour visualisation and organ retraction; keep the iceball off collecting system, ureter, vessels and bowel; RFA (radiofrequency ablation) heats tissue to 60°C to 100°C, monitored by temperature or impedance; avoid over 105°C (vaporisation).

Complications

  • Risk factors: obesity, comorbidity, prior surgery, larger and complex tumours; age alone is not one.
  • Urine leak (large, hilar tumours, collecting system repair, long ischaemia): most settle with percutaneous drainage alone; stent if that fails or there is distal obstruction.
  • Gallbladder injury: cholecystectomy. Unrecognised bowel injury is mostly thermal (about 50%) and presents late.
  • Gross haematuria after partial nephrectomy suggests pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula: go straight to angiography and selective embolisation.

Ch 120 Pharmacology of Storage and Emptying Failure

Muscarinic receptors

  • Bladder contraction is driven by acetylcholine on postganglionic parasympathetic muscarinic receptors. Detrusor and urothelium contain M2 and M3; M2 outnumber M3 at least 3:1, but M3 mediates contraction (via a rise in intracellular calcium from influx and stores).
  • Receptor function can change in non-neurogenic bladder disorders. Atropine resistance is mainly due to ATP (adenosine triphosphate), not noradrenaline (released by sympathetic hypogastric nerves).
  • Antimuscarinics raise volume at first involuntary contraction, lower its amplitude and raise capacity, without changing outlet resistance. At OAB (overactive bladder) doses they act in the storage phase, mainly by reducing C-fibre and Aδ afferent activity, micromotions and urothelial excitation, not by reducing the voiding contraction.
  • Side effects by subtype: M3 for dry mouth, constipation and blurred vision; M1 for cognition; M2 for heart rate (tachycardia when blocked); M4 and M5 none. QT prolongation (hERG channel) and reduced, not increased, sweating are not class effects.
  • Brain entry is favoured by high lipophilicity, small size and low charge. Tertiary amines enter the CNS; quaternary amines (trospium) are poorly absorbed and barely enter, so trospium is safest for cognition. Antimuscarinics may harm cognition in the elderly and may have cardiac effects. Update: AUA 2024 advises counselling on dementia risk with antimuscarinics.

Antimuscarinic drugs

  • Oxybutynin: antimuscarinic, direct relaxant and local anaesthetic; orally it acts mainly as an antimuscarinic (500 times weaker as a relaxant). Transdermal oxybutynin cuts the side-effect-causing metabolite; main problem is skin reaction (pruritus 14%, erythema 8.3%). Intravesical oxybutynin works in OAB and augmented bladders.
  • Darifenacin: relatively M3-selective. Trospium is renally excreted by proximal tubules; darifenacin, oxybutynin, solifenacin and tolterodine are metabolised by hepatic cytochrome P450.
  • ICI 2013 (Oxford grading): level 1, grade A for tolterodine, fesoterodine, darifenacin, solifenacin, propiverine (mixed action, not in USA), oxybutynin and trospium; flavoxate is level 2, grade D.
  • Always combine drugs with behavioural therapy. Calcium channel blockers and current potassium channel openers do not work for OAB.

β3 agonists, PDE5 inhibitors, botulinum toxin, vanilloids

  • Three β receptors in detrusor; β3 predominates and relaxes it. Mirabegron, the first β3 agonist, activates β3 and also inhibits filling-induced Aδ and C-fibre afferent activity. Update: vibegron is a second approved β3 agonist.
  • PDE5 (phosphodiesterase 5) inhibitors improve IIEF and IPSS scores but not peak flow or detrusor pressure; tadalafil is approved for LUTS from BPO.
  • Antimuscarinic plus α-blocker beats α-blocker alone in men with BPO and OAB without worsening emptying.
  • Botulinum toxin A cleaves SNAP-25, inactivating the SNARE complex; blocks acetylcholine release in striated and smooth muscle and reduces afferent firing; works in neurogenic and idiopathic DO and OAB without DO. Seven serotypes; A and B used. Intrasphincteric injection treats sphincter dyssynergia but causes, not treats, SUI.
  • Neurogenic DO failing or intolerant of antimuscarinics: intradetrusor botulinum toxin rather than more antimuscarinic. Repeat every 3 to 12 months; improves quality of life; efficacy does not fall with repeats; can cause retention; local anaesthesia suffices.
  • Vanilloids desensitise C fibres (perhaps Aδ too) after initial excitation; resiniferatoxin is 1000 times more potent than capsaicin for desensitisation, a few hundred times for excitation. DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide) 50% is for bladder pain syndrome, not DO.

Oestrogens, desmopressin, tricyclics

  • Oral oestrogen (± progestogen) worsens incontinence and causes new incontinence (HERS, WHI, Nurses' Health Study); no evidence supports oestrogen for incontinence. Vaginal oestrogen treats urogenital atrophy (dryness, pruritus, dyspareunia), better than oral.
  • Desmopressin: risk of hyponatraemia (confusion in the elderly); check sodium before and a few days after starting.
  • Imipramine side effects: antimuscarinic effects, weakness, fatigue, tremor, sedation, postural hypotension, sweating, delayed orgasm, arrhythmias; not priapism.

Outlet drugs

  • α-agonists cause hypertension, anxiety, insomnia, headache, tremor, palpitations and arrhythmias, not somnolence; caution in hypertension, heart disease or hyperthyroidism. Phenylpropanolamine raised haemorrhagic stroke risk in women under 50 and was withdrawn in the USA.
  • Propranolol might unmask α-tone and raise outlet resistance in a hypertensive patient with SUI (unproven).
  • Duloxetine (serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor) raises sphincter activity; it is hepatically metabolised, licensed in the EU for moderate to severe SUI (15 or more episodes a week) but not in the USA.
  • Lower tract has more α2 than α1 receptors, but contraction is mediated by α1 (α1A; α1D in detrusor). α-blockers: phenoxybenzamine and phentolamine are non-selective; prazosin was the first selective; terazosin, doxazosin, alfuzosin and tamsulosin are α1-selective. Tamsulosin gives more retrograde ejaculation and rhinitis; terazosin and doxazosin more dizziness and asthenia. α-blockers do not work in women and can worsen SUI.
  • Bethanechol is muscarinic-selective and cholinesterase-resistant, but no evidence shows it helps bladder emptying; oral 200 mg equals 5 mg subcutaneously.
  • Prostanoids potentiate acetylcholine release, not ATP release.
  • No drug selectively relaxes the pelvic floor striated muscle. GABA is the main CNS inhibitory transmitter. Benzodiazepines act on GABA-A; baclofen on GABA-B (spinal cord); dantrolene blocks calcium release in skeletal muscle.

Ch 142 Minimally Invasive Urinary Diversion

Background and selection

  • Robotic cystectomy gives less pain and blood loss and faster bowel recovery. Intracorporeal diversion is rising about 11% a year; ileal conduit is the commonest robotic diversion.
  • Selection has widened (high BMI, locally advanced disease, older age, prior surgery); Xi system and AirSeal allow less steep Trendelenburg; neobladder contraindications are as in open surgery.

Perioperative care

  • Avoid mechanical bowel preparation and oral antibiotics; IV broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour before incision.
  • VTE: haematological and vascular complications about 10%; mean diagnosis 15.2 days, 55% after discharge; give LMWH (low-molecular-weight heparin) for 4 weeks.
  • ERAS (enhanced recovery after surgery; over 20 elements: alvimopan, goal-directed fluids, early diet, non-opioid analgesia) reduces stay, complications and readmissions. Avoid overhydration in these older patients.

Technique

  • Six to seven ports placed cephalad; an extra robotic arm can go through a 15-mm port; a 0° lens throughout (30° down sometimes for a deep pelvis).
  • Marionette stitch is held, not tied, so the bowel can move. Leave enough ileum near the ileocaecal valve. Remove the guidewire only after the anchoring stitch (no tactile feedback).
  • Uretero-ileal anastomosis: cold-scissor button-hole enterotomy, no cautery (stricture), and retroperitonealise with a peritoneal flap.
  • Neobladder-urethral tension: reduce Trendelenburg or undock, do the anastomosis early, incise peritoneum over the mesentery, mobilise the ileocaecal area, move the sigmoid out of the pelvis, apply perineal pressure, use barbed sutures, detubularise, and use temporary traction (Penrose).
  • Nerve sparing only with safe tumour location, low burden and good function. Nerves are at risk at the common iliacs, presacral area and pelvic plexus near Denonvilliers fascia; spare the lower seminal vesicles. 3D reconstruction maps the pelvic nerves; the dorsal venous complex overlaps a third of the sphincter.

Outcomes

  • Operating time: no difference between intracorporeal and extracorporeal diversion (IRCC, 2134 patients); high-volume centres are faster.
  • 90-day complications 41% intracorporeal vs 49% extracorporeal (P = .05); gastrointestinal complications and sepsis dominate; sepsis was commonest with intracorporeal diversion.
  • Drain the pelvis and stent the ureters; irrigate a neobladder every 8 hours to prevent mucus blockage.

Ch 150 Prostate Biopsy

TRUS anatomy and ultrasound

  • Five divisions: anterior fibromuscular stroma, transition, central, periurethral and peripheral zones.
  • Corpora amylacea and calcifications lie along the surgical capsule (transition–peripheral zone junction); small diffuse calcifications are age-related; large symptomatic calculi suggest infection or inflammation.
  • Seminal vesicle: 4.5 to 5.5 cm long, 2 cm wide, symmetric. Cystic masses are benign; solid ones are occasionally malignant, and schistosomiasis is a cause in endemic regions.
  • Adult prostate is 20 to 25 g, stable until about age 50.
  • Higher probe frequency gives better resolution, lower frequency deeper penetration; remove any air interface.
  • Volume needs three dimensions (ellipsoid, sphere or prolate formula); planimetry is most accurate. Update: measure width and AP height on the mid-gland transverse view and length on the sagittal view; volume = length × width × height × 0.52.
  • Echogenicity: 39% of cancers are isoechoic, 1% hyperechoic; a hypoechoic lesion is cancer about 20% of the time and does not predict Gleason grade. Biopsy hypoechoic lesions; mimics are granulomatous prostatitis, infarct and lymphoma; haematological malignancies are often isoechoic.
  • MRI image case: a well-demarcated low-signal focus in the peripheral zone with intact capsule means organ-confined cancer.

Technique

  • No PSA level rules out cancer. Initial detection with PSA 4 to 10 ng/mL is 22% (book misprints μg/mL); repeat biopsies find 10%, 5% and 4%, with cancers of lower grade and stage.
  • Left lateral decubitus is commonest (lithotomy possible); self-administered enema usual. Best needle-path view is sagittal.
  • Analgesia: periprostatic block with 5 mL lidocaine each side at the seminal vesicle–base junction via a 22-gauge spinal needle through the biopsy channel.
  • At least 12 systematic cores (AUA); 18 to 20 cores add little. Isolated transition zone cancers are under 5%. After repeated negative biopsies target the anterior zone and apex (template or fusion).
  • Four seminal vesicle cores in a high-risk man means a misdirected biopsy: repeat it.
  • No rectum: transperineal US-guided or CT-guided biopsy. Transperineal sampling covers the apex better and may cause fewer infections. Update: EAU now prefers transperineal biopsy for its lower infection risk.
  • Brachytherapy: seeds evenly distributed with urethral sparing.

Imaging-targeted biopsy

  • MRI-TRUS fusion coregisters a prebiopsy multiparametric MRI with real-time ultrasound using software; the MRI must show targets. Other options: Doppler, elastography, endorectal MRI with dynamic contrast and diffusion, spectroscopy. Update: prebiopsy MRI with targeted biopsy is now standard (PRECISION 2018).

Complications and prophylaxis

  • Complications: febrile UTI, bacteraemia, prostatitis, bleeding, haematospermia (up to 4 to 6 weeks, harmless), retention and sepsis.
  • Infection risk factors: non-white race, comorbidities, diabetes, large prostate, foreign travel and recent antibiotics.
  • Prophylaxis reduces but does not eliminate febrile UTI. Book: fluoroquinolone 2 hours before and for 48 hours after; with endocarditis risk or prostheses, IV ampicillin (vancomycin if allergic) plus gentamicin, then fluoroquinolone. Update: fluoroquinolone restrictions (EMA 2019) and resistance favour targeted prophylaxis or the transperineal route.

Ch 32 Prune-Belly Syndrome

Antenatal picture

  • Antenatal ultrasound: dilated urinary tract, large full bladder, abnormal abdominal circumference; penis normal for age but scrotum empty (abdominal testes). Prune-belly syndrome (PBS) is not associated with ambiguous genitalia.
  • Bilateral hydronephrosis with a distended bladder is usually well tolerated and is not proof of obstruction; urinary ascites or pulmonary hypoplasia alone do not justify fetal drainage. Only progressive oligohydramnios (suggesting an atretic urethra with functioning kidneys) justifies fetal bladder decompression.

Urinary tract

  • Kidneys: variable dysplasia (one or both), not proportional to abdominal flaccidity, dilatation or reflux; bilateral dysplasia compromises survival.
  • Hydroureteronephrosis is often severe but calyces may be well preserved; it rarely affects initial renal function.
  • Dilatation is not proportional to abdominal wall laxity; the lower ureter is more dilated than the upper, with or without reflux; the proximal ureter has more normal muscle. Collagen-thickened pelvis or ureteral folds can mimic UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) stenosis.
  • Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) in two-thirds. Urachal diverticulum (pseudodiverticulum) is common; rarely a patent urachus drains the bladder when the urethra is atretic.
  • Bladder: large, thick-walled (more collagen, fewer smooth muscle cells), high capacity and compliance, poor sensation and contraction, low voiding pressure; emptying relies on abdominal straining, so large residuals are common.
  • Bladder neck is wide open (not elevated or hypertrophied); posterior urethra dilated from lack of prostatic support even with a normal distal urethra. Absent bladder neck hypertrophy distinguishes PBS from posterior urethral valves.
  • Megalourethra is commoner in PBS than any other syndrome, from segmental lack of spongiosum (not distal stenosis): scaphoid type (ventral urethra balloons on voiding) or fusiform type (whole phallus dilates).

Non-urinary features

  • Abdominal flaccidity is the hallmark: uneven deficiency (usually medial and lower), thin muscle interwoven with aponeurosis; impairs breathing and causes pectus carinatum or excavatum; lack of abdominal pressure contributes to cryptorchidism. Intestinal obstruction is not a primary feature (only after intraperitoneal surgery).
  • Flaccidity does not correlate with urological severity and does not shorten survival, but with orthopaedic problems it lowers quality of life in adolescence.
  • Pulmonary hypoplasia (from oligohydramnios due to dysplasia or severe outlet obstruction, worsened by poor abdominal support) is the main threat to early survival and can cause newborn death; cardiac, orthopaedic and endocrine problems rarely threaten early life.
  • Testes lie high in the abdomen on the dilated ureters and never descend spontaneously.
  • Infertility: testicular histological defects, ductal and prostatic abnormalities, retrograde ejaculation. Sperm can be found in ejaculate or post-masturbation urine, so assisted reproduction is possible.

Evaluation

  • Team: neonatology, nephrology, urology, cardiology and others.
  • Chest X-ray for pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum and pulmonary hypoplasia; baseline renal and bladder ultrasound, BUN (blood urea nitrogen), creatinine and electrolytes.
  • Neonatal VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) only if renal insufficiency or outlet obstruction, and only under antibiotic prophylaxis.

Management

  • Expectant: dilatation improves spontaneously with time (basis of care, especially in milder/grade III cases, as in megaureter and reflux); antibiotic prophylaxis for the vulnerable; timed voiding and/or CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) for high residuals. No role for antimuscarinics or α-blockers (no overactivity, no hypertrophic neck); upper tract diversion rarely needed.
  • Early intervention for outlet obstruction, preferably a percutaneous suprapubic tube.
  • Upper tract reconstruction is controversial but indicated for falling renal function with recurrent upper tract infection or progressive dilatation. It removes the dilated distal ureter and reimplants it, with excision of the urachal diverticulum and part of the dome; residuals may persist or recur even after reduction cystoplasty. It can be combined with abdominoplasty, orchidopexy and circumcision, with acceptable risk even in infants.
  • Circumcision is advisable if the penis is structurally normal.
  • Orchidopexy at 6–18 months, as in other cryptorchidism (timing adjusted to other major surgery); before age 2 Fowler-Stephens is usually unnecessary; early surgery favours a single stage and germ cell preservation; endocrine function is preserved, so hormone replacement is not needed.
  • Abdominal wall reconstruction improves bladder emptying, cough and defecation; long-term cosmetic benefit is debated.

Ch 33 Posterior Urethral Valves

Definition and epidemiology

  • Type 1 valves (commonest): leaflets from the verumontanum fusing anteriorly just proximal to the external sphincter. Type 3: a congenital obstructing membrane, likely perforated at the first postnatal catheterisation.
  • Incidence 1.6–2.1 per 10,000 births; up to 50% are diagnosed antenatally. Emerging genetics: chromosome 11 and copy number variants.

Pathophysiology

  • Renal impairment comes from renal dysplasia plus obstructive uropathy: sustained high bladder storage pressures are transmitted to ureter, pelvis and glomeruli. Renal dysfunction, VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) and voiding dysfunction are all mediated by the dysfunctional bladder.
  • VURD (vesicoureteral reflux and dysplasia) syndrome does not protect long-term renal function; the "good" kidney often has dysplasia too, so follow closely.
  • High pressure can rupture a calyceal fornix (perirenal urinoma) or rarely cause ascites; bladder perforation is not typical, because the bladder wall hypertrophies.
  • Pulmonary hypoplasia is the commonest cause of perinatal and early death.

Antenatal diagnosis and intervention

  • Thick dilated bladder plus bilateral upper tract dilatation with oligohydramnios is highly sensitive for valves (ultrasound, sometimes fetal MRI); genital anomalies are not part of the picture.
  • Severity: amniotic fluid volume, degree of dysplasia, fetal urinary markers.
  • Intervention criteria: favourable urine chemistry, singleton, normal karyotype, no significant dysplasia; options are vesicoamniotic shunting or fetal cystoscopic ablation (early, varied experience; highly selected cases only).
  • Vesicoamniotic shunting increases live births and may improve neonatal lung function but does not reduce renal failure. Update: the PLUTO randomised trial (2013) found the same: possible survival benefit, poor renal outcomes.

Postnatal diagnosis

  • Antenatal thick bladder with bilateral ureterectasis needs early postnatal ultrasound and VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram); contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography is an emerging alternative.
  • VCUG: irregular, hypertrophied bladder; elevated bladder neck; dilated elongated posterior urethra.
  • Late presentation after 6 months is not rare: recurrent UTIs, overflow incontinence, haematuria, renal dysfunction, rarely ejaculatory dysfunction; investigate with VCUG.
  • Electrolytes and creatinine; nadir creatinine predicts long-term renal function; a nadir under 0.8 mg/dL at 1 year means a much lower ESRD (end-stage renal disease) risk.

Treatment

  • Cystoscopic valve ablation is the preferred first treatment.
  • Vesicostomy when the urethra is too small (e.g. low-birth-weight infant with renal impairment): decompresses, keeps bladder cycling, managed in nappies; does not impair contractility. High (upper tract) diversion gives no renal protection, needs bilateral incisions and a complex reversal.
  • Circumcision should be encouraged, especially after any UTI.
  • VUR is secondary to high bladder pressure: treat the bladder (timed and double voiding, anticholinergics); reimplantation only if UTIs persist despite maximal bladder therapy.

Valve bladder and prognosis

Phase Bladder pattern
Infancy, early childhood Detrusor hyperreflexia, high pressure
Childhood Lower pressures, better compliance
Adolescence Large capacity, hypocontractility, atony
- Valve bladder syndrome: growth and polyuria (acquired nephrogenic diabetes insipidus) fill a bladder that empties poorly; constant stretch triggers gene-expression changes that further weaken contraction.
- Videourodynamics when the picture changes (worse dilatation, recurrent UTI); overnight bladder drainage for rising residuals, UTIs, worsening hydronephrosis or renal function.
- Prognostic factors: age at diagnosis, dysplasia with or without VUR, nadir creatinine in year 1, recurrent UTIs, bladder dysfunction.
- Transplant into a valve bladder: more ureteral obstruction (thick wall) but no increase in graft loss or death.

Other urethral conditions

  • Anterior urethral valves and urethral atresia are rarer causes of LUTO (lower urinary tract obstruction); outcomes similar to PUV if the neonate survives.
  • Urethral duplication: commoner in boys (rare in girls); most are continent (duplication distal to sphincter); UTI uncommon; blind-ending or orthotopic-ending duplications need no surgery; only functional problems need reconstruction.
  • Urethrorrhagia: terminal bleeding or spotting without pain, no UTI link; clinical diagnosis after excluding other haematuria causes; no imaging test; linked to dysfunctional voiding (turbulent flow); treat voiding habits conservatively; cystoscopy only for refractory cases or marked straining.
  • Anorectal malformation: renal ultrasound and VCUG (high rate of genitourinary anomalies).

Ch 82 Surgery for Benign Disorders of the Penis and Urethra

Grafts and flaps

  • A graft is excised tissue that revascularises in the host bed ("take", about 96 hours): imbibition (~48 hours, nutrients absorbed, graft cooler than core) then inosculation (~48 hours, true microcirculation, graft reaches core temperature). Anything harming host bed vascularity harms take.
  • Layers: superficial (intradermal) plexus near the epidermis; deep (subdermal) plexus at the junction of deep dermis and underlying tissue; deep (reticular) dermis carries most lymphatics and collagen and gives the physical characteristics. Extragenital full-thickness grafts are bulkier and take less reliably than genital skin.
  • Bladder epithelium: superficial and deep plexuses with many perforators. Buccal (oral) mucosa has a panlaminar plexus, so it can be thinned and has optimal vascular characteristics.
  • Split-thickness grafts (STSG) take more easily but contract and become brittle; full-thickness grafts take less easily but contract less and are durable. STSGs carry few lymphatics; full-thickness grafts carry a full complement.
  • Meshed STSG on the genitalia: do not expand it; the slits let collections escape.
  • Tunica vaginalis grafts become aneurysmal over large defects.
  • Urethra: flaps suit distal reconstruction, grafts proximal. Onlay grafts or flaps succeed more than tubularised grafts.
  • Genital skin island flaps (on penile dartos or scrotal tunica dartos) are one concept with many applications (Quartey's microinjection studies); skin must be non-hirsute; key factors are tissue nature, vasculature and mechanics of transfer; usable even after circumcision and for the fossa navicularis.

Anatomy

  • Corpora cavernosa form one space through an incompetent septum; erectile tissue is separated from the tunica by the space of Smith; Buck fascia abuts the tunica and envelops spongiosum and dorsal neurovascular bundle; dartos is loose areolar tissue under the skin carrying superficial external pudendal and posterior scrotal vessels.
  • Urethra: fossa navicularis in the glans; bulbar urethra has the thickest spongiosum and lies dorsally, covered by bulbospongiosus; the membranous urethra is the only segment not invested by another structure (delicate transitional epithelium).
  • Arteries: internal pudendal becomes the common penile artery after perineal and posterior scrotal branches; it ends as bulbourethral, dorsal (within Buck fascia) and cavernosal arteries; circumflex branches are inconsistent.
  • Veins: superficial dorsal vein to the (usually left) saphenous; deep dorsal and circumflex veins (beneath Buck fascia) to the periprostatic plexus.
  • Nerves: dorsal nerve is the first branch of the pudendal nerve in Alcock canal (sensory to tunica and skin); cavernosal nerves are autonomic (parasympathetic and visceral afferent); penile skin also from genitofemoral branches.

Lichen sclerosus, meatus, fistula, circumcision

  • LS (lichen sclerosus): cause unclear; infection has no proven link (Koebner, autoimmune and genetic theories). It is a skin disease, so genital skin should not be used for repair (recurrence); oral mucosa is best. It causes meatal stenosis, often with proximal strictures (full work-up), and may be premalignant: watch for squamous cell carcinoma.
  • Childhood meatal stenosis follows circumcision (ammoniacal meatitis causes ventral fusion); meatitis settles with a short course of dilation plus steroid cream; established stenosis: divide the fusion membrane (slit meatus).
  • Urethrocutaneous fistula causes: hypospadias or urethral surgery (haematoma, infection, tension), distal obstruction with high-pressure voiding, infected extravasation and abscess; not genital herpes. A spontaneous fistula or unexplained periurethral abscess may herald urethral carcinoma.
  • Circumcision: never in a newborn with any hypospadias (keep the foreskin); reduces heterosexual HIV acquisition by 50%–60% in endemic areas, plus HSV-2, HPV and genital ulcer disease; reduces infant UTI; most skin dehiscence heals conservatively. Paraphimosis recurs, so plan dorsal slit or circumcision.

Trauma, lymphoedema, curvature

  • Penile amputation (often self-inflicted in psychosis; replant unless the patient adamantly refuses, even via court order with two surgeons): microreplantation preferred (tunica, spatulated two-layer urethra, epineural nerve, dorsal vein and arteries; cavernosal arteries cannot be joined); McRoberts macroreplantation works well when micro is impossible but leaves distal numbness.
  • Genital lymphoedema (including from radiation): excise and cover with STSG; fix testes in the midline; meshed STSG on testes if the scrotum cannot close; excise the parietal tunica (hydroceles common) and graft onto the visceral layer. Lymphoedema recurs in full-thickness or thick grafts; spared posterior and lateral scrotal skin may allow closure; lymphangiectasia can mimic papilloma; cellulitis is a recurring problem.
  • Congenital curvature or chordee without hypospadias: usually ventral, with or without torsion, often with ventral skin abnormality; erect penis is large in congenital curvature; patients may need sex therapy; the limiting factor is usually inelastic ventral corpora, not the spongiosum; correct with dorsal plication or excision, mobilising the dorsal bundle with Buck fascia.
  • Acquired non-Peyronie curvature (subclinical fracture after "minimal" buckling trauma): lateral scar and indentation; usually no global CVOD (cavernosal veno-occlusive dysfunction) and no shortening, unlike Peyronie disease.
  • Peyronie curvature needing repair: plication rather than grafting (book).

Urethral stricture

  • Stricture (anterior urethra) involves epithelium and spongiosum (spongiofibrosis); scar contraction narrows the lumen; squamous metaplasia proximal to tight strictures. Posterior narrowing is a contracture or stenosis; pelvic fracture lesions are PFUI (pelvic fracture urethral injury), a distraction fibrosis, not a true stricture.
  • Planning: location, length, depth and density by contrast studies, urethroscopy, ultrasound and bougie à boule calibration; MRI is not useful for anterior strictures (except carcinoma) but helps distorted PFUI anatomy.
  • A suprapubic tube for 6–8 weeks (urethral rest) reveals hidden strictures; assess the whole urethra endoscopically and with bougies during surgery; coned-down proximal segments are involved; sonourethrography gives length but not spongiofibrosis depth.
  • Internal urethrotomy: cure 29%–30% overall; 75%–78% for bulbar strictures under 1.5 cm without dense spongiofibrosis; poor elsewhere. Commonest complication is recurrence; deep 12 o'clock distal incisions can enter the corpora and cause ED (erectile dysfunction); also bleeding and extravasation.
  • Excision and primary anastomosis (EPA) is the most dependable repair: excise all fibrosis, wide spatulation, tension-free, epithelial apposition; intracrural development and bulbospongiosus detachment allow long segments, more so near the membranous urethra.
  • Vessel-sparing EPA (Jordan 2007, Mundy 2010) keeps bulbar arteries; no proven benefit in outcome or de novo ED; most logical after a failed transecting repair or with prior hypospadias (poor distal supply). After hypospadias, avoid transecting repair of a bulbar stricture; use onlay or vessel-sparing.

Pelvic fracture urethral injury

  • Distraction is commonest at the bulbomembranous junction (not prostate apex), but can involve any part of the membranous urethra.
  • An aligning catheter at worst helps later repair, at best leaves an endoscopically manageable urethra.
  • Repair: mobilise spongiosum cautiously (retrograde blood supply may be tenuous); detach Buck fascia, intracrural development, infrapubectomy and spongiosum rerouting shorten the path; do not mobilise beyond the penoscrotal junction (chordee).
  • Continence is judged after urethral continuity is restored; bladder neck appearance does not predict it.
  • ED after PFUI: one patent internal pudendal artery: often potent, reconstruct directly; reconstituted vessels only: rarely potent but repairable; bilateral occlusion without reconstitution: risk of ischaemic stenosis, so revascularise first. No ED means normal haemodynamics.
  • Rectourethral or vesicourethrorectal fistula: interpose healthy tissue, ideally a gracilis flap.
  • Neophallus: insensate at transfer, sensation from 3–4 months; implant a prosthesis only after 1 year with protective sensation and a durable patent urethra.

Ch 88 Urological Complications of Renal Transplantation

Haematuria and stents

  • Most postoperative haematuria settles; persistent or severe haematuria after graft biopsy needs duplex ultrasound for AV (arteriovenous) fistula.
  • Stents reduce stricture and urine leak; removal can be individualised after 3 weeks; early removal (before day 15) lowers UTI without more leak or stenosis; 27% of stents colonise whatever the dwell time.

Ureteral obstruction, leak, reflux

  • The graft is denervated, so recipients rarely have classic colic: they present with falling function, low urine output, recurrent UTI or a vague ache.
  • After 3 months, strictures dominate obstruction; main cause is ischaemia; suspect with hydronephrosis plus falling function. Open repair gives the best long-term results (endourology is an option; only very frail patients are relative exceptions).
  • Urine leak: divert with Foley catheter and nephrostomy (or retrograde stent); drain percutaneously for large urinoma, obstruction or infection; fluid restriction plays no part.
  • VUR comes from technical error, outlet obstruction or high-pressure storage (overactivity, poor compliance), and develops with any anastomotic technique.
  • Lymphocele: from dissection of recipient iliac or donor hilar lymphatics; also obesity, age, dialysis duration, warm ischaemia, LMWH (low-molecular-weight heparin) prophylaxis, delayed function, rejection, redo transplant, mTOR inhibitors (not atherosclerosis). Success: aspiration 41%, sclerotherapy 69%, drain 50%, laparoscopic marsupialisation 92%, open 84%.

Stones, BPH and cancer

  • Stones: calcium-based 67% (oxalate and phosphate), struvite 20%, uric acid 13%.
  • BPH: medical therapy is fine; TURP or HoLEP are safe after 3 weeks; avoid TURP in the first 2 weeks or with a stent (sepsis, death).
  • Renal tumours mostly arise in native kidneys and are low grade; graft tumours are mostly indolent (papillary 56%; 65% of non-papillary are Fuhrman 1–2).
  • Urothelial carcinoma risk is threefold and more aggressive (recurrence, progression, metastasis): offer early cystectomy for high-grade NMIBC (non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer); BCG in selected cases; MIBC by chemoradiation or cystectomy.
  • Prostate cancer: active surveillance, radiation or surgery are all reasonable.

Ch 131 Surgical Procedures for Sphincteric Incontinence in the Male

Pathophysiology and evaluation

  • Male SUI (stress urinary incontinence) needs both internal and external sphincter impairment. Internal: radical prostatectomy, bladder neck injury, sympathetic neuropathy, congenital. External: prostatectomy (commonest), pelvic fracture, myelopathy, dysraphism, sacral agenesis, exstrophy-epispadias. Spinal cord injury causes dyssynergia and retention, not SUI.
  • Nerve sparing speeds return of continence but does not change final continence.
  • Initial evaluation: history, voiding diary, examination, urinalysis (culture if needed), uroflowmetry and PVR (post-void residual); pressure-flow studies are not routine, only for suspected obstruction, overactivity or poor contractility.
  • Cystoscopy if bladder neck, anastomotic or urethral pathology is suspected (e.g. high PVR after prostatectomy); urethrography is weaker for posterior strictures.

Choice of treatment

  • Surgery for ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency) SUI after failed conservative care; choice depends on severity, prior surgery, bladder function, cystoscopy, dexterity, cognition, long-term risks and preference.
  • Bulking agents are not part of the post-prostatectomy algorithm (maybe a trial in neurogenic male SUI). AUS (artificial urinary sphincter) and slings are first-line.
  • Slings: mild to moderate UI (24-hour pad weight under 150 g mild, under 400 g moderate), intact contractility, non-radiated well-vascularised urethra; use very selectively after radiation, erosion or severe gravitational leak (poor tissue elasticity).
  • Transobturator slings (Advance) reposition and lengthen the membranous urethra without compression (higher abdominal leak point pressure, no obstruction on urodynamics; MRI shows longer membranous urethra, elevated bladder neck and rhabdosphincter); they need an intact external sphincter and are durable. Quadratic fixation (Virtue) has limited data.
  • Severe UI with poor dexterity: Advance sling rather than AUS. AUS is avoided with poor dexterity, cognitive disability, poor urethral tissue or severely low compliance.
  • If an Advance sling loosens in the first 6 weeks (continent, then leaks after exertion): re-explore early, cut and remove the sling, place another.

AUS technique and complications

  • Bulbar cuff around spongiosum proximal to the corporal convergence; 61–70 cm H2O pressure-regulating balloon via abdominal, scrotal or perineal route; keep deactivated 4–6 weeks.
  • Retention after AUS: failed trial at 48 hours, place a suprapubic tube (image-guided to protect the balloon); avoid prolonged urethral catheter or CIC (erosion even if deactivated); retention beyond several weeks means an undersized cuff.
  • Infection: usually skin Staphylococcus (epidermidis, aureus); total explant usually, salvage washout and reimplant in selected cases. Erosion: total explant and catheter drainage. Erosion risk: radiation, catheterisation, prior revision, urethroplasty (not a prior Advance sling).
  • Recurrent leak needs systematic work-up; at 4 years the commonest non-mechanical cause is subcuff atrophy (mechanical failure grows with time). Atrophy: downsize, reposition, tandem cuff or transcorporal cuff.
  • Tandem cuffs erode more (distal cuff); 3.5-cm cuffs erode more after radiation; in irradiated or atrophic urethras use a transcorporal cuff at a new site.
  • Anastomotic stricture with an AUS: surgically uncouple the cuff, then endoscopic incision or dilation.
  • AUS remains reliable for all severities, first-time and complex reoperative cases.

Ch 140 Cutaneous Continent Urinary Diversion

Patient selection

  • Must be able and willing to self-catheterise; poor candidates: multiple sclerosis, quadriplegia, frailty, mental impairment. Always consent for a possible ileal conduit.
  • Renal function: creatinine clearance at least 60 mL/min (creatinine above 1.8 mg/dL suggests insufficient function). Even if creatinine returns to 1.8 mg/dL, clearance under 60 means no continent diversion (replace the conduit instead). With hydronephrosis, drain the upper tracts (stents or nephrostomies) and reassess. Update: many guidelines accept eGFR above about 50 mL/min.
  • Normal hepatic function is mandatory; otherwise, cystoprostatectomy with ileal conduit.
  • Weight loss of 20 lb: look for metastases; if malnourished, give preoperative hyperalimentation.
  • Any pouch using colon needs preoperative colonic evaluation (colonoscopy).
  • Mainz II and other anal-continence procedures need a sphincter test (oatmeal enema); contraindicated with dilated ureters, pelvic irradiation or lax anal tone.
  • Loss of the ileocaecal valve in neurological or bowel disorders causes debilitating diarrhoea; after repeated pipestem conduits in myelomeningocele with short bowel, revise the conduit rather than build a pouch.

Pouch and valve types

  • T pouch and Kock pouch (ileal) preserve the ileocaecal valve; right colon pouches sacrifice it.
  • Indiana, Le Bag and Mainz I remove the appendix (nidus of infection or abscess). Penn pouch was the first to use the Mitrofanoff appendix principle.
  • Short ureters (positive margins high up): right colon pouch with ureters into the terminal ileum plus a Mitrofanoff; the ileocaecal valve and isoperistaltic ileum reduce reflux.
  • Mitrofanoff problems (calibre, short or absent appendix, stenosis) all have surgical solutions; catheterise the appendix with a 14 Fr coudé (straight is harder); appendiceal channels drain mucus least well.
  • Existing conduit: incorporate it into the new pouch when possible (fewer metabolic effects and complications).
  • Gastric pouch: less electrolyte reabsorption, no loss of absorptive bowel, acid urine may limit colonisation; but low pH causes haematuria and skin erosion.
  • Benchekroun hydraulic ileal valve: continence depends on pouch pressure; worst long-term outcome of all mechanisms.
  • Initial capacity is lowest in ileal (T, Kock) pouches; small bowel pouches start much smaller than right colon pouches.

Technique

  • Single-J stents out through separate stab wounds plus a Malecot in the reservoir; fix the reservoir to the abdominal wall at the Malecot exit.
  • Antireflux ureteral anastomoses have uncertain benefit and more strictures.
  • Absorbable sutures only (never silk). Absorbable staples: faster, reliable, best in large bowel pouches (no ischaemia); never overlap them (they will not seat); evert the bowel to apply the second row. Overlapping staple lines in absorbable-stapled ileal pouches cause ischaemic contraction.
  • Fix the nipple valve to the side wall so filling lengthens it. Catheterise the channel intraoperatively to confirm easy passage.

Complications

  • Nipple valves: highest reoperation rate; most retention (catheter misdirected if the chimney is deep); atrophy needs a new valve from additional bowel; Kock pouch forms the most stones (staples).
  • Pouchitis: urine culture first, then antibiotics. Pyelonephritis with a nipple pouch: pouchogram for proximal valve failure and reflux. Asymptomatic bacteriuria needs no treatment.
  • Rupture: decompress, image and explore with repair plus broad antibiotics; small leaks without peritonitis may be managed with catheter and antibiotics under close watch.
  • Nocturnal emptying may be needed for any pouch; it is mandatory after ureterosigmoidostomy (faecal incontinence, acidosis). Rectal bladders cause hyperchloraemic acidosis, potassium depletion and rectal cancer.
  • Anastomotic urothelial cancer: distal ureterectomy and reimplantation (an extra ileal limb can bridge).
  • Cancer can arise in any bowel exposed to urine, highest when urothelium, urine and faeces meet (ureterosigmoidostomy): cytology and colonoscopy with faecal admixture; annual pouchoscopy and cytology for all large bowel pouches.

Outcomes

  • Continent diversion may improve psychosocial adjustment, but no randomised data show better quality of life for any specific type; most patients are satisfied with whatever diversion they have; the ileal conduit is the gold standard.

Ch 152 Diagnosis and Staging of Prostate Cancer

PSA biology and modifiers

  • 65%–90% of serum PSA is bound to α1-antichymotrypsin. Without cancer, PSA varies with age, race and volume (higher in African Americans).
  • Disease (cancer, BPH, prostatitis) is the main driver; manipulation (massage, biopsy, cystoscopy) also raises PSA; ejaculation within 24 hours may raise it; DRE does not change it significantly. Not all prostate disease raises PSA, and rises are not cancer-specific.
  • PSA is lower with hypogonadism, statins and BPH surgery.
  • Finasteride 5 mg halves PSA by 12 months: multiply by 2 (by 2.3 at 2 years, 2.5 at 7 years); get a baseline; any rise on finasteride suggests occult cancer.
  • Abnormal PSA: repeat after observation; AUA does not recommend empirical antibiotics.

Improving specificity and screening

  • PSA has the highest PPV (positive predictive value) of any single test. Refinements: PSA density, transition zone density, velocity, percent free PSA.
  • PSA 4–10 ng/mL: velocity over 0.75 ng/mL/year and PSAD are concerning; low free PSA predicts cancer (over 25% free is reassuring). Update: velocity is no longer recommended as a screening trigger.
  • PSA under 1 ng/mL at 40–45 years can set screening intervals.
  • Screening: one trial (ERSPC) reduced advanced disease and cancer death, another (PLCO) did not; harms are overdiagnosis and unnecessary biopsies. In 2017 USPSTF, AUA, ACS and EAU all advised shared decision-making.

Grading and pathological stage

  • Grade groups: GG1 ≤6, GG2 3+4, GG3 4+3, GG4 8 (including 4+4), GG5 9–10.
  • A small higher-grade focus is the tertiary pattern; the Gleason sum is primary plus highest grade.
  • PSA vs stage: under 4 ng/mL, 80% organ-confined; 4–10 ng/mL, about two-thirds; over 10, over 50% extraprostatic; over 20, nodes in nearly 20%; over 50, nodes in 75%.
  • Partin tables: clinical stage, PSA and Gleason sum give percentage probabilities of pathological stage (dashes mean insufficient data); used for counselling.
  • Pathological staging beats clinical staging (tumour volume, margins, capsular penetration, seminal vesicles, nodes; not PSA). Seminal vesicle invasion or node involvement predicts occult metastasis.

Clinical staging

  • Staging aims to predict prognosis and choose therapy; no imaging detects all nodal metastases. Tools: DRE, markers, grade, imaging, lymphadenectomy.
  • Bone scan only if PSA over 20, T3–T4 or bone symptoms (book). Update: PSMA PET is now more accurate than CT plus bone scan (proPSMA) for high-risk staging.
  • Multiparametric MRI (diffusion and dynamic contrast) improves specificity; a T2 low-signal peripheral zone lesion with restricted diffusion and early enhancement needs targeted biopsy. Update: MRI before biopsy is now standard (PRECISION).
  • Pelvic lymphadenectomy criteria are not firm; at least for Gleason over 8, enlarged nodes, T3, or PSA over 20 (book). Update: EAU uses a nomogram risk above about 5%–7% for extended node dissection.

Ch 34 Neuromuscular dysfunction of the lower urinary tract in children

Causes and goals

  • Most causes are congenital (spina bifida, sacral agenesis, anorectal malformation); cerebral palsy is acquired.
  • The primary goal is preservation of renal function. Secondary goals: urinary and faecal continence, no UTI (urinary tract infection), sexual function and fertility.
  • Renal function is preserved by keeping bladder filling pressures low, actively managing VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) and avoiding UTI.

Newborn with myelodysplasia: evaluation

  • Renal ultrasound and residual urine as soon as possible after birth; they are the baseline for spotting later deterioration or progressive denervation.
  • Identify infants at risk early (poorly compliant or overactive detrusor, DSD (detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia)) so prophylactic treatment starts before the upper tract deteriorates.
  • Three urodynamic patterns: synergic 26%, dyssynergic with or without poor compliance 37%, complete denervation 36%.
  • DSD is the pattern that compromises emptying and is most dangerous: over 70% deteriorate within 3 years, versus under a quarter with synergy or denervation. Low capacity and detrusor overactivity alone are not linked to upper tract deterioration.
  • Risk factors for renal decline: DSD, high detrusor pressure, detrusor overactivity, febrile UTI, VUR; manage each actively.
  • Renal function: nuclear renography is the gold standard for GFR (glomerular filtration rate); cystatin C is better than creatinine (low muscle mass).

Follow-up

  • ICCS (International Children's Continence Society) follow-up is based on developmental stage and the risk of secondary tethering.
  • Tethering is commonest during the fastest growth: newborn to toddler and adolescence to adulthood.
  • Signs of a significant change in bladder function: new UTI, hydronephrosis, worsening continence, change in leg function (improved continence is not one).
  • A CDC (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention)-endorsed protocol covers management from birth to 5 years.

Management

  • Least invasive first; antimuscarinics plus CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation) are the mainstay.
  • Early CIC and antimuscarinics improve urodynamics and reduce UTI, VUR, upper tract deterioration and ESRD (end-stage renal disease).
  • Antimuscarinics increase capacity and volume to first contraction, and reduce contractions, incontinence and catheterisations.
  • Start antimuscarinics for poor compliance with or without hydronephrosis.
  • Keep filling pressures below 30 cmH2O where possible.
  • Indications for CIC include poor emptying and high-grade reflux; a residual of 3 mL after Credé is normal and needs no CIC.
  • Intravesical botulinum toxin is well tolerated and avoids bigger surgery in many; pre-existing poor compliance predicts poor response (Kask 2013).
  • Enterocystoplasty protects the upper tract but has many complications. Ileum is the preferred segment: autoaugmentation fails, stomach causes haematuria-dysuria syndrome and metabolic problems, ileocaecal segment worsens bowel function in neural tube defects, tissue-engineered bladder failed in phase II.
  • Stones after augmentation are more likely with ileum and a stoma; urethral catheterisation, mobility and routine irrigation reduce them; antimuscarinics have no effect.
  • Cancer after augmentation presents at a younger age than usual bladder cancer, with atypical symptoms and advanced disease, usually at least 10 years after surgery.
  • Isolated bladder neck procedures (AUS (artificial urinary sphincter), slings, bladder neck repair) worsen bladder function in up to 30%, especially with pre-existing overactivity or poor compliance; follow closely.

Reflux

  • Low-grade VUR with good emptying and no outlet resistance: antibiotic prophylaxis alone; high-grade VUR or poor emptying: add CIC.
  • The Credé manoeuvre is contraindicated with reflux and a reactive external sphincter (reflex sphincter contraction worsens reflux).
  • Antireflux surgery indications and success match non-neurogenic children if emptying is addressed.
  • Prenatal closure of myelomeningocele (MOMS trial) reduces shunting and improves motor function, but increases preterm labour and does not improve bladder function.
  • Sexual function in men with spina bifida is best with a sacral lesion; worse with higher lesions, hydrocephalus, incontinence or living with parents.

Occult spinal dysraphism and other conditions

  • Occult neural tube defects present after age 3–4 with incontinence or constipation, or with new symptoms after a growth spurt (tethering).
  • 90% have a skin marker over the lower spine: dimple, hair tuft, skin tag, lipoma, vascular malformation, asymmetric gluteal cleft.
  • Imaging: spinal radiograph, MRI and renal ultrasound; spinal ultrasound only before 3 months (before ossification), MRI after.
  • Do urodynamics before and after detethering; early detethering does better.
  • Sacral agenesis: perineal sensation is usually preserved.
  • Anorectal malformation: spinal cord anomalies (tethered cord, thick or fatty filum, lipoma) in 18%–50%. Urodynamics if spinal anomaly, hydronephrosis, VUR or incontinence; a low fistula alone is not an indication.
  • Cerebral palsy: LUTS (lower urinary tract symptoms) in 16%–94%, commonest is incontinence; daytime dryness comes first but later than peers; symptoms increase with age; recurrent UTI or DSD symptoms (retention, interrupted stream, hesitancy) signal upper tract risk and warrant ultrasound and urodynamics.
  • Transverse myelitis: retention in 95% in the acute phase.
  • Spinal cord injury: spinal shock first (acontractile bladder, non-reactive sphincter), then usually overactive detrusor with DSD. Lesions above T6 cause autonomic dysreflexia during filling and contractions.

Ch 35 Functional disorders of the lower urinary tract in children

Normal development

  • In the first 2 years, immature detrusor-sphincter coordination (hypercontractility, interrupted voiding) causes functional outflow obstruction.
  • Even newborns do not void during sleep, showing higher-centre control.

Associations

  • BBD (bladder bowel dysfunction): constipation with urological problems; bowel problems are common in children with voiding dysfunction and with acquired sphincter dysfunction.
  • LUTD (lower urinary tract dysfunction) is linked to bowel dysfunction, UTI, VUR and psychiatric diagnoses: stasis causes UTI, detrusor hypertrophy may distort the ureterovesical junction, BBD lowers reflux resolution, and treating bowels alone can improve bladder function.
  • Bladder dysfunction is linked to non-resolution of high-grade VUR.
  • Behavioural and emotional disorders occur in 20%–30%.

Specific conditions

  • OAB (overactive bladder) is the commonest LUT disorder in children, peaking at 5–7 years.
  • Giggle incontinence (enuresis risoria): school-age girls, large-volume or complete emptying triggered by laughing; diagnosis of exclusion; treat with biofeedback or methylphenidate.
  • Vaginal reflux (vaginal voiding): prepubertal girls wet 10–15 minutes after a normal void; may cause labial adhesions; treat with reassurance and posture to empty the vagina.
  • Pollakiuria: daytime-only frequency, up to 50 times a day; age 4–6, both sexes; often after a family death or stress; self-limited over about 6 months. The child does not wake to void.
  • Dysfunctional voiding: staccato flow with incomplete emptying; biofeedback coordinates pelvic floor and abdominal muscles.
  • Botulinum toxin A in the external sphincter blocks presynaptic acetylcholine release, paralysing striated muscle; onset 5–7 days, peak 4–6 weeks, lasts 3–12 months, reversible.
  • All children with LUTD get behavioural therapy: demystification, education, counselling, timed voiding, relaxation, stool monitoring and bladder-bowel diaries.

Enuresis

  • Three systems: bladder (low nocturnal capacity), kidney (nocturnal polyuria) and brain (arousal); maturational lag in one or more.
  • Nocturnal urine output often exceeds sleep bladder capacity; many have reduced functional capacity and detrusor overactivity.
  • Alarm: motivated families, no polyuria, low voided volume. Desmopressin: nocturnal polyuria with normal capacity, infrequent wetting, or alarm failed or refused.

Ch 71 Disorders of male orgasm and ejaculation

Physiology

  • Many transmitters are involved; dopamine (D2) promotes ejaculation and serotonin inhibits it.
  • Three phases: emission, ejection, orgasm. Emission: seminal vesicle and prostate contraction into the posterior urethra, sympathetic and parasympathetic (T10–L2). Ejection: somatic (S2–S4, pudendal) rhythmic bulbospongiosus and pelvic floor contraction, a spinal reflex with little voluntary control. The bladder neck and internal sphincter contract to prevent retrograde flow; the external sphincter contracts intermittently.

Premature ejaculation (PE): definition and epidemiology

  • PE is common; reported prevalence 4%–39%, higher with self-report than with the strict ISSM (International Society for Sexual Medicine) definition.
  • Stopwatch IELT (intravaginal ejaculatory latency time) is positively skewed, median 5.4 minutes (0.55–44.1), falls with age, varies by country; under 1 minute occurs in only about 2.5% of men.
  • Lifelong PE: about 90% of treatment seekers ejaculate within 1 minute and 10% within 1–2 minutes (77% within 30 seconds); a 1-minute cut-off captures 80%–90%.
  • ISSM lifelong PE (2007): ejaculation always or nearly always before or within about 1 minute of vaginal penetration, inability to delay on nearly all penetrations, and negative personal consequences. ISSM 2013 added acquired PE: a bothersome fall in latency to about 3 minutes or less. It applies only to vaginal intercourse.
  • The ISSM definition should be the basis of office diagnosis. DSM-5 (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual) is based on it (latency, control, bother); earlier DSM versions had no latency.
  • Subtypes: lifelong, acquired, natural variable and subjective PE (normal or long IELT but a complaint).
  • Self-estimated IELT by patient and partner is acceptable in practice. Voluntary delay acts before or early in emission.
  • PE causes distress, bother, frustration and avoidance; it harms quality of life, relationships, self-esteem and confidence, and deters single men from new relationships.

Causes

  • Lifelong PE may have a genetic and neurobiological basis (5-HT2C hyposensitivity or 5-HT1A hypersensitivity). Acquired PE is usually from performance anxiety, psychological or relationship problems or ED (erectile dysfunction), and sometimes prostatitis, hyperthyroidism or drug withdrawal.
  • Thyroid: hyperthyroidism (not hypothyroidism) is linked to PE; PE fell from 50% to 15% after treating hyperthyroidism. Routine TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) screening is not indicated.
  • Up to half of men with ED also have PE: they rush intercourse or need more stimulation, and performance anxiety often worsens it.

Evaluation

  • Full medical and sexual history, examination (mandatory in acquired PE), erectile function inventory. Lifelong PE is diagnosed on history alone.
  • IIEF (International Index of Erectile Function) is not validated in PE: 33.3% of potent men with PE get a false-positive ED score. The IPE (Index of Premature Ejaculation) was not designed as a screening tool.

Treatment

  • Everyone gets basic psychosexual education. CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy) is limited first-line but useful with drugs, especially in acquired PE with anxiety; behavioural methods work short term but relapse long term.
  • Oral SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) and topical anaesthetics (lidocaine, prilocaine, benzocaine) are effective and safe. SSRIs block presynaptic serotonin reuptake, not a postsynaptic transporter. Daily dosing delays more than on-demand dosing 3–6 hours before sex.
  • Dapoxetine, a short-acting on-demand SSRI, is approved in over 50 countries but not by the FDA. Other options: clomipramine, tramadol, PDE5 (phosphodiesterase type 5) inhibitors, α-blockers.
  • PDE5 inhibitors are not recommended for lifelong PE with normal erections, but are recommended with comorbid ED. Treat acquired PE causes (ED, hyperthyroidism, infection, prostatodynia, CPPS (chronic pelvic pain syndrome)) alone or with an SSRI.

Delayed ejaculation (DE) and anejaculation

  • Causes are many. Most men ejaculate 4–10 minutes after intromission; DE is suspected beyond 25–30 minutes with distress. Lifelong 25%, acquired 75%; global or situational.
  • Psychogenic DE comes from performance anxiety or idiosyncratic vigorous masturbation that intercourse cannot replicate.
  • Common causes: psychogenic, ageing penile afferents and Pacinian corpuscles, hypogonadism, diabetic neuropathy, multiple sclerosis, SSRIs and major tranquillisers, prostatectomy, pelvic surgery or radiotherapy.
  • Treatment is cause-specific and addresses fertility; drugs have limited success.
  • Spinal cord injury: ejaculation improves with lower lesions (erection does not). Under 5% with complete upper motor neuron lesions ejaculate; about 15% with lower motor neuron lesions; 22% with incomplete upper and almost all with incomplete lower lesions. Vibratory stimulation works in up to 70%; electroejaculation is safe. Both risk autonomic dysreflexia; pre-treat with nifedipine (not tamsulosin).

Retrograde ejaculation

  • Orgasm without antegrade semen; confirm with sperm (and fructose) in first-void post-masturbation urine; this separates it from failure of emission.
  • Commonest causes: TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate, 42%–100%) and diabetic neuropathy (about 30% of diabetics, unrelated to duration or HbA1c). TUIP (transurethral incision) causes it in 5%–45%.
  • Drugs (variable success): pseudoephedrine, ephedrine, midodrine, phenylpropanolamine, and imipramine 25 mg twice daily.

Post-orgasmic illness syndrome (POIS)

  • Flu-like state after ejaculation: myalgia, fatigue, nasal congestion, itchy eyes; starts within 30 minutes in 87% and peaks on day 2.
  • Proposed type 1 hypersensitivity to own semen; hyposensitisation with subcutaneous autologous semen has been tried.

Ch 104 Treatment of advanced renal cell carcinoma

Biology

  • VHL (von Hippel-Lindau) inactivation by mutation or methylation in 70%–90% of clear cell RCC (renal cell carcinoma), upregulating HIF (hypoxia-inducible factor) targets such as VEGF; Raf-1 is not one of them.
  • Sirolimus and its analogues target mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin).

Prognosis

  • MSKCC (Memorial Sloan Kettering) factors include Karnofsky status under 80%. Update: the IMDC (International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium) model is now standard.

Cytoreductive nephrectomy

  • Two randomised trials in the cytokine era showed survival benefit for nephrectomy before IFN-α (interferon alpha).
  • Poor performance status, comorbidity, rapid progression or brain metastases predict no benefit.
  • CARMENA: in intermediate and poor-risk patients, sunitinib alone was not inferior to nephrectomy then sunitinib; SURTIME (deferred nephrectomy) closed early. So poor-risk patients get systemic therapy first.
  • Nephrectomy is still offered to well-selected patients (low metastatic burden, favourable features).
  • It can palliate pain, haematuria or paraneoplastic effects, but non-surgical palliation usually works, so purely palliative nephrectomy is uncommon.

Metastasectomy

  • Resection of isolated metastases is appropriate in selected patients; median survival rates of 35%–50% are reported.
  • Better outcome: complete resection, solitary lesion, age under 60, small size, lung site, metachronous disease. A late solitary lung nodule after nephrectomy can be resected.
  • No randomised trials; benefit may reflect selection bias or tumour biology.
  • Metastases may also be resected (often with radiotherapy or systemic therapy) to relieve or prevent disabling symptoms.

Systemic therapy

  • High-dose IL-2 (interleukin-2): complete response in 7%–9% of clear cell RCC, most lasting over 15 years; no randomised survival benefit; reserved for selected clear cell patients.
  • IL-2 plus interferon gives higher response than either alone but no survival gain; adding LAK (lymphokine-activated killer) cells does not help. Cytokines work mainly in clear cell RCC.
  • Nivolumab plus ipilimumab beats sunitinib in untreated intermediate/poor-risk clear cell RCC (objective response 42%, better survival); sunitinib is no longer standard.
  • Axitinib plus pembrolizumab or plus avelumab beat sunitinib; cabozantinib beat sunitinib in response and PFS (progression-free survival) in intermediate/poor risk (phase 2). Update: also cabozantinib plus nivolumab and lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab; avelumab-axitinib improved PFS only; adjuvant pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-564) for high-risk resected disease.
  • Axitinib beat sorafenib in PFS after first-line VEGF or cytokine therapy.
  • Temsirolimus beat interferon in poor-risk patients (3 or more adverse factors): median survival 10.9 vs 7.3 months.
  • Papillary RCC: no proven benefit from clear cell agents; MET (c-met) mutations make MET inhibitor trials reasonable. Update: cabozantinib beat sunitinib in papillary RCC (PAPMET).

Ch 118 The underactive detrusor and nocturia

Detrusor underactivity (DUA)

  • ICS (International Continence Society) 2002: a contraction of reduced strength and/or duration, causing prolonged or incomplete emptying in a normal time span. Acontractile detrusor: no contraction on urodynamics.
  • The book says there is no recognised definition of UAB (underactive bladder); its symptoms overlap with OAB. Update: ICS 2018 proposed a symptom definition (slow stream, hesitancy, straining, with or without incomplete emptying).
  • Urodynamic criteria mostly use contraction strength from detrusor pressure at maximum flow. The Watt factor measures bladder power, depends little on volume and is unaffected by outlet resistance, but has no validated cut-off.
  • Elbadawi's 'degeneration' pattern (disrupted myocytes, axonal degeneration) is linked to DUA.

Causes

  • DUA often coexists with other dysfunctions in the elderly; ageing reduces autonomic innervation and sensation.
  • Diabetes harms detrusor function by myogenic and autonomic damage. Lumbosacral cord or disc disease and pelvic surgery injure the pelvic plexus.
  • Multisystem atrophy (can mimic Parkinson disease) causes DUA in at least half through parasympathetic atrophy.
  • Afferent nerves are central to starting and sustaining a contraction; intact sensation predicts better treatment response (sphincter botulinum toxin, muscarinic agonists).

Management and natural history

  • Timed and double voiding, pelvic floor physiotherapy and intermittent self-catheterisation.
  • Muscarinic agonists are not used: little efficacy and severe cardiac depression including arrest.
  • Intravesical electrotherapy stimulates mechanosensitive myelinated Aδ afferents, restoring sensation and possibly voiding.
  • Untreated DUA in men showed no significant symptomatic or urodynamic deterioration over 10 years (Thomas).

Nocturia: definitions and epidemiology

  • Two or more voids a night cause bother and are clinically significant (one void is not).
  • 20% of people aged 20–40 void twice or more nightly versus 60% over 70. Younger people tend to have low nocturnal capacity; older people overproduce urine.
  • NUV (nocturnal urine volume) is all urine passed during the night plus the first morning void. The diary must record bedtime and waking time.
  • Nocturnal polyuria: NPi (nocturnal polyuria index = NUV/24-hour volume) over 0.20 in young adults and over 0.33 over 65, or NUV over 0.9 mL/min (54 mL/h) or 1.5 mL/min (90 mL/h); no universal definition. Global polyuria: over 40 mL/kg in 24 hours. Example: NUV 1000 of 2000 mL gives NPi 0.5, nocturnal polyuria.

Causes

  • Third-spaced fluid returning on lying down, higher supine blood pressure and loss of the normal night-time rise in ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
  • ADH is inhibited (diuresis) by PGE2, ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide), hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, lithium and tetracyclines (not hyperkalaemia).
  • Obstructive sleep apnoea is a common cause.

Treatment

  • Desmopressin acts on renal V2 receptors: more water permeability and reabsorption, diluted extracellular fluid, concentrated urine. It suits nocturnal polyuria.
  • Desmopressin can cause hyponatraemia; women are more sensitive. Over 65: monitor sodium closely and avoid with baseline hyponatraemia.
  • After 6–12 months nocturia fell 7% with watchful waiting, 17% with α-blockers, 32% with TUMT (transurethral microwave therapy) and 75% with TURP. α-Blockers, 5-ARIs (5α-reductase inhibitors) and antimuscarinics give statistically significant but clinically minimal reductions.

Ch 135 Tumours of the bladder

Epidemiology

  • Mean age at diagnosis in the USA is 73; incidence peaks in the seventh decade. Cancers in adolescents and young adults are usually well differentiated and non-invasive.
  • 63% of cases are in developed countries (55% in North America and Europe); highest rates in Southern and Eastern Europe, parts of Africa, the Middle East and North America.
  • Mortality has fallen 5% since 1990 (less smoking, fewer carcinogens). Lifetime risk for a white man is 3.7%, about 3 times white women or African American men.
  • Third commonest cancer death in men over 80; about 31% of bladder cancer deaths were judged avoidable, more in non-invasive disease.
  • Egypt: mortality 3 times Europe and 8 times North America because of squamous cancer; Schistosoma haematobium causes squamous cancer in endemic areas.
  • Histology: 90% urothelial, 5% squamous, under 2% adenocarcinoma and others; urothelial cancer is the second commonest cause of genitourinary cancer death.

Risk factors

  • Smoking causes 30%–50% of male cases (2- to 6-fold risk); risk rises linearly with intensity and duration with no plateau. After stopping, relative risk is 2.6 at 1–3 years and 1.1 after 15 years.
  • β-Naphthylamine (dye and rubber workers) was among the first chemicals implicated; NAT-2 and GSTM1 polymorphisms alter susceptibility.
  • Cyclophosphamide is the only chemotherapy proven to cause bladder cancer (dose-related; metabolite phosphoramide mustard).
  • High-dose radiation (over 500 mSv) doubles risk (relative risk 1.63 men, 1.74 women), latency 15–30 years, not age-related; nuclear and radiology workers are not at increased risk.
  • First-degree relatives have twofold risk, probably via low-penetrance genes; higher in women and non-smokers, not from passive smoking.
  • Healthy diet lowers risk. No convincing link with fluid intake, alcohol, sweeteners or analgesics; chronic irritation, infection and radiation are linked.
  • Spinal cord injury: older series 2.5%–10% squamous cancer after a mean 17 years; recent risk only 0.38% with better catheter care.
  • BCG plus oral vitamins A, B6, C, E and zinc may reduce recurrence (some evidence).

Benign lesions

  • Inverted papilloma: benign (about 1% recurrence), linked to chronic inflammation; sometimes coexists with urothelial cancer, more often in the upper tract; FISH (fluorescence in situ hybridisation) separates it from cancer with an inverted growth pattern. An unusual biopsy should be reviewed with the pathologist (can be mistaken for adenocarcinoma).
  • Cystitis glandularis may accompany pelvic lipomatosis. Ureteral pseudodiverticulosis is associated with bladder cancer in 30%, so watch the bladder.

Pathology and genetics

  • Normal urothelium is under 7 cell layers; papillary lesions are thicker. At presentation 80% are non-muscle-invasive (CIS, low- or high-grade papillary, sessile).
  • WHO grading: PUNLMP (papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential), low grade and high grade replace grades 1–3. PUNLMP: minimal atypia, often solitary on the trigone, no invasion or metastasis but frequent recurrence, so perioperative mitomycin C is given.
  • Low-grade tumours: chromosome 9 loss (80% of PUNLMP), FGFR3 (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3), HRAS and PI3K changes; cytokeratin-20 and CD44 positive.
  • High-grade and invasive: TP53, RB and PTEN loss (poor prognosis) and chromosome 17 loss; high-grade NMIBC has TP53 and RB deletions. All CIS is high grade with RB, TP53 and PTEN changes.
  • Tumour suppressor genes are inactivated by loss of one allele then a point mutation in the other (book says 'activated'); oncogenes are activated by point mutation, amplification or translocation.
  • MIBC (muscle-invasive bladder cancer) subtypes: basal tumours benefit most from neoadjuvant chemotherapy; DNA repair gene mutations (ERCC2, FANCC, ATM) predict response (SWOG S1314).
  • Natural history: low-grade papillary tumours recur in 60% but progress in under 10%; high-grade NMIBC recurs in 80% and high-grade T1 progresses in up to 50%. Angiolymphatic invasion is a poor sign.

Staging

  • AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer) 2017: stage 0 non-invasive, I lamina propria, II muscle-invasive organ-confined, III locally advanced or node-positive without distant spread (nodes were stage IV before), IV metastatic.
  • Muscularis propria visible on biopsy means at least T2.
  • Repeat TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour) for T1 tumours even if muscle was present, because of understaging.

Diagnosis

  • Painless gross haematuria in 85%; investigate any episode even if later urine is clear. 50% have a cause, 20% a urological cancer and 12% bladder cancer.
  • After a full negative work-up, cancer risk with recurrent haematuria is under 5% (near zero for 6 years).
  • Microhaematuria: age under 40 is not a risk factor (smoking, pelvic radiation, UTI and urological surgery are); the book advises 6-monthly urinalysis, cytology and blood pressure for high-risk patients. Update: AUA/SUFU 2020 uses low/intermediate/high risk groups with imaging by risk; cytology is not routine.
  • Urine FISH (UroVysion): aneuploidy of chromosomes 3, 7, 17 and 9p21 loss; sensitivity 79%, specificity 70%. Microsatellite analysis PCR-amplifies polymorphic repeats to detect loss of heterozygosity (sensitivity 72%–97%, specificity 80%–100%). No marker replaces cystoscopy.
  • Blue-light (porphyrin) and narrow-band imaging increase cystoscopic sensitivity.
  • CT urography with bladder masses and a ureteric filling defect needs cystoscopy and ureteroscopy (synchronous ureteral tumour).

Prostatic urethral involvement

  • 90% of prostatic urethral cancers occur with bladder cancer (mainly CIS, multifocal). Only 3% at first presentation; 15% at 5 years and 30% at 15 years after bladder cancer (with intravesical therapy); 40% at cystectomy.
  • Risk factors: CIS of trigone, bladder neck or distal ureters, recurrent tumours, intravesical therapy; low-grade tumours rarely involve it.
  • Non-invasive mucosal or ductal disease: complete TURP plus BCG induction and maintenance. Prognosis: good if mucosal, worse with stromal invasion, poor with direct invasion from the bladder.

Variants and non-urothelial tumours

  • Micropapillary and nested patterns carry poor prognosis.
  • Micropapillary: surgery is the best treatment; TUR and BCG fail unless completely resected; neoadjuvant chemotherapy seems ineffective (book).
  • Nested variant: male:female 6:1, mimics von Brunn nests, cystitis cystica and inverted papilloma; 70% die within 3 years.
  • Small cell carcinoma (under 1%): treat as metastatic; very chemosensitive; chemoradiation or chemotherapy then cystectomy.
  • Signet ring cell carcinoma: under 1%, may be urachal, presents high-grade and advanced, often peritoneal studding; CEA may rise; mean survival under 20 months despite cystectomy.
  • Sarcomas, most to least common: leiomyosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, angiosarcoma, osteosarcoma, carcinosarcoma; male:female 2:1, sixth decade, linked to radiation and chemotherapy, not smoking.

Ch 148 Epidemiology, aetiology and prevention of prostate cancer

Incidence and mortality

  • Commonest visceral cancer in US men and third leading cause of cancer death.
  • SEER (Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results): incidence peaked in 1992 after PSA (prostate-specific antigen) arrived, fell until 1995, then fluctuated; falling since 2008.
  • Mortality has fallen since 1991; for white men it is now below pre-PSA levels. PSA caused downward migration of age and stage.
  • Incidence is highest in high-income countries with most screening; highest in African American and Jamaican men. African Americans: 186.8 per 100,000, 1.75 times white incidence and 2.2 times mortality; causes are multifactorial with a biological basis.

Screening

  • PSA screening reduces prostate cancer mortality, but the absolute benefit is small given the numbers needed to screen and treat.
  • PLCO (Prostate, Lung, Colorectal and Ovarian) was criticised: 44% prescreened, poor biopsy compliance, 52% contamination of controls; analysed by actual screening, it matches ERSPC (European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer).

Genetics

  • Genetics and environment both matter. Family history raises risk 2–3 times, more with more and younger and closer relatives.
  • GWAS (genome-wide association studies) found over 100 risk SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphisms); single loci predict little, so multi-SNP models are needed; none predict aggressiveness.
  • HOXB13 and BRCA greatly raise risk; BRCA tumours are more aggressive.
  • TMPRSS2-ERG fusion (ERG at 21q22.3) in about 50% of localised cancers puts ETS oncogenes under androgen control; it is highly cancer-specific.
  • Seven molecular subtypes: four fusions (ERG, ETV1, ETV4, FLI1) and three mutations (SPOP, FOXA1, IDH1); methylation profiles further separate them.
  • SPOP mutation in about 10%; a SPINK1-overexpressing subgroup is commoner in African Americans with higher Gleason scores. Genomics differ between African American and European American men.
  • Epigenetic changes (methylation, histone modification and others) alter expression without changing DNA sequence.
  • Stem cells with multilineage potential are the origin of clonal expansion.

Inflammation, androgens and lifestyle

  • Chronic inflammation (from diet, infection, oestrogens) causes regenerative proliferation; weak oxidant defences and PIA (proliferative inflammatory atrophy) are key steps.
  • DHT (dihydrotestosterone) from type II 5α-reductase is the main prostatic androgen; androgen exposure before or at puberty is needed for cancer; intraprostatic levels are locally regulated, not set by serum levels; hypogonadal men can still get cancer.
  • Androgens matter but no dose-response with serum levels exists; long-term absence protects. AR (androgen receptor) and androgen-metabolism gene polymorphisms may modify risk; AR drives development and progression.
  • Vitamin D and its receptor modulate risk and aggressiveness.
  • Smoking increases recurrence and prostate cancer death.
  • Obesity: lower PSA (lower androgens, haemodilution) and bigger prostates, so fewer biopsies and more sampling error; more oxidative stress, higher free IGF-1, more high-grade disease, more treatment failure and death.

Chemoprevention

  • Primary chemoprevention aims to lower incidence, treatment harm and death, with non-toxic agents acting on specific steps; prostate cancer is a good target.
  • PCPT (finasteride) and REDUCE (dutasteride) cut diagnosis by about 25% and were well tolerated with BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia) benefit; extra high-grade cancers did not change mortality, and there was no effect on overall mortality. Update: the FDA declined a prevention indication because of the high-grade signal.
  • SELECT: neither vitamin E nor selenium prevents prostate cancer (HR 1.13 vitamin E, 1.04 selenium, 1.05 both); longer follow-up showed vitamin E increases risk (HR 1.17). The book prints the vitamin E CI as 0.95–1.13; the correct interval is 0.95–1.35.

Ch 36 Management of Defecation Disorders

Diagnosis of functional constipation

  • Functional constipation is a diagnosis of exclusion. Rome IV criteria: symptoms for at least 1 month, judged for developmental age, with no organic cause; criteria include painful defecation, large-diameter stools that may block the toilet and 2 or fewer defecations a week.
  • Constipation can cause significant voiding dysfunction; suspect it in every child with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). Treat it and dysfunctional voiding before any surgical correction; a paediatric urologist or nurse practitioner can do this with the primary care doctor.
  • Drugs for LUTS, especially antimuscarinics such as oxybutynin, commonly cause or worsen constipation; so do diet changes such as stopping breastfeeding.

History and examination

  • Age of onset is the easiest and most important history item: onset in infancy, before toilet training, suggests organic disease.
  • Also ask about: failed toilet training, pain or bleeding with defecation, abdominal pain, faecal incontinence, holding behaviour, vomiting, weight loss, growth, developmental delay.
  • Warning signs of an organic cause (not functional constipation): alternating constipation and diarrhoea, bowel obstruction, bilious vomiting, ribbon-like stools.
  • Record stool form with the Bristol scale (consistency, shape, difficulty of passage; odour is not part of it).
  • Examine weight, height, perineum, genital and perianal regions (anal position, soiling, trauma, fissures, sensation). Digital rectal examination is not routine in children; reserve it for difficult cases, by experienced staff, to look for anal stenosis, a large faecal mass or an empty rectum.

Organic causes

  • Organic causes: cystic fibrosis, hypothyroidism, coeliac disease, food allergy, Hirschsprung disease, anal stenosis, trisomy 21. In older children consider mental health problems, eating disorders, sexual abuse and irritable bowel syndrome.
  • Endocrine and metabolic causes: chronic dehydration (diabetes insipidus), hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, hypothyroidism, hypervitaminosis D. Precocious puberty does not cause constipation.
  • Suspected Hirschsprung disease: deep suction rectal biopsy (including submucosa), transanal, 2–3 cm above the dentate line. Diagnostic features: absent ganglion cells, hypertrophied nerve fibres, raised acetylcholinesterase in lamina propria and muscularis mucosae.

Investigations

  • Plain abdominal X-ray is of modest value but widely used. It shows faecal load and distribution and impaction, checks response to washout, convinces parents, and may show spinal dysraphism or sacral agenesis.
  • The amount and distribution of stool on one film do not predict response to treatment or recurrence.
  • Colonic transit studies: only for difficult or unresponsive cases. Contrast enema: for suspected Hirschsprung disease or repaired anorectal malformation.
  • Neuropathic concern: spinal ultrasound in the first 3–6 months (before vertebral ossification), lumbosacral MRI later.
  • Anorectal manometry: only for suspected Hirschsprung disease or internal sphincter achalasia (rectoanal relaxation reflex absent); adds little in functional constipation.

Faecal impaction

  • Suspect impaction with a lower abdominal or left lower quadrant mass, or a dilated stool-filled rectum on ultrasound or X-ray. About 30% of children with functional constipation present impacted.
  • First reduce the faecal load (disimpaction): enemas or suppositories, high-dose polyethylene glycol (PEG) with or without sodium chloride, phosphate or mineral oil enemas; sometimes digital disimpaction under anaesthesia.
  • Do not add fibre during initial treatment of impaction; it increases faecal load and worsens the problem.

Medical management

  • First line: behaviour change (regular toileting, activity), more fluid and fibre, stool softeners and laxatives, and careful use of enemas.
  • PEG is the preferred agent: nearly tasteless, dissolves fast, better tolerated than lactulose, mineral oil and milk of magnesia.
  • Prucalopride is an oral, selective, high-affinity 5-HT4 prokinetic for resistant constipation; it does not change stool consistency or bulk and is not antispasmodic. Update: it is a 5-HT4 receptor agonist (the book wrongly says antagonist).
  • Outcome: about 50% recover and stop medicines within 6–12 months; up to 80% are controlled; up to 50% relapse within 5 years.

Antegrade continence enemas (MACE and caecostomy)

  • Principle: regular, complete colonic washout gives faecal continence, and antegrade emptying is feasible.
  • MACE (Malone antegrade continence enema) channels and caecostomy (C) tubes give more independence than retrograde enemas. Open and laparoscopic results are similar.
  • Best results in motivated children aged 5–12 years, and in neuropathic bowel or anorectal malformation rather than idiopathic constipation; failures are commoner in younger children. The child may sit on the toilet up to an hour.
  • Access can be caecal or left colonic; a left-colon conduit shortens the washed segment in severe constipation with a redundant colon.
  • If the appendix is long enough with good blood supply it can be split to make both a MACE and a Mitrofanoff channel. Previous surgery is not an absolute contraindication to laparoscopy.
  • Caecostomy tubes suit: absent appendix, refusal of catheterisation, MACE stomal stenosis, a trial of antegrade enemas, choosing right vs left colon, or a non-operative permanent option. Drawback: unsightly granulation at the tube site.
  • Regimen: success is not immediate; reaching a reliable routine can take 6 months of trial and error. Start with daily washouts of 20 mL/kg, then try alternate days; usually given in the evening; tap or salt water with additives such as glycerin; fluid need not be sterile.
  • Commonest problem: pain or discomfort during instillation, usually settling within 3 months. Always exclude distal faecal impaction (a large rectal and descending colon load) as the cause. Perforation is very rare; retrograde ileal flow is not a usual cause of new pain.

Ch 64 Male Reproductive Physiology

Hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis

  • Normal testosterone and sperm production need pulsatile hypothalamic GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) and pituitary LH (luteinising hormone) and FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone). The axis is regulated mainly by negative feedback.
  • Testosterone feeds back on LH; inhibin and activin regulate FSH.
  • Exogenous testosterone or anabolic steroids suppress LH and FSH and cause azoospermia in most men (depends on dose, frequency, duration).
  • Hyperprolactinaemia inhibits GnRH and LH pulses, lowering testosterone.
  • Testosterone: made by Leydig cells, mostly bound in plasma, regulated mainly by LH, converted to DHT (dihydrotestosterone) in target tissue by 5α-reductase.
  • Testosterone peaks: first trimester of fetal life, about 2 months after birth (mini-puberty), and at puberty.
  • Ageing: lower testosterone, blunted feedback, irregular pulsatility. With paternal age, sperm structural chromosomal abnormalities, autosomal dominant mutations and epigenetic changes increase, causing disease in offspring.

Development

  • SRY on the Y chromosome determines maleness; WNT4 and DAX1 are anti-testis genes that can drive ovarian development.
  • The Wolffian ducts form epididymis, vas and seminal vesicles; Müllerian ducts regress; the gubernaculum guides the testis into the scrotum.

Testis structure

  • Normal adult testis volume is 18–22 mL (about 20 mL). Seminiferous tubules are 70%–80% of volume and interstitium 20%–30%.
  • Young adult testis: about 250 m of seminiferous tubules and 700 million Leydig cells.
  • Arterial supply of testis and epididymis: internal spermatic, deferential and cremasteric (external spermatic) arteries; not the pudendal artery.
  • Sertoli cells are regulated mainly by FSH and testosterone; they make androgen-binding protein (ABP).
  • Sertoli–Sertoli tight junctions form the blood–testis barrier, separating spermatogonia (basal) from meiotic and post-meiotic cells (adluminal).

Spermatogenesis

  • Sequence: dark type A (Ad), pale type A (Ap), type B spermatogonia; preleptotene, leptotene, zygotene, pachytene primary spermatocytes; secondary spermatocytes; spermatids Sa, Sb1, Sb2, Sc, Sd1, Sd2.
  • Type A spermatogonia are the only true stem cells (self-renew or differentiate).
  • Meiosis differs from mitosis by crossing-over (recombination), pairing of homologous chromosomes in meiosis I, and haploid daughter cells.
  • Spermiogenesis: round spermatids become mature sperm with no cell division, in the adluminal compartment; changes are nuclear compaction, cytoplasm loss, acrosome and flagellum formation, organelle migration.
  • Spermatogenesis is organised in stages, cycles and waves for constant output. Genes on both X and Y govern it and contribute to infertility.
  • Timing (book): 45–60 days in the testis, 2–12 days in the epididymis, plus days to weeks of storage in the cauda. Update: the full human spermatogenic cycle is usually quoted as about 64–74 days.

Epididymis and sperm maturation

  • Regions: caput, corpus, cauda, each with histological zones.
  • Cells: principal (absorptive and secretory), basal (said to derive from macrophages) and contractile cells for transport.
  • In transit sperm gain progressive motility and ability to bind and penetrate the zona pellucida; metabolic changes include increased glycolytic capacity and changes in pH, calcium, adenylate cyclase and phospholipids.
  • In obstruction (congenital absence of vas, post-vasectomy) the best motility is in proximal epididymal sperm.
  • Epididymal function depends on temperature and androgens (mainly DHT): relevant to cryptorchidism, varicocele and 5α-reductase inhibitors.

Vas, seminal vesicles and ejaculate

  • The vas is Wolffian (mesonephric) and carries sperm from cauda to ejaculatory duct during emission. After stimulation, vasal contents are pushed back toward the epididymis because the distal vas contracts more strongly.
  • Seminal vesicles give 65%–70% or more of ejaculate volume; the rest is vasal fluid with sperm, prostatic and periurethral secretions. The seminal vesicle–ejaculatory duct unit behaves like bladder and urethra, with physical or functional obstruction causing infertility.

Sperm structure and motility

  • Sperm are ciliated cells with a 9 + 2 axoneme; the midpiece has helical mitochondria around outer dense fibres.
  • 200–300 genes regulate motility. Motility defects (ciliary dyskinesias) are common: correctable (non-specific flagellar anomalies) or genetic (dysplasia of the fibrous sheath).
  • Sperm mitochondrial DNA is circular, histone- and intron-free, encodes respiratory chain proteins and mutates easily.

Ch 83 Surgery of the Scrotum and Seminal Vesicles

General scrotal surgery

  • Scrotal surgery is clean, not sterile: give prophylactic antibiotics; remove hair just before surgery. A drain does not reduce complications or haematoma.
  • Fournier gangrene: necrotising fasciitis of skin and subcutaneous tissue, limited by dartos (penis), Colles (perineum) and Scarpa (abdomen) fascia. Treat with fluids, broad antibiotics (third-generation cephalosporin, aminoglycoside, metronidazole), debridement once stable, and daily theatre debridement until all dead tissue is gone.

Vasectomy

  • The vas may get branches from several arteries but not from the superior vesical artery.
  • No-scalpel vasectomy: no incision with a scalpel, smaller wound, fewer haematomas and infections, less pain.
  • Occlusion: clips on both ends fail in under 1%; fascial (dartos) interposition between the ends brings recanalisation near zero. No method is 100% effective.
  • To ease later reversal: do not excise a long segment; divide the straight vas about 3 cm above the cauda epididymis; low-voltage cautery plus fascial interposition.
  • Open-ended vasectomy: sperm granulomas in 97%.
  • Over 80% are azoospermic at 3 months. Book: repeat vasectomy if motile sperm persist at 3 months. Update: AUA 2012 advises semen analysis at 8–16 weeks and repeat vasectomy if motile sperm persist beyond 6 months.
  • Vasectomy is not linked to prostate cancer, dementia, cardiovascular disease or atherosclerosis. Book: chronic scrotal pain in 10%. Update: AUA quotes 1%–2% with pain severe enough to seek care.
  • Vasectomy breaches the blood–testis barrier: antisperm antibodies in 60%–80%. Pressure damage falls mainly on epididymis and efferent ductules.
  • Any surgical manipulation of the epididymis causes azoospermia on that side.

Chronic orchialgia

  • Palpable varicocele with pain: varicocelectomy relieves pain in 50%.
  • Microsurgical denervation of the cord helps up to two-thirds in small series; consider only if a cord block works.
  • Orchiectomy: no level 1 evidence; if done, the inguinal route relieves pain better than the scrotal route.
  • Epididymectomy for chronic epididymitis works best with a palpable epididymal abnormality; worse with ultrasound changes only; no benefit with neither.
  • Up to 75% of epididymo-orchitis has no bacterial UTI, yet empirical antibiotics rose from 75% to 95% (1965–2005) and are not indicated without infection.

Hydrocele and other scrotal surgery

  • Jaboulay (bottleneck) repair sews the sac edges behind the cord and lowers recurrence. Excise long-standing, thick-walled or loculated sacs.
  • Large hydroceles splay the epididymis and vessels; identify them to avoid injury.
  • Most solid, non-transilluminating epididymal masses are benign adenomatoid tumours.
  • Retractile testis needing fixation: dartos pouch orchidopexy prevents retraction.

Seminal vesicles

  • Develop at about 12 weeks as a dorsolateral bud of the distal mesonephric duct; supply from the vesiculodeferential artery (branch of superior vesical). Give 60%–80% of ejaculate.
  • Unilateral agenesis (0.6%–1%) reflects an insult to the mesonephric duct before 7 weeks; may come with absent vas and ipsilateral renal anomalies.
  • Bilateral absence of vas or seminal vesicles: 70%–80% carry a CFTR (cystic fibrosis) mutation; 80%–95% of men with cystic fibrosis have it.
  • Cysts (congenital or acquired) result from ejaculatory duct obstruction; two-thirds have ipsilateral renal agenesis or dysplasia; linked to polycystic kidney disease.
  • Infections include tuberculosis and Schistosoma haematobium. Vesiculitis on MRI: low T1, T2 brighter than fat and normal vesicle.
  • Primary tumours are very rare; secondary spread from bladder, prostate, rectum or lymphoma is commoner.
  • Transrectal ultrasound is the first test (easy, allows biopsy); biopsy a mass.
  • Surgery: laparoscopic excision gives good view, low morbidity, shorter stay and allows dealing with the ipsilateral kidney. Transcoccygeal route suits men with previous suprapubic or perineal surgery.
  • Abscess near the prostate: deep transurethral unroofing at 5 or 7 o'clock just distal to the bladder neck; CT-guided aspiration and drainage is now the least traumatic preferred option.

Ch 97 Malignant Renal Tumors

Epidemiology and risk factors

  • Tobacco is the most accepted environmental risk factor (relative risk 1.4–2.3, dose-related); obesity and hypertension are also established. Acquired renal cystic disease raises risk.
  • Bilateral RCC (renal cell carcinoma), synchronous or metachronous: 2%–4%. Evidence of impaired immune surveillance is ample.

Imaging and biopsy

  • Triple-phase renal CT (computed tomography) is the key test; an enhancing mass (more than 15 HU, Hounsfield units) is RCC until proved otherwise.
  • About 20% of enhancing masses under 4 cm and 40% under 2 cm are benign.
  • Bosniak II: minimally complicated, benign; hyperdense cysts (old blood) are small (<3 cm), round, sharp, non-enhancing; 3 cm or more is IIF. Bosniak II risk of malignancy under 10%.
  • Bosniak IV: enhancing mural nodules (with dystrophic calcification) mean cystic RCC.
  • Fat under −20 HU on unenhanced CT is diagnostic of angiomyolipoma (AML). Fat with calcification is almost always RCC (reactive fat with necrosis); AML almost never calcifies.
  • Renal lymphoma has 5 radiological patterns, including a solitary mass that mimics RCC.
  • Tumour thrombus: CT and MRI both show presence and upper extent; multiplanar CT is enough at many centres.
  • FDG-PET-CT is not useful for staging RCC. Sestamibi uptake suggests oncocytoma or hybrid oncocytic tumours; girentuximab (carbonic anhydrase IX, clear cell) and PSMA imaging are in development.
  • Classic biopsy indications: suspected abscess or infected cyst (e.g. fever, UTI and a solid/cystic mass), or to separate RCC from metastasis or lymphoma; now used more widely when several options exist.
  • Biopsy limitation: oncocytoma vs eosinophilic RCC variants. Non-diagnostic in 10%–15%; complications low; tract seeding rare. Biopsy before or during thermal ablation.
  • Bone scan: not needed if ECOG performance status 0, no bone pain and no extraosseous metastases; in others bone metastases exceed 15%.
  • Perirenal nodes under 2 cm may be inflammatory; over 2 cm usually metastatic.

Pathology and genetics

  • Subtypes: clear cell 70%–80%, papillary 10%–15%, chromophobe 3%–5%, collecting duct under 1%, medullary rare.
  • Clear cell arises from the proximal tubule; oncocytoma and chromophobe from the distal tubule or cortical collecting duct.
  • Clear cell: chromosome 3 alterations; VHL inactivated in over 75% of sporadic cases.
  • Type 1 papillary: trisomy 7 and 17; papillary RCC tends to be multifocal. Update: WHO 2022 no longer splits papillary into types 1 and 2.
  • Medullary carcinoma: sickle cell trait, young Black patients (third decade), usually metastatic at diagnosis, death within months.
  • Collecting duct carcinoma: central, infiltrative, aggressive, Ulex europaeus lectin positive, chemoresistant; usually metastatic, so follow closely (cisplatin role undefined).
  • Sarcomatoid change: commonest with clear cell and chromophobe; can be grossly infiltrative (most RCCs have a pseudocapsule).
  • TFE3 fusion (Xp11 translocation) RCC is common in children: t(X;17) often indolent, t(X;1) may recur late in nodes.
  • WHO/ISUP grade 1–4, based on nuclear features.
  • P-glycoprotein (170 kDa efflux pump) in 80%–90% of RCCs pumps out cytotoxic drugs.

Hereditary syndromes

  • VHL disease: autosomal dominant tumour suppressor loss. Lost VHL stabilises HIF (hypoxia-inducible factor), upregulating VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor), so tumours are hypervascular.
  • VHL tumours: clear cell RCC, phaeochromocytoma, retinal angiomas, haemangioblastomas (brainstem, cerebellum, cord), plus inner ear, epididymis and pancreas; penetrance incomplete. RCC is now the leading cause of death.
  • VHL: treat all tumours in a kidney when the largest reaches 3 cm. Update: belzutifan (HIF-2α inhibitor) is approved for VHL-associated RCC.
  • Hereditary papillary RCC: autosomal dominant, activating MET mutation at 7q31 (receptor for hepatocyte growth factor); few non-renal tumours.
  • HLRCC (hereditary leiomyomatosis and RCC; fumarate hydratase): cutaneous and uterine leiomyomas; aggressive tumours, so operate early and widely.
  • BHD (Birt-Hogg-Dubé): facial fibrofolliculomas, lung cysts, pneumothorax, chromophobe RCC and oncocytoma.
  • Genetic counselling: all patients 46 years or younger, suspected SDH (succinate dehydrogenase) deficiency, suggestive history, relatives of known cases; not usually an elderly patient with multifocal discordant histologies.

Paraneoplastic syndromes

  • Hypercalcaemia (up to 13%; mainly PTH-related peptide, also vitamin D and prostaglandins, or bone lysis): hydrate, furosemide, bisphosphonates, steroids, calcitonin.
  • Others: hypertension, polycythaemia, Stauffer syndrome (non-metastatic liver dysfunction; if it persists after nephrectomy, disease persists).

Staging and prognosis (TNM 8th edition)

  • Renal vein, sinus or perinephric fat and collecting system invasion: T3a. Contiguous ipsilateral adrenal invasion: T4 (beyond Gerota). Non-contiguous or contralateral adrenal: M1. Any nodes: N1.
  • Medial (sinus fat) invasion is worse than lateral perinephric fat invasion; adrenal involvement is worse still.
  • Stage is the strongest single predictor; integrated nomograms (size, stage, grade, performance status, subtype) predict best. Age and comorbidity affect overall but not cancer-specific survival.
  • Worst signs: T4, nodes (N1) and metastases (M1); node involvement carries a dismal prognosis.
  • IVC (inferior vena cava) thrombus confined to the kidney: 31%–50% 5-year survival with surgery. Good if nodes negative and wall not invaded; very poor with wall invasion or nodal disease.
  • Small RCCs under 3.5 cm grow under 0.5 cm a year (some up to 1 cm).

Surgery for localised disease

  • Classic partial nephrectomy criteria: solitary tumour under 5 cm with a normal contralateral kidney.
  • Young patient with a peripheral exophytic lower-pole clear cell tumour: partial nephrectomy.
  • Margin width does not matter if the margin is negative.
  • Renal function after partial nephrectomy depends mainly on preoperative function and preserved vascularised parenchyma; prolonged warm ischaemia adds harm.
  • Renal vein thrombus up to the IVC junction in a large tumour: radical nephrectomy.
  • Ipsilateral adrenalectomy only for CT involvement, contiguous spread or large adjacent upper-pole tumours.
  • Lymphadenectomy gives staging, not proven therapeutic benefit; EORTC 30881 showed no survival gain. Extended dissection (interaortocaval and ipsilateral great-vessel nodes, crus to common iliac) is not better for most; still offered for bulky suspicious nodes.
  • Cryoablation needs rapid freezing, slow thawing and a double freeze–thaw cycle.
  • Thermal ablation: more local recurrence than partial nephrectomy, but similar control after repeat ablation; best under 3 cm; active surveillance is often better in the elderly.
  • Oncocytoma on biopsy in an elderly patient: observe with imaging every 6–12 months.

Renal function after surgery

  • Identify CKD (chronic kidney disease) by cause, eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate; MDRD or CKD-EPI) and albuminuria. Creatinine alone underestimates disease in thin elderly women. Measured GFR is costly; no urine test beats eGFR for screening.
  • After major nephron loss, check urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio yearly (proteinuria is the first sign of hyperfiltration injury).
  • CKD from medical causes raises cardiovascular events, hospitalisation and death; surgical CKD is more stable.
  • AUA 2017 nephrology referral: preoperative GFR under 45, confirmed proteinuria, diabetics with GFR under 60, or expected GFR under 30 after surgery. A patient with GFR 70 and equal split function having radical nephrectomy (new GFR about 40–42) need not be referred.

Follow-up

  • Recurrence risk is highest in the first 3 years. Low risk (pT1N0) after radical nephrectomy: chest X-ray yearly for 3 years.
  • Intermediate–high risk after partial nephrectomy: baseline CT or MRI at 3–6 months, then imaging every 6 months for at least 3 years and yearly to 5 years.

Metastatic and adjuvant treatment

  • Solitary metastasis (1.6%–3.2%): nephrectomy (partial if needed to avoid dialysis) with metastasectomy, possibly in one minimally invasive session.
  • Debulking alone is rarely the sole treatment.
  • Targeted drugs act on VEGF (sunitinib, sorafenib, axitinib, pazopanib, lenvatinib, cabozantinib) and mTOR (temsirolimus, everolimus); responses mainly in clear cell; non-clear cell care is undefined.
  • Pazopanib and axitinib are VEGF receptor TKIs (tyrosine kinase inhibitors). Nivolumab and pembrolizumab block PD-1 (activating immunity), atezolizumab blocks PD-L1, ipilimumab CTLA-4.
  • Adjuvant sunitinib (S-TRAC, 615 patients): longer disease-free survival (6.8 vs 5.6 years), more toxicity, no overall survival gain; other adjuvant agents failed. Update: adjuvant pembrolizumab (KEYNOTE-564) improved disease-free and overall survival and is now standard; first-line metastatic therapy is now immunotherapy-based combinations.

Benign and other renal tumours

  • Tuberous sclerosis: about 20% of AMLs; autosomal dominant; epilepsy, learning disability, adenoma sebaceum. Bleeding AML with TSC, bilateral disease or poor function: selective embolisation.
  • Cystic nephroma: benign, middle-aged women, Bosniak II–III without solid parts (related to MEST, mixed epithelial and stromal tumour, which looks solid or Bosniak IV).
  • Renal sarcoma: leiomyosarcoma commonest (50%–60%); in the retroperitoneum liposarcoma. Prognosis depends on margin and grade; pseudocapsule is not a safe plane; wide excision is essential.
  • Renal carcinoid has a good prognosis, unlike other rare renal tumours.
  • Metastases to the kidney occur in 12% of those dying of cancer: lung, breast, GI, melanoma, haematological.
  • An adrenal lesion bright on T2 MRI suggests phaeochromocytoma and should be removed with careful preparation.

Ch 115 Urinary Incontinence and Pelvic Prolapse: Epidemiology and Pathophysiology

Definitions and epidemiology

  • Urgency incontinence: leakage with urgency; DO (detrusor overactivity) need not be shown. Mixed incontinence: symptoms of both stress and urgency incontinence.
  • Incontinence affects 25%–40% of women, 10% weekly. Faecal incontinence in 17% of women with prolapse.
  • SUI (stress urinary incontinence) is commonest in young women; urgency and mixed become commoner with age. Continuous leakage is rare (fistula).
  • Age is strongly linked to all types; white women are at higher risk than Black and Asian women. OAB (overactive bladder) rises with age. 12% of men report terminal dribbling.
  • Risk factors include parity, obesity and others; caffeine is linked to urgency but mostly not to incontinence. Younger age at first pregnancy predicts later SUI.
  • Oral oestrogen is associated with incontinence; topical oestrogen is not linked to SUI.
  • Prolapse may mask incontinence. Only a vaginal bulge is consistently linked to prolapse; pressure and pain have other causes.

Continence mechanism

  • The female bladder neck is weaker than the male and often incompetent; most of the urethra is active sphincter, but the mid-urethra matters most.
  • Urethral mucosa and wall properties help continence. The anterior vaginal wall backs the urethra so it can be compressed; urethropelvic ligaments attach it laterally to the arcus tendineus.
  • VLPP (Valsalva leak point pressure) under 60 cmH2O suggests but does not confirm intrinsic sphincter deficiency.
  • Voiding: parasympathetic pelvic nerve active; sympathetic hypogastric and pudendal (Onuf nucleus) suppressed. A-delta afferents act during storage, closing the bladder neck reflexly.

POP-Q

  • Points are measured in cm from the hymen. Aa and Ap lie 3 cm inside the hymen, so they range only from −3 to +3 (Ap +4 is impossible). Ba and Bp mark the leading edge and can exceed +3. C is cervix or vault.
  • Stage by the leading edge: stage 1 more than 1 cm above the hymen; stage 2 within 1 cm above or below; stage 3 more than 1 cm beyond but not total; stage 4 complete eversion.
  • Posterior points beyond the hymen with C at the hymen: needs posterior repair plus apical support (e.g. posterior colporrhaphy and sacrospinous fixation).

Ch 156 Laparoscopic and Robotic-Assisted Radical Prostatectomy and Pelvic Lymphadenectomy

Patient selection and set-up

  • Contraindications: uncorrectable bleeding disorder, or unfit for general anaesthesia (severe cardiopulmonary disease).
  • Avoid complex cases early in experience (large gland, large median lobe, morbid obesity, previous pelvic surgery, radiation, TURP); these are not absolute contraindications and are best done in high-volume centres.
  • A skilled bedside assistant who knows the robot greatly helps.
  • Pneumoperitoneum with steep Trendelenburg: hypercarbia, acidosis, oliguria, corneal oedema, raised intraocular pressure, neurapraxia; pad pressure points.

Approach and technique

  • Transperitoneal vs extraperitoneal: surgeon preference; margins similar. Extraperitoneal avoids bowel handling, useful after major abdominal surgery.
  • The robot facilitates suturing and dissection, especially for surgeons without advanced laparoscopic skills.
  • Find the bladder neck–prostate junction with physical manoeuvres and visual cues; after dividing the anterior neck, check for a median lobe before the posterior neck.
  • Fully develop the plane between rectum and prostate (incise the posterior Denonvilliers layer) to avoid rectal injury and define pedicles and NVBs (neurovascular bundles).
  • The NVB lies between prostatic fascia and levator fascia; use interfascial dissection for nerve sparing in organ-confined cancer; minimise thermal energy near the nerves.
  • Antegrade approach divides pedicles early and the dorsal venous complex late, so less bleeding; retrograde does the reverse.
  • Watch for an anterior or posterior apical lip protruding under the urethra, to avoid an iatrogenic positive apical margin.
  • Bladder neck reconstruction may be needed for size mismatch (after TURP, median lobe, large gland).
  • Vesicourethral anastomosis: interrupted or running; a running suture with a single knot is most efficient.

Outcomes and complications

  • Blood loss is low because pneumoperitoneum tamponades venous sinuses; usually a few hundred mL, transfusion under 2%.
  • Positive margins fall with experience. Costs are driven by operative time, stay and disposables.
  • Book list: rectal injury rare (higher in salvage surgery), repair when seen; urine leak needs longer catheter; bladder neck stricture under 5%; machine failure under 0.5%.
  • Book: oncological outcomes equal to open, better margins in high-volume centres; potency better with robot; at least 20% not pad-free and 5% severely incontinent; discharge in 24 hours, normal activity in 3 weeks. Update: the randomised trial of robotic vs open prostatectomy (Yaxley, Lancet 2016) found similar urinary and sexual function at 12 weeks and 24 months.

Pelvic lymph node dissection

  • PLND (pelvic lymph node dissection) is advised for intermediate- or high-risk disease or nodal risk over 5% on a nomogram; laparoscopic yield equals open.
  • Extended PLND gives more nodes, but its benefit is debated against extra time and complications.
  • Lymphocele: clip visible lymphatics; transperitoneal approach does not prevent it; drain symptomatic ones (venous or bladder compression) percutaneously or laparoscopically.

Ch 39 Renal Dysgenesis and Cystic Disease of the Kidney

Dysgenesis and hypoplasia

  • Potter facies: hypertelorism, prominent inner canthal folds, recessive chin.
  • Dysplasia is histological: embryonic immature mesenchyme and primitive renal elements; often linked to ureteric bud abnormality and/or obstruction.
  • Hypoplasia: fewer than normal calyces and nephrons, with no dysplasia. Oligomeganephronia: fewer nephrons with hypertrophy of the rest; many reach renal failure.
  • Ask-Upmark kidney (segmental hypoplasia): mainly young women and girls; often with reflux; hallmark is severe hypertension (headache, encephalopathy, retinopathy); no renal artery intimal disease proven.

Multicystic dysplastic kidney (MCDK)

  • No identifiable normal parenchyma; commoner on the left at any age; unilateral MCDK commoner in males (2.4:1).
  • Large series: no increased risk of hypertension or malignancy.
  • Contralateral VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) 18%–43%, commoner than contralateral UPJ (ureteropelvic junction) obstruction.
  • US versus hydronephrosis: MCDK cysts are haphazard, varied in size, with small cysts between large ones, no large central/medial cyst and no communications. UPJ obstruction: calyces arranged peripherally, connecting to a central pelvis, no small interposed cysts; an identifiable renal sinus favours hydronephrosis.

Multilocular cyst, simple cysts and sinus cysts

  • Benign multilocular cyst (some prefer "cystic nephroma", implying benign neoplasm). Spectrum: benign multilocular cyst, then multilocular cyst with Wilms nodules, then cystic Wilms (malignant). Adults also have multilocular cystic RCC, so remove multilocular cystic lesions.
  • Age: presents before 4 years or after 30 years (only 5% between); under 4 it is 2× likelier male, over 30 8× likelier female.
  • Simple cysts increase with age: about 20% by 40 years and about 33% after 60 (CT). Fetal renal cysts usually resolve before birth (25 of 28; one persisting was early MCDK).
  • Simple cyst on US: no internal echoes; sharp thin smooth wall; good through-transmission with posterior acoustic enhancement; spherical/ovoid. CT: thin smooth wall, round/ovoid, homogeneous.
  • Renal sinus cysts are mostly lymphatic in origin.

Genetic syndromes

  • TSC (tuberous sclerosis complex): chromosomes 9 (TSC1) and 16 (TSC2). Angiomyolipoma in 40%–80%; RCC about 2%. Diagnosis: 2 major features (e.g. renal angiomyolipoma plus cardiac rhabdomyoma; others: facial angiofibroma, ungual fibroma, ≥3 hypomelanotic macules, shagreen patch, retinal hamartomas, cortical tuber, subependymal nodule or giant cell astrocytoma, lymphangioleiomyomatosis) or 1 major plus 2 minor (multiple renal cysts, non-renal hamartoma, rectal polyps, retinal achromic patch, white-matter migration lines, bone cysts, gingival fibromas, confetti skin, enamel pits).
  • VHL (von Hippel-Lindau): autosomal dominant, chromosome 3; cerebellar and retinal haemangioblastomas; cysts of pancreas, kidney and epididymis; epididymal cystadenoma; phaeochromocytoma; clear cell RCC (35%–38% in book). Renal cysts (76%), not RCC, are the commonest and earliest renal finding. VHL mutation is present in about 50% of sporadic clear cell RCC.
  • VHL tumours are multiple, bilateral and recurrent: aim is control and nephron preservation, not cure. Nephron-sparing surgery is standard for low-grade tumours (e.g. bilateral upper pole tumours: bilateral partial nephrectomy); recurrence 75%–85% but 10-year disease-specific survival 81%–94% (versus about 50% historically after nephrectomy); high-grade disease may need bilateral nephrectomy; ablation under study. Update: belzutifan (HIF-2α inhibitor) is FDA-approved (2021) for VHL-associated RCC not needing immediate surgery.

Medullary disorders and acquired cystic disease

  • Juvenile NPH (nephronophthisis): usually autosomal recessive (chromosome 2), ESRD at mean 13 years, almost always before 25; 20% of families have extrarenal features (e.g. retinitis pigmentosa). MCKD (medullary cystic kidney disease): usually autosomal dominant, milder, renal only, ESRD in 3rd–4th decade, present in all by 50. Both cause polyuria and polydipsia in over 80% (less than diabetes insipidus). Shared histology triad: tubular basement membrane thickening and disintegration; tubular atrophy with cysts; interstitial inflammation and fibrosis.
  • A child with this histology (interstitial nephritis-fibrosis, atrophic tubules, glomerular microcysts) has NPH: follow for hypertension and declining renal function.
  • Medullary sponge kidney: dilated collecting ducts; hypercalciuria (one third), hypocitraturia and stones.
  • ARCD (acquired renal cystic disease): related to duration of renal failure (a feature of ESRD), as common on peritoneal dialysis and even without dialysis. Presents with loin pain and/or haematuria; bleeding in up to 50%.
  • Bilateral congenital absence of the vas: cystic fibrosis. Unilateral absence: may have ipsilateral renal agenesis.

Ch 74 Sexual Function and Dysfunction in the Female

Physiology and concepts

  • Sexual health includes absence of dysfunction, mental well-being and human development and maturation; optimal health needs physical, emotional and mental well-being.
  • Acetylcholine has only a minor role in female genital response (VIP and nitric oxide matter more).
  • Vaginal pH 4–5, from lactic acid–producing flora.
  • Testosterone comes from ovaries and adrenals and declines gradually through life (oestrogen and progesterone fall abruptly at menopause).
  • Oestrogen keeps genital tissue thick and intact; deficiency impairs function through less vasocongestion and lubrication and more atrophy, not directly through desire.
  • Sexual neutrality (receptive rather than initiating) is normal; women may follow a circular or linear response model.
  • G-spot: anterior vaginal wall at the midurethra (Grafenberg described it as urethral); its anatomy remains controversial.

Evaluation

  • History is critical; assess the intimate relationship. Women with spinal cord injury can have orgasm. Up to half of women who have sex with women do not identify as lesbian or bisexual.
  • Laboratory tests are rarely needed unless a specific medical cause is suspected. Random testosterone is useless (no female normal range; levels do not correlate with libido). If a hormonal cause is suspected, screen prolactin, thyroid and adrenal function; DHEA-S if adrenal insufficiency is suspected.
  • Biothesiometry is rarely needed; history and examination give the same information. Vaginal photoplethysmography (vaginal pulse amplitude) is the most studied and validated physiological test.
  • FSFI (Female Sexual Function Index): 19 items, 6 domains (desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, pain), past 4 weeks, 10–15 minutes; range 2–36; under 26.55 means at risk of dysfunction; validated in research, cancer and pelvic pain; a 6-item version screens pre- and postmenopausal women. It does not measure distress.

DSM-5 classification and prevalence

  • DSM-5 merged hypoactive desire and arousal disorders into female sexual interest/arousal disorder, and dyspareunia plus vaginismus into genitopelvic pain/penetration disorder; FOD (female orgasmic disorder) carried over.
  • Criteria (except genitopelvic pain disorder): present 6 months, on at least 75% of occasions, causing clinically significant distress, not due to another mental disorder, relationship distress, stressors, drugs or illness.
  • FOD no longer requires a "normal excitement phase"; it often coexists with other problems (arousal 31%, lubrication 18%, desire 14%, pain 12%, vaginismus 0.9%); questionnaires alone cannot diagnose it but track response.
  • PRESIDE study (31,531 respondents): low desire 37.7%; distressing low desire (HSDD) 8.9% at 18–44, 12.3% at 45–64, 7.4% over 65. The book keys arousal disorder as most prevalent; Update: its own PRESIDE data show low desire is the most prevalent problem.
  • PGAD (persistent genital arousal disorder): spontaneous, intrusive, unwanted genital arousal without desire, lasting hours to days, not relieved by orgasm, triggered by sexual, non-sexual or no stimulus, distressing.
  • PVD (provoked vestibulodynia): primary (pain from first penetration attempt; early onset, longer duration, family history, enuresis, dysmenorrhoea; more nerve fibres and thicker vestibule; less inflammation; commoner in Hispanic women) versus secondary (acquired after pain-free period; larger area, worst at clitoral hood; commoner, 65% vs 35%). Pelvic floor hypertonicity is common.

Causes

  • Low desire: antidepressants, life stressors, hypoestrogenism, relationship problems. Depression is the one unambiguously linked condition.
  • Hormonal contraception, prolactin, surgical menopause and raised SHBG (sex hormone–binding globulin) lower bioavailable testosterone; this is the main proposed mechanism for contraceptive-related problems.
  • Incontinent women are up to 3 times more likely to have low arousal, infrequent orgasm and dyspareunia.
  • SSRIs inhibit desire, arousal and orgasm (30%–70% report dysfunction).

Treatment

  • Treat only bothersome problems (e.g. distressing loss of desire); not climaxing with penetration alone is normal; lubricant safely manages dryness.
  • Antidepressant-associated dysfunction: evidence for adjunctive antidepressant, cessation, drug holiday and sildenafil; reassurance has least evidence.
  • Muscle relaxants help sexual pain but not arousal.
  • Testosterone may help some women; low testosterone is not clearly linked to worse function in all women. Adverse effects: hirsutism, acne, lower HDL, vaginal bleeding; cardiovascular and cancer risks uncertain. Systemic testosterone (± oestrogen) and tibolone improve desire, arousal and orgasm in postmenopausal women with low hormone levels.
  • Flibanserin 100 mg at bedtime: postsynaptic 5-HT1A agonist and 5-HT2A antagonist, lowers serotonin and raises dopamine and noradrenaline; for premenopausal acquired generalised HSDD. Update: bremelanotide (melanocortin agonist, on-demand injection) was also FDA-approved in 2019, so flibanserin is no longer the only approved drug.

Ch 77 Surgery of Testicular Tumors

Orchiectomy and staging

  • Radical inguinal orchiectomy: the ilioinguinal nerve lies on the cord immediately under the external oblique (injury: numb medial thigh and scrotum); mobilise the cord 1–2 cm above the internal ring and ligate vas and vessels separately with silk so the stump can be found at RPLND (retroperitoneal lymph node dissection).
  • After scrotal orchiectomy: low-stage seminoma, extend radiation fields to groin and scrotum; low-stage NSGCT (non-seminomatous germ cell tumour), excise scrotal scar with cord remnant; after full platinum chemotherapy, remove only the cord stump at RPLND.
  • Partial orchiectomy suits a suspected benign tumour (also solitary testis or bilateral tumours); not for a large tumour with a normal contralateral testis.
  • Staging: orchiectomy (T), chest X-ray or CT, CT/MRI abdomen-pelvis (N, M), post-orchiectomy markers (S). PET has no role in initial staging; it is used for post-chemotherapy seminoma residual masses over 3 cm.
  • Lymphatics: right testis to interaortocaval, then paracaval/precaval nodes; left to para-aortic and preaortic nodes. Crossover is commoner from right-sided tumours.

Clinical stage I and primary RPLND

  • Clinical stage I: 20%–30% have positive nodes; about 25% relapse on surveillance, mostly within 2 years, rarely after 5. Risk factors: LVI (lymphovascular invasion), higher T stage, cord/capsule/scrotal involvement, high embryonal carcinoma percentage. Absence of teratoma in the primary does not exclude it in the retroperitoneum.
  • Persistently raised markers after orchiectomy need chemotherapy, not RPLND. Best RPLND candidates: stage IIA and low-volume IIB under 3 cm.
  • Suprahilar metastases are rare in low-stage NSGCT; 3%–23% of node-positive patients have extra-template disease; commonest residual suprahilar site is retrocrural.
  • Bilateral template is standard for pathological stage II and for documented or suspected metastases; nerve fibres may be preserved selectively.
  • After primary RPLND: reliable pN1 patients can be observed; pN2 is debated (2 cycles adjuvant or observation). No other histological variable reliably predicts relapse, except teratoma-only nodes, which have very low relapse and are observed. Observed pathological IIA–B patients relapse in 30%–50%. Adjuvant BEP×2 or EP×2 nearly eliminates relapse but does not improve cancer-specific or overall survival (about 100% either way); relapse needs full BEP×3 or EP×4.

Surgical anatomy and technique

  • Ureters mark the lateral borders of the para-aortic and paracaval packets; find the ureter at the lower pole, passing behind the gonadal vein; it crosses anterior to the common iliac artery.
  • Three paired lumbar arteries lie between renal hilum and aortic bifurcation in nearly all patients (sometimes a short common trunk); lumbar veins and the 4 postganglionic sympathetic roots per side vary; node counts vary widely.
  • Splitting over the aorta first lets you see accessory right lower pole renal arteries, but risks sympathetic fibres; stop at the IMA (inferior mesenteric artery) and identify efferents first. The split over the IVC is unlikely to harm nerves.
  • Antegrade ejaculation (emission and bladder neck closure) needs L1–L4 postganglionic sympathetic fibres; identify them before dissecting on the aorta. Erection is parasympathetic and not needed for ejaculation.
  • Cisterna chyli lies posteromedial to the aorta just above the right renal artery: clip or tie lymphatics there, at the top of the packets and in retrocrural dissection, to prevent chylous ascites.
  • If the aortic wall is stripped of adventitia, replace with a graft (delayed rupture). With complete or near-complete IVC occlusion, routine caval reconstruction is not needed.

Post-chemotherapy RPLND (PC-RPLND)

  • Indicated for NSGCT residual masses over 1 cm; may be omitted if the retroperitoneum was never involved. Resect with a complete bilateral dissection. SMA, coeliac or porta hepatis involvement usually precludes resection.
  • Residual mass histology: after first-line chemotherapy necrosis/fibrosis 40%, teratoma 45%, viable GCT 15%; after salvage chemotherapy viable 50%, teratoma 40%, fibrosis 10%. Teratoma is unpredictable, so resect completely.
  • Rising AFP/hCG after first-line chemotherapy usually means unresectable viable tumour: salvage chemotherapy (standard or high-dose), not surgery.
  • Desperation PC-RPLND (declining or plateaued AFP, slowly rising AFP after complete serological response, all chemotherapy options exhausted): 53.9% cancer-specific survival at 6 years.
  • Modified unilateral template candidates: well-defined mass ≤5 cm in the primary landing zone before and after chemotherapy, normal markers, IGCCCG (International Germ Cell Cancer Collaborative Group) good or intermediate risk. Results: in-field recurrence 0%–1%, cancer-specific survival 98%–100%, antegrade ejaculation in over 85%.
  • Auxiliary procedures in 23%–45%, most often nephrectomy then vascular. Nephrectomy (5%–31%) is likelier with salvage, desperation, reoperative or late-relapse surgery, large masses and left primaries.
  • Residual liver lesions: about 75% necrosis only; 94% concordance with retroperitoneal necrosis, so observe them if retroperitoneal necrosis would mean morbid hepatic resection. Retroperitoneal necrosis predicts necrotic chest lesions 89% of the time.
  • Pelvic disease is rare except after groin surgery, repeat RPLND or late relapse (Indiana: 134 of 2722; 14% prior groin surgery, 98% prior chemotherapy, 19.4% prior RPLND, 24% late relapse).
  • Higher post-surgery recurrence: somatic-type malignancy and mediastinal primary. Immature teratoma has no worse outcome than mature. Viable GCT at PC-RPLND: survival is predicted by completeness of resection, proportion viable and IGCCCG risk (Fizazi); prior salvage chemotherapy is worst.
  • No benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy after salvage chemotherapy with viable GCT, teratoma only or somatic transformation.
  • Seminoma residual masses rarely contain teratoma and are hard to remove: observe under 3 cm; PET if over 3 cm; viable seminoma means more chemotherapy or RPLND.

Reoperation and late relapse

  • Retroperitoneal recurrence after RPLND usually reflects technical failure (landing-zone recurrence, unligated lumbars, unresected gonadal vein, disease behind great vessels). Pelvic relapse does not, as pelvic nodes are not in the template.
  • Late relapse (2%–4%, over half beyond 10 years) needs long follow-up. After prior chemotherapy it is often yolk sac tumour and chemorefractory, so resect resectable disease; chemotherapy-naive late relapse responds better to chemotherapy.

Complications and fertility

  • Bleomycin causes restrictive pulmonary fibrosis and fluid sensitivity; postoperative ARDS (acute respiratory distress syndrome) is rare, likelier after 4 cycles and with pulmonary disease; risks are high FiO2 and high-volume fluids/blood (Trendelenburg is not a factor).
  • Chylous ascites is usually transient: drain, MCT (medium-chain triglyceride) diet, then TPN, ± octreotide; peritoneovenous shunt last. Preoperative fat restriction does not prevent it.
  • Spinal cord ischaemia is very rare; risk if more than 3 sequential lumbar artery pairs are divided (bulky para-aortic, retrocrural and mediastinal disease).
  • Platinum chemotherapy brings a sevenfold rise in cardiovascular complications.
  • Infertility or subfertility is present at diagnosis in 20%–60%; 60% have subnormal semen; offer sperm banking before RPLND. 65% on surveillance father children after orchiectomy. Spermatogenesis may take 3 years to recover after chemotherapy.

Ch 90 Upper Urinary Tract Trauma

Renal trauma: evaluation

  • Suspect blunt renal injury with a significant flank blow, lower rib fractures, flank ecchymosis or a fall from height. Rapid deceleration injures fixed points: UPJ and renal hilum (renal artery intimal tear).
  • Gross haematuria is the best indicator of significant injury, but its absence does not exclude it, and degree of haematuria does not track severity.
  • Children: higher risk of renal injury, often stay normotensive despite major blood loss, more renal anomalies.
  • Image: all penetrating trauma; rapid deceleration; blunt trauma with gross haematuria; blunt with microhaematuria and hypotension; children with haematuria. Adults with microhaematuria and no shock can be observed.
  • CT with IV contrast plus delayed images at 10–15 minutes (to show collecting system and ureter). Deep laceration in a stable patient: get delayed images; no surgery or embolisation needed.
  • CT signs of major injury: medial laceration, large perinephric haematoma, intravascular contrast extravasation, medial urinary extravasation, devitalised fragments. Absent enhancement of the whole kidney means main renal artery occlusion (intimal injury).

Renal trauma: management

  • Non-operative management is preferred in stable patients, especially grades I–III. Update: AAST (American Association for the Surgery of Trauma) 2018 grading adds vascular injury and active bleeding; most grade IV–V injuries in stable patients are also managed non-operatively or with angioembolisation.
  • Low-velocity renal gunshot wounds: may be observed if stable, contained haematoma, isolated parenchymal injury and no intra-abdominal injury.
  • Absolute indications for exploration: haemodynamic instability with shock, expanding or pulsatile haematoma, suspected pedicle avulsion, uncontained retroperitoneal haematoma. Relative: large perinephric haematoma, intravascular extravasation, devitalised fragments, arterial thrombosis, collecting system extravasation.
  • Damage control in unstable patients: control bleeding and faecal/urinary leak only; for the ureter, ligate plus nephrostomy, externalise or stent; repair later.
  • Post-injury hypertension is usually short-lived; also from vascular injury or Page kidney (subcapsular haematoma compression).

Ureteral injury

  • Causes: persisting with stone basketing after a ureteral tear (stop and stent); gynaecological laparoscopy, especially electrosurgical or laser lysis of endometriosis; aortic grafts near the ureter (periureteral inflammation, stenosis).
  • Ureteroscopic injury: stent; if not possible or inadequate, nephrostomy. Delayed injury in a stable patient: retrograde urogram is the best test.
  • Repair principles: debride to bleeding edges; spatulated, tension-free, stented, watertight anastomosis; closed suction drain; isolate the repair with omentum or peritoneum (from colon or vascular graft).
  • Timing: repair within a week or defer 6 weeks or more.
  • Upper and mid ureter: ureteroureterostomy (up to 32% of a large series; success up to 90%); gap bridged depends on mobilisation and elasticity. Distal: reimplant (psoas hitch or Boari flap for long gaps; ureteroureterostomy in selected cases).
  • High-velocity gunshot contusion: excise and do ureteroureterostomy (blast injury causes necrosis or stricture). Low-velocity contusion: stent.
  • Transureteroureterostomy is contraindicated with a history of stones (a distal stone would endanger both kidneys).

Ch 119 Nocturia

Definitions

  • Nocturia: voiding preceded and followed by sleep; prevalence rises with age; impairs sleep (efficiency, latency, slow-wave sleep), linked to falls and mortality. Two or more voids a night cause bother and reduced QoL (quality of life).
  • 20% of people aged 20–40 void twice or more at night versus 60% of those 70 or older. Younger people more often have reduced nocturnal bladder capacity; older people overproduce urine.
  • NUV (nocturnal urine volume): all urine passed during the night plus the first morning void.
  • Nocturnal polyuria: NPi (nocturnal polyuria index = NUV ÷ 24-hour volume) over 0.20 in young adults and over 0.33 over 65 years, with normal 24-hour output; other definitions NUV over 0.9 mL/min (54 mL/h) or over 1.5 mL/min (90 mL/h); no universal definition. Global polyuria: over 40 mL/kg per 24 hours.
  • Frequency-volume chart: record bedtime and waking time (and volumes) to count nocturnal voids and NUV; fluid intake is helpful but not essential.

Pathophysiology

  • Fluid third-spacing returning to circulation when lying down; higher supine arterial pressure; loss of the normal nocturnal rise in ADH (antidiuretic hormone). Obstructive sleep apnoea is a common cause.
  • ADH is inhibited (causing diuresis) by PGE2, ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide), hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, lithium and tetracyclines (not hyperkalaemia).

Treatment

  • Desmopressin acts on V2 receptors in collecting ducts: raises water permeability and reabsorption, dilutes extracellular fluid, concentrates urine. Best for nocturnal polyuria.
  • Hyponatraemia risk: women are more sensitive (lower doses); over 65, monitor sodium closely and avoid with baseline hyponatraemia.
  • At 6–12 months nocturia fell 7% with watchful waiting, 17% with α-blockers, 32% with TUMT and 75% with TURP; QoL gains followed nocturia reduction. α-Blockers, 5-ARIs, antimuscarinics and combinations reduce episodes significantly but with minimal clinical value.

Ch 125 Slings: Autologous, Biologic, Synthetic and Midurethral

Theory and evaluation

  • Integral theory: pubourethral ligaments, suburethral vaginal hammock and pubococcygeus preserve continence; injury from surgery, childbirth, ageing or hormone loss impairs midurethral function.
  • Female urethra has four layers: the middle muscular layer keeps resting closure and the outer seromuscular layer augments it; fast-twitch sphincter fibres give sudden protection and slow-twitch fibres passive guarding. Levator ani, urethropelvic ligament and pubocervical fascia support the bladder neck; the round ligament supports the uterus. Maximum urethral closure pressure is at the midurethra.
  • ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency) is considered the primary cause of SUI (stress urinary incontinence); hypermobility is secondary (many hypermobile women are dry).
  • AUA: urinalysis and PVR (post-void residual) in all; imaging not routine; do a focused neurological examination. Index patient options (book: all strongly recommended): synthetic MUS (midurethral sling), autologous PVS (pubovaginal sling), Burch, bulking agents.
  • Obese women should first be offered weight loss; trocar bladder injury is commoner in non-obese women.

Pubovaginal (bladder neck) slings

  • Slings are the procedure of choice for female SUI and are particularly useful for ISD. PVS is placed at the bladder neck.
  • PVS indications: ISD with hypermobility, SUI with cystocele, with diverticulum, after MUS urethral damage, with urethral defects needing reconstruction (e.g. fistula), neurogenic SUI. Avoid PVS when low outlet resistance coexists with poor compliance: treat compliance first (risk to upper tracts).
  • Materials: ideal graft is biocompatible with fibroblast ingrowth and neovascularisation; autologous tissue has the lowest erosion and infection; synthetic materials degrade least and show most ingrowth; freeze-dried fascia lata has lower stiffness and load to failure than autologous, solvent-dehydrated and dermal grafts; porcine SIS is weaker than cadaveric fascia; allograft HIV risk 1 in 1,667,600 and CJD 1 in 3.5 million. Synthetic material is no longer used for bladder neck slings (perforation 15 times and vaginal exposure 14 times commoner; urethral perforation presents at mean 9 months with retention, urgency, mixed leakage).
  • Tension the sling after removing the weighted speculum; close the abdomen after tensioning.
  • Cure 24%–97%; no factor predicts outcome. SISTEr: PVS cured more than Burch but caused more UTI, voiding difficulty and urge incontinence.
  • Voiding dysfunction after continence surgery 2.5%–35%; urgency/urge leakage (8%–25%) is commoner than retention and is the commonest cause of dissatisfaction. Transient retention usually resolves within 10 days; wait about 3 months before surgery after autologous PVS (shorter after MUS). First conservative: catheter, CIC, timed or double voiding, biofeedback, pelvic floor training, anticholinergics. Urodynamics do not predict outcome but help diagnose obstruction; recurrent SUI after urethrolysis 0%–18%.
  • Obstruction may present with retention, urgency, irritative symptoms, recurrent UTI, suprapubic pain or painful voiding. Subclinical impaired contractility, dysfunctional voiding and habitual straining raise risk; most needing CIC after PVS had a neurogenic bladder.

Midurethral slings

  • TVT (tension-free vaginal tape): retropubic trocars (suprapubic or vaginal approach), type 1 macroporous monofilament mesh, loose tension, no suture fixation, cystoscopy mandatory. MUS works by stabilising the posterior urethral wall (anterior/anteroinferior motion and lumen narrowing).
  • Success needs urethral mobility; lower with fixed urethra or low leak-point pressure. Durable: 5-, 7- and over 10-year results match 1-year results; better than laparoscopic colposuspension (open and laparoscopic Burch similar); less voiding dysfunction than colposuspension or PVS; mixed incontinence results acceptable but lower.
  • Elderly: more postoperative urgency, but similar cure, satisfaction, mixed incontinence resolution and retention. Salvage MUS: efficacy similar to primary, possibly more bladder perforation, less success with less mobility. With prolapse surgery: safe and effective, but tensioning for occult SUI is hard.
  • Recurrent SUI: meta-analysis of 10 RCTs found no difference in subjective cure between retropubic and transobturator MUS. Recurrent SUI after MUS: evidence poor (no trial met Cochrane criteria); urodynamics and cystoscopy help; bulking is inferior to repeat MUS; tightening unsupported. Periurethral bulking has limited success.
  • Transobturator sling: passes obturator internus, obturator membrane and obturator externus (not rectus fascia); stays above the perineal membrane, outside the true pelvis, not through levator ani; adductor longus tendon passage causes pain; gracilis and adductor brevis may be traversed; obturator vessels lie superolateral; dorsal clitoral nerve 1–2 cm away. Outside-in or inside-out both acceptable; cystoscopy still needed (bladder, urethra, even ureter can be injured). Outcomes similar to retropubic, also with ISD; less hypermobility lowers success. More thigh/groin pain, which lasts longer (most resolves by day 2).
  • Dorsal clitoral nerves cross under the pubis about 1.4 cm from midline: keep retropubic trocars at least 2 cm lateral.

MUS complications

  • Bladder perforation up to about 5%; voiding dysfunction 4%–20%; de novo urgency up to 12%; wound problems about 1%. Trocar injury is commoner with retropubic slings (case reports with transobturator too). Bowel and vascular injury can be lethal; perivesical vessel bleeding.
  • Haematoma over 5 cm in 12.9% (Tseng); nearly all resolve; bleeding mostly with retropubic slings.
  • Wound infection: vaginal 2% (Ward) and 0.7% (Richter); necrotising fasciitis linked to obesity (88%), hypertension (65%) and diabetes (47%).
  • De novo dyspareunia 3%–14.5%; sexual function may improve (less coital leakage) or worsen; sling removal can relieve dyspareunia.
  • MUS obstruction is usually transient; manage conservatively for days to a month, then release; midline incision of the sling is enough. Release resolves symptoms with continence kept in most.
  • Exposure and perforation are reduced by macroporous monofilament mesh (rates under 5%–10%); tension may still matter; they lower satisfaction. Remove or cover all exposed material (endoscopic removal possible if complete; small vaginal exposures may heal with oestrogen or closure); mesh in urethra or bladder must be removed. For urethral perforation use an inverted-U vaginal incision (exposure plus non-overlapping flap), not midline.
  • IUGA/ICS terms: synthetic mesh is a prosthesis, biological material a graft; mesh in the urinary tract is perforation; through skin or vagina is exposure.
  • FDA: 2011 panel deemed MUS safe and effective; 2012 ordered 522 post-market studies for prolapse mesh and single-incision slings; 2016 reclassified transvaginal prolapse mesh to class III (PMA required); full-length MUS and sacrocolpopexy mesh remain class II. Update: in 2019 the FDA ordered sale of transvaginal prolapse mesh to stop.

Ch 162 Treatment of Castration-Resistant Prostate Cancer

Concepts and monitoring

  • "Castration-resistant" replaced "hormone-resistant" because ADT is first-line, the AR (androgen receptor) still drives growth at castrate testosterone (under 50 ng/dL), further AR-targeted therapy still works, and castration resistance is not hormone resistance.
  • Castration resistance is defined by PSA or disease progression on primary and secondary hormonal therapy, found by careful clinical, radiological and PSA monitoring, not by Gleason score or extent. Most men on hormones eventually progress and need chemotherapy.
  • Androgen ablation induces apoptosis. The AR can be activated by oestrogens, progestins, growth factors and cytokines; AR signalling is upregulated in CRPC. These patients are not androgen-independent: continue ADT.
  • PSADT (PSA doubling time) predicts bone scan progression and survival; under 3 months means a rapid course. Progression-free survival is a better endpoint than response rate; PSA may not reflect drug efficacy (circulating tumour cells may be better).

Chemotherapy

  • Docetaxel every 3 weeks (up to 10 cycles) with prednisone is first-line chemotherapy: longer survival and better QoL than mitoxantrone, regardless of age, performance status or pain; fit patients without visceral disease and with normal haemoglobin and LDH often survive over 2 years. Toxicity: fatigue, myelosuppression, neuropathy, lacrimation, nail changes. Do not stop for a PSA rise in the first 3–4 cycles (early flare).
  • Cabazitaxel (active in docetaxel-resistant models): TROPIC showed longer survival than mitoxantrone after docetaxel, including early progressors; survival over 12 months possible; less neuropathy, fatigue, lacrimation and nail change but more neutropenic fever and diarrhoea than docetaxel.

AR-targeted therapy

  • Abiraterone (CYP17 inhibitor) blocks adrenal and intratumoural androgen synthesis; benefits before and after docetaxel (more so before). Causes mineralocorticoid excess (hypertension, hypokalaemia), so given with prednisone (occasionally a mineralocorticoid antagonist); also fatigue, abnormal liver tests, possible cardiac toxicity, drug interactions.
  • Enzalutamide: greater AR affinity than flutamide, bicalutamide, nilutamide and blocks nuclear translocation and DNA binding; superior to placebo.
  • M0 CRPC: apalutamide and enzalutamide approved, not abiraterone (M1 only); enzalutamide also for M1 CRPC. Update: darolutamide is also approved for M0 CRPC (ARAMIS); PARP inhibitors (e.g. olaparib, PROfound) for HRR-mutated mCRPC and Lu-177-PSMA-617 (VISION) after ARPI and taxane are now standard options.

Immunotherapy and radiopharmaceuticals

  • Sipuleucel-T (autologous vaccine): for asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic metastatic CRPC; very safe; no symptom relief, PSA fall or delay in progression, but prolongs survival versus placebo.
  • Radium-223 (α-emitter): survival benefit over best supportive care; α particles are about 7000 times heavier than β, need 1–2 hits to kill, travel under 100 µm, so marrow toxicity is low. For bone metastases with haemoglobin over 10 g/dL (book writes g/L) and nodes under 3 cm; approved with or without prior docetaxel. β-emitters strontium-89 and samarium-153 palliate pain but can cause severe myelotoxicity.

Complications of advanced disease

  • Back pain with bone metastases: assess for cord or root compression; give high-dose dexamethasone, get early MRI, then radiotherapy or decompression. Do not assume stability from PSA or bone scan; systemic therapy does not treat compression.
  • Cord compression in a man not yet castrated: immediate orchiectomy, or aminoglutethimide or ketoconazole with high-dose steroids; consider laminectomy and radiotherapy. Update: a GnRH antagonist (degarelix) gives rapid castration without flare; aminoglutethimide is obsolete.
  • Bone metastases are usually blastic; hypercalcaemia is rare. Bisphosphonates or denosumab reduce skeletal events, with calcium and vitamin D.
  • Neuroendocrine/anaplastic transformation: rapid visceral and brain spread, low or no PSA (PSA stain and AR often negative), hormone-resistant; biopsy changes management. Small-cell tumours get platinum chemotherapy; non-small-cell anaplastic tumours taxanes.

Development and prepuce

  • DHT (dihydrotestosterone) acting on androgen receptors at 9–13 weeks turns the genital tubercle, genital folds and genital swellings into glans, penile shaft and scrotum.
  • Primary phimosis resolves spontaneously in most boys: 90% retractable by 3 years; fewer than 1% have phimosis at 17 years.
  • Reasons to help retraction: persistent primary phimosis, secondary phimosis, balanitis, posthitis, BXO (balanitis xerotica obliterans) and UTI (urinary tract infection).
  • Topical corticosteroids relax the phimotic ring in 70%–80%, but they do not resolve penile adhesions.
  • BXO is a chronic infiltrative, scarring (cicatrix) skin condition. Steroids help only mild preputial BXO; circumcision is the preferred treatment.

Circumcision

  • The AAP (American Academy of Pediatrics) says benefits justify access to newborn circumcision and third-party payment.
  • Benefits: fewer penile cancers, STIs (sexually transmitted infections) including HIV, UTIs, balanitis and phimosis.
  • Give local or topical anaesthesia routinely in neonates. Revisions need general anaesthesia.
  • Defer it if the newborn has a penile anomaly that may need surgery: hypospadias, penile curvature, dorsal hood, buried or webbed penis.
  • Complications occur in 0.2%–5%. Bleeding is the commonest (about 0.1%, more in older boys). Meatal stenosis occurs only in the circumcised penis.

Penile anomalies

  • Penile agenesis (aphallia, failed genital tubercle) occurs in 1 in 10–30 million births. It is almost always 46,XY, with a normal scrotum, descended testes and an anteriorly displaced anus. Associations: cryptorchidism, VUR (vesicoureteral reflux), horseshoe kidney, renal agenesis, imperforate anus, skeletal and cardiopulmonary defects (not UPJ obstruction).
  • Buried penis has three causes: poor penopubic skin fixation, obesity, and a trapped penis from a cicatrix after surgery (usually circumcision). It is a normal-length penis, so not micropenis.
  • A trapped penis in a young child: office dilation of the cicatrix under local anaesthesia, or topical betamethasone with manual retraction.
  • Stretched length is measured from the pubis to the glans tip with the fat pad fully depressed. A term neonate should be at least 1.9 cm (2.5 SD below the mean of 4.0 cm).
  • Micropenis: a normally formed penis at least 2.5 SD below the mean stretched length (under 1.9 cm in neonates). The length-to-girth ratio is usually normal; the testes are often small and undescended and the scrotum small.
  • Causes: hypogonadotropic hypogonadism is commonest (low GnRH: Prader-Willi, Kallmann, Laurence-Moon-Biedl, CHARGE), then hypergonadotropic hypogonadism.
  • Work-up: karyotype, LH and FSH, testosterone before and after hCG stimulation, anterior pituitary tests and brain MRI. Most adults with micropenis have male identity and satisfactory sexual function.
  • Congenital penile naevi are superficial and benign; they are excised.
  • Penoscrotal transposition usually accompanies perineal, scrotal or penoscrotal hypospadias with chordee. It is linked to caudal regression, sex chromosome anomalies and Aarskog syndrome. Up to 75% with complete transposition have significant urinary tract anomalies (renal agenesis, dysplasia).

Priapism in boys (see Ch 70 for full details)

  • Types are ischaemic (low flow), non-ischaemic (arterial, high flow) and stuttering. Sickle cell disease is the commonest cause in children.
  • Sickle cell low-flow priapism (book): hydration, oxygen, alkalinisation, analgesia and transfusion, with aspiration, irrigation and α-adrenergic (not β) injection concurrently or after failure; shunts if these fail. Update: AUA/SMSNA say intracavernous treatment should not wait for systemic therapy.
  • High-flow priapism usually follows perineal (straddle) trauma. Aspirate is bright red like arterial blood; colour Doppler often shows the fistula. Observe first, as many resolve; embolise if it persists.
  • Stuttering episodes (under 2 hours) can be treated with bedtime pseudoephedrine.

Acute scrotum

  • Delay in presentation comes from embarrassment, denial and poor public awareness.
  • A careful history and examination give the diagnosis in most cases; ultrasound only confirms.
  • Torsion risk relates to an abnormal tunica vaginalis (bell clapper), not a patent processus. Irreversible damage can begin 4 hours after cord occlusion.
  • Ultrasound signs: absent flow, the "snail sign" (twisted cord) and heterogeneous parenchyma. A cord twist on high-resolution Doppler is the most specific sign.
  • Salvage falls with duration and relates to the degree of twist, so do not delay surgery for imaging.
  • Manual detorsion ("open book", outward) may be incomplete, and one third twist the opposite way. Exploration is still mandatory and detorsion must never delay theatre.
  • Suspected intermittent torsion: elective exploration with bilateral fixation (a bell clapper is expected).
  • Perinatal torsion is rarely salvageable when found in the nursery. A change after birth needs emergency exploration with contralateral fixation.
  • Appendage torsion is the commonest acute scrotum before puberty. A "blue dot" may show at the upper pole. It is self-limiting: rest and analgesia.
  • Infant epididymitis: most have sterile urine and normal urinary tracts on imaging.
  • Abdominoscrotal hydrocele has a closed processus. It is linked to an elongated, dysmorphic testis, raised tunical pressure and hydronephrosis. Epididymal anomalies are not a typical association.

Varicocele

  • Prevalence is about 15% in adolescents and adults alike. It is commoner in tall thin boys (genetic tendency).
  • Most are asymptomatic and found on examination; pain is the least common presentation.
  • Testes should differ by no more than 15%–20% in volume; growth can be asynchronous in puberty. Ultrasound measures volume most accurately.
  • Hypotrophy of 15%–20% may catch up after repair, or spontaneously. Grade and catch-up growth do not reliably predict semen quality. A poorer sperm trend may affect only a subset.
  • Repair indications: hypotrophy of at least 15%–20% on serial yearly exams, significant pain, small total testicular volume, or oligospermia. Semen analysis is the best fertility indicator. Continuous reflux on Doppler alone is not an indication.
  • Retroperitoneal (open or laparoscopic) and subinguinal repairs have similar success. Hydrocele is commonest after retroperitoneal mass ligation and least likely after transvenous embolisation. Lymphatic-sparing reduces it, and most post-repair hydroceles need surgery.

Inguinal hernia and hydrocele

  • Hernias occur in 1%–5% of children, more in boys and premature infants. The right side predominates (book 3:1, from later right descent); usually quoted as about 60% right, 30% left, 10% bilateral. Bilaterality is more likely in girls, prematurity, age under 1 year and cryptorchidism.
  • An intermittent bulge separates hernia or communicating hydrocele from a scrotal or cord hydrocele. Parents' photographs are reliable.
  • Repair hernias soon after diagnosis. Observe hydroceles, which often resolve.
  • Approaches are inguinal (highest success), scrotal or laparoscopic. Transperitoneal laparoscopy inspects the contralateral ring quickly and accurately.
  • Recurrence (0.5%–1%) follows a missed or poorly ligated sac, a torn sac, floor damage, a weak internal ring or a missed direct hernia.

Ch 70 Priapism

Types and causes

  • Ischaemic priapism: a rigid erection with little or no cavernous arterial inflow. Progressive hypoxia, hypercarbia and acidosis occur; pain starts after 6–8 hours. The aspirate is dark, not bright red.
  • Pathology: interstitial oedema by 12 hours, then endothelial loss and platelet adherence. By 48 hours there is sinusoidal thrombus and smooth muscle necrosis. Hypoxic smooth muscle stops contracting to α-agonists.
  • Most ischaemic priapism is idiopathic. Associations: psychotropics, alcohol and drugs, blood dyscrasias, metastases, perineal trauma, intracavernous injection and SCD (sickle cell disease).
  • In men over 40, intracavernous ED (erectile dysfunction) drugs are the usual cause. With alprostadil, prolonged erection (4–6 h) occurred in 5% and priapism (over 6 h) in 1%; with trimix programmes prolonged erections occur in 5%–35%.
  • PDE5 (phosphodiesterase type 5) inhibitors rarely cause priapism, usually with other risks. Daily tadalafil caused none in trials, and no PDE5 inhibitor is more likely than another. Risk factors: SCD, spinal cord injury, recreational use, use with injections, penile trauma, psychotropics and narcotic abuse.
  • Non-ischaemic (high-flow) priapism is much rarer. It usually follows trauma that lacerates the cavernous artery, most often a straddle injury to the crura. Other causes: coital trauma, kicks, pelvic fracture, birth trauma, needle injury and metastatic erosion (not haemodialysis). The corpora are tumescent but not rigid.
  • Stuttering priapism: recurrent, unwanted, painful erections, usually in SCD.

Pathophysiology in SCD

  • HbS polymerises when deoxygenated. This causes haemolysis, vaso-occlusion and adhesion of sickle cells, endothelium and leukocytes. Free haemoglobin scavenges NO (nitric oxide), and arginase removes NO substrate. Decreased plasma HbS is not a mechanism.
  • Haemolysis and low NO drive priapism, pulmonary hypertension, leg ulcers and stroke. Viscosity drives pain crises, osteonecrosis and acute chest syndrome. SCD priapism carries a fivefold risk of pulmonary hypertension, lower haemoglobin and higher haemolytic markers (reticulocytes, bilirubin, LDH, AST).
  • Ischaemic and stuttering priapism reflect NO imbalance with PDE5 downregulation: low endothelial NO gives low basal cGMP, and so little PDE5.

Evaluation

  • Blood gas: ischaemic is hypoxic, hypercarbic and acidotic. Normal mixed venous values (e.g. PO2 40, PCO2 50, pH 7.35) after treatment with a turgid penis mean oedema, so observe.
  • CDU (colour duplex ultrasound) is the most reliable, least invasive test. Ischaemic shows absent cavernous flow (the waveform returns with detumescence); high-flow shows normal or high velocities and may show the fistula. Scan the shaft and the perineum (crura) after trauma.
  • Use CDU for a persisting erection after treatment: resolved ischaemia with oedema, persistent ischaemia, or conversion to high flow (a rare event).
  • Arteriography is too invasive for diagnosis; keep it for embolisation.

Treatment of ischaemic priapism

  • First step: aspiration to decompress, repeated until bright red blood returns. It relieves pain and clears acidotic blood and cures about a third (36%). Irrigation is not proven better than aspiration alone. Oral agents have no role in acute priapism over 4 hours.
  • If aspiration fails, inject phenylephrine 200 µg/mL in 0.5-mL aliquots (up to 1 mg in total). It is the preferred agent: α1-selective, with minimal cardiac β effects. Aspiration plus sympathomimetic is the AUA standard.
  • SCD: hydration, alkalinisation and exchange transfusion are no longer first line (neurological complications; transfusion may be ineffective). Treat with aspiration and phenylephrine, with haematology input. Hydroxyurea raises HbF and NO.
  • Shunt if medical treatment fails. All shunts reopen venous outflow so inflow returns. A distal cavernoglanular shunt comes first (easier; percutaneous under local anaesthetic is possible). If it fails, consider a proximal shunt. T-shunt with "snake" tunnelling works if under 24 hours, poorly after 48 hours.
  • Function after reversal: 100% have erections if under 12 h, 78% at 12–24 h, 44% at 24–36 h, none after 36 h. Interventions after 48 h relieve pain but preserve little potency.
  • Refractory or untreated priapism leads to fibrosis, length loss and complete ED (after 36–72 h). Early prosthesis preserves length and is easier than delayed implantation into fibrotic corpora.

Stuttering priapism

  • Aim: prevent episodes and preserve function. Options include daily oral sympathomimetic, daily PDE5 inhibitor (used in SCD) or self-injection of phenylephrine. GnRH agonists or antiandrogens are for adults only, not before full sexual maturity.

Treatment of high-flow priapism

  • It is not an emergency: observe, as about two thirds resolve spontaneously (up to 62%). Aspiration is diagnostic only.
  • Embolise if the patient prefers after counselling on spontaneous resolution and on ED risk. Success is high but recurrence after one treatment is 30%–40%. Surgical ligation (under CDU guidance, after a pseudocapsule forms) if embolisation fails.

Ch 87 Renal insufficiency and ischaemic nephropathy

Acute renal failure

  • ARF (acute renal failure; now AKI) is prerenal, intrarenal or postrenal.
  • Prerenal: low renal blood flow or high nitrogen load. BUN-to-creatinine ratio over 10:1 (book), low urine volume, high osmolality and very low sodium. Update: usually quoted as above 20:1.
  • Intrarenal: parenchymal disease of the glomerulus (acute GN), tubule (ATN, acute tubular necrosis) or interstitium (acute interstitial nephritis). Ratio about 10:1, variable output, urine sodium over 20 mEq/L (over 40 in ATN).
  • Postrenal: obstruction of the whole nephron mass; ratio 10:1. Anuria suggests complete obstruction, cortical necrosis or bilateral vascular occlusion.
  • A low FENa (fractional excretion of sodium) occurs in prerenal azotemia and in acute GN. The urinalysis separates them: bland in prerenal, protein and RBC casts in GN.
  • ATN: renal blood flow falls 50% or more, worst in the outer medulla, with increased tubular permeability. ATP depletion is the sentinel event. The S3 proximal tubule and the medullary thick ascending limb are most injured.
  • Angiotensin II constricts the efferent more than the afferent arteriole; prostaglandins dilate the afferent. ACE inhibitors, ARBs and NSAIDs can therefore cause ARF (efferent dilatation, loss of afferent dilatation).
  • Creatinine and GFR are not linear: a rise from 0.9 to 1.2 mg/dL can mean GFR falling from 120 to 60 mL/min.
  • Rhabdomyolysis (prolonged exaggerated lithotomy or lateral decubitus) causes myoglobin injury to the proximal tubule. Recognise it early and give forced alkaline diuresis.
  • Urinary leak can be shown by drain fluid creatinine, IV indigo carmine or methylene blue, or imaging (isotope renography, RPG, cystogram, CT). A renal scan shows perfusion and extravasation.
  • A bilateral persistent dense nephrogram at 24 hours after contrast means ATN (contrast nephropathy). Arterial occlusion is unilateral with absent nephrogram.

Management of ARF

  • Prerenal: restore circulating volume with isotonic fluid.
  • Loop diuretics cut NaCl transport in the thick ascending limb, lowering metabolic demand. Dopamine is unproven. The book says mannitol before an ischaemic insult helps. Update: an RCT in partial nephrectomy showed no benefit from mannitol.
  • Hyperkalaemia with ECG changes (peaked T, long PR, wide QRS): IV calcium first (immediate, short-lived), then insulin-glucose or bicarbonate, then removal with Kayexalate (risk of colonic necrosis) or dialysis.
  • Dialysis indications: volume overload, severe hyperkalaemia, severe acidosis, pericarditis, some poisonings and uraemic symptoms. Peritoneal dialysis is gentler on haemodynamics.
  • ATN mortality is about 50%; 5% of survivors need chronic dialysis.
  • Contrast nephropathy: pre-hydrate with IV saline and use non-ionic contrast. Loop diuretic or mannitol adds nothing (Solomon). Update: PRESERVE found bicarbonate and N-acetylcysteine no better than saline.
  • Gadolinium is not nephrotoxic, but it causes NSF (nephrogenic systemic fibrosis) in advanced CKD, which can be fatal. Use it only after careful thought. Update: the ACR allows group II agents when needed.

Chronic kidney disease

  • CKD is a GFR under 60 mL/min (book). Update: KDIGO: GFR under 60 or markers of damage (e.g. albuminuria), for over 3 months.
  • An MDRD eGFR of about 52 after nephrectomy is stage 3 CKD: refer to a nephrologist and start protective measures.
  • Causes: diabetes and hypertension most, then glomerular disease (FSGS, membranous), then systemic disease (SLE, Wegener).
  • Lost nephron mass triggers hyperfiltration (high glomerular pressure), glomerular hypertrophy, sympathetic activation and interstitial fibrosis (not intrarenal vascular occlusion). Once GFR is under 50% of normal, decline continues even if the cause is gone.
  • Fibrosis is promoted by many mediators; ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide) may oppose it.
  • Progression risk depends on cause, GFR and albuminuria. A one-off loss after nephrectomy without albuminuria has the best prognosis. Albuminuria and a family history of ESRD (end-stage renal disease) predict decline.
  • eGFR equations (Cockcroft-Gault, MDRD, CKD-EPI) are inaccurate with changing function, extremes of age or size, obesity, low muscle mass or advanced illness. Cystatin C may be better. Tubular creatinine secretion overestimates GFR at low levels; cimetidine and trimethoprim block it.
  • Renal biopsy is needed for a definitive diagnosis when function is abnormal.
  • ACE inhibitors and ARBs slow progression by haemodynamic and non-haemodynamic effects. They can reduce function in bilateral renal artery stenosis or ADPKD with cysts over 10 cm.

ESRD

  • Preemptive transplantation is the best treatment.
  • In haemodialysis patients, access catheter infection is the commonest reason for admission. Sepsis mortality is one to several hundred times that of the general population. Remaining lifetime is 15%–25% of normal.
  • Predictors of admission: low albumin and poor nutrition, low activity, diabetic ESRD, PVD, white race, age, CHF and inflammation (CRP).

Ch 117 Overactive bladder

  • Terms: "detrusor hyperreflexia" is now NDO (neurogenic detrusor overactivity); "detrusor instability" is not recommended.
  • OAB (overactive bladder) is a symptom diagnosis: urgency, with or without urgency incontinence, usually with frequency and nocturia, without infection or other pathology. DO (detrusor overactivity) is urodynamic. The book says urgency plus one other symptom is essential. Update: in the ICS definition, urgency itself is the core symptom.
  • Urgency is of rapid onset and may, but need not, lead to incontinence; urgency incontinence affects many OAB patients. Mixed incontinence is stress plus urgency incontinence.
  • Prevalence is 10%–20% (16% in North American and European surveys, about 12% in EPIC). Over 50% have at least one LUTS.
  • Contributors: ageing, neurological disease, female sex, BOO (bladder outlet obstruction) and the metabolic syndrome.
  • DO causes may be neurogenic or myogenic. Neurogenic: reduced suprapontine inhibition, re-emerged spinal reflexes, new C-fibre reflexes (plasticity) and sensitised afferent terminals. Myogenic: spontaneous excitation from upregulated membrane receptors.
  • DO means involuntary contractions during filling, of any size. Patients cannot suppress them voluntarily.
  • The two main urodynamic findings are DO and increased filling sensation. Voided volumes are erratic.
  • A voiding diary is essential. The ICIQ (International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire) is a modular, formally validated system.
  • Treatment runs from conservative and lifestyle measures, to drugs, to nerve stimulation or bladder surgery for refractory cases. Update: AUA 2024 advises discussing dementia risk with antimuscarinics.

Ch 124 Pelvic organ prolapse surgery

Anatomy and principles

  • The iliococcygeus fuses in the midline to the coccyx to form the levator plate, which supports the upper vagina and cervix. The book names coccygeus; DeLancey describes iliococcygeus plus the posterior pubococcygeus fibres.
  • The urethra is fused to the anterior vaginal wall for most of its length. Endopelvic connective tissue supports the vagina.
  • DeLancey levels: level I, cardinal and uterosacral ligaments (uterus and upper vagina); level II, endopelvic and pubocervical fascia to the arcus tendineus (mid vagina); level III, levator ani and perineal body (distal vagina).
  • Prolapse is mainly a quality-of-life issue. Repair only symptomatic prolapse (e.g. Ba 0 with a bulge). Anterior defects are central, lateral or both.
  • Screen all advanced anterior prolapse for occult SUI (stress urinary incontinence).
  • Operative order: apex, anterior, sling, posterior. Do the sling before the posterior repair so a rectal injury can be fixed with the mesh uninvolved.
  • If the bladder or rectum is injured, do not use mesh.

Anterior and posterior repair

  • Anterior colporrhaphy restores level II support but does not suspend the apex. Neither it nor paravaginal repair treats SUI.
  • Stage 3 anterior prolapse with apical descent needs vault suspension plus anterior repair (anterior repair alone fails too often). Add no sling without demonstrated SUI, and no posterior repair if that compartment is intact.
  • After anterior colporrhaphy, cystoscopy with indigo carmine or methylene blue is recommended routinely. If there is no efflux, catheterise or take down plication sutures.
  • Traditional posterior colporrhaphy is preferred. Mesh and biological grafts add complications without better success. Levator plication causes de novo dyspareunia and is largely abandoned.
  • Repair improves bulge, pressure, splinting and dyspareunia but not constipation (a dysmotility problem).

Apical and uterine-sparing procedures

  • Sacrospinous fixation corrects the apex but displaces it posteriorly, raising the risk of anterior prolapse. Gluteal or posterior-leg neuropathic pain occurs in 15%.
  • Sacrospinous suture: the book says 1 cm lateral and slightly superior to the ischial spine. The pudendal nerve and vessels lie just behind the spine; the inferior gluteal vessels and hypogastric plexus lie above. Update: standard teaching places the suture 1.5–2 cm medial to the spine.
  • Abdominal sacrocolpopexy maximises functional vaginal length without distorting the vaginal axis. A supracervical hysterectomy with sacrocervicopexy reduces mesh extrusion in a sexually active woman.
  • Sacral osteomyelitis is a rare complication of sacrocolpopexy: back pain, fever, lethargy and discharge.
  • Uterine sparing:
  • Abdominal sacrohysteropexy with mesh: for a woman wanting fertility and the most durable repair; also for connective tissue disorders and exstrophy (mesh from the lower uterus or cervix to S1–S2).
  • Manchester-Fothergill: for an elongated cervix with a well-supported corpus.
  • Sacrospinous hysteropexy: when abdominal access is hard (adhesions, previous hernia mesh).
  • Uterosacral hysteropexy (vaginal or laparoscopic): avoids mesh when the woman has had mesh problems.
  • Colpocleisis obliterates part of the vagina and precludes intercourse. It has the lowest recurrence and reoperation rates. Le Fort (partial) colpocleisis can be done under local anaesthesia in frail women.

Complications

  • Synthetic mesh erodes in about 10%. Mesh raises reoperation rates compared with native tissue. After failed prolapse and incontinence surgery, 29% need reoperation.
  • Posterior colporrhaphy has the highest dyspareunia rate (about 18%, range 0%–46%). Causes: poor lateral dissection, aggressive plication or skin trimming, a narrow introitus, scarring and atrophy. Sexual function usually improves after prolapse repair. Uterosacral suspension can entrap sacral nerves (1%–2%).
  • Uterosacral suspension carries the highest ureteric injury risk (0%–11%).
  • Positioning: sciatic injury from hip hyperflexion and external rotation with the knee extended (foot drop, calf and lateral foot numbness). Femoral injury from hip flexion, abduction and external rotation (weak quadriceps, anterior thigh numbness). Risks are BMI under 20, diabetes and each extra hour in lithotomy.

Ch 138 Surgical management of bladder cancer

TURBT

  • Image the upper tracts before endoscopic treatment.
  • Give routine antibiotic prophylaxis 30–60 minutes before TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour).
  • Initial resection should include muscle, with a 2-cm visibly clear surface margin.
  • Lateral wall tumours trigger the obturator reflex and perforation. Reduce it with low distension, bipolar current and paralysis under general anaesthesia.
  • A ureteric orifice resected with cutting current does not routinely need a stent.
  • A single immediate instillation of epirubicin or mitomycin C modestly reduces recurrence, not progression. BCG must never be instilled immediately after resection.

Radical cystectomy

  • Neoadjuvant MVAC (Grossman 2003, SWOG 8710) improved median survival from 46 to 77 months. Update: NIAGARA (2024) added perioperative durvalumab to cisplatin-gemcitabine.
  • Staging upper tract urothelial cancer needs parenchymal and luminal imaging. PET/CT is limited inside the urinary tract (excreted tracer).
  • Gadolinium (book): caution at GFR 30–60 and absolutely contraindicated below 30 because of NSF (the book calls it "sclerosis"). Update: the ACR allows group II agents when needed.
  • Preparation: the stoma site is marked by a stoma nurse with the patient awake. Bowel preparation is controversial (the evidence is from general surgery, where bowel is not opened into the peritoneum). Give IV antibiotics 30–60 minutes before incision. In women, prep the vagina into the field.
  • Standard node template: genitofemoral nerve laterally, internal iliac artery medially, Cooper ligament caudally, ureter crossing the common iliac cranially. Herr: more than 10 nodes gave about 15% (15 months) better survival. Update: LEA and SWOG S1011 showed no gain from extended dissection.
  • Urethral recurrence is about 8% at 5 years. A positive frozen-section urethral margin contraindicates orthotopic diversion.
  • Natural potency is lower than after prostatectomy. Prostate- or capsule-sparing improves function, but rule out occult urothelial or prostate cancer first (local recurrence risk). Capsule-sparing and nerve-sparing give similar function.
  • Female anterior exenteration removes the whole urethra including the meatus but keeps the introitus. Vaginal capacity can be kept with either approach; no routine colpocleisis. Update: the urethra can be spared in selected women having orthotopic diversion.
  • Sealing devices near the rectum can injure it by radiated heat.
  • Partial cystectomy: a solitary lesion (e.g. 1-cm T2 at the dome) without CIS, a 2-cm margin, away from the orifices and neck. Results then approach radical cystectomy.
  • Robotic vs open: RAZOR (2018) showed non-inferior 2-year progression-free survival. Robotic surgery reduced blood loss in RAZOR and the MSKCC trial (diversion done open).

Recovery and complications

  • Bowel recovery: early feeding, neostigmine and alvimopan help; routine nasogastric suction does not (use only with poor airway protection).
  • Extended VTE (venous thromboembolism) prophylaxis for 4 weeks cut events from 4.6% to 0.8% (Kakkar 2010), in addition to pre-incision prophylaxis.
  • 64% have a complication within 3 months (13% high grade), mostly gastrointestinal. 90-day mortality is about 3%.

  • CRT (conformal RT) shapes the dose to the prostate by computer. IMRT varies beam intensity across the field and spares rectum, bladder and bowel better. IMRT dose is 75–79 Gy.

  • Brachytherapy has a rapid dose fall-off within millimetres. I-125 half-life is about 60 days (dose 140–160 Gy); Pd-103 is 17 days (110–125 Gy).

Follow-up and complications

  • PSA bounce after brachytherapy: classic at 12–30 months, in 65% of men under 55 (more if sexually active), and can mimic failure. Review the dosimetry, check PSA every 3 months and biopsy if no decline by 30 months.
  • Early, fast PSA rise after IMRT plus ADT suggests distant failure. Stage systemically (with or without mpMRI) before any salvage or ADT.
  • Radiation distorts histology; biopsies are interpretable 30 months after treatment.
  • After conformal EBRT, about 50% of potent men are impotent at 5 years and 10% have rectal bleeding. Less often: urinary and faecal incontinence, haemorrhagic cystitis and stricture.
  • After brachytherapy, contrast filling the rectum from the prostatic urethra means a urethrorectal fistula.
  • Impending cord compression: immediate androgen ablation (orchiectomy is fastest), steroids and RT; occasionally emergency laminectomy. Update: avoid LHRH agonist flare (use orchiectomy or an antagonist).

Ch 45 Hypospadias

Definition and embryology

  • Hypospadias is an ectopic urethral meatus below the centre of the glans. Most boys have no anomalies outside the genitourinary tract.
  • About 10% are proximal; a bifid scrotum is typical of proximal hypospadias.
  • Ventral curvature with a normally placed meatus is "chordee without hypospadias"; it can usually be corrected without urethral repair.
  • The urethral groove forms between the urethral folds and becomes the urethra. The preputial folds fuse as the urethra closes, which explains the typical hypospadiac prepuce.
  • DHT (dihydrotestosterone) at 8–12 weeks' gestation is the key mediator of penile development.

Aetiology and risk

  • The cause is multifactorial. Placental disruption and androgen disruption, partly from environmental endocrine-disrupting chemicals, may explain a rising incidence.
  • A shorter AGD (anogenital distance) reflects disruption of this androgen cascade.
  • Risk is higher after assisted reproduction (IVF, in-vitro fertilisation) and in monozygotic twins, partly from hormone disruption.
  • TDS (testicular dysgenesis syndrome) links poor sperm quality, cryptorchidism and hypospadias to fetal androgen disruption.
  • A brother of a boy with hypospadias has up to 15% risk, higher in twins. The overall risk stays small, so another son most likely will not be affected.

Evaluation

  • Examination may show a ventrally deficient prepuce, downward glans tilt, a deviated median raphe, ventral curvature, scrotal encroachment onto the shaft, a scrotal cleft and penoscrotal transposition.
  • Grade the severity after degloving, using meatal position plus degree of curvature. Glans separation is common and does not correlate with severity.
  • Hypospadias with an undescended testis may indicate a DSD (difference of sex development). This is commoner with a proximal meatus and a non-palpable gonad, but obtain a karyotype in any boy with hypospadias plus cryptorchidism.
  • Isolated hypospadias, even proximal, needs neither karyotype nor urinary tract imaging; renal anomalies are rare in non-syndromic hypospadias. A karyotype can be offered for proximal cases. Proceed with surgery: delay does not increase penile growth.
  • Hypospadias with aniridia suggests WAGR (Wilms tumour, aniridia, genital anomalies, retardation): do a renal ultrasound to screen for Wilms tumour.
  • A glanular meatus with no stenosis and no curvature needs no surgery. Any curvature, even with a mildly displaced meatus, warrants repair.
  • Counsel parents on early anaesthesia exposure versus the benefits of surgery in infancy.
  • Curvature with penoscrotal webbing suggests a severely hypoplastic urethra that may need a two-stage repair. Even distal repairs have about a 10% complication rate, not 5%.

Operative principles

  • Preoperative androgen stimulation transiently increases penile length and glans width (a small glans raises complications). Its effect on complication rates is controversial.
  • Side effects of androgen stimulation (more erections, thin pubic hair, aggression) are transient. It does not reduce adult penile size.
  • An enlarged utricle, commoner in severe hypospadias, is the usual cause of difficult catheterisation. Do not excise it at primary repair.
  • Dorsal penile nerves lie at 11 and 1 o'clock; a midline dorsal plication avoids them. A perineal block suits older ambulatory boys who would not tolerate leg weakness after a caudal block.
  • Measure curvature by artificial erection after degloving.
  • Curvature under 30° after degloving: a single midline dorsal plication, without clinically apparent shortening. Even 15° should be corrected, or a second operation becomes likely.
  • Curvature over 30° after degloving and dartos dissection: urethral plate transection and/or ventral corporal lengthening (grafting).
  • Chordee without hypospadias usually comes from short ventral skin and dartos; it corrects with degloving and ventral dartos dissection. Avoid multiple plications.
  • Severe curvature (e.g. 50°) after plate division needs a two-stage repair with corporal lengthening (dermal graft, corporotomies or tunica vaginalis flap). Dorsal plication alone risks recurrence.
  • MIP (megameatus intact prepuce) is a hypospadias variant, not a circumcision injury. Tubularisation of local skin flaps usually suffices.
  • After foreskin reconstruction, do not retract the foreskin for 6 weeks. Complication rates match circumcision. Do not compromise the repair to preserve the prepuce.
  • Suture type does not change outcome. Subepithelial neourethral suturing and extra vascularised layers (barrier flaps, tunica vaginalis) do help.
  • Risk factors for urethroplasty complications: proximal meatus, reoperation, glans width under 14 mm, no barrier flap.

Complications and reoperation

  • Urethrocutaneous fistula is the commonest complication. Fistulas rarely, if ever, close with catheter drainage.
  • Fistula with supple tissue and good glans: primary closure plus a ventral dartos barrier flap. Thin glans tissue, a coronal or distal fistula, or a fistula close to the meatus: redo distal urethroplasty and glansplasty.
  • Always look for distal obstruction (meatal stenosis) with a fistula; treat it by reoperation, not dilatation.
  • Meatal stenosis after TIP (tubularised incised plate) repair is usually from suturing the plate too far distally into the glans.
  • After Thiersch-Duplay repair the meatus may look small. Calibrate it: an 8–10 Fr sound passes if it is not stenotic. Examine under anaesthesia only if symptomatic (UTI, straining, prolonged stream).
  • Urethral diverticulum follows Byars or preputial onlay flaps: prolonged stream and post-void dribbling, ventral ballooning. Calibrate the meatus. Repair with de-epithelialised redundant tissue as extra layers. It does not cause erectile dysfunction.
  • Distal narrowing with slow stream and high PVR (post-void residual): dorsal inlay graft (buccal or skin). DVIU (direct vision internal urethrotomy) succeeds under 10% even with CIC (clean intermittent catheterisation); if the inlay fails, do a staged buccal repair.
  • BXO (balanitis xerotica obliterans, lichen sclerosus) after repair (white meatus, stenosis): meatotomy and skin flaps fail. Do cystoscopy to find the proximal extent, excise all affected tissue and do a staged buccal graft repair.
  • Office biopsy for BXO is not advised. Topical steroids help only temporarily. Failure rate is up to 40%, so follow closely.
  • Scarred tissue is a relative contraindication to TIP or flip-flap redo. With webbing and skin shortage: excise the scar, do a two-stage buccal graft, and recheck curvature.
  • Use lip mucosa in the glans (thinner, fewer complications) and cheek mucosa on the shaft. Excise flat glans skin to make a deep groove.
  • Haematoma under adjacent skin or scrotum after a buccal graft: observe with bed rest. Quilting and tie-over dressings prevent haematoma under the graft.
  • Late penile deviation toward a tunica vaginalis flap means it was not mobilised to the external ring. Transect it through a small scrotal incision.
  • Recurrent UTI after repair: do a VCUG (voiding cystourethrogram) for stricture, new reflux or poor emptying; then cystoscopy if abnormal.
  • Recurrent curvature: diagnose and treat aggressively; plication alone often fails.

Long-term outcome

  • 80% of complications appear within 1 year, but stricture and curvature can appear late, so follow boys (especially proximal) into puberty.
  • Adults after repair report more ejaculatory problems, less satisfaction with sexual function and with penile appearance. Patient-reported outcomes need more study.

Ch 49 Surgery for DSD, cloacal and anorectal malformations

Urogenital sinus and CAH

  • UGS (urogenital sinus) anomalies are commonest in DSD, mainly CAH (congenital adrenal hyperplasia); mild non-classic CAH can be as frequent as 1 in 500.
  • The vagina usually joins the urinary tract at the mid to distal urethra.
  • In CAH the level of the vaginal–urethral confluence is the critical determinant of surgery.
  • Hydrometrocolpos (urine draining into a poorly drained vagina) is often the first sign of a UGS anomaly. It is a cystic pelvic mass between bladder and rectum.
  • Persistent clitoral hypertrophy can occur in premature infants without DSD.
  • A cervical impression at the vaginal dome on genitography means normal female internal organs.
  • Neonatal gonadal biopsy should be deep, because the ovarian part of an ovotestis may cover the testicular part.
  • Adult women with CAH are less satisfied with their genitalia than controls.

Feminising genitoplasty

  • Reconstruction addresses clitoroplasty, labiaplasty and vaginoplasty.
  • Clitoroplasty preserves the glans, tunics and neurovascular bundles; do not mobilise the bundles.
  • Timing is controversial. A single-stage repair (vaginoplasty with clitoroplasty and labiaplasty) uses redundant phallic skin, and maternal oestrogen thickens and vascularises vaginal tissue. Less scarring is claimed; less stenosis is not proven.
  • Neonatal vaginoplasty usually needs a secondary procedure after puberty.

Ch 86 Renal physiology and renovascular hypertension

GFR

  • GFR (glomerular filtration rate) reflects total renal function and is approximated by creatinine clearance.
  • Formulas using age, weight and creatinine estimate GFR best. Update: CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) is now the standard equation.
  • Plasma creatinine is not sensitive to early GFR loss. Inulin clearance is accurate but impractical; 24-hour creatinine clearance overestimates GFR by 10%–20%; the MDRD (Modification of Diet in Renal Disease) formula improves accuracy.
  • GFR is regulated by afferent arteriolar tone, distal tubular chloride (tubuloglomerular feedback), angiotensin II and nitric oxide, not serum osmolality.
  • Renal vascular tone: the RAS (renin-angiotensin system) is one of several mediators. Endothelin is the most potent vasoconstrictor; nitric oxide and carbon monoxide are potent vasodilators.

Tubule

  • Each nephron segment has a distinct homeostatic role.
  • The PCT (proximal convoluted tubule) reabsorbs about 65% of sodium (the most of any segment) and most bicarbonate and other ions.
  • The thin ascending limb reabsorbs NaCl passively and is water-impermeable. The book adds urea, but urea enters the thin limbs from the medulla (urea recycling).
  • Loop of Henle architecture builds a hypertonic interstitium, essential for maximal concentration.

Water and sodium

  • Water movement sets solute concentration, especially sodium. It depends on ADH (antidiuretic hormone), which inserts aquaporin channels.
  • Judge sodium disorders by volume status; history, volume status and urine sodium best show the cause.
  • Normal compensation for hyponatraemia is lower ADH secretion and suppressed thirst.
  • Correct severe sodium deficit or excess slowly.
  • Desmopressin treats hypernatraemia due to diabetes insipidus.

Potassium

  • Potassium is mainly intracellular. Serum K reflects total body K and the intra/extracellular balance.
  • ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors cause hyperkalaemia, not hypokalaemia.
  • Symptomatic hyperkalaemia needs urgent treatment to protect the heart. Mild, chronic hyperkalaemia (e.g. K above 5.5 without ECG changes) does not need emergency therapy.

Acid–base

  • Body chemistry needs a narrow pH range. Acid is excreted by lungs and kidneys.
  • First decide whether kidney (HCO3−) or lung (pCO2) is primary, then whether compensation is appropriate.
  • Type 1 (distal) RTA (renal tubular acidosis) is the only type with stones. Type 4 RTA causes hyperkalaemia (aldosterone deficiency or resistance lowers distal K secretion).

Hormones and bone

  • Low oxygen tension increases erythropoiesis.
  • Kidney hormones influence bone. Skin cholecalciferol is hydroxylated at the 25-position (liver) and 1-position (kidney) to calcitriol, 100 times more potent, which maintains calcium and phosphate.
  • PTH (parathyroid hormone) increases renal calcium reabsorption and increases phosphate excretion.

Renovascular hypertension

  • Renal artery stenosis is the commonest cause; atherosclerosis causes about two-thirds.
  • FMD (fibromuscular dysplasia) has four types; medial fibroplasia is commonest and least likely to progress.
  • Suspect it with severe or abrupt-onset hypertension without risk factors.
  • Goldblatt dog models: two-kidney one-clip (angiotensin-dependent, because the contralateral kidney allows natriuresis) and one-kidney one-clip (volume-dependent).
  • Screen non-invasively with MR angiography, CT angiography or duplex ultrasound.
  • Medical therapy is the mainstay. CORAL found stenting no better than medical therapy.
  • Update: CORAL's medical arm used candesartan (an angiotensin receptor blocker) ± thiazide, amlodipine, atorvastatin and antiplatelet; the book says ACE inhibitor.
  • Stable CKD on RAS blockade and diuretic: no further imaging or angiography. Gadolinium in significant renal failure risks NSF (nephrogenic systemic fibrosis).
  • Percutaneous intervention may work, but evidence questions long-term BP or renal benefit. Surgery is no longer first line but suits selected patients.
  • Surgical revascularisation does better when collaterals fill the distal renal artery. Kidneys under 8 cm do poorly. Most patients have vascular disease elsewhere. Aortic aneurysm repair need not be simultaneous. Ostial lesions may be stented.
  • Ischaemic nephropathy is driven by proinflammatory cytokines and/or angiotensin II, not by failed autoregulation alone. Flow may be preserved, collaterals are common, and it can reverse.

Ch 106 Adrenal disorders

Anatomy and physiology

  • The right adrenal is triangular, the left crescentic.
  • The neonatal gland has fetal and adult components, weighs twice the adult gland and atrophies after birth. The zona reticularis develops last, by 3 years.
  • With renal agenesis the adrenal is usually in its normal place; adrenal agenesis is very rare.
  • Testicular adrenal rests occur in CAH; remember them to avoid needless orchidectomy.
  • Zona glomerulosa makes mineralocorticoids (its sole source of aldosterone); fasciculata makes glucocorticoids; reticularis makes sex steroids.
  • Output per day: adrenal androgens over 20 mg (most abundant), cortisol 10–20 mg, aldosterone 100–150 µg. Adrenal androgens are ACTH-controlled.
  • Aldosterone is regulated mainly by angiotensin II and potassium; ACTH is a weak stimulus, so the glomerulosa does not atrophy in pituitary failure.
  • Cortisol is circadian: peak early morning, nadir 11 pm.
  • Renin is released with low renal perfusion pressure, renal sympathetic activity and low sodium.
  • Mineralocorticoid excess first retains sodium and expands volume; the kidney then "escapes".
  • PNMT (phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase) converts noradrenaline to adrenaline. It is essentially confined to the adrenal medulla (also brain and organ of Zuckerkandl), so an adrenaline-dominant tumour suggests adrenal origin.
  • COMT (catechol-O-methyltransferase) makes metanephrine (from adrenaline) and normetanephrine (from noradrenaline), together "metanephrines". "Free" means not sulphate-conjugated; "total" includes conjugated; "fractionated" means reported separately.

Cushing syndrome

  • Cushing disease (pituitary ACTH, adrenocorticotropic hormone) is about 70% of endogenous Cushing syndrome. Ectopic ACTH almost always comes from malignant tissue.
  • Stones occur in up to 50%; investigate cushingoid stone formers. Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism also occurs.
  • Overnight 1-mg dexamethasone test: 1 mg at 11 pm, cortisol next morning; book cut-off 5 µg/dL or more. Update: current cut-off is above 1.8 µg/dL.
  • Oral contraceptives raise cortisol-binding globulin and total cortisol, giving up to 50% false positives.
  • Transsphenoidal surgery cures 60%–80%, with about 25% relapse. Bilateral adrenalectomy for refractory cases needs an MDT decision: Nelson syndrome (enlarging pituitary adenoma, raised pressure, chiasm compression) in up to 30%.

Primary aldosteronism

  • Only 9%–37% are hypokalaemic at diagnosis.
  • Familial hyperaldosteronism type I: a chimeric 11β-hydroxylase promoter/aldosterone synthase gene makes aldosterone ACTH-driven.
  • 50%–70% with a positive screen are confirmed. Idiopathic hyperplasia is about 60%.
  • Stop spironolactone and eplerenone for at least 6 weeks before testing.
  • Sodium loading suppresses renin and aldosterone unless secretion is autonomous.
  • Lateralisation is the main determinant of surgical success. AVS (adrenal vein sampling): cortisol gradients confirm placement; book example ratio 2.5:1 lateralises left, so counsel left adrenalectomy. Update: a lateralisation index of 4:1 (with cosyntropin) is the usual cut-off.
  • Persistent hypertension after adrenalectomy is predicted by age over 50, more than two drugs, a hypertensive first-degree relative, long duration and renal insufficiency.

Phaeochromocytoma

Adrenal insufficiency

  • Commonest cause: autoimmune adrenalitis in the USA, tuberculosis in developing countries.
  • Crisis mimics an acute abdomen; children may have hypoglycaemic seizures; priapism is not a feature.

Other masses

  • All lesions except an aldosteronoma can be extra-adrenal (myelolipoma, ganglioneuroma, phaeo, oncocytoma).
  • Ganglioneuroma: rare, often calcified, may secrete VIP (vasoactive intestinal polypeptide) causing profound diarrhoea; most are silent.
  • Myelolipoma (fat with haematopoietic elements) is benign: no treatment.
  • Adrenal cysts: pseudocyst, endothelial, epithelial, parasitic; 7% have associated malignancy.
  • CAH carries a high risk of benign adrenocortical adenomas. Metastases to the adrenal are common.
  • Every adrenal mass needs testing for cortisol and catecholamine excess (and aldosterone if hypertensive): late-night salivary cortisol, plasma aldosterone and renin activity, plasma free metanephrines.
  • Even a likely RCC metastasis to the adrenal needs functional testing; a phaeo may be lurking.
  • Under 10 HU on unenhanced CT strongly suggests adenoma.

Adrenocortical carcinoma

  • Up to 74% of functional tumours secrete cortisol.
  • Weiss criteria: 3 or more suggest malignancy; use with caution in children and oncocytic tumours. Ki-67 is perhaps the best marker.
  • Marked atypia, mitoses and Ki-67 positivity mean carcinoma: no more pathology needed; follow with short-interval imaging and give mitotane if metastases appear.
  • Children survive better: 5-year 54% vs 20%–47% in adults.
  • Complete resection gives the best chance of cure. Adjuvant RT lowers local recurrence without OS gain; functional status does not consistently affect survival.
  • Book: adjuvant mitotane improves RFS and OS. Update: ADIUVO (2023) showed no benefit in low-to-intermediate risk.
  • Metastatic: mitotane alone or with cytotoxics. Update: FIRM-ACT showed EDP (etoposide, doxorubicin, cisplatin) plus mitotane beat streptozotocin plus mitotane for PFS, without clear OS gain.
  • Radical nephrectomy need not include adrenalectomy, except for large (T2) upper-pole tumours, an abnormal gland on imaging, or thrombus at adrenal level.

Ch 114 Urodynamics

Indications

  • Level-1 indications are lacking. Use UDS (urodynamic studies) when there is a clear question and the answer will guide treatment; it matters most in neuropathic dysfunction and may be unnecessary in pure SUI (stress urinary incontinence) seen clinically.
  • Male obstruction nomograms exist, but indications in men are no clearer than in women.
  • The AUA/SUFU guideline helps select tests after evaluation; it is mostly based on lower-level evidence and expert opinion.
  • In pure or stress-predominant SUI with normal emptying, UDS has not beaten office evaluation in RCTs or predicted surgical outcome. It is considered useful with urgency incontinence or emptying problems before surgery.
  • UDS is done in an unnatural setting and may not reflect daily activity.

Flow, filling and compliance

  • Normal uroflow is bell-shaped.
  • CMG (cystometrogram) measures bladder pressure during filling. Compliance = change in volume over change in pressure; mean 40–120 mL/cmH2O.
  • Pdet (detrusor pressure) = Pves − Pabd and stays near zero during filling. Rectal or vaginal catheters measure Pabd and best show abdominal straining.
  • Compliance is hard to interpret, so filling pressures are used more to predict outcome. Sustained Pdet over 40 cmH2O damages the upper tracts.
  • VUR (vesicoureteral reflux) acts as a pop-off and makes compliance look better than it is. Outlet obstruction, radiation and TB (tuberculosis) truly reduce compliance. Fast filling and involuntary contractions make it look worse. Catheter type does not matter.
  • DO (detrusor overactivity) is a urodynamic observation of involuntary contractions; it occurs in asymptomatic people. OAB (overactive bladder) is a symptom diagnosis. Poor compliance, not DO, threatens the kidneys.

Urethral function and leak points

  • ALPP (abdominal leak point pressure): intravesical pressure at leakage from raised abdominal pressure. DLPP (detrusor leak point pressure): detrusor pressure at leakage without contraction or straining, used in poor compliance or lower motor neuron disease.
  • MUCP (maximum urethral closure pressure) = peak urethral minus intravesical pressure, normally 40–60 cmH2O.
  • ALPP and MUCP do not reliably define ISD (intrinsic sphincter deficiency) or predict outcome, and are not used alone to grade severity (ICS, International Continence Society). If UDS is done before SUI surgery, AUA/SUFU advises measuring one.
  • Functional classification: SUI is failure to store due to an underactive outlet.
  • Occult (latent) SUI appears only when prolapse is reduced.

Voiding and obstruction

  • Obstruction = high-pressure, low-flow voiding, with or without residual; impaired contractility may follow.
  • BOOI (bladder outlet obstruction index) = PdetQmax − 2Qmax: over 40 obstructed, under 20 unobstructed.
  • Women: Qmax under 12 mL/s with Pdet over 25 cmH2O suggests obstruction.

Sphincter and EMG

  • The external sphincter contracts progressively with filling and during involuntary contractions (guarding reflex). It relaxes first, before the detrusor contracts.
  • DESD (detrusor external sphincter dyssynergia) needs a neurological lesion above the sacral micturition centre; a learned version is dysfunctional voiding (intermittent flow in a neurologically normal person).
  • Patch EMG (electromyography) electrodes assume perineal muscle reflects the sphincter; needle electrodes are needed for the sphincter itself.

Video-urodynamics

  • VUDS (video-urodynamics) is the procedure of choice for bladder neck dysfunction. Internal sphincter dyssynergia needs VUDS and a cord lesion above sympathetic outflow (T10–L1).
  • VUDS helps in complex or neuropathic dysfunction, unexplained retention in women, after radical pelvic surgery, diversion, transplant or pelvic radiation. It is not needed to diagnose DO.

Ch 134 Urology in transgender individuals

  • The urologist has an important role in treatment and follow-up.
  • "Gender dysphoria" is the ICD/DSM term (the book's ICD sense). Update: DSM-5 uses gender dysphoria; ICD-11 uses "gender incongruence"; "gender identity disorder" is obsolete.
  • It can be diagnosed in childhood, is commoner in birth-assigned males, and is not due to hormonal imbalance. Psychotherapy as "treatment" is no longer recommended.

Transwomen

  • Urinary incontinence is the commonest complication.
  • The prostate remains: be alert to prostate disease. The neovagina lies between rectum and prostate, so examine the prostate transvaginally.

Transmen

  • Pre-op assessment: urological history, urinary tract ultrasound and uroflowmetry.
  • Discuss whether to perform vaginectomy; rectal injury occurs in about 0.9%.
  • Scrotum is made from the labia majora (embryological homologue).
  • Overall complication rate of phalloplasty is about 40%. Urethral fistula is the commonest urological complication; fistulas and strictures occur mostly at the anastomosis between the fixed and phallic urethra.
  • Catheterisation is hard because of the sharp curve between the native urethra and the pars fixa.
  • Metoidioplasty is an alternative for those unwilling to accept phalloplasty complications.
  • Erectile prosthesis: implant about 1 year after phalloplasty, when nerve anastomosis gives sensation. Complications are higher than in native males.

Ch 161 Hormonal therapy for prostate cancer

Mechanisms

  • All ADT (androgen deprivation therapy) lowers androgen or blocks its binding; the AR (androgen receptor) itself is not directly targeted, so CRPC (castration-resistant prostate cancer) is thought to be AR reactivation.
  • The AR stays responsive in CRPC, so continue ADT.
  • Oestrogen acts mainly by negative feedback on LH (luteinising hormone); oestradiol is 1000 times more potent than testosterone at suppressing LH and FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone).
  • Aminoglutethimide blocks cholesterol to pregnenolone.
  • Antiandrogens bind the AR competitively and are steroidal or non-steroidal.
  • Steroidal (cyproterone acetate) also suppress LH centrally (progestational). It causes fluid retention and thromboembolism.
  • Non-steroidal agents block central ARs too, so LH, testosterone and oestrogen all rise, causing gynaecomastia and mastodynia.
  • Antiandrogen monotherapy aims to preserve potency, but long-term sexual function is only about 20%, no better than castration.
  • All antiandrogens can act as agonists; withdrawal can lower PSA and cause responses.
  • Flutamide causes diarrhoea most. All non-steroidals can cause hepatitis up to fulminant failure: monitor liver tests.
  • Nilutamide: delayed dark adaptation in about a quarter; interstitial pneumonitis in about 1%.
  • LHRH (luteinising hormone-releasing hormone) agonists desensitise pituitary receptors. Early agents needed daily dosing; analogues are longer acting. All are given IM or SC.
  • The testosterone flare lasts 10–20 days. Start an antiandrogen together with the agonist and continue it about 3 weeks (21–28 days).
  • Survival with LHRH agonists equals orchidectomy. The antagonist abarelix caused severe allergic reactions. Update: relugolix is an oral GnRH antagonist.
  • Bilateral orchidectomy lowers testosterone by 90% within 24 hours. Double-ligate each cord, one with a transfixion suture.
  • Ketoconazole, aminoglutethimide and abiraterone need glucocorticoid replacement. Aminoglutethimide also needs mineralocorticoid. Abiraterone raises mineralocorticoids, causing hypokalaemia and hypertension.
  • Cost: DES (diethylstilbestrol) 1–3 mg/day without breast irradiation is cheapest; LHRH agonists beat orchidectomy on cost only if survival is a few months; CAB (combined androgen blockade) is most expensive.

Response

  • Not reaching an undetectable PSA gives 15-fold higher odds of castration-resistant progression at 24 months.
  • Size and speed of PSA fall, pre-ADT PSA doubling time and baseline testosterone also predict response. Each unit rise in Gleason score raises the hazard of CRPC by nearly 70%.

Side effects

  • ADT causes bone loss, sexual dysfunction, hot flushes, cognitive decline, muscle loss, fat gain, anaemia, gynaecomastia, diabetes and higher cardiovascular mortality.
  • Up to 20% keep some sexual activity, but only about 5% (1 in 20) keep high libido.
  • Over half meet osteopenia or osteoporosis criteria; the average man is osteopenic by 4 years.
  • Hot flushes affect 50%–80%; treat only if bothersome (megestrol, cyproterone, DES, oestradiol, medroxyprogesterone, gabapentin).
  • Prophylactic breast RT (10 Gy) prevents gynaecomastia but does not help once present; mastectomy may then be needed.

Indications and timing

  • ADT is indicated for symptomatic metastatic disease in hormone-intact men. It has no role in PIN (prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia).
  • Update: metastatic hormone-sensitive disease now gets ADT plus docetaxel and/or an AR pathway inhibitor.
  • Neoadjuvant ADT before prostatectomy cuts positive margins (about 50% to 15%) but not PSA progression in four RCTs; 3 months of any form does not lower biochemical failure.
  • Book: ADT with EBRT (external beam radiotherapy) benefits only locally advanced and/or high-grade disease. Update: short-course ADT also helps intermediate risk (RTOG 9408).
  • CAB targets adrenal androgens. With cyproterone it is slightly worse (more non-cancer deaths). Non-steroidal CAB shows a slight survival gain. Adding an antiandrogen at PSA rise is secondary hormonal manipulation.
  • Orchidectomy plus flutamide showed no benefit over orchidectomy alone in minimal metastatic disease (INT-0105).
  • Early ADT does not prevent CRPC and in most studies does not improve OS; in localised low-risk disease it increases overall deaths.
  • After PSA failure post-prostatectomy the course is protracted: median time to metastasis about 8 years (Pound 1999; book says 10). Many men never need ADT.
  • Immediate ADT for nodes found at prostatectomy (prostatectomy completed) improved OS (Messing). EORTC (no prostatectomy) showed no benefit.
  • 20% of men with metastatic prostate cancer die of other causes.

Intermittent ADT

  • Book: not superior and possibly worse. Update: NCIC PR.7 showed non-inferior OS after radiotherapy; SWOG 9346 could not prove non-inferiority in metastatic disease.
  • Off-treatment intervals shorten: median 20.1, 13.2, 9.1 months, then 4–5 months. Attrition was 5% in the first interval and 68% by the third.
  • Prostate cancer deaths: 120 intermittent vs 94 continuous; other deaths 148 vs 162. Quality of life was better with intermittent ADT (hot flushes, sexual desire, urinary symptoms).
