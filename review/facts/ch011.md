# Chapter 11: Lower Urinary Tract Catheterization

## Book answers

- **c011-a01** 1. d. 20. The male urethra is 18 to 20 cm. The anterior urethra is 16 cm.
- **c011-a02** 2. c. Both a and b. The anterior male urethra is surrounded by both corpus spongiosum and bulbospongiosus muscle at the bulbar urethra.
- **c011-a03** 3. a. 4. The female urethra is approximately 4 cm long
- **c011-a04** 4. d. 5.3. To calculate French size approximately, divide by 3.
- **c011-a05** 5. a. Latex. Rigidity from least to greatest: latex, silicone, PVC. This is important to understand, and different catheter materials may be more appropriate for different clinical situations.
- **c011-a06** 6. b. Seldinger. The Seldinger technique involves gaining access into the bladder with a 21-gauge needle. I wire is placed once urine return is identified. Various catheters or kits can be used to place a catheter over the wire. The Lowsley retractor is a modified male urethral sound that can be used to place a suprapubic catheter. There are trocars that can be placed under vision with a cystoscope that can accommodate a catheter once the inner section is removed and the sheath left in place.
- **c011-a07** 7. c. Overdistension. Overdistension is the most common reason for catheterization difficulty. If the catheter is forced, a perforation in the channel will be likely. A 21-gauge needle can be placed into the reservoir. After drainage, a catheter will usually pass without difficulty.
- **c011-a08** 8. b. F. Catheterizing a urostomy gently can be performed without risk. Occasionally, catheterizing the stoma may be therapeutic in cases such as stomal stenosis or parastomal hernia.
- **c011-a09** 9. a. T. A history of bladder neck reconstruction is one of the few contraindications to urethral catheterization.
- **c011-a10** 10. a. T. Unfortunately, this is true based on a Centers for Disease Control and Prevention report. Decreasing catheter use should decrease the hospital-acquired infection rates.
- **c011-a11** 11. a. 1%. There are retrospective studies that do show what appears to be an increased risk of bladder cancer. Some recommend surveillance after 8 years of chronic catheterization.

## Chapter Review points

- **c011-r01** 1. There are many indications for urethral catheterization and many sizes and types of catheters that can be used.
- **c011-r02** 2. As the French size increases, the diameter of the catheter lumen increases.
- **c011-r03** 3. Catheter rigidity will vary by material.
- **c011-r04** 4. Three-way hematuria catheters should be used if clot retention is suspect.
- **c011-r05** 5. The urologist should be prepared to attain lower urinary drainage in almost all situations.
- **c011-r06** 6. The clinician should closely review the patient’s history and prior operative reports prior to instrumenting the lower urinary tract.
- **c011-r07** 7. If benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is expected, the practitioner should use an 18-Fr Coudé catheter for lower urinary tract drainage.
- **c011-r08** 8. A history of bladder neck repair or closure are a contraindication to urethral catheterization.
- **c011-r09** 9. Catheter placement should be avoided in patients with an artificial urinary sphincter (AUS). A 12-Fr catheter should be used after deactivation of the sphincter if necessary.
- **c011-r10** 10. Catheter-associated urinary tract infections are the most common hospital-acquired infection.
- **c011-r11** 11. Catheters may lead to trauma, stricture, and erosion.
- **c011-r12** 12. Chronic catheterization can lead to bladder inflammation and increase the risk of bladder cancer.
