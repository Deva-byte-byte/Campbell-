Chapter 26 — Infections of the Urinary Tract

  • Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are common, vary widely in presentation and sequelae, and are one of the commonest reasons for prescribing antibiotics.
  • Most cause morbidity and rarely become life-threatening; in certain populations, undiagnosed and untreated UTI carries significant mortality.
  • Infection follows when bacterial virulence rises or host defence falls → inoculation → colonisation → infection. Careful diagnosis and treatment resolve most episodes.
  • Urine is not sterile; bacteriuria may even be protective against UTI.
  • With multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms spreading, clinical evaluation plus urinalysis and culture must precede antibiotics; guidelines should govern both agent selection and duration; education of practitioners and patients is paramount.

DEFINITIONS

  • UTI — an inflammatory response of the urothelium to bacterial invasion, usually with bacteriuria and pyuria.
  • Bacteriuria — bacteria in the urine; usually indicates colonisation or infection, but bacteria can sit in the urothelium without bacteriuria, and bacteriuria may be contamination at collection.
  • Pyuria — white blood cells (WBCs) in the urine; indicates infection and/or inflammation from bacteria, stones, foreign body or other causes.

  • Cystitis — dysuria, frequency, urgency, ± suprapubic pain. The same symptoms occur with urethral or vaginal infection and non-infective conditions: interstitial cystitis/painful bladder syndrome (IC/PBS), bladder carcinoma, calculi.
  • Acute pyelonephritis — chills, fever, flank pain plus bacteriuria and pyuria; this combination is reasonably specific for acute bacterial renal infection.
    • Diagnose on clinical presentation, not on radiological perinephric inflammation alone.
    • Presentation is atypical in spinal cord injury (SCI) — flank symptoms may be absent.
  • Chronic pyelonephritis — atrophic, fibrotic kidney with morphological, radiological or functional renal disease; may be post-infective but often not linked to current infection.
    • Infective scar: focal, coarse cortical scar overlying a calyx, almost always with calyceal distortion — radiologically identical to scarring from vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) or calyceal stone disease.
    • Less often: generalised cortical thinning (atrophic pyelonephritis) → small kidney resembling post-obstructive atrophy.
    • Fig. 26.1 (excretory urogram): focal coarse scarring with blunted calyces, one calyx reaching the capsule, in a young adult with febrile infections and VUR in infancy — reinfections later stopped on prophylaxis, with normal blood pressure and creatinine.
    • Fig. 26.2: uniform, regular cortical atrophy in spina bifida with neurogenic bladder and childhood febrile bacteriuria — reflux of bacteria into virtually all nephrons; this uncommon pattern is characteristic of obstruction with superimposed infection.
    • Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis (XGP) — rare chronic form, usually with stones; granulomatous replacement of parenchyma → ipsilateral loss of function.

KEY POINTS: DEFINITIONS

  • Bacteriuria and pyuria are not synonymous with UTI.
  • UTIs are classified by presumed site of origin.
  • Uncomplicated = healthy host, normal tract; complicated = factors raising acquisition and lowering treatment efficacy.

Concept — Every definition here separates finding from disease: bacteriuria, pyuria and scarring are observations, whereas "infection", "complicated" and "persistence" are judgements about host, tract and organism — and it is the judgement, not the finding, that dictates evaluation and treatment.

INCIDENCE AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

  • UTI complaints are among the commonest primary diagnoses in women attending emergency departments (USA); millions of office and emergency visits annually.
  • Almost half of all women have a UTI in their lifetime; nearly a third have needed antibiotics for one by age 24; about one in ten adult women has one UTI per year.
  • Self-reported cystitis and pyelonephritis admissions are several-fold higher in women than men.
  • Costs are large, and resistant organisms cost more to treat than susceptible ones. A European survey of women with recurrent UTI (GESPRIT) documented repeated doctor visits, sick leave and limited-activity days each year.
  • Bacteriuria rises with institutionalisation, hospitalisation and comorbidity. Roughly one in ten hospitalised urological patients worldwide develops a complicated UTI during admission.
  • CAUTIs are the commonest nosocomial infection, constituting about 80% of nosocomial UTIs; they account for a large share of complicated UTIs, most being hospital or health care–acquired.
  • Incidence is also raised in pregnancy, SCI, diabetes mellitus (DM), multiple sclerosis (MS), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) and organ transplant recipients.
  • Probability of recurrence rises with the number of previous infections and falls with the interval since the last infection; established historically and confirmed in modern cohorts.
    • Highest in young, sexually active women: about a quarter have a second infection within 6 months of a first.
    • Women with ≥2 previous UTIs carry two to five times the 1-year recurrence risk of those with 0–1.
    • Postmenopausal women with prior UTI are similarly at risk; even with daily trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) or lactobacilli prophylaxis most recur within a year.
    • Recurrent bacteriuria segregates into a small subgroup with very high recurrence and a majority with low recurrence.
  • About 70% of recurrences are reinfection with a different organism.
  • No association between uncomplicated recurrent UTI and renal scarring, hypertension or progressive azotaemia has been established.

KEY POINTS: INCIDENCE AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

  • UTI is the commonest bacterial infection, with a large health care cost.
  • Bacteriuria rises with institutionalisation/hospitalisation and comorbidities that immunosuppress or alter lower tract function (e.g. catheterisation).
  • Probability of recurrent UTI (rUTI) rises with the number of previous infections.

Concept — The best predictor of the next UTI is the last one: recurrence clusters in a susceptible minority and is mostly reinfection rather than relapse, which is why the chapter later locates the problem in the host's introital and bladder biology rather than in a hidden focus.

PATHOGENESIS

  • UTI results from uropathogen–host interaction: virulence factors, inoculum size and inadequacy of host defence determine infection and the extent of colonisation and damage.
  • Governing asymmetry: strong host → only highly virulent bacteria infect; compromised host → bacteria with minimal virulence suffice.

Routes of Infection

Ascending Route

  • Most bacteria enter from the bowel and skin reservoir via the urethra into the bladder; adherence to introital and urothelial mucosa is central.
  • Risk factors: faecal soiling of the perineum, spermicide use, intermittent or indwelling catheters.
  • Most pyelonephritis is retrograde ascent bladder → ureter → pelvis → parenchyma.
    • Reflux probably is not required; cystitis-related oedema may distort the vesicoureteric junction enough to permit reflux (Fig. 26.3).
    • Ascent is aided by anything that impairs ureteric peristalsis: Gram-negative bacteria and endotoxins, pregnancy, ureteric obstruction, high lower-tract pressure (all antiperistaltic).
    • Entry to parenchyma is via collecting ducts at the papillary tips → ascent in collecting tubules; hastened by raised intrapelvic pressure (obstruction, VUR), especially with intrarenal reflux.

  • Sequence: periurethral contamination by bowel-colonising uropathogenic E. coli → type 1-fimbriated E. coli selected in bladder (high colony-forming units [CFU], low oxygen), invasion and intracellular multiplication → ascent → P fimbriae bind renal tubular cells → haemolysin damages epithelium, cytokine induction → crosses tubular epithelium → bacteraemia.

Haematogenous Route

  • Uncommon in normal individuals; kidney occasionally seeded by Staphylococcus aureus bacteraemia (e.g. oral source) or Candida fungaemia. Obstruction enhances haematogenous infection experimentally.

Lymphatic Route

  • Direct lymphatic extension from adjacent organs (severe bowel infection, retroperitoneal abscess) — rare; little role in most UTIs.

Urinary Pathogens

  • Most UTIs are due to facultative anaerobes from bowel flora; Staphylococcus epidermidis and Candida albicans come from vaginal or perineal skin flora.
  • Escherichia coli is by far the commonest cause — about 85% of community-acquired and 50% of hospital-acquired UTIs.
  • Remaining community infections: other Enterobacteriaceae (Proteus, Klebsiella) and Gram-positive Enterococcus faecalis and Staphylococcus saprophyticus.
  • S. saprophyticus causes about 10% of symptomatic lower UTIs in young, sexually active women; rare in men and the elderly (age shapes the organism).
  • Nosocomial organisms: E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Citrobacter, Serratia, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Providencia, E. faecalis, S. epidermidis.
  • Less common: Gardnerella vaginalis, Mycoplasma, Ureaplasma urealyticum (some in catheterised patients). U. urealyticum and Mycoplasma hominis found in women with chronic symptoms and negative standard cultures — targeted treatment improved symptoms.

Fastidious Organisms

Anaerobes in the Urinary Tract

  • Symptomatic anaerobic UTI is uncommon, though distal urethra, perineum and vagina are colonised; anaerobes are a frequent midstream contaminant but rare in suprapubic aspirates.
  • Suspect them when persistent irritative symptoms, cocci or Gram-negative rods on microscopy of centrifuged urine, and routine aerobic culture is sterile.
  • Common in enteric fistulae and suppurative genitourinary (GU) infection — the large majority of scrotal, prostatic and perinephric abscesses in men contain anaerobes: Bacteroides (incl. B. fragilis), Fusobacterium, anaerobic cocci, Clostridium perfringens.

Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Other Nontuberculous Mycobacteria

  • Urinary tuberculosis (UTB) presents with haematuria (gross or micro) and/or sterile pyuria, storage symptoms, dysuria, ± fever and weakness.
  • Diagnosis is delayed by nonspecific symptoms → risk of renal impairment and failure.
  • PCR for M. tuberculosis has replaced acid-fast staining as the ideal diagnostic method.

Chlamydia

  • Not grown in routine aerobic culture; implicated in GU infection.

Bacterial Virulence Factors

  • UPEC resides in bowel flora and infects not by chance but by virulence factors that allow adherence to perineum and urethra, migration, and an inflammatory response in the urothelium; the same factors appear in rUTI strains.
  • Many determinants lie on about 20 UPEC-specific pathogenicity-associated islands (PAIs), which enlarge the genome >20% over commensal strains. Phylogroups are defined by PAIs and their virulence factors (adhesins, toxins, surface polysaccharides, flagella, iron acquisition).

Early Events in Uropathogenic Escherichia coli Pathogenesis

  • Sequence: colonisation of periurethral/vaginal tissue and urethra → ascent into bladder → adherence to surface urothelium (engaging epithelial defence) → biofilm → invasion and replication as intracellular bacterial communities (IBCs), which can form quiescent intracellular reservoirs (QIRs) dormant in underlying urothelium → in some, renal colonisation, tissue damage and sepsis.

  • Type 1 pili bind uroplakins Ia and IIIa → signalling (partly unknown) → invasion and apoptosis.
  • Type 1 pili binding α3β1 integrins → internalisation into superficial facet (umbrella) cells → IBCs ("pods").

Bacterial Adherence

  • Adherence to vaginal and urothelial cells is an essential first step; governed by bacterial adhesins, epithelial receptivity and the fluid bathing both; it helps determine organism, host and site.
  • Adhesins are fimbrial (pili) or afimbrial. A piliated cell carries 100–400 pili, each 5–10 nm wide, up to 2 µm long, built from pilin subunits. Pili are defined by haemagglutination of specific erythrocytes.

  • P pili frequency falls stepwise: pyelonephritis (most, about 90%) > cystitis > ASB > faecal isolates (the classic graded correlation).

Phase Variation of Bacterial Pili in Vivo

  • Phase variation — rapid switching between piliated and non-piliated phases with environment (e.g. broth vs solid medium); occurs in vivo (immunofluorescence of fresh urine shows mixed populations; eFig. 26.7, online only). Strains from different urogenital sites differ in piliation.
  • Sequence of advantage: type 1 pili help initial bladder adherence → become a liability in suspension (enhance apoptosis, phagocytosis and neutrophil killing) → P pili take over in the kidney via glycolipid receptors.

Epithelial Cell Receptivity

Vaginal Cells

  • E. coli adheres more avidly to vaginal cells of women with recurrent cystitis — the first demonstrable biological difference in UTI-susceptible women (Stamey's work).
  • The increased adherence is also present on buccal cells → receptor differences are not vagina-specific and may be genetic.
  • Certain Lewis blood-group phenotypes correlate with rUTI in women.
  • Conclusion: increased epithelial receptivity (introital, urethral, buccal) characterises susceptible women and may be inherited.

Bladder Cells

  • Bladder lumen lined by umbrella cells whose apical surface is a quasi-crystalline array of hexagonal uroplakin complexes (four integral membrane proteins).
  • FimH binds mannosylated uroplakins; UPIa and UPIb bind type 1-piliated UPEC; pilus tips sit in the central cavity of the uroplakin hexamers, and contact triggers local actin rearrangement and membrane protrusion around the bacterium (Fig. 26.8). FimH binding is the initial step of the cascade.
  • FimH–UPIb binding → phosphorylation of UPIII → raised intracellular calcium (immediate urothelial response).
  • Host elimination mechanisms: (1) urothelial exfoliation, (2) exocytosis from infected cells, (3) autophagy.
  • UPEC persistence in the bladder: rapid internalisation into superficial cells (a niche protected from innate immunity) → early IBC: rods about 3 µm, loose random clusters → at 6–8 h growth slows (doubling >60 min), rods shorten to about 0.7 µm, switch to a biofilm-like community.
    • IBC-like cells occur in about a fifth of voided urines from women with E. coli UTI, and such isolates recreate IBCs in mice.
  • Biofilms raise antibiotic resistance by (1) poor antibiotic penetration of matrix, (2) slow growth and altered physiology, (3) expression of factors that inhibit antibiotic activity; they also exclude neutrophils.
  • Fluxing — edge bacteria detach, resume rod form, become motile and escape into the lumen; may become filamentous (≥70 µm), by about 24 h; filaments may evade immunity. Flagellar motility is common in UPEC and aids adherence and dispersal.
  • Escaped bacteria reinvade → second and further IBC rounds → after a few days, quiescence. Exfoliation exposes deeper layers → QIRs in underlying transitional epithelium; urothelial disruption can reactivate them → rUTI from an intracellular bladder reservoir.

Natural Defences of the Urinary Tract

Periurethral and Urethral Region

  • Normal flora of introitus, periurethra and urethra — lactobacilli, CoNS, corynebacteria, streptococci — forms a barrier to uropathogen colonisation.
  • Colonisation shifts with oestrogen and pH, but acute change is most often due to antimicrobials and spermicides that alter flora and increase epithelial receptivity.
  • Colonisation of periurethra/urethra is a prerequisite for most infections, but overcoming urine and bladder defences is pivotal; urethral defence relies on regular urine flow and indigenous flora.

Urine

  • Antibacterial when the inoculum is small. Main inhibitory factors: osmolality, urea, organic acids, pH. Growth is deterred by very dilute urine or high osmolality with low pH; clinically these do not separate susceptible from resistant patients.
  • Uromodulin (Tamm–Horsfall protein) — the most abundant urinary protein, made in the kidney; competes with uroplakins for type 1 pili, blocking adhesion.

Bladder

  • Bacteria probably enter the bladder frequently; whether they establish infection depends partly on the bladder's ability to empty. Bacterial recognition up-regulates antimicrobial peptides from urothelium and resident immune cells.

Other Conditions That Increase Risk of Urinary Tract Infections

Obstruction

  • Obstruction at any level (anatomical or high storage pressure) is a key host susceptibility factor: stasis impairs bladder and renal defences, promotes bacterial growth and adherence.
  • Experimentally the kidney resists haematogenous infection unless the ureter is ligated — then only the obstructed kidney is infected. Obstruction converts cystitis or pyelonephritis into a life-threatening infection.

Vesicoureteral Reflux

  • Association of VUR + UTI with calyceal blunting and parenchymal scarring first described by Hodson and Edwards (1960).
  • Children with high-grade VUR and UTI → progressive scarring, proteinuria, renal failure.
  • Adults — VUR does not reduce renal function unless combined with high storage pressure or stasis and concurrent UTI.

Underlying Disease

  • High incidence of renal scarring with causes of chronic interstitial nephritis, almost all of which damage the renal papilla first: DM, sickle cell disorders, adult nephrocalcinosis, hyperphosphataemia, hypokalaemia, analgesic abuse, sulfonamide nephropathy, gout, heavy-metal poisoning, ageing.

Diabetes Mellitus

  • Increased rUTI from impaired local defence: glycosuria, neutrophil dysfunction, increased uroepithelial adherence; plus incomplete emptying from diabetic neuropathy and chronic kidney disease reducing anti-infective efficacy.
  • More severe infections: acute pyelonephritis, renal abscess, emphysematous pyelonephritis; atypical organisms (yeast); sequelae include papillary necrosis and perinephric abscess.

Renal Papillary Necrosis

  • Role of infection is controversial. Predisposing: DM, analgesic abuse, sickle cell haemoglobinopathy, obstruction (Box 26.2).
  • Spectrum: acute fulminating illness ↔ chronic, incidental. Some pass necrotic tissue chronically; some never do. Retained papillae may calcify (especially with infection) and act as a nidus for chronic infection; opportunistic fungal infection reported.
  • Management: antimicrobials to control infection + early recognition and relief of ureteric obstruction by sloughed papillae limit functional decline.

BOX 26.2 Conditions Associated With Renal Papillary Necrosis

  • Diabetes mellitus; pyelonephritis; urinary tract obstruction; analgesic abuse; sickle cell haemoglobinopathies; renal transplant rejection; cirrhosis; dehydration, hypoxia and jaundice of infants.
  • Miscellaneous: renal vein thrombosis, cryoglobulinaemia, renal candidiasis, contrast injection, amyloidosis, calyceal arteritis, necrotising angiitis, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, hypotensive shock, acute pancreatitis.
  • Data from Eknoyan et al., 1982. Pattern: nearly all compromise medullary blood flow or oxygenation of the papilla. The classic mnemonic group is DM, obstruction/pyelonephritis, analgesics, sickle cell.

Human Immunodeficiency Virus

  • UTIs about fivefold commoner in HIV-positive individuals; more Acinetobacter and Salmonella.
  • AIDS (AIDS-defining illness and/or CD4 <200 cells/mm³) → atypical infection: fungal (Candida, Aspergillus, Cryptococcus neoformans, endemic fungi such as Histoplasma), mycobacterial (tuberculosis [TB] and non-tuberculous), viral (cytomegalovirus, adenovirus).
  • CD4 <200 → Pneumocystis pneumonia prophylaxis, typically TMP-SMX, which also lowers UTI risk (unless sulfa allergy or glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] deficiency).

Renal Transplantation

  • UTIs are among the commonest infections after renal transplant (more than a third of recipients have at least one) and may worsen graft outcome.
  • More resistant organisms and recurrent symptomatic episodes (urinary tract colonisation).
  • Risk factors: age, female sex, long pre-transplant dialysis, urinary tract obstruction.
  • Acute pyelonephritis of the graft is an independent risk factor for functional decline.

Menopause

  • UTI frequency rises after menopause (mechanism and treatment later).

Pregnancy

  • See Bacteriuria in Pregnancy.

Spinal Cord Injury With High-Pressure Bladders

  • No bacteriuric group compares in severity and morbidity with SCI. Nearly all need early catheterisation (spinal shock, poor contractility); long term → poor compliance, high storage pressure → VUR, ureterectasis, hydronephrosis, renal impairment.

KEY POINTS: PATHOGENESIS

  • Most UTIs in women are ascending, from bowel and skin flora.
  • Virulence and host factors determine which bacteria invade and how far.
  • Increased epithelial receptivity predisposes to rUTI and may be genetic.
  • Obstruction and immunocompromise are key host susceptibility factors.

Concept — Pathogenesis is a contest between adhesion and clearance: every bacterial virulence factor (pili, biofilm, intracellular hiding) is a way of resisting being washed out or shed, and every host risk factor (obstruction, stasis, residual urine, altered flora) is a failure of washing out or shedding — which is why obstruction, not organism, most often makes an infection dangerous.

EVALUATION

  • Requires thorough history (urinary symptoms + infection risk factors); examination necessary but often unrevealing; diagnosis depends on a properly collected urine sample; extra tests guided by history.

Physical examination (abdomen and pelvis)

  • Suprapubic distension → raised postvoid residual (PVR); CVAT if pyelonephritis suspected.

  • Men: digital rectal examination (DRE) of prostate.
  • Focused perineal neurological examination (sensation).

Diagnosis by Urine Testing

Urine Collection

  • Contamination reduction improves accuracy; meatal cleansing lacks proven benefit but is universally endorsed.
  • Men: cleanse glans (retract foreskin if uncircumcised) with 2% castile soap towelette → midstream sample in sterile cup.
  • Women: spread labia → cleanse periurethral area → midstream sample. Vaginal epithelial cells and lactobacilli = contaminated sample.

BOX 26.4 Factors That Affect Ability to Provide Adequate Midstream Clean-Catch Sample

  • Increased body mass index (BMI); vaginal atrophy; poor manual dexterity; inability to bear weight; intravaginal pessary; non-sterile collection receptacle.

  • With a pessary, a catheterised sample is preferable.

  • Bedpan or non-sterile cup samples should be disregarded — yet patients are often treated on them regardless of symptoms.

  • Indwelling Foley catheter: remove it and obtain the sample from a newly, sterilely placed catheter — otherwise you culture catheter/bag colonisers.
  • Stoma: never sample the appliance; catheterise (intubate) the stoma.
  • Mid-catheter specimen — more accurate than voided; use when voided sample contaminated (many squamous cells), pessary, cannot give clean catch, or cannot void. Iatrogenic infection risk is low; in patients with equivocal symptoms it roughly halves antibiotic prescribing that a positive voided sample would have triggered.
  • Suprapubic aspiration — highly accurate, samples bladder urine without urethral bacteria; some morbidity, so limited to those unable to void on command (e.g. SCI) — even there, catheterised samples are preferred.

  • Key issues in analysis:
    • Leucocyte esterase — from WBC breakdown → indicates pyuria, not bacteria; common with vaginal contamination.
    • Nitrite — bacterial nitrate reductase converts dietary nitrate. All Enterobacteriaceae produce nitrite (E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Proteus, Citrobacter, Morganella, Salmonella). Non-producers: all Gram-positives and Pseudomonas/Acinetobacter. Low-nitrate diet → false negative.
    • Nitrite: high specificity, low sensitivity. Esterase and nitrite are more reliable at >10⁵ CFU/mL.
    • Cloudy urine = pyuria or phosphate crystals in alkaline/concentrated urine.
    • Analyse promptly; standing urine alkalinises, bacteria multiply, red cells lyse → refrigerate if not analysed within a few hours (unless preservative). Hydration dilutes constituents.
  • Urine dipsticks: most useful to rule out UTI; each parameter has false positives and negatives.
    • In strongly suggestive symptoms a positive dipstick supports starting antibiotics; positive nitrite is the single variable most likely to rule in UTI (with symptoms).
    • Best combination: positive nitrite, or positive leucocyte esterase plus blood.
    • Borderline sensitivity → cannot replace microscopy in symptomatic patients.
    • Errors: expired or badly stored strips; nitrite pad is air-sensitive (open container → future false positives); leucocyte reaction takes up to 5 min (read early → false negative).
  • Urinalysis: automated analysis detects haematuria and bacteria more sensitively than dipstick.
    • Preferred tubes: non-mercuric preservative (chlorhexidine, ethylparaben, sodium propionate) → sample stable up to 72 h without refrigeration.
    • Technique: 6–8 mL → centrifuge 1800–2000 rpm for 4 min → microscopy for red blood cells (RBCs), WBCs, squamous cells, bacteria, crystals, casts. Squamous cells = quality marker.
    • No single constituent is pathognomonic; interpret together. Pyuria = at least 5–10 leucocytes per high-power field (HPF).
    • Culture takes 48–72 h, so urinalysis gives the early answer.
    • Strongest predictors: moderate bacteriuria, moderate pyuria and nitrite. Moderate pyuria (WBC >50) with typical symptoms justifies antibiotics.
    • Other causes of pyuria: GU tuberculosis, urolithiasis, urothelial injury (incl. chlamydial urethritis), interstitial nephritis.
    • No pyuria → question the diagnosis until culture returns; culture positive without pyuria → consider a catheterised sample.
    • False-positive bacteria on microscopy with negative culture — usually vaginal lactobacilli and corynebacteria.
    • A negative automated urinalysis reliably rules out UTI (can obviate culture); ruling in is harder — abnormal parameters occur in healthy asymptomatic women, so urinalysis alone overdiagnoses.
  • Urine culture: the gold standard for bacteriuria, supporting UTI in a symptomatic patient (technique from the 1950s). Collect midstream or catheterised into a vacutainer with freeze-dried preservative (practically eliminates false positives) → blood agar and MacConkey plates.

KEY POINTS: EVALUATION

  • Urine results cannot be interpreted without signs and symptoms.
  • Collect urine to minimise contamination.
  • Formal urinalysis is preferred over dipstick.
  • Culture provides bacterial sensitivities.

Concept — Urine tests are better at excluding than confirming UTI: a negative urinalysis or dipstick is reassuring, but positives are diluted by contamination, colonisation and non-infective pyuria — so the quality of the sample and the presence of symptoms decide what a positive result means.

LOCALISATION

Ureteral Catheterisation

  • Used in bacterial persistence to locate the source: separates upper vs lower tract, one kidney vs the other, and can localise to ectopic ureters or non-refluxing ureteric stumps (saline irrigation) — Stamey's technique.
  • Particularly important with fever, flank pain or CVAT, and in renal transplant recipients to decide whether native kidneys harbour infection.

Stone Cultures

  • Culture removed stones to identify organisms and sensitivities in their interstices; analyse separately from urine — results may be discordant.
  • UTI is the commonest complication after stone procedures; manipulating infected stones can release endotoxin → systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) or fatal urosepsis.
  • Stone culture predicts postoperative sepsis and SIRS better than periprocedural urine culture; a systematic review supports it for selecting antibiotics to prevent future UTI, though its role in preventing postoperative UTI needs further study.

Concept — When bladder urine and the infecting focus can differ, sample the focus: ureteric urine localises the side, and the stone — not the urine — tells you what will cause postoperative sepsis.

IMAGING

  • Not required in most UTIs; clinical and laboratory findings suffice.
  • Image (to find abnormalities needing changed medical management or percutaneous/surgical intervention): most men, compromised hosts, febrile infections, features of obstruction, failure to respond, recurrence pattern suggesting persistence (Box 26.5).
  • UTI with possible obstruction must be evaluated urgently (causes in Box 26.5).
  • DM → emphysematous pyelonephritis, papillary necrosis (impacted papilla → acute obstruction). Polycystic kidneys on dialysis → prone to perinephric abscess.
  • Image if pyelonephritis persists after several days of appropriate antibiotics (renal/perinephric abscess), and with unusual organisms, especially urea-splitters (Proteus) → look for obstructing stones, strictures, fungal balls.
  • Second purpose: find a focus of persistence when bacteriuria fails to resolve or recurs rapidly — uncommon but often surgically correctable (Box 26.6).

BOX 26.5 Indications for Radiologic Investigation in Acute Clinical Pyelonephritis

  • Potential ureteric obstruction (stone, stricture, tumour); history of calculi, especially struvite
  • Potential papillary necrosis (sickle cell, severe DM, analgesic abuse)
  • GU surgery predisposing to obstruction (reimplantation, diversion)
  • Poor response after 5–6 days of appropriate antimicrobials
  • DM; polycystic kidneys on dialysis or with severe renal insufficiency; neuropathic bladder
  • Unusual organisms: TB, fungi, urea-splitters (Proteus)

BOX 26.6 Correctable Urologic Abnormalities That Cause Bacterial Persistence

  • Infection stones; chronic bacterial prostatitis; unilateral infected atrophic kidney; ureteric duplication and ectopic ureters; foreign bodies
  • Urethral diverticula and infected periurethral glands; unilateral medullary sponge kidney; non-refluxing, normal-appearing infected ureteric stumps after nephrectomy
  • Infected urachal cysts; infected communicating calyceal cysts; papillary necrosis; perivesical abscess with fistula to bladder
  • Pattern: each is a nidus that antibiotics cannot sterilise, so each is cured only by removal or drainage.

Radionuclide Studies

  • Rarely used now. Iodine-131 hippuran, technetium-99m (⁹⁹ᵐTc) glucoheptonate and dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA) show focal parenchymal damage, impaired function and reduced perfusion in acute infection.
  • Gallium-67 — useful in pyelonephritis/abscess but positive in non-infective disease too. Indium-111 WBC — limited when the presentation is not suggestive of infection.

Ultrasonography

  • Non-invasive, quick, no radiation or contrast; good for calculi, hydronephrosis, pyonephrosis, perirenal abscess, PVR; can be paired with a plain radiograph for stones.
  • Limits: operator-dependent; poor with obesity, dressings, drains, wounds; less sensitive than computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for parenchymal change in pyelonephritis; inferior to CT for small renal and most ureteric stones and for ureteric obstruction.

Computed Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging

  • Best anatomical detail; more sensitive than excretory urography or ultrasound for acute focal bacterial nephritis (AFBN), renal/perirenal abscess and radiolucent calculi.
  • Non-contrast CT = best test for urinary calculi. Contrast CT localises abscesses and guides (percutaneous) drainage.
  • MRI has not replaced CT for renal inflammation but better shows extrarenal extension. Pelvic MRI with intravenous (IV) contrast = best test for urethral diverticulum.

Voiding Cystourethrogram

  • Voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) assesses VUR — perform in women with febrile UTIs, childhood VUR, or recurrent adult pyelonephritis; consider with rUTI plus hydronephrosis.
  • Double-balloon VCUG for urethral diverticulum is historical; pelvic MRI is now standard.

MICROBIOME

  • Urine is not sterile. 16S ribosomal RNA NGS shows many species in the urine of healthy, asymptomatic people; standard culture detects only fast-growing, oxygen-tolerant organisms and misses fastidious and anaerobic ones.
  • EQUC — larger urine volume, varied atmospheres, longer incubation — grew bacteria from most samples that were negative on standard culture, identifying dozens of genera and species (Table 26.1) → confirms a female urinary microbiome of mostly non-pathogenic organisms.
  • Implications: bacteria will never be eradicated from urine, nor should that be the goal; many microbiome bacteria are protective. Research asks whether dysbiosis underlies urgency incontinence or painful bladder syndrome.
  • Commercial NGS (not FDA-approved): no susceptibility testing, costly, risks inappropriate treatment, and reports go directly to patients without clinician interpretation → confusion; clinical utility still to be established.

KEY POINTS: MICROBIOME

  • Urine is not sterile.
  • The female urinary microbiome, identified by sequencing, should inform UTI management and interpretation of EQUC.

Concept — The microbiome turns the question from "are bacteria present?" to "is this organism causing harm?" — the more sensitive the detection, the more the decision to treat must rest on symptoms rather than on the laboratory report.

  • Landmark trial — Cai et al. KEY POINTS: ASYMPTOMATIC BACTERIURIA

  • Prevalence varies with age, sex and comorbidity.
  • Untreated ASB is not associated with hypertension or renal insufficiency.
  • Guidelines advise not screening or treating except in specific groups.
  • Screen and treat in pregnancy and before urological procedures with anticipated mucosal bleeding.
  • Treating ASB breeds MDR symptomatic UTI.

Concept — ASB is treated only where the bacteria are about to be given a route into blood or tissue — pregnancy (hydroureter and pyelonephritis risk) and mucosa-breaching urological procedures; everywhere else the resident strain is harmless or protective, and eradicating it selects resistance and invites a virulent replacement.

PRINCIPLES OF ANTIMICROBIAL THERAPY

  • Agent and duration depend on spectrum against the known or probable pathogen (by source of acquisition), uncomplicated vs complicated, adverse effects, cost.
  • Serum levels are irrelevant in uncomplicated UTI (urine levels matter) but critical in bacteraemia and febrile parenchymal infection (kidney, prostate).

Which drug level matters

Urinary concentration matters Serum and tissue concentration matter
  • Renal insufficiency: adjust dose of renally cleared drugs; the failing kidney may not concentrate the drug in urine → eradication difficult. Obstruction also lowers urinary drug levels.
  • Consider the drug's effect on bowel and vaginal flora and the hospital ecology; susceptibility varies by exposure and setting.
  • Do not treat a positive culture alone; weigh the likelihood of benefit vs harm. Uncomplicated cystitis rarely progresses even untreated — the goal is symptom relief. In placebo arms of trials, a substantial minority of women were symptom-free or improved within about 9 days, and progression to pyelonephritis was rare.
  • Finucane's framing: ask not whether it is a "real UTI" but whether antibiotics directed at bacteriuria are more likely to benefit than harm.
  • Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) alone vs antibiotics (meta-analysis of randomised trials, non-pregnant women): antibiotics are more effective (moderate evidence); NSAIDs cure a proportion but with higher symptom burden, longer symptoms and more pyelonephritis. Role of analgesics combined with antibiotics remains to be defined.

Ecological Impact and Collateral Damage

  • Collateral damage = ecological adverse effects — resistance, selection and MDR colonisation — linked especially to broad-spectrum cephalosporins and quinolones.
  • Particularly relevant to uncomplicated cystitis because (1) minimal risk of invasion/sepsis and (2) it is one of the commonest antibiotic exposures in healthy people.
  • Resistance rises from microbial traits, antibiotic selection pressure and spread; varies geographically.

Nitrofurantoin

  • Active against most common uropathogens; rapidly excreted in urine, but no therapeutic tissue levels (including gut) → useless for upper tract, complicated or bloodstream infection.
  • Minimal effect on bowel and vaginal flora; acquired resistance exceedingly low.
  • Adverse: GI upset; rare pulmonary fibrosis with chronic use; haemolytic anaemia in G6PD deficiency.
  • Renal threshold (table below): caution in significant impairment, especially long courses — in a large cohort CrCl 30–50 did not reduce efficacy, but CrCl <50 predicted pulmonary adverse events needing admission.

Trimethoprim–Sulfamethoxazole

  • Widely used; therapeutic levels in most tissues; covers most uropathogens except Enterococcus and Pseudomonas; cheap; minimal bowel flora effect.
  • Adverse: common rash and GI upset; rare Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis — TMP-SMX is the antibiotic most often implicated.

Fosfomycin

  • Bactericidal, oral and IV, phosphonic acid–like; active against most uropathogens.
  • Advantages: limited cross-resistance, activity against most Gram-negatives and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE), single-dose efficacy in empirical uncomplicated cystitis → excellent oral option for MDR organisms that would otherwise need IV therapy.
  • Well tolerated (low GI upset, headache). Traditionally not for urosepsis, though emerging data suggest a role in bacteraemic MDR E. coli UTI. Cost (3 g sachet) may limit use.

Fluoroquinolones

  • Descend from nalidixic acid; inhibit DNA gyrase; broad spectrum — Enterobacteriaceae and P. aeruginosa, high activity against S. aureus and S. saprophyticus, marginal streptococcal cover; most anaerobes resistant → vaginal and bowel flora spared.
  • Resistance has risen with indiscriminate use.
  • Not nephrotoxic, but renal insufficiency prolongs half-life → adjust dose at CrCl <30 mL/min.
  • Adverse: GI (commonest; commensal disruption), hypersensitivity, skin reactions, serious central and peripheral nervous system effects, acute renal failure; anxiety, depression, hallucinations, confusion, suicidal thoughts.
  • Achilles tendinopathy/rupture — class effect; stop at the first sign of tendon pain. Risk: advanced age, chronic renal failure, male sex, normal BMI, corticosteroids. Mechanism: ciprofloxacin stimulates fibroblast matrix-degrading proteases and inhibits matrix synthesis. Also paraesthesiae, myalgia, arthralgia.
  • Cartilage damage in immature animals → contraindicated in children, adolescents, pregnancy and breastfeeding.
  • Interactions (warfarin — a World Health Organization [WHO] warning — antacids, theophylline): see Table 26.6.

Cephalosporins

  • Five generations; as a group good against Enterobacteriaceae, poor against enterococci.
  • 1st — more Gram-positive activity plus E. coli, K. pneumoniae; 2nd — anaerobic activity; 3rd — most reliable β-lactams against community and nosocomial Gram-negatives; 4th/5th — usable in complicated UTI.
  • β-lactams are for uncomplicated cystitis only when first-line agents cannot be used.

Carbapenems

  • β-lactams, broad spectrum; bind and inactivate penicillin-binding proteins; stable to most β-lactamases including AmpC and ESBLs; cover many Gram-positive, Gram-negative and anaerobic organisms.
  • Gaps: E. faecium and MRSA. Ertapenem additionally lacks P. aeruginosa and Enterococcus cover.

Aminoglycosides

  • With TMP-SMX or ampicillin, effective for febrile UTI, especially in hospital; nephro- and ototoxicity → monitor renal and auditory function.
  • Once-daily dosing exploits concentration-dependent killing (peak:MIC), time-dependent toxicity and a prolonged post-antibiotic effect.
  • Regimen: fixed 5–7 mg/kg gentamicin or tobramycin; interval from one serum level plotted on a nomogram (Fig. 26.12) — e.g. 7 mg/L at 10 h → redose 7 mg/kg every 36 h. Effective, less nephrotoxic, cheaper (fewer levels and staff time).

Aminopenicillins

  • Resistance in 40–60% of urinary isolates has limited ampicillin/amoxicillin; they disturb bowel and vaginal flora → resistant reinfection and candidal vaginitis.
  • Clavulanate restores activity against β-lactamase producers, but cost and GI effects limit it.
  • Extended-spectrum penicillins (pivmecillinam, piperacillin, mezlocillin, azlocillin) keep enterococcal activity and add many ampicillin-resistant Gram-negatives → suited to nosocomial UTI and initial parenteral therapy of community pyelonephritis, though cheaper agents may be equal.

Aztreonam

  • Spectrum similar to aminoglycosides, not nephrotoxic (as all β-lactams), but narrower than 3rd-generation cephalosporins; use mainly in penicillin allergy.

Pivmecillinam

  • Prodrug of mecillinam, penicillin-like β-lactam; high Gram-negative activity; used mainly in Nordic countries for empirical uncomplicated cystitis; not available in the USA; very low E. coli resistance (about 2%), safe and effective.

Concept — The formulary is organised by a single trade-off: spectrum and tissue penetration buy efficacy in complicated infection but cost collateral damage — so the narrow, urine-confined, flora-sparing agents (nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, pivmecillinam) lead in cystitis, and the broad, tissue-penetrating agents (fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, carbapenems) are held back for infections that invade.

Choice of Antimicrobial Agents

  • Empirical choice: complicated vs uncomplicated, spectrum vs probable pathogen, hypersensitivity, renal/hepatic toxicity, ecological impact, cost; use regional antibiograms.
  • C. difficile infection (CDI): increasingly common and resistant; large US burden and mortality.
    • Most likely culprits: cephalosporins, clindamycin, carbapenems, TMP-SMX, fluoroquinolones, penicillin combinations. Not associated: macrolides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides.
    • Diagnosis requires both (1) diarrhoea (≥3 unformed stools in 24 h) or radiological ileus/toxic megacolon, and (2) stool positive for toxigenic C. difficile or its toxins, or histological/colonoscopic pseudomembranous colitis.
    • Treatment by severity and recurrence risk. The text's older statement (metronidazole for mild, vancomycin for severe, as metronidazole fails more with severity) is superseded by the 2021 IDSA/Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America (SHEA) focused update (Table 26.7), which adds fidaxomicin (macrolide) and bezlotoxumab (monoclonal antibody to toxin B).

Duration of Therapy

  • Determined by extent and duration of tissue invasion, urinary bacterial concentration, achievable urinary drug level, and host risk factors.

KEY POINTS: PRINCIPLES OF ANTIMICROBIAL THERAPY

  • Antimicrobial resistance is increasing.
  • Selection weighs efficacy, safety, cost and compliance.
  • Agent and duration are critical to limiting resistance and adverse events.

Concept — Resistance is manufactured mainly in the bowel, not the bladder: plasmid transfer among gut flora dwarfs mutant selection in urine, so the ecological footprint of an antibiotic — breadth, duration, repetition — matters more than its MIC against the organism in front of you.

BLADDER INFECTIONS

Uncomplicated Cystitis

  • Acute uncomplicated cystitis is the overwhelming majority of UTIs and is often managed by non-urologists.
  • Annually about one in ten women reports a UTI, and most women have at least one in their lifetime; incidence is low before puberty and rises sharply in late adolescence and the second to fourth decades; about a third of women have had a physician-diagnosed UTI by age 26.
  • E. coli causes 75–90% of acute cystitis in young women; S. saprophyticus (a skin commensal) is the next common cause; less often Klebsiella, Proteus, Enterococcus. In men, E. coli and other Enterobacteriaceae.

Clinical Presentation

  • The validated UTI Symptom Assessment questionnaire scores seven symptoms — frequency, urgency, painful urination, incomplete emptying, suprapubic pain/pressure, low back pain, haematuria — for presence, severity and bother.
  • Cystitis is a superficial mucosal infection → fever and chills are usually absent.
  • Malodorous or turbid urine alone is not a reliable sign of UTI → advise hydration and reassess.
  • More than one irritative symptom raises the likelihood, but dysuria alone is the most reliable single indicator and drives the highest severity and bother scores.
  • In baseline voiding dysfunction, establish how symptoms differ from baseline. Distinguish dysuria (burning on voiding) from constant vaginal/pelvic burning (painful bladder syndrome, genitourinary syndrome of menopause [GSM]).
  • Dysuria + frequency without vaginal discharge or irritation predicts UTI in about 90%; probability rises further with haematuria, back pain and CVAT.
  • Symptom meta-analysis: frequency, dysuria, haematuria, nocturia, urgency increase probability; vaginal discharge decreases it; haematuria + dipstick nitrite → probability ≥90%.
  • Bottom line: acute-onset dysuria and a change from baseline voiding symptoms best indicate acute uncomplicated cystitis.

Laboratory Diagnosis

  • As in Evaluation (urine testing).

Management

  • Antimicrobial selection (IDSA/European Society for Microbiology and Infectious Diseases [ESCMID] guideline, Fig. 26.13): individualise by allergy, compliance, local antibiogram/practice, availability, cost.

Follow-Up

  • No follow-up visit or culture in asymptomatic women after treatment; no urological evaluation if they respond.
  • UTI in most men is complicated and linked to functional/anatomical pathology — in a small cohort of men evaluated after UTI, about half had an abnormality, commonest incomplete emptying.
  • Non-response → microbiological and urological evaluation for unresolved/complicated UTI.
  • Compliance with recommended agents: about half of surveyed physicians did not know the IDSA guideline and nearly a third did not choose a first-line agent → education and outreach needed.

  • Occur in a compromised urinary tract or with a resistant pathogen (Table 26.8); complicating factors may be obvious from severity/history or revealed only by treatment failure.

Emphysematous Cystitis

  • Rare, potentially life-threatening complicated cystitis; mortality up to 7%. Pathognomonic: gas within the bladder wall on cross-sectional imaging.
  • Typical patient: elderly woman (60–70 years) with poorly controlled DM; also incomplete emptying/stasis (NGB, BOO).
  • Organisms: E. coli (about 60%), K. pneumoniae (10–20%).
  • Clinical presentation: asymptomatic (up to 7%) to overt sepsis; abdominal pain commonest (about 80%), then gross haematuria (about 60%), obstructive symptoms; fever in only 30–50%.
  • Radiological findings: plain film — curvilinear radiolucency outlining the bladder wall, separate from rectal gas; "cobblestone" or "beaded necklace" pattern of submucosal blebs. CT is required to confirm and exclude other pelvic gas (fistula, trauma, gangrene of adjacent organs) (Fig. 26.14: intramural and intraluminal gas).
  • Management: about 90% medical alone — (often IV) antibiotics with broad Gram-negative cover, bladder drainage, control of DM → narrow by culture. Gram-positive cocci on Gram stain → add ampicillin/amoxicillin (enterococcus). Surgery rare — poor response or severe necrosis: debridement, partial or total cystectomy.

Concept — Emphysematous cystitis is diabetic stasis cystitis with gas as its signature — it is managed like any severe complicated cystitis (drain, treat, control glucose), and CT's real job is to prove the gas is intramural rather than from a fistula or gangrene that would need surgery.

Unresolved Urinary Tract Infections

Clinical Presentation

  • Unresolved = initial therapy failed to eliminate symptoms and/or bacterial growth. If symptoms persist at the end of treatment or recur shortly after → urinalysis and culture with susceptibilities; if symptoms are significant and not relieved by urinary analgesics, start broad-spectrum empirical therapy pending culture.

Recurrent Urinary Tract Infections

  • Recurrent UTI (rUTI) = 2 UTIs in 6 months or ≥3 in 12 months.
  • Common in healthy women — about a fifth of college women have another culture-proven UTI within 6 months; 20–30% recurrence overall. A recent prior UTI is one of the strongest risk factors.

Evaluation

  • Aim: prevent recurrence by identifying risk factors and modifiable behaviour; exclude external anatomical or functional abnormality (if present → complicated).
  • History: number, frequency, culture results, symptoms, triggers; pneumaturia, faecaluria, obstipation, diverticulitis, pelvic surgery or radiotherapy → fistula; bowel symptoms (constipation, diarrhoea, faecal incontinence); prior treatments and response.
  • Risk factors in Box 26.11. Nonoxynol-9 kills lactobacilli but not E. coli; spermicide-coated condoms increase UTI.

Bacterial Persistence

  • Failure of an appropriate course for culture-proven acute infection → suspect persistence — rare in rUTI but critical to find; requires cystoscopy and imaging.
  • Usually a correctable urological abnormality (Box 26.6) where antibiotics cannot penetrate or eliminate the nidus; surgical removal can cure (Stamey).
  • Work-up: cystoscopy, ultrasound, CT; VCUG for VUR or non-refluxing ureteric stump; pelvic MRI = standard for urethral diverticulum; retrograde urethrography for suspected stricture in men.
  • Struvite (infection) stones are one of the commonest causes. Urease-producing organisms (P. mirabilis) alkalinise urine → precipitation of calcium, magnesium, ammonium and phosphate → branched struvite stones. Bacteria shelter within the stone — post-treatment urine may be sterile, misleadingly. Oxalate and apatite stones may be secondarily colonised. Risk: chronic catheters, urinary diversion, stasis.
    • AUA (Medical Management of Kidney Stones): surgically remove infection stones in all patients fit for intervention. Non-surgical candidates with residual/recurrent struvite → acetohydroxamic acid + antibiotic suppression.
  • Non-obstructing stones with rUTI — controversial: half of patients remain infection-free a year after complete stone extraction; recurrence more likely with type 2 DM, hypertension, Black race. AUA permits observation of asymptomatic non-obstructing stones ≤10 mm. For stones <6 mm with UTI, minimise recurrences before considering removal. Whether clearing small stones reduces rUTI is unknown.

Management

  • Most women with rUTI have no correctable abnormality. Options in order: behavioural modification → non-antibiotic therapy → antibiotic treatment/prophylaxis as a last resort.
  • AUA 2019 guideline on recurrent uncomplicated UTI in women (revalidated 2021); EAU, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and other societies' guidelines conflict on prevention → confusion.
  • Quality of life (QoL): patients fear the next UTI; anxiety, fragmented care from providers unaware of guidelines. Clinicians should educate and set a protocol for future episodes; patients increasingly understand antibiotic risks and want stewardship and mental-health acknowledgement. Education/counselling on risk factors reduced symptomatic recurrence and improved QoL.
  • Antibiotic resistance: the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists it among the most serious health threats — millions of resistant infections and tens of thousands of US deaths annually; similar European burden; hospital-acquired MDR infections rose during COVID-19.
  • Antibiotic stewardship programmes improve prescribing, outcomes, adverse effects and susceptibility; a Cochrane review showed better guideline compliance and shorter courses → UTI management must follow stewardship (Fig. 26.15).

  • Behavioural modification — low risk, supports bladder health:

    • Hydration: flushes adherent bacteria and sheds urothelial cells. Landmark trial (Hooton et al., 2018): premenopausal women with rUTI who added ≥1.5 L water daily had about half as many cystitis episodes over a year (1.7 vs 3.2). Meta-analysis: fewer patients with rUTI at ≤6 months with increased fluids (water, juices, D-mannose in water).
    • Frequent, complete voiding; void after (and, where possible, before) intercourse — intercourse causes transient bacteriuria.
    • Avoid spermicides (nonoxynol-9) — deplete vaginal flora and raise pH.
    • Pubic shaving and thong underwear: no evidence.
  • Self-start therapy — patient starts antibiotics at symptom onset, often without a sample:
    • Older studies suggested patients diagnose accurately; originally required a sample and follow-up — that expectation has eroded.
    • Fluoroquinolones were once "ideal" self-start agents; the practice perpetuates overuse; anxious patients treat minor changes; in the authors' view risks outweigh benefits — not sustainable.
    • Preferred approach: at symptom onset → urinary analgesic + urine sample + observation → IDSA-recommended agent (per antibiogram) only if symptoms persist, tailored to culture. Many episodes are transient and non-bacterial.
  • Low-dose continuous prophylaxis:
    • Rationale: eradicates uropathogens from introital and bowel reservoirs without (it was thought) fostering resistance. Older trials (TMP-SMX, TMP, nitrofurantoin, cephalexin, fluoroquinolones) for 6–12 months reduced UTIs; some noted more non–E. coli UTIs afterwards.
    • Cochrane 2004 (randomised controlled trials [RCTs]; latest 1995): 6–12 months of prophylaxis reduces clinical and microbiological recurrence while taken, with more adverse effects; after stopping, recurrence reverts to placebo levels. Postcoital prophylaxis is effective and preferable to daily in intercourse-related rUTI.
    • Caveats: poor-quality, decades-old trials, inconsistent definitions, no agreed duration, pre-dating current resistance; long-term suppression drives MDR at a societal level.
    • AUA: clinicians "may prescribe" prophylaxis after weighing risks, benefits and alternatives. The authors (and the EAU) regard long-term daily prophylaxis as a last resort; patients wanting indefinite prophylaxis must be told there are no long-term data and of toxicity and MDR risks.
    • Postcoital prophylaxis is acceptable with a clear intercourse–UTI link when other measures fail; choose by prior cultures, allergy, local resistance, ecology.
    • Nitrofurantoin — popular (urine-concentrated, broad, little resistance) but pulmonary fibrosis, renal toxicity and neurotoxicity with long-term use are rarely discussed; a meta-analysis found it as effective as norfloxacin, TMP, TMP-SMX, cefaclor, oestriol or methenamine but with more (mainly GI) adverse effects.

  • Non-antibiotic management — the focus of the last decade's research; a meta-analysis found OM-89, Urovac, vaginal oestrogen, cranberry and acupuncture of varying benefit; oral oestrogen and lactobacilli ineffective; vitamin C ineffective. Head-to-head trials against antibiotic prophylaxis (the gold standard) and standardised products are needed.

  • Cranberry:

    • Active ingredient type A proanthocyanidin (PAC) — blocks P-fimbrial adhesion of E. coli to uroepithelium; may also act via gut microbiota.
    • Cochrane reviews conflict: 2008 — effective in women with rUTI (not in older people or catheterised patients); 2012 — no significant overall benefit, including rUTI subgroup, though equivalent to antibiotic prophylaxis in two trials; 2023 (largest) — supports use in women with rUTI, children, and after interventions.
    • Problems: heterogeneous products/doses, high dropout with juice, PAC content rarely reported.
    • Dose: 36 mg PAC daily effective; 72 mg may be better (dose-dependent antiadhesion in urine 6 h after dosing, Fig. 26.16); antiadhesion is short-lived → take twice daily; high-dose PAC (2 × 18.5 mg) helped women with <5 UTIs/year.
    • Nursing-home trial of 72 mg PAC: no benefit — but most had no prior bacteriuria/rUTI, samples were voided not catheterised, and PVR/incontinence unmeasured → not grounds to abandon cranberry in all women.
    • Other meta-analyses: benefit, especially after rUTI and gynaecological surgery; combinations with lactobacilli, D-mannose, propolis add nothing.
    • Warfarin: FDA warning removed 2011; international normalised ratio (INR) interaction not substantiated → safe with warfarin.
    • AUA supports offering cranberry; authors recommend ≥36 mg PAC.

  • Oestrogen:

    • Postmenopausal reinfection linked to residual urine (prolapse) and oestrogen loss → atrophic epithelium, raised vaginal pH, loss of lactobacilli, E. coli colonisation.
    • Landmark trial — Raz and Stamm (1993), intravaginal oestriol vs placebo: UTI rate reduced about 90% (0.5 vs 5.9 episodes per patient-year); mechanism return of lactobacilli and fall in vaginal pH; more dropouts on oestriol (local burning, itching, irritation). Oestradiol vaginal ring also reduced UTIs.
    • Cochrane 2008: vaginal oestrogen effective; oral oestrogen not (AUA concurs). Benefit takes ≥12 weeks. Adverse: breast tenderness, discharge, spotting, local irritation. All forms (cream, tablet, ring) improve atrophy; recent meta-analysis and a multicentre placebo RCT confirm vaginal (not oral) oestrogen reduces rUTI.

    • Boxed warnings apply to all routes and are not supported for vaginal use; low-dose vaginal preparations keep plasma levels within normal postmenopausal range.
    • Observational data (incl. Women's Health Initiative Observational Study): no increased fracture, breast cancer, other cancer or cardiovascular disease.

  • Laser therapy for GSM (fractional CO₂, erbium:YAG; collagen stimulation): small observational studies; efficacy and safety for GSM unestablished; no data for rUTI.
  • Probiotics:
    • Lactobacilli are the dominant vaginal/periurethral commensal; some strains (e.g. hydrogen peroxide producers) interfere with uropathogens.
    • Early products (Lactobacillus GC drink; L. casei v rhamnosus suppository) failed; L. crispatus vaginal suppositories and oral L. rhamnosus GR-1/L. reuteri RC-14 more promising.
    • Cochrane 2015 and later meta-analyses: not recommended (small, flawed trials). Strain, dose and route unknown.
  • Methenamine:
    • Used for about a century; 70–90% renally excreted; in acid urine (pH <6) converts to ammonia and formaldehyde (bactericidal/bacteriostatic by concentration). Optimal formaldehyde in vitro at pH <5.85; whether acidification (vitamin C 1–4 g) is needed in vivo is unknown.
    • Two FDA-approved salts: hippurate (1 g twice daily) and mandelate. Early placebo RCTs: substantial (56–75%) reduction in rUTI.
    • Acid salts are non-specifically bacteriostatic → ineffective against urease producers (which alkalinise urine).
    • Adverse: GI upset, dysuria, rash, raised liver enzymes. Contraindicated: CrCl <50 mL/min, severe liver disease, hypersensitivity; avoid with sulfonamides, antacids, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.
    • Cochrane 2012 (hippurate): heterogeneous; benefit in normal urinary tracts, especially short-term (≤1 week) therapy; no benefit with anatomical abnormalities; adverse effects infrequent (nausea, diarrhoea).
    • Recent: high success in a tertiary rUTI cohort; RCT: methenamine hippurate non-inferior to daily antibiotic prophylaxis over 12 months, cheaper and with less resistance; another small RCT: as effective as TMP.
    • Formaldehyde carcinogenicity relates to nasopharyngeal, sinonasal and lymphohaematopoietic cancers, not urothelial → bladder cancer risk is theoretical.

KEY POINTS: RECURRENT URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS

  • Thorough history: prior symptoms, urinalysis/culture results, triggers.
  • Identify and eradicate sources of persistence.
  • Imaging and cystoscopy are not recommended for recurrent uncomplicated UTI.
  • Prevention should focus on non-antibiotic interventions.

Concept — Recurrent UTI in women is a problem of reinfection in a receptive host, not of a hidden focus — so investigation is reserved for features of persistence or complication, and prevention targets the introital and bladder environment (flora, pH, adhesion, washout) rather than repeatedly sterilising the urine.

KIDNEY INFECTIONS

Acute Pyelonephritis

  • Defined as inflammation of kidney and renal pelvis, but diagnosed clinically; upper tract infection can be proven by ureteric catheterisation or bladder washout, which are impractical and unnecessary in most; no non-invasive localisation test is fully reliable.

Clinical Presentation

  • Spectrum: Gram-negative sepsis ↔ cystitis with mild flank pain.
  • Classic: abrupt chills, fever ≥38 °C (100.4 °F), unilateral or bilateral flank/costovertebral angle pain or tenderness; lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) often absent.
  • Symptoms correlate poorly with the true site of infection (localisation studies by ureteric catheterisation and bladder washout).
  • Deep CVAT on examination. May mimic GI disease (abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea).
  • Asymptomatic progression to chronic pyelonephritis can occur in compromised hosts; acute renal failure is rare.

Laboratory Diagnosis

  • Neutrophil leucocytosis; urinalysis — numerous WBCs, often clumped, bacterial rods or chains of cocci.
  • Granular or leucocyte casts suggest pyelonephritis; a cast containing bacteria in its matrix is specific (Fig. 26.21).
  • Blood: raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine (if renal failure), reduced CrCl; blood cultures may be positive.

Bacteriology

  • Urine cultures positive, though a minority have <10⁵ CFU/mL and a negative urine Gram stain.
  • E. coli (a special virulent subgroup) causes most cases. Without VUR, patients of P blood-group phenotype are susceptible to recurrent pyelonephritis from P-piliated E. coli binding P antigen receptors. Much community pyelonephritis comes from a few resistant clonal groups.
  • Suspect resistant species (Proteus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, Serratia, Enterobacter, Citrobacter) with rUTI, hospitalisation, catheters, recent instrumentation.
  • Gram-positives rarely cause pyelonephritis except E. faecalis, S. epidermidis, S. aureus.
  • Blood cultures if systemic toxicity, hospitalisation, or risk factors such as pregnancy.

Renal Ultrasonography, Computed Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging

  • Ultrasound: focal swelling, increased or decreased echogenicity (Fig. 26.22: echogenic, swollen upper pole).
  • CT/MRI: focal swelling, diminished, heterogeneous enhancement (Fig. 26.23: patchy); unenhanced CT — reduced density; MRI — restricted diffusion.
  • Changes usually resolve, though chronic scarring can follow.

Differential Diagnosis

  • Appendicitis, diverticulitis, pancreatitis — similar pain intensity, different location, usually normal urine.
  • Herpes zoster — superficial loin pain without urinary symptoms; obvious once the rash appears.

Management

  • Initial management — three groups:

  • Urine Gram stain guides initial choice; therapy must reach renal tissue and urine.

Acute Focal or Multifocal Bacterial Nephritis

  • Uncommon, severe renal infection with dense leucocyte infiltrate in one lobe (focal) or several (multifocal).
  • Clinical presentation: like pyelonephritis but more severe; about half diabetic; sepsis common; leucocytosis, Gram-negative UTI, about half bacteraemic. AFBN lies midway between pyelonephritis and renal abscess.
  • Radiological findings: diagnosis is radiological; lesion slightly less dense than surrounding parenchyma.
    • Ultrasound: poorly marginated, relatively sonolucent mass with low-amplitude echoes disrupting the corticomedullary junction (the Fig. 26.25 example is slightly more echogenic than normal parenchyma; lesions resolve after antibiotics).
    • CT requires contrast (barely visible unenhanced): wedge-shaped zones of reduced enhancement, no wall, no liquefaction.
    • Gallium uptake larger than the mass. Multifocal disease — same findings in several lobes.

Emphysematous Pyelonephritis

  • Urological emergency — acute necrotising parenchymal and perirenal infection by gas-forming organisms.
  • Pathogenesis unclear: high tissue glucose fermented to CO₂ (e.g. by E. coli) is proposed, but does not explain its rarity among the many diabetic Gram-negative UTIs or non-diabetic cases.
  • Besides DM, many have obstruction (calculi, papillary necrosis) and significant renal impairment. Mortality about 19–43% historically.

Clinical Presentation

  • Adults (juvenile diabetics not at risk); women more than men.
  • Severe acute pyelonephritis, sometimes after chronic infection; classic triad: fever, vomiting, flank pain.
  • Occurs in insulin-dependent and non-insulin-dependent diabetics, even without obstruction; non-diabetics usually have ureteric obstruction and less extensive disease.
  • Pneumaturia absent unless the collecting system is involved. Urine culture invariably positive — E. coli commonest; Klebsiella, Proteus less so.

Radiological Findings

  • Plain film: mottled gas over the kidney (often mistaken for bowel gas); a crescent of gas over the upper pole is more distinctive; later gas extends to perinephric space and retroperitoneum (Fig. 26.26).
  • Ultrasound: strong focal echoes of intraparenchymal gas.
  • CT is the imaging of choice — defines extent, guides management (Fig. 26.27).

  • Most are septic → fluid resuscitation, glucose and electrolyte control, broad-spectrum antibiotics; relieve obstruction by nephrostomy or stent.
  • Adverse prognostic factors: hypoalbuminaemia, shock at presentation, bacteraemia, need for haemodialysis, thrombocytopenia, altered mental status, polymicrobial infection.

  • Early diagnosis and treatment of UTI in diabetics may prevent progression and nephrectomy.

Concept — Fluid is a good sign and streaky gas a bad one: a contained, liquefied collection can be drained, whereas diffuse streaky gas without fluid means parenchyma is necrosing faster than the host can wall it off — the same principle that moved treatment from routine nephrectomy to drainage with nephrectomy reserved for diffuse destruction.

  • Collection of pus confined to the renal parenchyma.

  • Risk factors: UTI, calculi, DM (DM and longer hospital stay very significant); link with VUR less clear than for pyelonephritis.

Clinical Presentation

  • Fever, chills, abdominal or flank pain, sometimes weight loss and malaise, ± cystitis symptoms; may be vague, delaying diagnosis (historically to exploration or autopsy).
  • History may reveal a Gram-positive source 1–8 weeks earlier (skin carbuncles, IV drug use) or recent UTI/pyelonephritis. Complicated UTI (stasis, calculi, pregnancy, NGB, DM) predisposes.
  • Even with normal tracts, nearly all have a predisposing factor: DM, HIV, steroids, IV drug use, liver disease, UTI, pregnancy.

Laboratory Diagnosis

  • Marked leucocytosis. Blood cultures positive in only about a quarter to a third; urine, blood and abscess isolates rarely match (few concordant).
  • Pyuria and bacteriuria may be absent unless the abscess communicates with the collecting system. Haematogenous Gram-positive abscesses → urine sterile or a different organism.
  • Ultrasound — quickest, cheapest: echo-free or low-echo space-occupying lesion with increased through-transmission (Fig. 26.28); margins indistinct acutely with oedematous surrounding parenchyma, later well-defined; internal echoes vary with debris; air → strong echo with shadow. Abscess vs tumour may be hard to separate.
  • CT — diagnostic procedure of choice: well defined before and after contrast. Early: renal enlargement, rounded low-attenuation foci (Fig. 26.29); after days, thick fibrotic wall; chronic: obliterated tissue planes, thickened Gerota's fascia, low-attenuation mass with an enhancing "ring sign" (vascular wall) (Fig. 26.30).

  • Classic treatment percutaneous or open drainage, but IV antibiotics and observation are appropriate for small abscesses (≤3 cm) in stable patients; early antibiotics may obviate intervention.
  • CT/ultrasound-guided aspiration to distinguish abscess from cystic tumour; culture the aspirate.

Infected Hydronephrosis and Pyonephrosis

  • Infected hydronephrosis = bacterial infection of a hydronephrotic kidney. Pyonephrosis = infected hydronephrosis with suppurative parenchymal destruction and total or near-total loss of function (Fig. 26.31: thinned cortex/medulla, pus-distended pelvicalyceal system, distal ureteric obstruction). The boundary is clinically indistinct; rapid diagnosis and treatment prevent permanent loss of function and sepsis.
  • Clinical presentation: usually very ill — high fever, chills, flank pain and tenderness; occasionally only fever and vague GI discomfort. Prior calculi, infection or surgery common. Bacteriuria may be absent if the ureter is completely obstructed.
  • Radiological findings: ultrasound — internal echoes in the dependent part of a dilated collecting system; fluid–debris levels; focal hypoechoic areas in the parenchyma suggest pyonephrosis. CT non-specific — thick pelvis, perirenal stranding, striated nephrogram (Fig. 26.32: echogenic septa, debris-filled calyces; antegrade pyelogram shows pus-filled calyces, scarred pelvis, stone — kidney did not recover).
  • Management: antibiotics + drainage of the infected pelvis by ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy; once stable, identify and treat the cause of obstruction.

Concept — An infected, obstructed kidney is an abscess with a collecting system — antibiotics cannot sterilise it until it is drained, and a sterile urine may simply mean the ureter is completely blocked.

Perinephric Abscess

  • Beyond the renal capsule but within Gerota's fascia. Sources: rupture of a cortical abscess, extravasation of infected urine from obstruction, haematogenous seeding (about a third, usually skin). Pyonephrosis with calculus predisposes; about a third are diabetic. Infected perirenal haematoma (haematogenous or direct).
  • Rupture through Gerota's fascia → paranephric abscess; paranephric abscess also arises from bowel, pancreas or pleura; conversely perinephric/psoas abscess may follow bowel perforation, Crohn disease, thoracolumbar osteomyelitis.
  • Organisms: E. coli, Proteus, S. aureus.

Clinical Presentation

  • Insidious; symptoms >5 days in most; resembles pyelonephritis but more than a third are afebrile; flank or abdominal mass in about half; CVAT usual. Limp with flexed, externally rotated hip → psoas abscess.
  • Labs: leucocytosis, raised creatinine, pyuria. Urine culture predicts the abscess organism in only about a third; polymicrobial blood culture suggests perinephric abscess but identifies all organisms in under half; another series found urine the most sensitive → treat culture results with caution.
  • Pyelonephritis responds in 4–5 days; perinephric abscess does not → suspect with UTI plus flank mass or fever persisting after 4 days of antibiotics. Often coexists with renal abscess.
  • CT shows the primary abscess, extension to flank or psoas (Fig. 26.33), route of spread and obstruction (Fig. 26.34: pararenal abscess involving adrenal) → plans drainage. Ultrasound: anechoic mass displacing kidney ↔ echogenic collection blending with perirenal fat; can delineate collections outside Gerota's fascia.
  • Mortality fell from 40–50% to about 12% with imaging, yet diagnosis still lags by about 3–4 days and only about a third are correctly diagnosed at presentation — delay contributes to nearly all deaths → keep a low threshold for imaging.

Management

  • Immediate antibiotics, guided by Gram stain: aminoglycoside + antistaphylococcal penicillin (methicillin/oxacillin); penicillin allergy → cephalosporin or vancomycin.
  • Small (<3 cm) abscesses in immunocompetent patients may resolve with antibiotics alone; delay and size >3 cm predict failure and death → prompt diagnosis and drainage of collections >3 cm.
  • Larger or non-responding → intervention: classic surgical drainage or nephrectomy (non-functioning/severely infected kidney), now largely CT/ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage; the threshold is lower than for renal abscess.
  • Then treat the underlying cause — cortical abscess or enteric communication promptly; nephrectomy for pyonephrosis at the same time if feasible, otherwise staged. A proportion of large drained abscesses still need nephrectomy for non-functioning kidneys — but far fewer than historically.

Xanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis

  • Rare, severe, chronic infection → diffuse renal destruction; mostly unilateral, enlarged, non-functioning kidney with obstruction from stones; lipid-laden foamy macrophages; starts in pelvis and calyces, then destroys parenchyma and adjacent tissue.
  • Imitates almost every renal inflammatory disease and renal cell carcinoma (RCC) radiologically; on frozen section mimics clear cell carcinoma → unnecessary radical nephrectomy.
  • Under 1% of histologically documented chronic pyelonephritis, but up to about a fifth of nephrectomies for pyelonephritis.

Pathogenesis

  • Triad: nephrolithiasis, obstruction, infection. Stones in up to about 80%, about half staghorn.
  • Obstruction then E. coli infection reproduces tissue destruction and lipid collection by macrophages (xanthoma cells) — sheets around abscesses and calyces with lymphocytes, giant cells, plasma cells.
  • Bacteria of low virulence (spontaneous bacteraemia rare). Other factors: venous occlusion and haemorrhage, abnormal lipid metabolism, lymphatic blockage, failed antibiotic therapy, altered immunity, ischaemia.
  • No single cause: an inadequate acute inflammatory response in an obstructed, ischaemic or necrotic kidney.

Pathology

  • Massively enlarged kidney with normal contour. Diffuse (>80%) or segmental (parenchyma around one or more calyces, or one pole of a duplex).
  • Cut surface: stones, peripelvic fibrosis, dilated pus-filled calyces, pelvis not dilated (encased by fibrosis), papillary necrosis; advanced — multiple abscesses with viscous pus lined by yellow tissue, thin cortex replaced by xanthogranulomatous tissue, thickened capsule, frequent perinephric/paranephric extension (Fig. 26.37A).
  • Microscopy: yellow nodules = sheets of foamy histiocytes (small dark nuclei, clear cytoplasm) with lymphocytes, giant cells, plasma cells (Fig. 26.37B). Xanthoma cells are not specific — seen wherever inflammation and obstruction coexist. Lipid (cholesterol esters) may derive from lysed red cells after haemorrhage.

Clinical Presentation

  • Suspect with UTI + unilateral enlarged, non- or poorly functioning kidney + stone or mass indistinguishable from tumour.
  • Flank pain, fever and chills (about two-thirds each), persistent bacteriuria (about half); malaise; flank mass in about 60%, prior calculi in about a third; less often hypertension, haematuria, hepatomegaly. History of UTI and instrumentation; DM a risk factor.
  • Peak fifth to seventh decades; women more often; no side predilection.

Bacteriology and Laboratory Diagnosis

  • Proteus is the commonest organism (linked to stone formation), E. coli also common; tissue cultures almost always positive; anaerobes occur; about 10% mixed. About a third have sterile urine (prior antibiotics) — organism may be found only in operative tissue culture.
  • Urinalysis: pus and protein. Blood: anaemia; hepatic dysfunction in up to half.
  • Almost always unilateral → azotaemia/renal failure uncommon.

Imaging

  • CT is the most useful (Fig. 26.38): classic triad in 50–80% — unilateral enlargement, little or no function, large renal pelvic calculus.
    • Reniform mass; pelvis tightly contracted around a central calcification, without pelvic dilatation; parenchyma replaced by water-density masses (dilated calyces/abscesses).
    • Cavity walls blush on contrast (vascular granulation tissue) but cavities do not enhance — tumours and other inflammatory lesions usually do.
    • Shows extent and involvement of adjacent organs/abdominal wall.
  • Ultrasound: global enlargement; multiple hypoechoic fluid-filled masses (debris-filled calyces, destruction); focal (tumefactive) XGP = solid segmental mass ± stone → RCC in differential.
  • ⁹⁹ᵐTc-DMSA quantifies lost function. MRI: better for extrarenal extension; lesions intermediate on T1, hyperintense on T2. Imaging, though distinctive, often cannot separate XGP from RCC.

Management

  • Main obstacle is misdiagnosis; with CT, about 90% are diagnosed preoperatively.
  • Antibiotics to stabilise; rarely, long-term antibiotics eradicate infection and restore function.
  • Nephrectomy usually (diffuse disease or cannot exclude tumour); localised XGP → partial nephrectomy.
  • Frozen section may mimic clear cell carcinoma, and XGP coexists with RCC, papillary urothelial carcinoma of pelvis or bladder, and squamous cell carcinoma of the pelvis → if malignancy cannot be excluded, perform nephrectomy.
  • Extensive retroperitoneal disease → remove kidney and perinephric fat; may require dissection from diaphragm, great vessels and bowel.   • Laparoscopic nephrectomy: early series found no benefit; later, larger series deem it reasonable (retroperitoneal, or transperitoneal hand-assisted) but difficult with high conversion rates.

Concept — XGP is chronic obstruction plus low-grade infection in a host that cannot mount an acute response — hence the stone, the non-functioning kidney, the fat-laden macrophages and the resemblance to cancer; because the tissue itself is infected and may harbour or mimic carcinoma, the answer is excision of the whole mass, not drainage.

Malakoplakia

  • "Soft plaque" — unusual inflammatory disease first described in the bladder, also in GU and GI tracts, skin, lungs, bones, mesenteric nodes. Described by Michaelis and Gutmann (1902); characterised by von Hansemann (1903) as soft yellow-brown plaques with histiocytes containing basophilic lysosomal inclusions (Michaelis–Gutmann bodies).
  • Abnormal macrophage function in response to bacterial (mostly E. coli) infection.

Pathogenesis

  • Bacteria or fragments form the nidus for calcium phosphate crystals that laminate Michaelis–Gutmann bodies. Leading theory: defective phagocytic/degradative function of histiocytes → chronic inflammation, often in immunocompromised hosts.

Pathology

  • Diagnosis by biopsy. Grossly: confluent tumour-like masses and firm plaque-like cortical lesions (Fig. 26.39). Large histiocytes (von Hansemann cells) and small basophilic intra- or extracytoplasmic calculospherules — Michaelis–Gutmann bodies, pathognomonic (Fig. 26.39C: incompletely destroyed bacteria with lipoprotein membrane).
  • Electron microscopy: intact coliforms and fragments within phagolysosomes.
  • Michaelis–Gutmann bodies may be absent early and are not required for diagnosis.
  • Macrophages contain abundant α1-antitrypsin, unchanged through early, granulomatous and fibrosing phases; lookalike processes lack it (except a few macrophages in TB and XGP) → α1-antitrypsin immunostaining aids early diagnosis.

Clinical Presentation

  • Most >50 years; urinary tract female:male 4:1 (not in other organs). Any part of the GU tract, frequently bladder.
  • Patients often debilitated, immunosuppressed, chronically ill; notably after solid organ (especially renal) transplantation — graft kidney or ureter, GI and skin.
  • Bladder: irritative LUTS, haematuria; cystoscopy — mucosal plaques or nodules → fungating, sessile masses causing filling defects; distal ureteric stricture → obstruction or non-function.
  • Renal parenchymal: one or more masses with chronic E. coli infection; may cause renal vein and inferior vena cava thrombosis. Testis → epididymo-orchitis. Prostate — rare, mimics carcinoma.
  • Mortality can exceed 50%.

Radiological Findings

  • Multifocal: enlarged kidneys with multiple filling defects; no calcification, lithiasis or hydronephrosis; best seen on ultrasound (confluent masses raise parenchymal echogenicity, distort central echo complex), CT (foci less dense than enhanced parenchyma) or arteriography.
  • Unifocal: non-calcified mass indistinguishable from other inflammatory or neoplastic lesions; solid or cystic depending on necrosis. CT best shows extrarenal extension.

Differential Diagnosis

  • Consider malakoplakia with renal mass(es) ± collecting-system filling defects in women with recurrent E. coli UTI or altered immunity, and in a renal transplant recipient with persistent UTI despite appropriate therapy.
  • Exclude cystic disease (ultrasound/CT); metastases/lymphoma (late disease); multifocal RCC (von Hippel–Lindau context); XGP (enlarged kidney but calculi and obstruction common); multiple abscesses (haematogenous).

Management

  • Control the UTI to stabilise disease. Agents with intracellular bactericidal activity preferred: sulfonamides, rifampicin, doxycycline, TMP; fluoroquinolones (concentrated in macrophages) effective.
  • Bladder disease: ascorbic acid and cholinergics (bethanechol) with antibiotics — both raise intracellular cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP), the postulated macrophage defect.
  • Reduce immunosuppression where possible; surgery if progression despite medical therapy; nephrectomy for symptomatic unilateral renal disease.

Concept — Malakoplakia is a failure to digest what has been swallowed — macrophages ingest E. coli but cannot degrade it, leaving mineralised remnants (Michaelis–Gutmann bodies); treatment therefore uses antibiotics that work inside macrophages and agents that correct the degradative defect, not merely drugs that sterilise urine.

KEY POINTS: KIDNEY INFECTIONS

  • Acute pyelonephritis: abrupt chills, fever, flank/CVA tenderness — spectrum from cystitis-like to sepsis.
  • Emphysematous pyelonephritis: life-threatening; diagnosed radiologically by parenchymal gas; percutaneous drainage or surgery.

Concept — Across renal infections the governing axis is diffuse inflammation versus contained collection: pyelonephritis and AFBN are treated with antibiotics; pyonephrosis, abscess and perinephric abscess add drainage once a collection is large or obstructed; and XGP and extensive emphysematous disease, where the kidney itself is the infected tissue, end in nephrectomy.

Fournier's Gangrene

  • Potentially life-threatening necrotising fasciitis of the external genitalia, perineum and/or perianal region. Synonyms: idiopathic gangrene of the scrotum, streptococcal scrotal gangrene, perineal phlegmon, spontaneous fulminant gangrene of the scrotum.
  • Described by Baurienne (1764) and Fournier (1883) as abrupt, idiopathic fulminant genital gangrene in healthy young men.

Clinical Presentation

  • History of perineal trauma, instrumentation, sexually transmitted disease (STD)–related urethral stricture, urethrocutaneous fistula.
  • Clues to source: rectal — pain, rectal bleeding, anal fissures; dermal — acute/chronic scrotal infection, recurrent hidradenitis suppurativa, balanitis.
  • Sequence: cellulitis at the portal of entry → swollen, erythematous, tender as deep fascia is involved; pain prominent, fever and systemic toxicity marked → scrotal swelling and crepitus increase rapidly → dark-purple areas → extensive gangrene.   • GU symptoms: dysuria, urethral discharge, obstructed voiding. Sepsis markers: altered mental state, tachypnoea, tachycardia, temperature >38.3 °C (101 °F) or <35.6 °C (96 °F).

  • Anaemia (thrombosis and ecchymosis reduce functioning red cell mass; sepsis reduces production); raised creatinine, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia — bacterial lipases free fatty acids that chelate ionised calcium.

  • Prompt diagnosis is critical. Early signs (pain, oedema, erythema) resemble cellulitis; systemic toxicity out of proportion to local signs, and gas on imaging, are the warnings.

  • Fournier's gangrene = necrotising fasciitis of external genitalia, perineum and/or perianal region.
  • Emergent surgical debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics are the essentials.

BACTERIURIA IN PREGNANCY

Pregnancy

  • Bacteriuria in pregnancy → pyelonephritis → adverse birth outcomes; hence pregnancy is one of the few indications to treat ASB (IDSA). American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG): screening urine culture at the first visit.
  • Prevalence of ASB is not raised by pregnancy (about 2–7% in the text; 1.9–9.5% in Table 26.2 — different source series). Risk rises with low socioeconomic status, multiparity, sickle cell trait.
  • Non-pregnant women often clear ASB; pregnant women tend to stay bacteriuric and become symptomatic.
  • Pyelonephritis: 1–4% of all pregnancies; historically 15–45% of untreated bacteriuric pregnancies (contemporary uncomplicated singleton data much lower); 60–75% in the third trimester (peak hydronephrosis and stasis); 10–20% recur before or soon after delivery.

Anatomical and Physiological Changes During Pregnancy

  • Increase in renal size: length + about 1 cm — vascular and interstitial volume, not true hypertrophy; biopsies show no histological change.
  • Smooth muscle atony of collecting system and bladder: reduced ureteric peristalsis; most women have significant ureteric dilatation by the third trimester — from progesterone relaxation and uterine compression at the pelvic brim (the mechanical effect dominating later); progesterone also increases bladder capacity.
  • Bladder changes: uterus displaces bladder superiorly and anteriorly; bladder hyperaemic/congested endoscopically; oestrogen → bladder hypertrophy and urethral squamous change.
  • Augmented renal function: GFR rises 30–50% (raised cardiac output) with more proteinuria → "normal" non-pregnant creatinine may signify renal insufficiency in pregnancy; proteinuria abnormal only >300 mg/24 h.
  • These changes (from the first trimester) cause stasis and mild hydroureteronephrosis → pyelonephritis.

BACTERIURIA AND URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS IN THE ELDERLY

  • The US population aged ≥65 is projected nearly to double by 2050 → growing public health importance.

Epidemiology

  • Bacteriuria rises with age; the female excess narrows in old age (still commoner in women); most elderly bacteriuric patients are asymptomatic.
  • Prevalence rises with age and comorbidity, exceeding 50% in selected groups (figures in Table 26.2); functional impairment doubles it.

Screening for Bacteriuria

  • Do not screen community or long-term care elderly. No link between ASB and deteriorating renal function; treating ASB does not improve incontinence; the association of bacteriuria with reduced survival is not shown to be causal.

Pathogenesis

  • Multifactorial: declining cell-mediated immunity, incomplete emptying, NGB, perineal soiling from faecal/urinary incontinence, more catheterisation, oestrogen depletion (women).
  • E. coli remains commonest (50–75%), but more Proteus, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Serratia, Pseudomonas and enterococci. Enterococci often resolve untreated (colonisation). Polymicrobial bacteriuria commoner.
  • The shift, polymicrobial pattern and resistance are driven by institutionalisation, hospitalisation, catheterisation and antibiotic use.

Diagnosis

  • Subtle presentations (abdominal discomfort, delirium); even upper tract infection may lack fever or leucocytosis.
  • New localised lower tract symptoms — especially dysuria — remain the key criteria.
  • Bacteriuria + altered mental state ≠ UTI; no data show antibiotics improve confusion in bacteriuric elderly.
  • Do not culture for non-specific symptoms (dizziness, confusion), chronic symptoms (incontinence, frequency) or asymptomatic patients.
  • Confusion without urinary symptoms → hydrate and reassess; test urine only if altered mental state or change in urine persists after fluids, or in dementia unresponsive to other measures.
  • Meta-analysis: delirium is associated with UTI but not with ASB; strongest effect modifiers retention, dehydration, catheters; whether treating bacteriuria helps delirium is uncertain.
  • Never culture to document clearance.
  • Thresholds: ≥10⁵ CFU/mL with symptoms; ≥10² CFU/mL in catheterised specimens.
  • Dipstick is better at ruling out than ruling in (moderate sensitivity, lower specificity). Pyuria is common and a poor predictor of bacteriuria in the elderly — many women with pyuria have no bacteriuria — and pyuria alone is not an indication for treatment; absence of pyuria reliably predicts absence of bacteriuria.
  • Catheterised specimen may be the only clean sample in institutionalised or dexterity-limited patients.
  • Urological evaluation (creatinine, CT, ultrasound, urodynamic studies [UDS], cystoscopy) for renal dysfunction, calculi, hydronephrosis, retention, NGB; timing by clinical setting.
  • Prompt therapy needed when bacteriuria → UTI with structural abnormality (obstruction/hydronephrosis), severe DM, or urea-splitters (Proteus, Klebsiella) → infection stones and renal damage.
  • Sepsis increasingly common in the elderly: catheters and invasive devices, prostheses, chemotherapy, corticosteroids, and longer survival with metabolic, neoplastic and immune disorders.

Management

  • Randomised trials in nursing-home residents: treating ASB gives no benefit, only adverse effects, resistant reinfection and cost → do not treat ASB in long-term care.
  • IDSA 2019 recommends against screening for or treating ASB in functionally impaired community-dwelling older people and in long-term care residents.
  • McGeer surveillance criteria (Table 26.16) standardise UTI diagnosis in aged-care residents; limited in cognitive impairment.
  • Mild LUTS → await culture; start empirical antibiotics only if immediate treatment needed.
  • Duration: traditionally 7 days; Cochrane: short (3–6 days) = long (7–14 days) for uncomplicated symptomatic UTI; short courses better than single dose for persistence; fever or systemic infection → 10–14 days. Goal: eliminate symptoms, not sterilise urine.
  • Greater drug toxicity: impaired metabolism/excretion → high serum levels and renal damage; interactions; narrow therapeutic margin → dose and monitor carefully. Fluoroquinolones: tendon rupture risk greatest over 60 (boxed warning) and CNS effects.

KEY POINTS: BACTERIURIA IN THE ELDERLY

  • Do not screen; do not treat ASB — no link to uncomplicated UTI or renal deterioration.
  • UTI may present subtly; a high index of suspicion is needed.
  • Treatment must allow for age-related physiology and comorbidity.
  • Never culture to document clearance.

Concept — In the elderly bacteriuria is the background, not the signal: because a positive culture is expected, diagnosis must rest on new localising symptoms (or, with a catheter, systemic signs without another source), and non-specific decline should be met with fluids and reassessment before antibiotics.

CATHETER-ASSOCIATED URINARY TRACT INFECTION

  • Definition (2015 guidelines): UTI after an indwelling catheter has been in place >2 days, with ≥1 symptom (suprapubic or CVA tenderness, frequency, urgency, dysuria, fever >38 °C) and a single organism ≥10⁵ CFU/mL.
  • Catheter-associated bacteriuria is the commonest hospital-acquired infection and inevitable — about 5–10% per catheter-day (text gives about 10% in one place and 5–10% in another). Intermittent catheterisation: 1–3% per catheterisation.
  • Risk factors: duration, female sex, no systemic antibiotics, breaches of catheter care.
  • Mostly asymptomatic: short term — only a minority of bacteriuric episodes are symptomatic; long term — about 1 febrile episode per 100 catheter-days.
  • Cost is substantial and higher for resistant Gram-negatives (parenteral therapy). 2008: US Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) stopped reimbursing CAUTI costs.
  • Perioperative catheter indications: intraoperative output monitoring, prolonged surgery, urological surgery — and limit duration.
  • Bundled "6-C" approach (Mayo Clinic; large fall in CAUTI):

Pathogenesis

  • Entry: at insertion (urethral bacteria pushed in, poor technique); thereafter periurethral (extraluminal — predominant in women, most had prior urethral colonisation with the infecting strain) or intraluminal (from the drainage bag — commoner when catheterised patients are clustered).

  • Two populations: planktonic (urine) and biofilm (catheter surface). Biofilm — organisms in matrix of bacterial and host products → encrustation; shields bacteria from antibiotics → CAUTI and resistance; forms within minutes, grows with dwell time.
  • Pseudomonas and Proteus are adept biofilm formers → prevalent here. E. coli still commonest, with Pseudomonas, Proteus, Enterococcus. Long-term (>30 days): polymicrobial, often 4–5 organisms; populations dynamic.

Clinical Presentation

  • Suprapubic discomfort, fever, chills or flank pain suggest symptomatic UTI.

Laboratory Diagnosis

  • ≥10² CFU/mL is significant — even low counts progress to ≥10⁵ in almost all. Pyuria does not discriminate infection in catheterised patients.

Management

  • Aseptic insertion and a closed, dependent drainage system are essential.
  • Clean the catheter–meatal junction daily with water; avoid antimicrobial agents (select resistant organisms such as Pseudomonas).
  • Silicone = latex for CAUTI rates.

KEY POINTS: CATHETER-ASSOCIATED BACTERIURIA

  • Aseptic insertion and closed, dependent drainage minimise bacteriuria.
  • Catheter-associated bacteriuria is inevitable.
  • Only symptomatic CAUTI needs treatment.

Concept — The catheter, not the bladder, is the infected organ: a biofilm forms within minutes and is beyond the reach of antibiotics, so prevention is about minimising catheter-days and keeping the system closed, and treatment of a symptomatic episode includes removing the colonised surface by changing the catheter.

MANAGEMENT OF URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS IN PATIENTS WITH SPINAL CORD INJURY

  • All UTIs in SCI are complicated. No bacteriuric group compares in severity and morbidity. Early catheterisation is near-universal (overactivity or flaccidity); many develop ureterectasis, hydronephrosis, VUR, calculi. Bacteriological and urodynamic advances have greatly reduced morbidity and mortality.

Epidemiology

  • Before CIC and modern antibiotics, UTI and renal failure caused most SCI deaths; both have fallen since.
  • UTI is among the commonest urological complications — about a third bacteriuric at any time; almost all eventually; significant bacteriuria and febrile UTI recur several times per person-year on intermittent or condom drainage. UTI is the commonest cause of fever in SCI.
  • Risk factors (National Institute on Disability and Rehabilitation Research Consensus Conference [NIDRRCC], 1992): impaired voiding, overdistension, raised intravesical pressure, obstruction, VUR, instrumentation, stones; also low fluid intake, poor hygiene, perineal colonisation, pressure sores and local trauma, reduced host defence.

Pathogenesis

  • Bladder management method profoundly affects UTI incidence.

Clinical Presentation

  • Most bacteriuric SCI patients are asymptomatic → do not treat ASB; do not screen asymptomatic patients on indwelling, condom or intermittent catheters.
  • No standard UTI definition in SCI. Sensory loss → frequency, urgency, dysuria usually absent. Instead: flank, back or abdominal discomfort; leakage between catheterisations; increased spasticity; malaise, lethargy; cloudy, malodorous urine; fever (commonest cause).

Bacteriology and Laboratory Diagnosis

  • Bacteriuria and pyuria; pyuria is not diagnostic (catheter irritation).

  • Post-treatment cultures unnecessary (recolonisation common) — except urea-splitters, which must be shown eradicated.

KEY POINTS: MANAGEMENT OF UTIS IN PATIENTS WITH SCI

  • Most bacteriuric SCI patients are asymptomatic; treat only symptomatic patients.
  • Symptoms: fever; flank, back or abdominal discomfort; leakage between catheterisations; spasticity; malaise, lethargy; cloudy, malodorous urine.
  • Culture before empirical therapy — diverse, resistant flora.
  • CIC carries the lowest risk of long-term complications.
  • Chronic infection can be carcinogenic.

Concept — In SCI the bladder-management method is the main modifiable determinant of infection: low-pressure, intermittent emptying (CIC) prevents the stasis, high pressure and reflux that make infection dangerous, whereas antibiotics can neither prevent colonisation nor safely eradicate it — so they are reserved for symptoms, which must be recognised through non-classical signs.

Endoscopic Procedures: Lower Urinary Tract

Simple Cystoscopy (Without Manipulation)

  • Older trials (sterile urine, no AP): UTI in a few per cent (higher with prior UTI; one series much higher); single-dose TMP or ciprofloxacin reduced infection to 1–5%; no serious systemic infections.
  • Recent evidence: AP unnecessary for diagnostic cystoscopy — large series without AP: few febrile UTIs, all settled on oral antibiotics, none needing admission (including patients with ASB); meta-analysis: no difference; ciprofloxacin or fosfomycin not indicated for flexible cystoscopy.
  • Risk factors: recent antibiotics or hospitalisation; a risk-plus-antibiogram strategy reduced AP without more UTIs.
  • EAU 2017: strongly recommends against AP for cystourethroscopy (flexible or rigid) — low absolute risk, huge volume, resistance risk.

Transurethral Resection of the Prostate and Bladder (Cystourethroscopy With Manipulation)

  • Higher risk than diagnostic cystoscopy — mucosal trauma, longer/harder procedures, pressurised irrigation, resection of infected material.
  • TURP (most studied) — meta-analysis: AP markedly reduced postoperative bacteriuria and septicaemia; most effective: fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, cephalosporins, TMP-SMX. Short courses (2–5 days while the catheter remains) beat single doses, and continuing the agent briefly with the catheter does not increase resistance.
  • TURBT: no recent trials; TURP data suggest benefit, but EAU (two RCTs) finds no benefit — AP only in high-risk TURBT; a prospective study found no benefit.
  • Other manipulations (laser prostatectomy, bladder biopsy): extrapolated from TURP/TURBT; office biopsy without AP = theatre biopsy with AP for UTI and febrile UTI — yet CMS mandates AP for cystoscopy with biopsy in theatre, and IDSA and AUA concur. IDSA: fluoroquinolone, TMP-SMX or cefazolin for lower tract instrumentation with risk factors.
  • Risk factors: obesity, DM, smoking, immunosuppression, malnutrition; urological — anomalies, obstruction, stones, indwelling or externalised catheters.
  • Known preoperative UTI → eradicate first (this is treatment); operating on unsterilised urine → bacteraemia in about half.
  • Pressurised-irrigant diagnostic/therapeutic upper tract studies → AP covering uropathogens.

Ureteroscopy

  • Higher risk than cystoscopy (mucosal trauma, duration, irrigant pressure, infected material). RCT: fluoroquinolone AP reduced post-ureteroscopy bacteriuria in healthy patients with sterile urine.

Ureteral Stenting

  • Give AP at stent removal; culture the stent in high-risk patients to guide later treatment.
  • Stent colonisation far exceeds urine colonisation; dwell time is the main determinant and is not reduced by AP; urine culture identifies the stent organism in only a small minority.

Percutaneous Renal Surgery (Prophylaxis Indicated in All Patients)

  • For large stones, pelviureteric junction obstruction, urothelial carcinoma surveillance. Pyrexia and bacteraemia are frequent — parenchymal injury, pressurised irrigation, infected stones; risk rises with procedure duration and irrigant volume. No RCTs.
  • Positive preoperative culture → treat first; negative → AP against common uropathogens.

Open, Laparoscopic and Robotic Surgery

  • Wound classes (Table 26.18): AP for clean-contaminated and contaminated; treatment for dirty. Laparoscopy lowers SSI (general surgery data).
  • Clean — e.g. radical nephrectomy without entering the urinary tract; elective opening of the urinary tract = Class II; entering an infected tract = Class III (higher SSI).
  • Give within 1 h; stop by 24 h — longer courses show no benefit.
  • First-generation cephalosporins standard for clean-contaminated (few allergies, long half-life, cheap); β-lactam allergy → vancomycin or clindamycin (National Surgical Infection Prevention Project [NSIPP] 2004).
  • Intestinal reconstruction → 1st-generation cephalosporin + metronidazole.
  • Colon/appendix: oral neomycin + erythromycin or neomycin + metronidazole 18–24 h before + IV cefotetan or cefoxitin 30–60 min before incision (NSIPP, WHO); β-lactam allergy → clindamycin + gentamicin, aztreonam, or ciprofloxacin.
  • Dirty wounds (all abscesses, traumatic GU perforation) → broad empirical cover + intraoperative cultures, then tailor.

Skin Preparation for Surgery

  • Pre-admission bathing, mechanical bowel preparation, hair removal and skin preparation all help prevent SSI; details in the AUA BPS.

Vaginal Surgery

  • No urological RCTs; vaginal hysterectomy data: AP markedly reduces pelvic infection; ≤24 h as effective as longer courses.
  • AUA BPS (2010, 2019): AP for all vaginal procedures, including midurethral sling (MUS) — expert opinion. MUS studies (uncontrolled series, an RCT stopped early for low event rates, decision analysis) suggest AP may be unnecessary because infection is rare.

Special Considerations

Patients With Risk of Endocarditis

  • Risk of infective endocarditis (IE) after urological procedures is low.
  • American Heart Association (AHA) 2007: AP solely to prevent IE is not recommended for GU procedures, though GU instrumentation can cause transient enterococcal bacteraemia. American College of Cardiology (ACC)/AHA 2017: no benefit; IE is likelier from random bacteraemia — not even for high-risk patients (prosthetic valve, previous IE, cardiac transplant with valvulopathy).
  • Exception (Class IIb): high-risk cardiac condition + active urinary infection or colonisation before GU manipulation (incl. elective cystoscopy) → treat to sterilise the urine — amoxicillin/ampicillin first line (enterococcus), vancomycin if intolerant, or culture-directed.

Statement Position

KEY POINTS: ANTIMICROBIAL PROPHYLAXIS FOR COMMON UROLOGIC PROCEDURES

  • AP = antimicrobial before and for a limited time after a procedure to prevent local/systemic infection.
  • Procedure type and host defences determine need.
  • Special considerations: TRUS biopsy, endocarditis risk with bacteriuria, orthopaedic hardware.

Concept — Prophylaxis is justified by the product of wound contamination and host vulnerability: where neither is raised (diagnostic cystoscopy, SWL with sterile urine, UDS in index patients) the ecological cost outweighs the benefit; where either is (bowel, infected stones, transrectal biopsy, prostheses, immunocompromise) a single, well-timed, flora-matched dose is the default, and anything longer is treatment, not prophylaxis.
