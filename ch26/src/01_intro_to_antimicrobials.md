<div class="chapter-open" markdown="1">

- **Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are common**, vary widely in presentation and sequelae, and are **one of the commonest reasons for prescribing antibiotics**.
- Most cause morbidity and **rarely become life-threatening**; in certain populations, **undiagnosed and untreated UTI carries significant mortality**.
- Infection follows when **bacterial virulence rises or host defence falls** → inoculation → colonisation → infection. Careful diagnosis and treatment resolve most episodes.
- **Urine is not sterile**; bacteriuria may even be **protective against UTI**.
- With **multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms** spreading, **clinical evaluation plus urinalysis and culture must precede antibiotics**; guidelines should govern **both agent selection and duration**; education of practitioners and patients is paramount.

</div>

## DEFINITIONS

- **UTI** — an **inflammatory response of the urothelium to bacterial invasion**, usually with bacteriuria and pyuria.
- **Bacteriuria** — bacteria in the urine; usually indicates colonisation or infection, but **bacteria can sit in the urothelium without bacteriuria**, and bacteriuria may be **contamination** at collection.
- **Pyuria** — white blood cells (WBCs) in the urine; indicates infection and/or **inflammation from bacteria, stones, foreign body or other causes**.

<p class="tt ct">Bacteriuria versus pyuria</p>

| Finding | Usual meaning |
|---|---|
| **Bacteriuria without pyuria** | **Colonisation** without overt infection |
| **Pyuria without bacteriuria (sterile pyuria)** | **Warrants further evaluation** |

- **Cystitis** — dysuria, frequency, urgency, ± suprapubic pain. The same symptoms occur with **urethral or vaginal infection** and **non-infective conditions**: interstitial cystitis/painful bladder syndrome (IC/PBS), **bladder carcinoma**, calculi.
- **Acute pyelonephritis** — chills, fever, flank pain **plus bacteriuria and pyuria**; this combination is reasonably specific for acute bacterial renal infection.
    - Diagnose on **clinical presentation, not on radiological perinephric inflammation alone**.
    - Presentation is **atypical in spinal cord injury (SCI)** — flank symptoms may be absent.
- **Chronic pyelonephritis** — **atrophic, fibrotic kidney** with morphological, radiological or functional renal disease; may be post-infective but **often not linked to current infection**.
    - Infective scar: **focal, coarse cortical scar overlying a calyx, almost always with calyceal distortion** — radiologically **identical to scarring from vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) or calyceal stone disease**.
    - Less often: **generalised cortical thinning** (atrophic pyelonephritis) → small kidney resembling **post-obstructive atrophy**.
    - **Fig. 26.1** (excretory urogram): **focal coarse scarring with blunted calyces**, one calyx reaching the capsule, in a young adult with **febrile infections and VUR in infancy** — reinfections later stopped on prophylaxis, with normal blood pressure and creatinine.
    - **Fig. 26.2**: **uniform, regular cortical atrophy** in spina bifida with neurogenic bladder and childhood febrile bacteriuria — reflux of bacteria into virtually all nephrons; this uncommon pattern is **characteristic of obstruction with superimposed infection**.
    - **Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis (XGP)** — rare chronic form, **usually with stones**; granulomatous replacement of parenchyma → **ipsilateral loss of function**.

<p class="tt ct">Uncomplicated versus complicated UTI</p>

| | Uncomplicated UTI | Complicated UTI |
|---|---|---|
| Host | **Healthy, non-pregnant female** | Compromised host, and/or **virulent or resistant organism** |
| Tract | **Structurally and functionally normal; no obstruction** | **Structurally or functionally abnormal** |
| Organism | Usually eradicated by **short oral course** | **Frequently MDR** |
| Typical patient | Women with isolated or recurrent cystitis | See Box 26.1 |

- **US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) 2018 definition of complicated UTI** — pyuria + documented pathogen in **urine or blood culture** + local/systemic features (**fever >38 °C**, chills, malaise, flank/back pain, costovertebral angle pain or tenderness) **in the presence of a functional or anatomical abnormality or catheterisation**.
- **Functional abnormalities:** renal disease impairing **urinary concentration** (tubulointerstitial: **post-obstructive, sickle cell, lithium, chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis, medullary cystic kidney disease**); **bladder outlet obstruction (BOO), neurogenic bladder (NGB), detrusor underactivity**.
- **Anatomical abnormalities:** **benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)**; sites of residual urine (**calyceal, urethral or bladder diverticula**); extrinsic or intrinsic **ureteric obstruction**.
- The **European Section of Infections in Urology classification** combines clinical presentation, severity, host and pathogen risk factors — useful for practice and trial design.

<p class="tt">BOX 26.1 Factors That Suggest a Complicated Urinary Tract Infection</p>

- **Functional or anatomical abnormality** of the tract; **male sex**; **pregnancy**; **elderly**; **diabetes**; **immunosuppression**
- **Childhood UTI**; **recent antimicrobial use**; **indwelling catheter**; **instrumentation**; **hospital-acquired infection**
- **Symptoms >7 days at presentation**
- *Data from Schaeffer, 2002.* Pattern: every item either **impairs host defence or selects a resistant organism**. Trap: **male sex alone** makes a UTI complicated.

<p class="tt ct">Infections defined by their relationship to other UTIs</p>

| Term | Definition | Key discriminator |
|---|---|---|
| **First / isolated** | No prior UTI, or one remote UTI | — |
| **Unresolved** | **Failed to respond to therapy**; same organism, similar resistance | Never cleared |
| **Recurrent — reinfection** | **New event**; bacteria reintroduced | Cleared, then new inoculation |
| **Recurrent — bacterial persistence** | **Same organism re-emerging from a focus in the tract** (infected stone, prostate); "relapse" used interchangeably | Cleared from urine but **focus remains** |

- These distinctions **determine the type and extent of evaluation and treatment**.
- **Antimicrobial prophylaxis (AP)** — prevention of **reinfection**; implies bacteria were **eradicated before** prophylaxis began. **Surgical AP** — before and for a limited time after a procedure (American Urological Association [AUA] 2019 statement).
- **Antimicrobial suppression** — prevents growth of a **focus of persistence that cannot be eradicated**. Contrast: **prophylaxis presumes a sterile tract; suppression presumes an ineradicable focus**.
- **Domiciliary (outpatient)** — acquired outside hospital/institution; **nosocomial (health care–associated)** — acquired while hospitalised or institutionalised.
- **Catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI)** — in patients with indwelling drainage catheters; **the commonest cause of secondary bacteraemia**; raised morbidity and mortality. Risk factors: **prolonged catheterisation, female sex, advanced age, diabetes**.

**KEY POINTS: DEFINITIONS**

- Bacteriuria and pyuria are **not synonymous with UTI**.
- UTIs are classified by **presumed site of origin**.
- **Uncomplicated** = healthy host, normal tract; **complicated** = factors raising acquisition and lowering treatment efficacy.
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> Every definition here separates <em>finding</em> from <em>disease</em>: bacteriuria, pyuria and scarring are observations, whereas "infection", "complicated" and "persistence" are judgements about host, tract and organism — and it is the judgement, not the finding, that dictates evaluation and treatment.</p>

## INCIDENCE AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

- UTI complaints are **among the commonest primary diagnoses in women attending emergency departments** (USA); millions of office and emergency visits annually.
- **Almost half of all women have a UTI in their lifetime**; nearly a third have needed antibiotics for one **by age 24**; about one in ten adult women has **one UTI per year**.
- Self-reported cystitis and pyelonephritis admissions are **several-fold higher in women than men**.
- **Costs are large**, and **resistant organisms cost more to treat** than susceptible ones. A European survey of women with recurrent UTI (GESPRIT) documented repeated doctor visits, sick leave and limited-activity days each year.
- **Bacteriuria rises with institutionalisation, hospitalisation and comorbidity.** Roughly one in ten hospitalised urological patients worldwide develops a complicated UTI during admission.
- **CAUTIs are the commonest nosocomial infection**, constituting **about 80% of nosocomial UTIs**; they account for a large share of complicated UTIs, most being hospital or health care–acquired.
- Incidence is also raised in **pregnancy, SCI, diabetes mellitus (DM), multiple sclerosis (MS), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS)** and **organ transplant recipients**.
- **Probability of recurrence rises with the number of previous infections** and **falls with the interval since the last infection**; established historically and confirmed in modern cohorts.
    - Highest in **young, sexually active women**: about a quarter have a second infection within 6 months of a first.
    - Women with **≥2 previous UTIs** carry **two to five times** the 1-year recurrence risk of those with 0–1.
    - **Postmenopausal women** with prior UTI are similarly at risk; even with daily trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) or lactobacilli prophylaxis most recur within a year.
    - Recurrent bacteriuria segregates into a **small subgroup with very high recurrence** and a majority with low recurrence.
- **About 70% of recurrences are reinfection with a different organism**.
- **No association** between **uncomplicated recurrent UTI** and **renal scarring, hypertension or progressive azotaemia** has been established.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: INCIDENCE AND EPIDEMIOLOGY**

- UTI is **the commonest bacterial infection**, with a large health care cost.
- Bacteriuria rises with **institutionalisation/hospitalisation** and comorbidities that **immunosuppress or alter lower tract function** (e.g. catheterisation).
- Probability of recurrent UTI (rUTI) **rises with the number of previous infections**.
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> <strong>The best predictor of the next UTI is the last one</strong>: recurrence clusters in a susceptible minority and is mostly reinfection rather than relapse, which is why the chapter later locates the problem in the host's introital and bladder biology rather than in a hidden focus.</p>

## PATHOGENESIS

- UTI results from **uropathogen–host interaction**: **virulence factors, inoculum size and inadequacy of host defence** determine infection and the extent of colonisation and damage.
- **Governing asymmetry:** **strong host → only highly virulent bacteria infect; compromised host → bacteria with minimal virulence suffice.**

### Routes of Infection

#### Ascending Route

- **Most bacteria enter from the bowel and skin reservoir via the urethra into the bladder**; **adherence to introital and urothelial mucosa** is central.
- Risk factors: **faecal soiling of the perineum**, **spermicide use**, **intermittent or indwelling catheters**.
- **Most pyelonephritis is retrograde ascent** bladder → ureter → pelvis → parenchyma.
    - Reflux probably is **not required**; **cystitis-related oedema** may distort the vesicoureteric junction enough to permit reflux (Fig. 26.3).
    - Ascent is aided by anything that **impairs ureteric peristalsis**: **Gram-negative bacteria and endotoxins, pregnancy, ureteric obstruction, high lower-tract pressure** (all antiperistaltic).
    - Entry to parenchyma is via **collecting ducts at the papillary tips** → ascent in collecting tubules; hastened by **raised intrapelvic pressure** (obstruction, VUR), especially with **intrarenal reflux**.

<p class="tt">Fig. 26.3 Bacterial adherence in ascending UTI (modified from Glover et al., 2014)</p>

- Sequence: **periurethral contamination by bowel-colonising uropathogenic *E. coli*** → **type 1-fimbriated *E. coli* selected in bladder** (high colony-forming units [CFU], low oxygen), invasion and intracellular multiplication → ascent → **P fimbriae bind renal tubular cells** → **haemolysin damages epithelium**, cytokine induction → **crosses tubular epithelium → bacteraemia**.

#### Haematogenous Route

- **Uncommon in normal individuals**; kidney occasionally seeded by ***Staphylococcus aureus* bacteraemia** (e.g. oral source) or ***Candida* fungaemia**. **Obstruction enhances** haematogenous infection experimentally.

#### Lymphatic Route

- Direct lymphatic extension from adjacent organs (**severe bowel infection, retroperitoneal abscess**) — **rare**; little role in most UTIs.

<p class="tt ct">Routes of infection compared</p>

| Route | Frequency | Typical organism/setting |
|---|---|---|
| **Ascending** | **Commonest** | Bowel/skin flora; *E. coli* |
| **Haematogenous** | Uncommon | ***S. aureus*, *Candida***; obstructed kidney |
| **Lymphatic** | Rare | Severe bowel infection, retroperitoneal abscess |

### Urinary Pathogens

- **Most UTIs are due to facultative anaerobes from bowel flora**; ***Staphylococcus epidermidis* and *Candida albicans*** come from **vaginal or perineal skin flora**.
- ***Escherichia coli* is by far the commonest cause** — **about 85% of community-acquired and 50% of hospital-acquired UTIs**.
- Remaining community infections: other **Enterobacteriaceae (*Proteus*, *Klebsiella*)** and Gram-positive ***Enterococcus faecalis* and *Staphylococcus saprophyticus***.
- ***S. saprophyticus* causes about 10% of symptomatic lower UTIs in young, sexually active women**; **rare in men and the elderly** (age shapes the organism).
- **Nosocomial organisms:** *E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Citrobacter, Serratia, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Providencia, E. faecalis, S. epidermidis*.
- **Less common:** *Gardnerella vaginalis*, *Mycoplasma*, *Ureaplasma urealyticum* (some in catheterised patients). ***U. urealyticum* and *Mycoplasma hominis*** found in women with **chronic symptoms and negative standard cultures** — **targeted treatment improved symptoms**.
- **Emerging uropathogens** (identified by better culture and microbiome work):

| ***Aerococcus urinae*** | **Mistaken for *Staphylococcus/Streptococcus/Enterococcus***; older, comorbid adults, some urological malignancy |
| ***Raoultella planticola*** | Gram-negative rod; **immunocompromised** |
| ***Myroides odoratimimus*** | Gram-negative environmental (soil) rod; immunocompromised; **extensive drug resistance** |

- *E. coli* phylogeny: extraintestinal strains grouped by multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR); **most uropathogenic *E. coli* (UPEC) fall in the B2 group**.
- **Sequence type ST131 (serotype O25b:H4)** — rapidly emerging cause of **MDR infection**; first noted for **extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs)**; **fluoroquinolone resistance is its hallmark phenotype**.

- ***S. saprophyticus* appears only in uncomplicated cystitis.**

### Fastidious Organisms

#### Anaerobes in the Urinary Tract

- **Symptomatic anaerobic UTI is uncommon**, though distal urethra, perineum and vagina are colonised; anaerobes are a frequent **midstream contaminant** but **rare in suprapubic aspirates**.
- **Suspect them** when **persistent irritative symptoms**, **cocci or Gram-negative rods on microscopy of centrifuged urine**, and **routine aerobic culture is sterile**.
- Common in **enteric fistulae** and **suppurative genitourinary (GU) infection** — the large majority of **scrotal, prostatic and perinephric abscesses** in men contain anaerobes: ***Bacteroides* (incl. *B. fragilis*), *Fusobacterium*, anaerobic cocci, *Clostridium perfringens***.

#### *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and Other Nontuberculous Mycobacteria

- **Urinary tuberculosis (UTB)** presents with **haematuria (gross or micro) and/or sterile pyuria**, storage symptoms, dysuria, ± fever and weakness.
- **Diagnosis is delayed** by nonspecific symptoms → risk of **renal impairment and failure**.
- **PCR for *M. tuberculosis* has replaced acid-fast staining** as the ideal diagnostic method.

#### Chlamydia

- **Not grown in routine aerobic culture**; implicated in GU infection.

### Bacterial Virulence Factors

- UPEC resides in **bowel flora** and infects not by chance but by **virulence factors** that allow **adherence to perineum and urethra**, migration, and an inflammatory response in the urothelium; the **same factors appear in rUTI strains**.
- Many determinants lie on **about 20 UPEC-specific pathogenicity-associated islands (PAIs)**, which enlarge the genome **>20%** over commensal strains. **Phylogroups** are defined by PAIs and their virulence factors (**adhesins, toxins, surface polysaccharides, flagella, iron acquisition**).

#### Early Events in Uropathogenic *Escherichia coli* Pathogenesis

- **Sequence:** colonisation of **periurethral/vaginal tissue and urethra** → **ascent into bladder** → **adherence to surface urothelium** (engaging epithelial defence) → **biofilm** → **invasion and replication as intracellular bacterial communities (IBCs)**, which can form **quiescent intracellular reservoirs (QIRs)** dormant in underlying urothelium → in some, **renal colonisation, tissue damage and sepsis**.

<p class="tt">Fig. 26.6 UPEC molecular pathogenesis (modified from Croxen and Finlay, 2010)</p>

- **Type 1 pili bind uroplakins Ia and IIIa** → signalling (partly unknown) → **invasion and apoptosis**.
- Type 1 pili binding **α3β1 integrins** → internalisation into **superficial facet (umbrella) cells** → IBCs ("pods").
- **Exfoliation** exposes deeper transitional cells → further invasion → **QIRs implicated in recurrence**. In the kidney, **haemolysin (HlyA) and secreted autotransporter toxin (Sat)** cause tissue damage with neutrophil influx.

#### Bacterial Adherence

- **Adherence to vaginal and urothelial cells is an essential first step**; governed by **bacterial adhesins, epithelial receptivity and the fluid bathing both**; it helps determine **organism, host and site**.
- **Adhesins** are **fimbrial (pili) or afimbrial**. A piliated cell carries **100–400 pili**, each **5–10 nm wide, up to 2 µm long**, built from **pilin** subunits. Pili are defined by **haemagglutination of specific erythrocytes**.

<p class="tt ct">Type 1 versus P pili</p>

| | **Type 1 (mannose-sensitive)** | **P (mannose-resistant)** |
|---|---|---|
| Structure / gene | Helical rod of **FimA** with adhesin **FimH**; ***fim* operon — constitutive** | ***pap* operon**, located on a **PAI** with other virulence factors |
| Haemagglutination | Guinea pig erythrocytes; **inhibited by mannose** (MSHA) | **Not inhibited by mannose** |
| Distribution | **Pathogenic and non-pathogenic *E. coli*** | **Most UPEC causing upper tract infection** |
| Receptor | **Mannosylated uroplakins** on bladder umbrella cells | **Glycolipid receptors** in kidney |
| Tropism | **Bladder colonisation (cystitis)** | **Kidney — "P" = pyelonephritis** |

- **Adherence correlates with severity**: strains from **acute pyelonephritis are highly adhesive**; those from **asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) or healthy faeces are poorly adhesive**.
- **P pili frequency falls stepwise**: **pyelonephritis (most, about 90%) > cystitis > ASB > faecal isolates** (the classic graded correlation).

#### Phase Variation of Bacterial Pili in Vivo

- **Phase variation** — rapid switching between **piliated and non-piliated phases** with environment (e.g. broth vs solid medium); **occurs in vivo** (immunofluorescence of fresh urine shows mixed populations; eFig. 26.7, online only). Strains from different urogenital sites differ in piliation.
- **Sequence of advantage:** **type 1 pili help initial bladder adherence** → **become a liability in suspension** (enhance **apoptosis, phagocytosis and neutrophil killing**) → **P pili take over in the kidney** via glycolipid receptors.

### Epithelial Cell Receptivity

#### Vaginal Cells

- ***E. coli* adheres more avidly to vaginal cells of women with recurrent cystitis** — **the first demonstrable biological difference in UTI-susceptible women** (Stamey's work).
- The increased adherence is **also present on buccal cells** → receptor differences are **not vagina-specific** and may be **genetic**.
- Certain **Lewis blood-group phenotypes** correlate with rUTI in women.
- Conclusion: **increased epithelial receptivity (introital, urethral, buccal) characterises susceptible women and may be inherited**.

#### Bladder Cells

- Bladder lumen lined by **umbrella cells** whose apical surface is a **quasi-crystalline array of hexagonal uroplakin complexes** (four integral membrane proteins).
- **FimH binds mannosylated uroplakins**; **UPIa and UPIb** bind type 1-piliated UPEC; pilus tips sit **in the central cavity of the uroplakin hexamers**, and contact triggers **local actin rearrangement and membrane protrusion** around the bacterium (Fig. 26.8). **FimH binding is the initial step of the cascade.**
- FimH–UPIb binding → **phosphorylation of UPIII** → **raised intracellular calcium** (immediate urothelial response).
- **Host elimination mechanisms:** (1) **urothelial exfoliation**, (2) **exocytosis** from infected cells, (3) **autophagy**.
- ***UPEC persistence in the bladder:*** rapid **internalisation into superficial cells** (a niche protected from innate immunity) → **early IBC**: rods about 3 µm, loose random clusters → at **6–8 h** growth slows (doubling **>60 min**), rods **shorten to about 0.7 µm**, **switch to a biofilm-like community**.
    - **IBC-like cells occur in about a fifth of voided urines** from women with *E. coli* UTI, and such isolates **recreate IBCs in mice**.
- **Biofilms raise antibiotic resistance** by (1) **poor antibiotic penetration of matrix**, (2) **slow growth and altered physiology**, (3) **expression of factors that inhibit antibiotic activity**; they also **exclude neutrophils**.
- **Fluxing** — edge bacteria detach, resume rod form, become **motile** and escape into the lumen; may become **filamentous (≥70 µm)**, by about 24 h; filaments may **evade immunity**. **Flagellar motility** is common in UPEC and aids adherence and dispersal.
- Escaped bacteria **reinvade → second and further IBC rounds** → after a few days, **quiescence**. Exfoliation exposes deeper layers → **QIRs in underlying transitional epithelium**; **urothelial disruption can reactivate them** → **rUTI from an intracellular bladder reservoir**.

### Natural Defences of the Urinary Tract

#### Periurethral and Urethral Region

- Normal flora of **introitus, periurethra and urethra** — **lactobacilli, CoNS, corynebacteria, streptococci** — forms a **barrier to uropathogen colonisation**.
- Colonisation shifts with **oestrogen and pH**, but acute change is most often due to **antimicrobials and spermicides** that **alter flora and increase epithelial receptivity**.
- **Colonisation of periurethra/urethra is a prerequisite for most infections**, but overcoming **urine and bladder defences** is pivotal; urethral defence relies on **regular urine flow** and **indigenous flora**.

#### Urine

- Antibacterial when the **inoculum is small**. Main inhibitory factors: **osmolality, urea, organic acids, pH**. Growth is deterred by **very dilute urine** or **high osmolality with low pH**; clinically these **do not separate susceptible from resistant patients**.
- **Uromodulin (Tamm–Horsfall protein)** — **the most abundant urinary protein**, made in the kidney; **competes with uroplakins for type 1 pili**, blocking adhesion.

#### Bladder

- Bacteria probably enter the bladder frequently; whether they establish infection depends partly on **the bladder's ability to empty**. Bacterial recognition **up-regulates antimicrobial peptides** from urothelium and resident immune cells.

### Other Conditions That Increase Risk of Urinary Tract Infections

#### Obstruction

- **Obstruction at any level** (anatomical or high storage pressure) is **a key host susceptibility factor**: **stasis** impairs bladder and renal defences, **promotes bacterial growth and adherence**.
- Experimentally **the kidney resists haematogenous infection unless the ureter is ligated** — then **only the obstructed kidney is infected**. **Obstruction converts cystitis or pyelonephritis into a life-threatening infection.**

#### Vesicoureteral Reflux

- Association of **VUR + UTI with calyceal blunting and parenchymal scarring** first described by **Hodson and Edwards (1960)**.
- **Children** with high-grade VUR and UTI → **progressive scarring, proteinuria, renal failure**.
- **Adults** — VUR **does not reduce renal function unless combined with high storage pressure or stasis and concurrent UTI**.

<p class="tt ct">VUR: children versus adults</p>

| VUR in children | VUR in adults |
|---|---|
| High-grade VUR + UTI → **progressive scarring, proteinuria, renal failure** | **No functional loss** unless **high pressure/stasis + UTI** |

#### Underlying Disease

- **High incidence of renal scarring** with causes of **chronic interstitial nephritis**, almost all of which **damage the renal papilla first**: **DM, sickle cell disorders, adult nephrocalcinosis, hyperphosphataemia, hypokalaemia, analgesic abuse, sulfonamide nephropathy, gout, heavy-metal poisoning, ageing**.

#### Diabetes Mellitus

- **Increased rUTI** from impaired local defence: **glycosuria, neutrophil dysfunction, increased uroepithelial adherence**; plus **incomplete emptying from diabetic neuropathy** and **chronic kidney disease reducing anti-infective efficacy**.
- **More severe infections**: **acute pyelonephritis, renal abscess, emphysematous pyelonephritis**; **atypical organisms (yeast)**; sequelae include **papillary necrosis and perinephric abscess**.

#### Renal Papillary Necrosis

- **Role of infection is controversial.** Predisposing: **DM, analgesic abuse, sickle cell haemoglobinopathy, obstruction** (Box 26.2).
- **Spectrum**: acute fulminating illness ↔ chronic, incidental. Some **pass necrotic tissue** chronically; some never do. **Retained papillae may calcify** (especially with infection) and act as a **nidus for chronic infection**; **opportunistic fungal infection** reported.
- Management: **antimicrobials to control infection** + **early recognition and relief of ureteric obstruction by sloughed papillae** limit functional decline.

<p class="tt">BOX 26.2 Conditions Associated With Renal Papillary Necrosis</p>

- **Diabetes mellitus**; **pyelonephritis**; **urinary tract obstruction**; **analgesic abuse**; **sickle cell haemoglobinopathies**; **renal transplant rejection**; **cirrhosis**; **dehydration, hypoxia and jaundice of infants**.
- Miscellaneous: **renal vein thrombosis, cryoglobulinaemia, renal candidiasis, contrast injection, amyloidosis, calyceal arteritis, necrotising angiitis, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, hypotensive shock, acute pancreatitis**.
- *Data from Eknoyan et al., 1982.* Pattern: nearly all **compromise medullary blood flow or oxygenation** of the papilla. The classic mnemonic group is **DM, obstruction/pyelonephritis, analgesics, sickle cell**.

#### Human Immunodeficiency Virus

- **UTIs about fivefold commoner** in HIV-positive individuals; more ***Acinetobacter* and *Salmonella***.
- **AIDS (AIDS-defining illness and/or CD4 <200 cells/mm³)** → **atypical infection**: fungal (***Candida, Aspergillus, Cryptococcus neoformans*, endemic fungi such as *Histoplasma***), mycobacterial (tuberculosis [TB] and non-tuberculous), viral (**cytomegalovirus, adenovirus**).
- **CD4 <200** → *Pneumocystis* pneumonia prophylaxis, typically **TMP-SMX, which also lowers UTI risk** (unless sulfa allergy or glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase [G6PD] deficiency).

#### Renal Transplantation

- **UTIs are among the commonest infections after renal transplant** (more than a third of recipients have at least one) and **may worsen graft outcome**.
- **More resistant organisms** and **recurrent symptomatic episodes** (urinary tract colonisation).
- Risk factors: **age, female sex, long pre-transplant dialysis, urinary tract obstruction**.
- **Acute pyelonephritis of the graft is an independent risk factor for functional decline.**

#### Menopause

- **UTI frequency rises after menopause** (mechanism and treatment later).

#### Pregnancy

- See Bacteriuria in Pregnancy.

#### Spinal Cord Injury With High-Pressure Bladders

- **No bacteriuric group compares in severity and morbidity with SCI.** Nearly all need early catheterisation (**spinal shock, poor contractility**); long term → **poor compliance, high storage pressure → VUR, ureterectasis, hydronephrosis, renal impairment**.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: PATHOGENESIS**

- Most UTIs in women are **ascending**, from **bowel and skin flora**.
- **Virulence and host factors** determine which bacteria invade and how far.
- **Increased epithelial receptivity** predisposes to rUTI and may be **genetic**.
- **Obstruction and immunocompromise** are key host susceptibility factors.
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> Pathogenesis is a <strong>contest between adhesion and clearance</strong>: every bacterial virulence factor (pili, biofilm, intracellular hiding) is a way of resisting being washed out or shed, and every host risk factor (obstruction, stasis, residual urine, altered flora) is a failure of washing out or shedding — which is why obstruction, not organism, most often makes an infection dangerous.</p>

## EVALUATION

- Requires **thorough history** (urinary symptoms + infection risk factors); **examination necessary but often unrevealing**; diagnosis **depends on a properly collected urine sample**; extra tests guided by history.

### Signs and Symptoms

<p class="tt ct">Presenting syndromes</p>

| Syndrome | Features |
|---|---|
| **Cystitis** | **Dysuria, frequency, urgency** ± suprapubic pain, haematuria, fever |
| **Pyelonephritis** | **Fever, chills, flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness (CVAT)** ± nausea, vomiting, malaise; urinary symptoms **may be absent** |
| **Fistula** | **Pneumaturia or faecaluria → vesicoenteric or vesicovaginal fistula** |
| **Renal/perirenal abscess** | **Indolent fever, flank mass and tenderness** |
| **Indwelling catheter** | Usually **ASB**; but **fever with bacteraemia can arise rapidly and become life-threatening** |

- **Elderly**: diagnosis uniquely difficult (see Elderly section).
- History must cover the items in Box 26.3.

<p class="tt">BOX 26.3 Important Information in Evaluation of Urinary Tract Infections</p>

- **Recent infections/antibiotic use**; **recent hospitalisation**; **comorbidities**; **history of paediatric voiding dysfunction**; **sexual and reproductive history**; **anatomical urological abnormalities**; **prior surgery of GU tract, reproductive organs or spine**; **family history**; **current medications**.

**Differential diagnosis**

- **Voiding dysfunction not due to bacteria will not improve with antibiotics** — e.g. **IC/PBS** mimics UTI and patients receive **repeated antibiotic courses despite negative cultures**.

<p class="tt ct">Cystitis versus vaginitis versus urethritis</p>

| | **Cystitis** | **Vaginitis** | **Urethritis** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Acute | **Subacute** | **Subacute** |
| History | — | **Vaginal discharge/odour; new or multiple partners** | **Discharge; new or multiple partners** |
| Frequency/urgency | Prominent | **Absent** (also no haematuria, no suprapubic pain) | Present but **less pronounced**; **no fever or chills** |
| Examination | — | **Vaginal discharge; inflammatory cells in vaginal fluid** | **Urethral discharge with inflammatory cells, or initial-stream pyuria in men** |
| Causes | *E. coli* etc. | **HSV, gonorrhoea, *Chlamydia*, trichomoniasis, yeast, bacterial vaginosis** | ***N. gonorrhoeae*, *Chlamydia*, herpes simplex virus (HSV), trichomoniasis** |

- **Urethral injury** (intercourse, chemical irritants, allergy) causes dysuria with **trauma/irritant history and no discharge or pyuria**.
- **Rule: painless gross haematuria, or microhaematuria with a negative culture, always → upper and lower tract evaluation to exclude urological malignancy.**

**Physical examination** (abdomen and pelvis)

- **Suprapubic distension** → raised **postvoid residual (PVR)**; **CVAT** if pyelonephritis suspected.
- Women: **vaginal discharge**; palpate urethra for **urethral diverticulum or Skene's gland infection** (cystic structure, expressible fluid, tenderness); **atrophic epithelium = hypo-oestrogenic state (a UTI risk factor)**; **pelvic organ prolapse (POP)** → incomplete emptying.
- Men: **digital rectal examination (DRE)** of prostate.
- **Focused perineal neurological examination** (sensation).

### Diagnosis by Urine Testing

#### Urine Collection

- **Contamination reduction** improves accuracy; meatal cleansing lacks proven benefit but is **universally endorsed**.
- **Men:** cleanse glans (**retract foreskin** if uncircumcised) with **2% castile soap towelette** → **midstream sample in sterile cup**.
- **Women:** **spread labia → cleanse periurethral area → midstream sample**. **Vaginal epithelial cells and lactobacilli = contaminated sample.**

<p class="tt">BOX 26.4 Factors That Affect Ability to Provide Adequate Midstream Clean-Catch Sample</p>

- **Increased body mass index (BMI)**; **vaginal atrophy**; **poor manual dexterity**; **inability to bear weight**; **intravaginal pessary**; **non-sterile collection receptacle**.
- With a **pessary**, a **catheterised sample is preferable**.

- **Bedpan or non-sterile cup samples should be disregarded** — yet patients are often treated on them regardless of symptoms.
- **Indwelling Foley catheter:** **remove it and obtain the sample from a newly, sterilely placed catheter** — otherwise you culture **catheter/bag colonisers**.
- **Stoma:** **never sample the appliance; catheterise (intubate) the stoma.**
- **Mid-catheter specimen** — more accurate than voided; use when **voided sample contaminated (many squamous cells), pessary, cannot give clean catch, or cannot void**. **Iatrogenic infection risk is low**; in patients with equivocal symptoms it **roughly halves antibiotic prescribing** that a positive voided sample would have triggered.
- **Suprapubic aspiration** — **highly accurate**, samples bladder urine **without urethral bacteria**; some morbidity, so limited to those **unable to void on command (e.g. SCI)** — **even there, catheterised samples are preferred**.

<p class="tt ct">Urine collection methods</p>

| Method | Accuracy | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Midstream clean catch** | Contamination common in women | Default |
| **Mid-catheter** | **More accurate** | Contaminated voided sample, pessary, poor technique, cannot void, equivocal symptoms |
| **Suprapubic aspirate** | **Most accurate** | Rarely; cannot void on command |

- ***Key issues in analysis:***
    - **Leucocyte esterase** — from WBC breakdown → indicates **pyuria, not bacteria**; common with **vaginal contamination**.
    - **Nitrite** — bacterial **nitrate reductase** converts dietary nitrate. **All Enterobacteriaceae produce nitrite** (*E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Proteus, Citrobacter, Morganella, Salmonella*). **Non-producers: all Gram-positives and *Pseudomonas*/*Acinetobacter*.** **Low-nitrate diet → false negative.**
    - **Nitrite: high specificity, low sensitivity.** Esterase and nitrite are **more reliable at >10⁵ CFU/mL**.
    - **Cloudy urine** = pyuria **or phosphate crystals** in alkaline/concentrated urine.
    - Analyse promptly; standing urine **alkalinises**, bacteria multiply, red cells lyse → **refrigerate if not analysed within a few hours** (unless preservative). **Hydration dilutes** constituents.
- ***Urine dipsticks:*** **most useful to rule out UTI**; each parameter has false positives and negatives.
    - In strongly suggestive symptoms a positive dipstick supports starting antibiotics; **positive nitrite is the single variable most likely to rule in UTI** (with symptoms).
    - **Best combination: positive nitrite, or positive leucocyte esterase plus blood.**
    - Borderline sensitivity → **cannot replace microscopy** in symptomatic patients.
    - Errors: **expired or badly stored strips**; **nitrite pad is air-sensitive** (open container → future false positives); **leucocyte reaction takes up to 5 min** (read early → false negative).
- ***Urinalysis:*** automated analysis detects haematuria and bacteria **more sensitively than dipstick**.
    - Preferred tubes: **non-mercuric preservative** (chlorhexidine, ethylparaben, sodium propionate) → sample stable **up to 72 h without refrigeration**.
    - Technique: **6–8 mL → centrifuge 1800–2000 rpm for 4 min** → microscopy for red blood cells (RBCs), WBCs, squamous cells, bacteria, crystals, casts. **Squamous cells = quality marker.**
    - **No single constituent is pathognomonic**; interpret together. **Pyuria = at least 5–10 leucocytes per high-power field (HPF)**.
    - Culture takes **48–72 h**, so urinalysis gives the early answer.
    - **Strongest predictors: moderate bacteriuria, moderate pyuria and nitrite.** **Moderate pyuria (WBC >50) with typical symptoms justifies antibiotics.**
    - **Other causes of pyuria:** **GU tuberculosis, urolithiasis, urothelial injury (incl. chlamydial urethritis), interstitial nephritis**.
    - **No pyuria → question the diagnosis** until culture returns; **culture positive without pyuria → consider a catheterised sample**.
    - **False-positive bacteria on microscopy** with negative culture — usually **vaginal lactobacilli and corynebacteria**.
    - **A negative automated urinalysis reliably rules out UTI** (can obviate culture); **ruling in is harder** — abnormal parameters occur in **healthy asymptomatic women**, so urinalysis alone **overdiagnoses**.
- ***Urine culture:*** **the gold standard for bacteriuria**, supporting UTI in a **symptomatic** patient (technique from the 1950s). Collect midstream or catheterised into a **vacutainer with freeze-dried preservative** (practically eliminates false positives) → **blood agar and MacConkey plates**.

<p class="tt ct">Culture thresholds for significant bacteriuria</p>

| Threshold | Origin / use |
|---|---|
| **≥10⁵ CFU/mL** | **Kass (1956)** — originally to confirm **pyelonephritis**, later extended to cystitis |
| **≥10² CFU/mL of a known pathogen** | **Appropriate threshold in women with dysuria**; a fifth to two-fifths of symptomatic women have **10²–10⁴ CFU/mL** |

- **Why low counts in cystitis:** bacterial **doubling time in urine 30–45 min** versus **voiding every 15–30 min** from irritation — bacteria are **washed out before reaching 10⁵**.
- Reports: **negative, commensal flora, or positive**. **Commensal flora = CoNS, α- and non-haemolytic streptococci, diphtheroids, non-pathogenic *Neisseria*, yeast.**
- **Susceptibility: S (sensitive), I (indeterminate/intermediate), R (resistant)**, followed by **minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)** — the **lowest concentration inhibiting growth**.

<p class="tt ct">Susceptibility categories</p>

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **S** | Inhibited by **serum concentration achieved with the recommended dose** |
| **I** | May still work **where the drug is concentrated (urine)** or at a **higher dose** |
| **R** | Resistant to **standard achievable serum levels** |

- **Efficacy depends on urinary drug level and time above MIC**; resolution tracks **susceptibility to the urinary concentration**. **Urine levels are often several hundred times serum levels**; inhibitory urine levels are reached orally by **all common agents except macrolides (e.g. erythromycin)**.
- Choose on **site of infection and fluid (urine vs blood)** and **route**, not MIC alone — **the lowest MIC is not always the best drug**; route matters especially with **outpatient MDR organisms**.

<p class="tt">Fig. 26.10 MIC report for *E. coli* UTI</p>

- Example report (>10⁵ CFU/mL *E. coli*, clean catch): **susceptible to most agents; resistant to ampicillin, tetracycline and TMP-SMX; intermediate to ampicillin–sulbactam**. The legend groups ampicillin–sulbactam with the resistant agents.
- Reading point: an **"I" result is not "R"** — urinary concentration may still suffice.

- ***Which organism matters:*** **Gram-negative rods in a symptomatic patient → treat** (consensus). **Gram-positives in women often represent periurethral contamination.**
    - In symptomatic premenopausal women, ***E. coli* in a midstream sample strongly predicts bladder bacteriuria even at low counts**; **enterococci and group B streptococci predict nothing at any count**, and **catheterised samples from such women usually grow *E. coli***. → **Gram-positives rarely cause acute cystitis in isolation.**
- **Symptoms + suggestive urinalysis + negative standard culture → culture for atypical organisms (*U. urealyticum*, *M. hominis*).**
- **Next-generation sequencing (NGS)** and **expanded quantitative urine culture (EQUC)** detect far more organisms, challenging **colony-count thresholds** and the dismissal of **polymicrobial growth as contamination**; some argue standard culture is **no longer the gold standard**. **Clinical judgement is paramount to prevent overtreatment.**

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: EVALUATION**

- Urine results **cannot be interpreted without signs and symptoms**.
- Collect urine to **minimise contamination**.
- **Formal urinalysis is preferred over dipstick.**
- Culture provides **bacterial sensitivities**.
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> <strong>Urine tests are better at excluding than confirming UTI</strong>: a negative urinalysis or dipstick is reassuring, but positives are diluted by contamination, colonisation and non-infective pyuria — so the quality of the sample and the presence of symptoms decide what a positive result means.</p>

## LOCALISATION

### Ureteral Catheterisation

- Used in **bacterial persistence** to locate the source: separates **upper vs lower tract**, **one kidney vs the other**, and can localise to **ectopic ureters or non-refluxing ureteric stumps** (saline irrigation) — **Stamey's technique**.
- Particularly important with **fever, flank pain or CVAT**, and in **renal transplant recipients** to decide whether **native kidneys harbour infection**.

### Stone Cultures

- **Culture removed stones** to identify organisms and sensitivities in their interstices; analyse **separately from urine — results may be discordant**.
- **UTI is the commonest complication after stone procedures**; manipulating infected stones can release endotoxin → **systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) or fatal urosepsis**.
- **Stone culture predicts postoperative sepsis and SIRS better than periprocedural urine culture**; a systematic review supports it for **selecting antibiotics to prevent future UTI**, though its role in preventing postoperative UTI needs further study.

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> When bladder urine and the infecting focus can differ, <strong>sample the focus</strong>: ureteric urine localises the side, and the stone — not the urine — tells you what will cause postoperative sepsis.</p>

## IMAGING

- **Not required in most UTIs**; clinical and laboratory findings suffice.
- **Image** (to find abnormalities needing changed medical management or percutaneous/surgical intervention): **most men, compromised hosts, febrile infections, features of obstruction, failure to respond, recurrence pattern suggesting persistence** (Box 26.5).
- **UTI with possible obstruction must be evaluated urgently** (causes in Box 26.5).
- **DM** → **emphysematous pyelonephritis, papillary necrosis** (impacted papilla → acute obstruction). **Polycystic kidneys on dialysis → prone to perinephric abscess.**
- **Image if pyelonephritis persists after several days of appropriate antibiotics** (renal/perinephric abscess), and with **unusual organisms, especially urea-splitters (*Proteus*)** → look for **obstructing stones, strictures, fungal balls**.
- Second purpose: **find a focus of persistence** when bacteriuria **fails to resolve or recurs rapidly** — uncommon but often **surgically correctable** (Box 26.6).

<p class="tt">BOX 26.5 Indications for Radiologic Investigation in Acute Clinical Pyelonephritis</p>

- **Potential ureteric obstruction** (stone, stricture, tumour); **history of calculi, especially struvite**
- **Potential papillary necrosis** (sickle cell, severe DM, analgesic abuse)
- **GU surgery predisposing to obstruction** (reimplantation, diversion)
- **Poor response after 5–6 days** of appropriate antimicrobials
- **DM**; **polycystic kidneys on dialysis or with severe renal insufficiency**; **neuropathic bladder**
- **Unusual organisms**: **TB, fungi, urea-splitters (*Proteus*)**

<p class="tt">BOX 26.6 Correctable Urologic Abnormalities That Cause Bacterial Persistence</p>

- **Infection stones**; **chronic bacterial prostatitis**; **unilateral infected atrophic kidney**; **ureteric duplication and ectopic ureters**; **foreign bodies**
- **Urethral diverticula and infected periurethral glands**; **unilateral medullary sponge kidney**; **non-refluxing, normal-appearing infected ureteric stumps after nephrectomy**
- **Infected urachal cysts**; **infected communicating calyceal cysts**; **papillary necrosis**; **perivesical abscess with fistula to bladder**
- Pattern: each is a **nidus that antibiotics cannot sterilise**, so each is **cured only by removal or drainage**.

### Radionuclide Studies

- **Rarely used now.** **Iodine-131 hippuran, technetium-99m (⁹⁹ᵐTc) glucoheptonate and dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA)** show focal parenchymal damage, impaired function and reduced perfusion in acute infection.
- **Gallium-67** — useful in pyelonephritis/abscess but **positive in non-infective disease too**. **Indium-111 WBC** — limited when the presentation is not suggestive of infection.

### Ultrasonography

- **Non-invasive, quick, no radiation or contrast**; good for **calculi, hydronephrosis, pyonephrosis, perirenal abscess, PVR**; can be paired with a **plain radiograph** for stones.
- Limits: **operator-dependent**; poor with **obesity, dressings, drains, wounds**; **less sensitive than computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for parenchymal change in pyelonephritis**; **inferior to CT for small renal and most ureteric stones and for ureteric obstruction**.

### Computed Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging

- **Best anatomical detail**; more sensitive than excretory urography or ultrasound for **acute focal bacterial nephritis (AFBN), renal/perirenal abscess and radiolucent calculi**.
- **Non-contrast CT = best test for urinary calculi.** **Contrast CT localises abscesses and guides (percutaneous) drainage.**
- **MRI has not replaced CT** for renal inflammation but better shows **extrarenal extension**. **Pelvic MRI with intravenous (IV) contrast = best test for urethral diverticulum.**

### Voiding Cystourethrogram

- **Voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG)** assesses VUR — perform in women with **febrile UTIs, childhood VUR, or recurrent adult pyelonephritis**; consider with **rUTI plus hydronephrosis**.
- **Double-balloon VCUG for urethral diverticulum is historical; pelvic MRI is now standard.**

<p class="tt ct">Imaging modality by clinical question</p>

| Question | Best test |
|---|---|
| **Stones** | **Non-contrast CT** |
| **Abscess localisation / drainage** | **Contrast-enhanced CT** |
| **Extrarenal extension** | MRI |
| **Urethral diverticulum** | **Pelvic MRI with contrast** (not double-balloon VCUG) |
| **VUR / non-refluxing stump suspicion** | **VCUG** |
| **Bedside: hydronephrosis, pyonephrosis, PVR** | **Ultrasound** |

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> Imaging answers only two questions in UTI — <strong>is there obstruction or abscess needing drainage, and is there a focus sustaining persistence</strong> — so it is indicated by features that raise either possibility (male sex, fever, non-response, rapid recurrence, unusual organisms), not by the infection itself.</p>

## MICROBIOME

- **Urine is not sterile.** **16S ribosomal RNA NGS** shows many species in the urine of **healthy, asymptomatic** people; standard culture detects only **fast-growing, oxygen-tolerant** organisms and misses fastidious and anaerobic ones.
- **EQUC** — **larger urine volume, varied atmospheres, longer incubation** — grew bacteria from most samples that were **negative on standard culture**, identifying dozens of genera and species (Table 26.1) → confirms a **female urinary microbiome** of mostly **non-pathogenic** organisms.
- Implications: **bacteria will never be eradicated from urine, nor should that be the goal**; **many microbiome bacteria are protective**. Research asks whether **dysbiosis** underlies **urgency incontinence or painful bladder syndrome**.
- **Commercial NGS** (not FDA-approved): **no susceptibility testing, costly**, risks **inappropriate treatment**, and **reports go directly to patients** without clinician interpretation → confusion; **clinical utility still to be established**.

<p class="tt">TABLE 26.1 List of Bacterial Species Cultured in Different Conditions (Hilt et al., 2014)</p>

| Culture conditions tested | Aerobic 35 °C; aerobic 30 °C; CO₂ 35 °C; anaerobic 35 °C; Campylobacter atmosphere (6% O₂, 10% CO₂) 35 °C |
|---|---|
| **Genera most often isolated** | ***Lactobacillus*** (*gasseri, jensenii, crispatus, iners*), ***Streptococcus*** (esp. *anginosus*), ***Corynebacterium***, ***Actinomyces***, ***Gardnerella***, ***Staphylococcus*** (*epidermidis* and others), ***Micrococcus***, ***Aerococcus*** (*urinae, sanguinicola*), ***Bifidobacterium*** |
| Classic uropathogens also found | *E. coli, E. faecalis, K. pneumoniae, Enterobacter aerogenes, P. aeruginosa, Streptococcus agalactiae* |
| Fastidious / anaerobic organisms | *Actinobaculum schaalii*, *Alloscardovia*, *Facklamia*, *Finegoldia magna*, *Fusobacterium nucleatum*, *Peptoniphilus*, *Prevotella bivia*, *Propionimicrobium*, *Oligella*, *Trueperella*, *Campylobacter ureolyticus*; *Candida glabrata*; several unclassified isolates |

- Purpose: to show that **many organisms need non-standard atmospheres or low-dilution plating**, so **"no growth" on standard culture ≠ sterile urine**. Pattern: **lactobacilli and other commensals dominate**. Trap: the presence of ***Aerococcus*, *Gardnerella* or even *E. coli* in an asymptomatic woman does not, by itself, mean infection**.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: MICROBIOME**

- **Urine is not sterile.**
- The **female urinary microbiome**, identified by sequencing, should inform UTI management and interpretation of **EQUC**.
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> The microbiome turns the question from <strong>"are bacteria present?" to "is this organism causing harm?"</strong> — the more sensitive the detection, the more the decision to treat must rest on symptoms rather than on the laboratory report.</p>

## ASYMPTOMATIC BACTERIURIA

- The microbiome **renders the term ASB essentially obsolete**, but it remains in use: **no signs or symptoms of UTI, yet bacteria in a non-contaminated sample**.

<p class="tt">BOX 26.7 Diagnosis of Asymptomatic Bacteriuria (Infectious Diseases Society of America [IDSA]: Nicolle et al., 2005; Nicolle, 2019)</p>

| Group | Specimen | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| **Asymptomatic women** | **Two consecutive voided** specimens, **same strain** | **≥10⁵ CFU/mL** |
| **Asymptomatic men** | **Single voided** specimen, one species | **≥10⁵ CFU/mL** |
| **Either sex** | **Single catheterised** specimen, one species | **≥10² CFU/mL** |

- Prerequisites: **no signs or symptoms**; specimen **collected to minimise contamination**.
- Trap: women need **two** voided samples (contamination risk), men only **one**; a catheter sample needs only **100 CFU/mL**.

- **Prevalence rises with age** — **<1% of schoolgirls → about a fifth of healthy community women over 80**; tracks **sexual activity** (married women far above age-matched nuns); rises after **menopause**; uncommon in young men, increasing with age.

<p class="tt">TABLE 26.2 Prevalence of Asymptomatic Bacteriuria in Selected Populations (data from Nicolle et al., 2005)</p>

| Population | Prevalence (%) |
|---|---|
| Healthy premenopausal women | 1–5 |
| **Pregnant women** | **1.9–9.5** |
| Postmenopausal women 50–70 years | 2.8–8.6 |
| Diabetes — women / men | **9–27** / 0.7–11 |
| Elderly in community — women / men | 10.8–16 / 3.6–19 |
| **Elderly in long-term care — women / men** | **25–50 / 14–50** |
| **SCI — intermittent catheterisation** | **23–89** |
| SCI — sphincterotomy + condom catheter | 57 |
| Haemodialysis | 28 |
| Indwelling catheter — short term | 9–23 |
| **Indwelling catheter — long term** | **100** |

- Purpose: shows **risk factors for ASB — advanced age, female sex, institutionalisation, DM, clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC) or indwelling catheter**. Pattern: prevalence climbs with **instrumentation and institutionalisation**, peaking at **100% with long-term catheters**. Trap: **high prevalence is exactly why these groups should not be treated** — only pregnancy, with a modest prevalence, is screened.
- The text gives **6–15%** for men over 75 in the community and **15–40%** for men in long-term care; the table gives 3.6–19% and 14–50% — **different source studies** for overlapping populations.
- **DM**: ASB more common in **women than men**; associated with **duration of disease and poor control**.

- **Organisms:** community elderly women — ***E. coli* predominates** (others: *K. pneumoniae, P. mirabilis, E. faecalis*). **ASB *E. coli* carry fewer virulence factors**; some are true commensals or **virulent strains turned commensal**. Men — Gram-negative bacilli, enterococci, CoNS. **Institutionalised/catheterised → polymicrobial**, with ***P. aeruginosa, Morganella morganii, Providencia stuartii***.
- **Untreated ASB does not cause rUTI, hypertension, renal insufficiency or urinary tract cancer.** **Treating ASB is potentially harmful** → **more subsequent symptomatic UTIs**, cost, adverse effects.

<p class="tt ct">Guideline bodies on treating ASB</p>

| Body | Year | Position |
|---|---|---|
| **IDSA** (AUA-endorsed) | 2005 | **Do not treat** most adults; treat only as in Box 26.8; no recommendation for transplant/immunocompromised |
| **IDSA update** | 2019 | Reaffirms; **extends “do not treat”** to children, transplant recipients and non-urological surgery (Box 26.8) |
| **US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF)** | 2008, reaffirmed 2019 | **Screen only pregnant women** |
| **Cochrane review** (randomised/quasi-randomised trials; excluded pregnancy, catheters, stents, transplant, SCI, recent procedures, inpatients) | 2015 | Treatment **eradicates bacteriuria more often** but **no difference in symptomatic UTI, complications or death**; **more adverse effects**; **no renal decline** untreated |

<p class="tt">BOX 26.8 Treat or Not Treat Asymptomatic Bacteriuria (IDSA 2005; 2019)</p>

| **Do NOT treat** | **Treat** |
|---|---|
| Premenopausal non-pregnant women; women with DM; older community dwellers; elderly institutionalised; SCI; indwelling catheters; **pyuria with ASB**; infants and children; **DM including men**; renal transplant **>1 month**; non-renal solid organ transplant; **non-urological surgery** | **Pregnancy**; **procedures with anticipated transmucosal bleeding** |
| **No consensus: neutropenia** | |

- **Landmark trial — Cai et al. (young women with rUTI and ASB, observe vs treat):** **treating ASB increased symptomatic UTI** over 1 year; **antibiotic use was an independent risk factor (hazard ratio [HR] about 3)**. Most untreated women who stayed well carried **asymptomatic *E. faecalis*** → **treatment removes a protective strain**. At about 3 years, recurrence was **roughly 38% untreated vs 70% treated**, with **more resistant *E. coli*** (amoxicillin–clavulanate, TMP-SMX, ciprofloxacin) in treated women **after ≥2 years**.
- **Barriers to guideline uptake**: lack of awareness, confusion, **fear of not prescribing**; **about half of practitioners across specialties are unaware of IDSA guidance** on ASB and cystitis.
- **Bacterial interference** — deliberately establishing **avirulent bacteriuria** to block virulent organisms; **efficacy not yet established**; merits study.
- ***ASB before non-urological (joint) surgery:*** in arthroplasty, **ASB was associated with more prosthetic infection**, but **preoperative treatment did not change it** and the **joint and urine organisms differed** → **ASB is a surrogate marker of infection risk, not the source**. **Do not treat ASB before orthopaedic surgery.** Dropping routine preoperative urine culture **did not change joint infection rates and saved money**.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: ASYMPTOMATIC BACTERIURIA**

- Prevalence varies with **age, sex and comorbidity**.
- Untreated ASB is **not associated with hypertension or renal insufficiency**.
- Guidelines advise **not screening or treating** except in specific groups.
- **Screen and treat in pregnancy and before urological procedures with anticipated mucosal bleeding.**
- **Treating ASB breeds MDR symptomatic UTI.**
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> <strong>ASB is treated only where the bacteria are about to be given a route into blood or tissue</strong> — pregnancy (hydroureter and pyelonephritis risk) and mucosa-breaching urological procedures; everywhere else the resident strain is harmless or protective, and eradicating it selects resistance and invites a virulent replacement.</p>

## PRINCIPLES OF ANTIMICROBIAL THERAPY

- Agent and duration depend on **spectrum against the known or probable pathogen** (by source of acquisition), **uncomplicated vs complicated**, **adverse effects, cost**.
- **Serum levels are irrelevant in uncomplicated UTI** (urine levels matter) but **critical in bacteraemia and febrile parenchymal infection (kidney, prostate)**.

<p class="tt ct">Which drug level matters</p>

| Uncomplicated cystitis | Febrile / parenchymal infection |
|---|---|
| **Urinary concentration** matters | **Serum and tissue concentration** matter |

- **Renal insufficiency:** adjust dose of **renally cleared** drugs; the failing kidney **may not concentrate the drug in urine** → eradication difficult. **Obstruction also lowers urinary drug levels.**
- Consider the drug's effect on **bowel and vaginal flora and the hospital ecology**; susceptibility varies by exposure and setting.
- **Do not treat a positive culture alone**; weigh the **likelihood of benefit vs harm**. **Uncomplicated cystitis rarely progresses even untreated — the goal is symptom relief.** In placebo arms of trials, a substantial minority of women were **symptom-free or improved within about 9 days**, and **progression to pyelonephritis was rare**.
- Finucane's framing: ask not whether it is a "real UTI" but **whether antibiotics directed at bacteriuria are more likely to benefit than harm**.
- **Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) alone vs antibiotics** (meta-analysis of randomised trials, non-pregnant women): **antibiotics are more effective** (moderate evidence); NSAIDs cure a proportion but with **higher symptom burden, longer symptoms and more pyelonephritis**. Role of analgesics **combined** with antibiotics remains to be defined.

### Ecological Impact and Collateral Damage

- **Collateral damage** = ecological adverse effects — **resistance, selection and MDR colonisation** — linked especially to **broad-spectrum cephalosporins and quinolones**.
- Particularly relevant to **uncomplicated cystitis** because (1) **minimal risk of invasion/sepsis** and (2) it is **one of the commonest antibiotic exposures in healthy people**.
- Resistance rises from **microbial traits, antibiotic selection pressure and spread**; **varies geographically**.

<p class="tt ct">Types of bacterial resistance</p>

| Resistance type | Mechanism | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Intrinsic chromosomal** | Species **lacks the drug's target/mechanism** | Predictable from species |
| **Acquired chromosomal** | **Resistant mutants selected during therapy**; repeat culture at **24–48 h** shows high counts of resistant mutants | Occurs when **urine level ≈ or < MIC**: **underdosing, non-compliance** → choose drug with urine level ≫ MIC, dose fully, stress compliance |
| **Extrachromosomal (plasmid)** | **Horizontal gene transfer**, mostly **plasmids**; arises in **bowel flora**; **far commoner** than urinary mutant selection | **All classes can induce it**; transferable **within and across genera** — e.g. tetracycline exposure → bowel strains resistant to **tetracycline, ampicillin, sulfonamides and TMP** → post-antibiotic UTIs often **MDR** |

<p class="tt ct">Specific β-lactamase and carbapenemase mechanisms</p>

| Mechanism | Encoded | Confers resistance to | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ESBL** (first reported 1983) | **Plasmid** | **Most β-lactams incl. 3rd-generation cephalosporins** | ESBL *E. coli* often **co-resistant to fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides** |
| **AmpC β-lactamase** | **Chromosomal** in many Enterobacteriaceae; now also **transmissible plasmids** (e.g. into *E. coli*) | **Broad-spectrum cephalosporins** | — |
| ***K. pneumoniae* carbapenemase (KPC)** (first 2001) | — | **Carbapenems** (highly drug-resistant Gram-negatives) | Prevalent US mechanism; mostly *K. pneumoniae* but spread to other Gram-negative bacilli |

- **Resistance scales with duration and quantity of use** (e.g. hospital fluoroquinolone use → ***Pseudomonas* quinolone resistance**); **reducing use can restore susceptibility**.
- **Fluoroquinolone overuse** → rising quinolone resistance across Europe, **higher still in ESBL isolates in institutions**, and **highest in transplant units using quinolone prophylaxis**. **Prior quinolone use and underlying urological disease** are the strongest determinants. **Fluoroquinolone resistance is a harbinger of MDR** and of resistance to first-line agents.
- **Commensal disruption:** gut → ***Clostridioides difficile*** and **vancomycin-resistant colonisation**, and susceptibility to enteric infection; vagina → **candidiasis**, related to **duration and breadth** of antibiotic use and **prior *Candida* episodes**. → **Narrow the spectrum and shorten the course, especially in uncomplicated UTI.**

#### Nitrofurantoin

- Active against most common uropathogens; **rapidly excreted in urine, but no therapeutic tissue levels (including gut)** → **useless for upper tract, complicated or bloodstream infection**.
- **Minimal effect on bowel and vaginal flora; acquired resistance exceedingly low.**
- Adverse: **GI upset; rare pulmonary fibrosis with chronic use; haemolytic anaemia in G6PD deficiency**.
- **Renal threshold** (table below): caution in significant impairment, especially **long courses** — in a large cohort **CrCl 30–50 did not reduce efficacy**, but **CrCl <50 predicted pulmonary adverse events needing admission**.

<p class="tt ct">Nitrofurantoin renal cut-offs by source</p>

| Source | Nitrofurantoin avoid below |
|---|---|
| Historical recommendation | CrCl **<60 mL/min** |
| **American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria (2015)** | CrCl **<30 mL/min** |
| Table 26.6 (older formulary data) | CrCl **<50 mL/min** |

- **Beers Criteria strongly advise against long-term suppressive use**, but **not against short courses** in selected elderly with normal renal function. The authors keep it **first-line for acute uncomplicated cystitis in selected elderly**, though the blanket long-term warning creates **insurance barriers** even to short courses.

#### Trimethoprim–Sulfamethoxazole

- Widely used; **therapeutic levels in most tissues**; covers most uropathogens **except *Enterococcus* and *Pseudomonas***; **cheap; minimal bowel flora effect**.
- Adverse: common **rash and GI upset**; rare **Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis** — **TMP-SMX is the antibiotic most often implicated**.

#### Fosfomycin

- **Bactericidal**, oral and IV, phosphonic acid–like; active against most uropathogens.
- Advantages: **limited cross-resistance**, activity against **most Gram-negatives and vancomycin-resistant *Enterococcus* (VRE)**, **single-dose efficacy** in empirical uncomplicated cystitis → **excellent oral option for MDR organisms that would otherwise need IV therapy**.
- **Well tolerated** (low GI upset, headache). **Traditionally not for urosepsis**, though emerging data suggest a role in **bacteraemic MDR *E. coli* UTI**. **Cost** (3 g sachet) may limit use.

#### Fluoroquinolones

- Descend from **nalidixic acid**; **inhibit DNA gyrase**; **broad spectrum** — **Enterobacteriaceae and *P. aeruginosa***, **high activity against *S. aureus* and *S. saprophyticus***, **marginal streptococcal** cover; **most anaerobes resistant → vaginal and bowel flora spared**.
- Resistance has **risen with indiscriminate use**.
- **Not nephrotoxic**, but renal insufficiency prolongs half-life → **adjust dose at CrCl <30 mL/min**.
- Adverse: **GI (commonest; commensal disruption)**, hypersensitivity, skin reactions, **serious central and peripheral nervous system effects**, acute renal failure; **anxiety, depression, hallucinations, confusion, suicidal thoughts**.
- **Achilles tendinopathy/rupture** — class effect; **stop at the first sign of tendon pain**. Risk: **advanced age, chronic renal failure, male sex, normal BMI, corticosteroids**. Mechanism: ciprofloxacin **stimulates fibroblast matrix-degrading proteases** and **inhibits matrix synthesis**. Also paraesthesiae, myalgia, arthralgia.
- **Cartilage damage in immature animals → contraindicated in children, adolescents, pregnancy and breastfeeding.**
- Interactions (warfarin — a World Health Organization [WHO] warning — antacids, theophylline): see Table 26.6.

| Year | Warning |
|---|---|
| **2008** | **Boxed warning: tendon rupture** |
| **2013** | **Irreversible peripheral nerve damage** added |
| **2015** | **Fluoroquinolone-associated disability (FQAD)** recognised (chronic pain commonest) |

- The **European Commission** has imposed similarly stringent restrictions. Mechanism of serious toxicity (**mitochondrial damage** proposed) is unproven.

#### Cephalosporins

- **Five generations**; as a group **good against Enterobacteriaceae, poor against enterococci**.
- **1st** — more Gram-positive activity plus *E. coli*, *K. pneumoniae*; **2nd** — **anaerobic activity**; **3rd** — **most reliable β-lactams against community and nosocomial Gram-negatives**; **4th/5th** — usable in complicated UTI.
- **β-lactams are for uncomplicated cystitis only when first-line agents cannot be used.**

#### Carbapenems

- β-lactams, broad spectrum; bind and inactivate **penicillin-binding proteins**; **stable to most β-lactamases including AmpC and ESBLs**; cover many Gram-positive, Gram-negative and anaerobic organisms.
- **Gaps: *E. faecium* and MRSA.** **Ertapenem additionally lacks *P. aeruginosa* and *Enterococcus* cover.**

#### Aminoglycosides

- **With TMP-SMX or ampicillin**, effective for **febrile UTI, especially in hospital**; **nephro- and ototoxicity** → monitor renal and auditory function.
- **Once-daily dosing** exploits **concentration-dependent killing (peak:MIC)**, **time-dependent toxicity** and a **prolonged post-antibiotic effect**.
- Regimen: **fixed 5–7 mg/kg gentamicin or tobramycin**; interval from **one serum level plotted on a nomogram** (Fig. 26.12) — e.g. **7 mg/L at 10 h → redose 7 mg/kg every 36 h**. **Effective, less nephrotoxic, cheaper** (fewer levels and staff time).

<p class="tt">Figs. 26.11–26.12 Once-daily aminoglycoside dosing (Nicolau et al., 1995)</p>

- **Fig. 26.11:** once-daily **7 mg/kg/24 h** gives a **high peak then a long trough**; conventional **1.5 mg/kg/8 h** gives **low, repeated peaks** — once-daily maximises peak:MIC and minimises time at toxic trough levels.
- **Fig. 26.12 nomogram** (gentamicin/tobramycin 7 mg/kg): the level drawn **6–14 h after the start of infusion** falls in a zone → **redose every 24, 36 or 48 h**.

#### Aminopenicillins

- **Resistance in 40–60% of urinary isolates** has limited ampicillin/amoxicillin; they disturb bowel and vaginal flora → **resistant reinfection and candidal vaginitis**.
- **Clavulanate** restores activity against β-lactamase producers, but **cost and GI effects** limit it.
- **Extended-spectrum penicillins** (pivmecillinam, piperacillin, mezlocillin, azlocillin) keep **enterococcal activity** and add many **ampicillin-resistant Gram-negatives** → suited to **nosocomial UTI** and **initial parenteral therapy of community pyelonephritis**, though cheaper agents may be equal.

#### Aztreonam

- **Spectrum similar to aminoglycosides**, **not nephrotoxic** (as all β-lactams), but narrower than 3rd-generation cephalosporins; use **mainly in penicillin allergy**.

#### Pivmecillinam

- **Prodrug of mecillinam**, penicillin-like β-lactam; **high Gram-negative activity**; used mainly in **Nordic countries** for empirical uncomplicated cystitis; **not available in the USA**; **very low *E. coli* resistance (about 2%)**, safe and effective.

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> The formulary is organised by a single trade-off: <strong>spectrum and tissue penetration buy efficacy in complicated infection but cost collateral damage</strong> — so the narrow, urine-confined, flora-sparing agents (nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, pivmecillinam) lead in cystitis, and the broad, tissue-penetrating agents (fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, carbapenems) are held back for infections that invade.</p>

### Choice of Antimicrobial Agents

- Empirical choice: **complicated vs uncomplicated**, **spectrum vs probable pathogen**, **hypersensitivity**, **renal/hepatic toxicity**, **ecological impact**, **cost**; use **regional antibiograms**.
- ***C. difficile* infection (CDI):** increasingly common and resistant; large US burden and mortality.
    - **Most likely culprits: cephalosporins, clindamycin, carbapenems, TMP-SMX, fluoroquinolones, penicillin combinations.** **Not associated: macrolides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides.**
    - **Diagnosis requires both** (1) **diarrhoea (≥3 unformed stools in 24 h)** or radiological **ileus/toxic megacolon**, and (2) **stool positive for toxigenic *C. difficile* or its toxins**, or **histological/colonoscopic pseudomembranous colitis**.
    - Treatment by **severity and recurrence risk**. The text's older statement (metronidazole for mild, vancomycin for severe, as metronidazole fails more with severity) is **superseded by the 2021 IDSA/Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America (SHEA) focused update** (Table 26.7), which adds **fidaxomicin** (macrolide) and **bezlotoxumab** (monoclonal antibody to **toxin B**).

<p class="tt">TABLE 26.7 Recommendations for the Treatment of *Clostridioides difficile* Infection in Adults (Johnson et al., IDSA/SHEA 2021)</p>

| Presentation | Preferred | Alternatives / adjuncts | Comments |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Initial episode** | **Fidaxomicin 200 mg twice daily × 10 days** | **Oral vancomycin 125 mg four times daily × 10 days**; if unavailable and **non-severe**: **metronidazole 500 mg three times daily × 10–14 days** | **Non-severe = WBC ≤15,000/µL and creatinine <1.5 mg/dL** |
| **First recurrence** | **Fidaxomicin** 200 mg twice daily × 10 days, **or** twice daily × 5 days then **alternate days × 20 days** | **Tapered/pulsed vancomycin**; standard vancomycin × 10 days; **adjunct bezlotoxumab 10 mg/kg IV once** | Taper example: 125 mg four times daily 10–14 d → twice daily 7 d → daily 7 d → every 2–3 days for 2–8 weeks; **standard vancomycin if metronidazole was used first** |
| **Second or later recurrence** | Fidaxomicin (either regimen); tapered/pulsed vancomycin; **vancomycin then rifaximin 400 mg three times daily × 20 days**; **faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)** | **Adjunct bezlotoxumab** | **Try antibiotics for ≥2 recurrences (3 episodes) before FMT** |
| **Fulminant** (hypotension/shock, ileus, megacolon) | **Vancomycin 500 mg four times daily orally/nasogastric** + **IV metronidazole 500 mg 8-hourly**, especially with ileus | **Rectal vancomycin if ileus** | — |

- Bezlotoxumab: also for other recurrence risks (**age >65, immunocompromise, severe CDI at presentation**); **FDA caution in congestive heart failure** — reserve where benefit outweighs risk.
- Purpose: severity- and recurrence-staged escalation. Pattern: **fidaxomicin first line throughout**, **vancomycin always acceptable**, **metronidazole only a fallback for non-severe first episodes (and IV in fulminant disease)**. Trap: **oral metronidazole is no longer first-line**; **FMT only after ≥2 recurrences**.

### Duration of Therapy

- Determined by **extent and duration of tissue invasion, urinary bacterial concentration, achievable urinary drug level, and host risk factors**.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: PRINCIPLES OF ANTIMICROBIAL THERAPY**

- **Antimicrobial resistance is increasing.**
- Selection weighs **efficacy, safety, cost and compliance**.
- **Agent and duration** are critical to limiting resistance and adverse events.
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> <strong>Resistance is manufactured mainly in the bowel, not the bladder</strong>: plasmid transfer among gut flora dwarfs mutant selection in urine, so the ecological footprint of an antibiotic — breadth, duration, repetition — matters more than its MIC against the organism in front of you.</p>
