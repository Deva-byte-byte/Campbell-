## BLADDER INFECTIONS

### Uncomplicated Cystitis

- **Acute uncomplicated cystitis is the overwhelming majority of UTIs** and is often managed by non-urologists.
- Annually about one in ten women reports a UTI, and **most women have at least one in their lifetime**; incidence is low before puberty and **rises sharply in late adolescence and the second to fourth decades**; about a third of women have had a physician-diagnosed UTI **by age 26**.
- ***E. coli* causes 75–90% of acute cystitis in young women**; ***S. saprophyticus*** (a skin commensal) is the next common cause; less often *Klebsiella, Proteus, Enterococcus*. In men, *E. coli* and other Enterobacteriaceae.

<p class="tt">BOX 26.9 Risk Factors for Urinary Tract Infections</p>

| Mechanism | Risk factors |
|---|---|
| **Reduced urine flow** | **Outflow obstruction** (prostatic hyperplasia, prostatic carcinoma, urethral stricture, foreign body/calculus); **neurogenic bladder**; **inadequate fluid intake (dehydration)** |
| **Promote colonisation** | **Sexual activity** (↑ inoculation); **spermicide** (↑ binding); **oestrogen depletion** (↑ binding); **antimicrobials** (↓ indigenous flora) |
| **Facilitate ascent** | **Catheterisation**; **urinary incontinence**; **faecal incontinence**; **residual urine with bladder wall ischaemia** |

- Purpose: sorts risk factors by **which host defence they defeat** — washout, flora barrier or the distance to ascend. Trap: **spermicide and oestrogen loss act by increasing bacterial binding**, not by increasing inoculum.

#### Clinical Presentation

- The validated **UTI Symptom Assessment questionnaire** scores seven symptoms — **frequency, urgency, painful urination, incomplete emptying, suprapubic pain/pressure, low back pain, haematuria** — for presence, **severity and bother**.
- **Cystitis is a superficial mucosal infection → fever and chills are usually absent.**
- **Malodorous or turbid urine alone is not a reliable sign of UTI** → advise **hydration and reassess**.
- **More than one irritative symptom raises the likelihood**, but **dysuria alone is the most reliable single indicator** and drives the highest severity and bother scores.
- In **baseline voiding dysfunction**, establish how symptoms **differ from baseline**. Distinguish **dysuria (burning on voiding)** from **constant vaginal/pelvic burning** (painful bladder syndrome, **genitourinary syndrome of menopause [GSM]**).
- **Dysuria + frequency without vaginal discharge or irritation predicts UTI in about 90%**; probability rises further with **haematuria, back pain and CVAT**.
- Symptom meta-analysis: **frequency, dysuria, haematuria, nocturia, urgency increase** probability; **vaginal discharge decreases** it; **haematuria + dipstick nitrite → probability ≥90%**.
- **Bottom line: acute-onset dysuria and a change from baseline voiding symptoms** best indicate acute uncomplicated cystitis.

#### Laboratory Diagnosis

- As in Evaluation (urine testing).

#### Management

- ***Antimicrobial selection*** (IDSA/European Society for Microbiology and Infectious Diseases [ESCMID] guideline, Fig. 26.13): individualise by **allergy, compliance, local antibiogram/practice, availability, cost**.

<p class="tt">Fig. 26.13 IDSA-recommended treatment of acute uncomplicated cystitis in women</p>

- **Entry:** woman with acute uncomplicated cystitis, **no fever or flank pain or other suspicion of pyelonephritis**, **able to take oral medication** — if not, **treat as pyelonephritis/complicated UTI**.
- **Can a first-line agent be used (availability, allergy, tolerance)?** → Yes → first-line; No → second-line.

<p class="tt ct">Uncomplicated cystitis regimens: first-line versus second-line</p>

| Tier | Agent and regimen | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **First-line** | **Nitrofurantoin monohydrate/macrocrystals 100 mg twice daily × 5 days** | Avoid if **early pyelonephritis** suspected; efficacy ≈ 3-day TMP-SMX |
| **First-line** | **TMP-SMX 160/800 mg (one double-strength [DS] tablet) twice daily × 3 days** | **Avoid if local resistance >20% or used for UTI in previous 3 months** |
| **First-line** | **Fosfomycin trometamol 3 g single dose** | **Lower efficacy**; avoid if early pyelonephritis |
| **First-line** | **Pivmecillinam 400 mg twice daily × 5 days** (3–7 days in text) | **Lower efficacy**; not available in North America; avoid if early pyelonephritis |
| **Second-line** | **Fluoroquinolones × 3 days** | **High collateral damage; reserve as last resort** (FDA 2016); resistance high in some areas |
| **Second-line** | **β-lactams** (amoxicillin–clavulanate, cefdinir, cefaclor, cefpodoxime-proxetil) × 3–7 days; EAU: **cefadroxil 500 mg twice daily × 3 days** | **Avoid ampicillin/amoxicillin alone**; lower efficacy; close follow-up |

- **TMP-SMX/TMP** achieve bacteriological cure within 7 days in **>90%**; use where *E. coli* resistance is **≤20%**. **TMP alone** suits **postcoital prophylaxis** and **sulfa allergy** — but **TMP itself causes rash/hypersensitivity** that may be wrongly blamed on sulfa.
- Pattern: **first-line agents share low resistance and low collateral damage**; the fluoroquinolones are demoted **not for inefficacy but for ecology and toxicity**.

- ***Duration of therapy:***
    - **Three days is the preferred duration for uncomplicated cystitis in women.** Across hundreds of trials, **3 days beats single dose** for cure, and **matches 7 days for cure with adverse effects as low as a single dose**; **7 days only with complicating factors**.
    - Exceptions to "3 days": **nitrofurantoin 5 days; fosfomycin single dose**.
    - **Men — landmark trial (Drekonja et al., 2021):** in **afebrile men** with suspected UTI, **7 days of ciprofloxacin or TMP-SMX was non-inferior to 14 days** for symptom resolution. **European Association of Urology (EAU): TMP-SMX 160/800 twice daily × 7 days first-line for cystitis in men**; fluoroquinolones per local susceptibility.

<p class="tt ct">Duration of therapy for cystitis</p>

| Group | Duration |
|---|---|
| **Women — TMP-SMX, fluoroquinolone, β-lactam** | **3 days** (β-lactams 3–7) |
| **Women — nitrofurantoin** | **5 days** |
| **Women — fosfomycin** | **Single dose** |
| **Women with complicating factors** | 7 days |
| **Afebrile men** | **7 days (= 14 days)** |

- ***Cost of therapy:*** cost-effectiveness depends on **efficacy against *E. coli*** (failures → revisits, pyelonephritis, follow-up); **drug price is a poor predictor**.

#### Follow-Up

- **No follow-up visit or culture in asymptomatic women after treatment**; no urological evaluation if they respond.
- **UTI in most men is complicated** and linked to functional/anatomical pathology — in a small cohort of men evaluated after UTI, **about half had an abnormality, commonest incomplete emptying**.
- **Non-response → microbiological and urological evaluation** for unresolved/complicated UTI.
- ***Compliance with recommended agents:*** about half of surveyed physicians **did not know the IDSA guideline** and nearly a third **did not choose a first-line agent** → education and outreach needed.

### Complicated Urinary Tract Infections

- Occur in a **compromised urinary tract** or with a **resistant pathogen** (Table 26.8); complicating factors may be **obvious** from severity/history or **revealed only by treatment failure**.

<p class="tt">TABLE 26.8 Host Factors Classifying a Urinary Tract Infection as Complicated (Dason et al., Canadian Urological Association, 2011)</p>

| Complication | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Anatomical abnormality** | **Cystocele, fistula, diverticulum** |
| **Iatrogenic** | **Nosocomial infection, surgery, indwelling catheter** |
| **Urinary tract obstruction** | **BOO, pelviureteric junction obstruction, ureteric stricture** |
| **Voiding dysfunction** | **Neurological disease, pelvic floor dysfunction, VUR, incontinence, high PVR** |
| **Other** | **Pregnancy, urolithiasis, diabetes, other immunosuppression** |

- Purpose: a checklist of **host features that should change the default short oral course**. Pattern: each **creates stasis, a foreign surface or immune compromise**. Trap: **VUR and high PVR are classified as voiding dysfunction**, and **pregnancy and stones** sit under "other".

- Spectrum: **mild cystitis → life-threatening pyelonephritis and urosepsis**; often **MDR** → **urine culture is mandatory**.
- Empirical guidance is limited (heterogeneous hosts, few trials).
    - **Mild–moderate, oral outpatient therapy:** traditionally **fluoroquinolones for 10–14 days** (urinary concentration and tissue penetration) — now tempered by **resistance and toxicity**.
    - **Hospitalised:** **IV agents chosen by local susceptibility**.
- **Correct the underlying abnormality (e.g. obstruction) and host factors** — otherwise therapy fails.
- **Total 10–14 days of culture-directed therapy; switch IV → oral once afebrile and stable; repeat culture if no response.**

#### Emphysematous Cystitis

- **Rare, potentially life-threatening** complicated cystitis; **mortality up to 7%**. **Pathognomonic: gas within the bladder wall on cross-sectional imaging.**
- Typical patient: **elderly woman (60–70 years) with poorly controlled DM**; also **incomplete emptying/stasis (NGB, BOO)**.
- Organisms: ***E. coli* (about 60%)**, ***K. pneumoniae* (10–20%)**.
- ***Clinical presentation:*** **asymptomatic (up to 7%) to overt sepsis**; **abdominal pain commonest (about 80%)**, then **gross haematuria (about 60%)**, obstructive symptoms; **fever in only 30–50%**.
- ***Radiological findings:*** plain film — **curvilinear radiolucency outlining the bladder wall**, separate from rectal gas; **"cobblestone" or "beaded necklace"** pattern of submucosal blebs. **CT is required** to confirm and **exclude other pelvic gas (fistula, trauma, gangrene of adjacent organs)** (Fig. 26.14: intramural and intraluminal gas).
- ***Management:*** **about 90% medical alone** — **(often IV) antibiotics with broad Gram-negative cover, bladder drainage, control of DM** → narrow by culture. **Gram-positive cocci on Gram stain → add ampicillin/amoxicillin (enterococcus).** **Surgery rare** — poor response or severe necrosis: **debridement, partial or total cystectomy**.

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> Emphysematous cystitis is <strong>diabetic stasis cystitis with gas as its signature</strong> — it is managed like any severe complicated cystitis (drain, treat, control glucose), and CT's real job is to prove the gas is intramural rather than from a fistula or gangrene that would need surgery.</p>

### Unresolved Urinary Tract Infections

#### Clinical Presentation

- **Unresolved = initial therapy failed to eliminate symptoms and/or bacterial growth.** If symptoms persist at the end of treatment or recur shortly after → **urinalysis and culture with susceptibilities**; if symptoms are significant and not relieved by **urinary analgesics**, start **broad-spectrum empirical therapy** pending culture.

<p class="tt">BOX 26.10 Causes of Unresolved Bacteriuria, in Descending Order of Importance</p>

1. **Bacterial resistance to the drug selected**
2. **Development of resistance in initially susceptible bacteria**
3. **Two species with mutually exclusive susceptibilities**
4. **Rapid reinfection with a new resistant species during therapy**
5. **Azotaemia**
6. **Papillary necrosis from analgesic abuse**
7. **Giant staghorn calculi** — "critical mass" of susceptible bacteria too great
8. **Self-inflicted infection or deception about taking drugs** (Munchausen variant)

<p class="tt ct">Mechanisms of unresolved bacteriuria</p>

| Mechanism | Explanation | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Pre-existing resistance** (commonest) | Recent antibiotics → **bowel colonisation with resistant bacteria**; **β-lactams, tetracycline, sulfonamides** notorious for **plasmid R factors carrying multi-resistance** | Organism resistant from the outset |
| **2. Emergent resistance** | Low-count resistant subpopulation **selected when urine level < killing concentration** | **On-treatment culture: same species, now resistant** |
| **3. Unsuspected second pathogen** | Treating the dominant strain **unmasks** a resistant second strain | New species present from the start |
| **4. Rapid reinfection** | New resistant species introduced during therapy | **Suspect enterovesical fistula** |
| **5–7. Organism still susceptible** | Drug can't reach adequate urine level (**azotaemia; papillary necrosis dilutes drug**), or bacterial mass overwhelms it (**staghorn — even susceptible bacteria cannot be inhibited beyond a critical density, especially on a foreign body**) | **On-treatment culture: same species, still sensitive** |
| **8. Non-compliance/deception** | — | — |

#### Laboratory Diagnosis

- **Urinalysis and culture mandatory.** **Causes 1–4 (resistant bacteria) need no further work-up.** If the organism is **still sensitive** to the current drug → **renal function and imaging** for renal/urinary tract abnormalities.

#### Management

- Significant symptoms → broad-spectrum empirical therapy **assuming resistance** → **choose a different agent from the original**.

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> <strong>The on-treatment culture sorts every unresolved infection into two bins</strong>: if the organism is now resistant, the problem is microbiological and needs only a new drug; if it is still sensitive, the problem is the host (drug delivery or bacterial mass) and needs investigation.</p>

### Recurrent Urinary Tract Infections

- **Recurrent UTI (rUTI) = 2 UTIs in 6 months or ≥3 in 12 months.**
- Common in healthy women — about a fifth of college women have another culture-proven UTI **within 6 months**; **20–30% recurrence** overall. **A recent prior UTI is one of the strongest risk factors.**

<p class="tt ct">Bacterial persistence versus reinfection</p>

| **Bacterial persistence** | **Reinfection** |
|---|---|
| **Same strain** from a focus in the tract | **Same organism >2 weeks after treatment, or a different organism** |
| Recurs **quickly** | Recurs **later** |
| **Uncommon** | **Commoner** — ascent from **faecal flora** or **re-emergence from intracellular urothelial reservoirs** |

- **In men, reinfection raises suspicion of an anatomical abnormality.**

#### Evaluation

- Aim: **prevent recurrence** by identifying risk factors and modifiable behaviour; exclude external anatomical or functional abnormality (if present → **complicated**).
- **History:** number, frequency, **culture results**, symptoms, triggers; **pneumaturia, faecaluria, obstipation, diverticulitis, pelvic surgery or radiotherapy → fistula**; **bowel symptoms** (constipation, diarrhoea, faecal incontinence); prior treatments and response.
- **Risk factors** in Box 26.11. **Nonoxynol-9 kills lactobacilli but not *E. coli***; **spermicide-coated condoms increase UTI**.

<p class="tt">BOX 26.11 Risk Factors for Recurrent Urinary Tract Infections</p>

- **Sexual activity**; **new partner within past year**; **family history of UTI in a first-degree female relative**; **recent antimicrobial use**; **spermicide use**; **UTI before menopause**; **menopause**; **incontinence, raised PVR, cystocele (postmenopausal)**.

- **Examination:** suprapubic palpation (**distended bladder; PVR drives bacteriuria**); pelvic examination — **vaginal epithelium (postmenopausal), POP, urethral diverticulum**.
- **Urinalysis and culture are imperative**: many are treated for symptoms that are really **irritative storage symptoms**; document **bacteriuria, burden, species and resistance**. **Culturing in uncomplicated rUTI reduced hospitalisation and IV antibiotic use.**
- **PVR and uroflow**: essential with emptying symptoms or a distended bladder; in postmenopausal women with rUTI, **higher PVR and lower flow** are associated with recurrence; abnormal values may **reclassify the UTI as complicated**.
- **Imaging and cystoscopy are not routine** — **low yield in uncomplicated rUTI**, and **not recommended by the AUA, American College of Radiology, Canadian Urological Association or EAU**. **With complicated risk factors → imaging and cystoscopy** (Table 26.9).
    - **Preferred imaging: renal and bladder ultrasound ± plain abdominal film**; **CT** when suspicion of complicated infection remains high despite normal imaging, or for **stones/obstruction**.
    - **Pregnancy test before ionising imaging** in premenopausal women if uncertain.
    - **Cystoscopy adds nothing beyond imaging in uncomplicated rUTI**; useful in **complicated UTI**.

<p class="tt">TABLE 26.9 Indications for Further Investigation of Recurrent Urinary Tract Infection (Dason et al., 2011)</p>

- **Previous urinary tract trauma or surgery**; **previous bladder or renal calculi**; **gross haematuria after infection resolves**; **obstructive symptoms, low flow or high PVR**
- **Urea-splitting bacteria on culture**; **previous abdominopelvic malignancy**; **persistence despite sensitivity-based therapy**; **diabetes or other immune compromise**
- **Pneumaturia, faecaluria, anaerobes, or diverticulitis history**; **repeated pyelonephritis**; **asymptomatic microhaematuria after infection resolves**
- Pattern: each indication signals **stone, obstruction, fistula, malignancy or persistence**. Trap: **recurrence frequency alone is not on the list**.

#### Bacterial Persistence

- **Failure of an appropriate course for culture-proven acute infection** → suspect persistence — **rare in rUTI but critical to find**; requires **cystoscopy and imaging**.
- Usually a **correctable urological abnormality** (Box 26.6) where antibiotics **cannot penetrate or eliminate the nidus**; **surgical removal can cure** (Stamey).
- **Work-up:** cystoscopy, ultrasound, CT; **VCUG** for VUR or **non-refluxing ureteric stump**; **pelvic MRI = standard for urethral diverticulum**; **retrograde urethrography** for suspected stricture in men.
- **Struvite (infection) stones are one of the commonest causes.** **Urease-producing organisms (*P. mirabilis*)** alkalinise urine → precipitation of **calcium, magnesium, ammonium and phosphate** → **branched struvite stones**. Bacteria **shelter within the stone** — **post-treatment urine may be sterile, misleadingly**. **Oxalate and apatite stones may be secondarily colonised.** Risk: **chronic catheters, urinary diversion, stasis**.
    - **AUA (Medical Management of Kidney Stones): surgically remove infection stones in all patients fit for intervention.** **Non-surgical candidates with residual/recurrent struvite → acetohydroxamic acid + antibiotic suppression.**
- **Non-obstructing stones with rUTI — controversial:** half of patients remain infection-free a year after complete stone extraction; recurrence more likely with **type 2 DM, hypertension, Black race**. **AUA permits observation of asymptomatic non-obstructing stones ≤10 mm.** For **stones <6 mm with UTI**, **minimise recurrences before considering removal**. Whether clearing small stones reduces rUTI is unknown.

#### Management

- **Most women with rUTI have no correctable abnormality.** Options in order: **behavioural modification → non-antibiotic therapy → antibiotic treatment/prophylaxis as a last resort**.
- **AUA 2019 guideline** on recurrent uncomplicated UTI in women (**revalidated 2021**); EAU, **National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE)** and other societies' guidelines **conflict** on prevention → confusion.
- **Quality of life (QoL):** patients fear the next UTI; anxiety, **fragmented care from providers unaware of guidelines**. Clinicians should **educate and set a protocol for future episodes**; patients increasingly **understand antibiotic risks** and want **stewardship and mental-health acknowledgement**. **Education/counselling on risk factors reduced symptomatic recurrence and improved QoL.**
- **Antibiotic resistance:** the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists it among the **most serious health threats** — **millions of resistant infections and tens of thousands of US deaths annually**; similar European burden; **hospital-acquired MDR infections rose during COVID-19**.
- **Antibiotic stewardship programmes** improve prescribing, outcomes, adverse effects and susceptibility; a Cochrane review showed **better guideline compliance and shorter courses** → **UTI management must follow stewardship** (Fig. 26.15).

<p class="tt">Fig. 26.15 Algorithm for management of recurrent UTI in women</p>

- **Entry:** UTI-specific symptoms (**acute dysuria + change from baseline**) **with positive urinalysis and culture** (midstream clean-catch [MSCC] or catheterised) **and ≥3 episodes in 12 months or 2 in 6 months**.
    - **Not met:** negative culture or atypical symptoms → **consider another diagnosis**; true UTI but below the threshold → **treat each episode**.
- **Same organism recurring at short intervals?** → **Yes: evaluate for bacterial persistence** (urological evaluation incl. **upper tract imaging and cystoscopy**) → nidus found → **remove it**; nothing found → manage as reinfection.
- **Reinfection** → **behavioural modification** (hydration, frequent voiding — not evidence-based) → **modifiable risk factors?**
    - **Emptying symptoms → check PVR**; **intercourse-related → avoid spermicide, void pre- and postcoitally** (not evidence-based).
- → **Cranberry, ideally ≥36 mg PAC** → then:
    - **Coitus-related → postcoital antibiotics**; **postmenopausal → vaginal oestrogen**; neither → **methenamine with vitamin C if renal function normal**.
- Note that **continuous daily antibiotic prophylaxis does not appear** in the algorithm.

- ***Behavioural modification*** — low risk, supports bladder health:
    - **Hydration**: flushes adherent bacteria and sheds urothelial cells. **Landmark trial (Hooton et al., 2018):** premenopausal women with rUTI who **added ≥1.5 L water daily** had **about half as many cystitis episodes** over a year (1.7 vs 3.2). Meta-analysis: **fewer patients with rUTI at ≤6 months** with increased fluids (water, juices, D-mannose in water).
    - **Frequent, complete voiding**; **void after (and, where possible, before) intercourse** — intercourse causes transient bacteriuria.
    - **Avoid spermicides (nonoxynol-9)** — deplete vaginal flora and raise pH.
    - **Pubic shaving and thong underwear: no evidence.**
- ***Self-start therapy*** — patient starts antibiotics at symptom onset, often **without a sample**:
    - Older studies suggested patients diagnose accurately; originally required a sample and follow-up — **that expectation has eroded**.
    - **Fluoroquinolones were once "ideal"** self-start agents; the practice **perpetuates overuse**; anxious patients **treat minor changes**; in the authors' view **risks outweigh benefits — not sustainable**.
    - **Preferred approach:** at symptom onset → **urinary analgesic + urine sample + observation** → **IDSA-recommended agent (per antibiogram) only if symptoms persist**, tailored to culture. **Many episodes are transient and non-bacterial.**
- ***Low-dose continuous prophylaxis:***
    - Rationale: eradicates uropathogens from **introital and bowel reservoirs** without (it was thought) fostering resistance. Older trials (TMP-SMX, TMP, nitrofurantoin, cephalexin, fluoroquinolones) for **6–12 months** reduced UTIs; some noted **more non–*E. coli* UTIs** afterwards.
    - **Cochrane 2004** (randomised controlled trials [RCTs]; latest 1995): **6–12 months of prophylaxis reduces clinical and microbiological recurrence while taken**, with **more adverse effects**; **after stopping, recurrence reverts to placebo levels**. **Postcoital prophylaxis is effective and preferable to daily** in intercourse-related rUTI.
    - Caveats: **poor-quality, decades-old trials**, inconsistent definitions, **no agreed duration**, **pre-dating current resistance**; long-term suppression **drives MDR at a societal level**.
    - **AUA: clinicians "may prescribe" prophylaxis** after weighing risks, benefits and alternatives. **The authors (and the EAU) regard long-term daily prophylaxis as a last resort**; patients wanting indefinite prophylaxis must be told there are **no long-term data** and of **toxicity and MDR** risks.
    - **Postcoital prophylaxis is acceptable** with a **clear intercourse–UTI link** when other measures fail; choose by **prior cultures, allergy, local resistance, ecology**.
    - **Nitrofurantoin** — popular (urine-concentrated, broad, little resistance) but **pulmonary fibrosis, renal toxicity and neurotoxicity with long-term use are rarely discussed**; a meta-analysis found it **as effective as norfloxacin, TMP, TMP-SMX, cefaclor, oestriol or methenamine** but with **more (mainly GI) adverse effects**.

<p class="tt ct">Antibiotic prophylaxis strategies compared</p>

| Prophylaxis strategy | Evidence | Position |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuous daily low-dose** | Effective **only while taken**; old trials; adverse effects; MDR | AUA: "may prescribe"; authors/EAU: **last resort** |
| **Postcoital** | Effective; **preferable to daily** for intercourse-related rUTI | **Acceptable** when other options fail |
| **Self-start** | Old data only | **Discouraged**; analgesic + sample + observe instead |

- ***Non-antibiotic management*** — the focus of the last decade's research; a meta-analysis found **OM-89, Urovac, vaginal oestrogen, cranberry and acupuncture** of varying benefit; **oral oestrogen and lactobacilli ineffective**; **vitamin C ineffective**. Head-to-head trials against **antibiotic prophylaxis (the gold standard)** and standardised products are needed.

- ***Cranberry:***
    - Active ingredient **type A proanthocyanidin (PAC)** — **blocks P-fimbrial adhesion of *E. coli*** to uroepithelium; may also act via **gut microbiota**.
    - **Cochrane reviews conflict:** 2008 — **effective in women with rUTI** (not in older people or catheterised patients); **2012** — **no significant overall benefit**, including rUTI subgroup, though **equivalent to antibiotic prophylaxis** in two trials; **2023 (largest)** — **supports use in women with rUTI, children, and after interventions**.
    - Problems: **heterogeneous products/doses**, **high dropout with juice**, **PAC content rarely reported**.
    - **Dose: 36 mg PAC daily effective; 72 mg may be better** (dose-dependent antiadhesion in urine 6 h after dosing, Fig. 26.16); **antiadhesion is short-lived → take twice daily**; high-dose PAC (2 × 18.5 mg) helped women with **<5 UTIs/year**.
    - **Nursing-home trial of 72 mg PAC: no benefit** — but most had **no prior bacteriuria/rUTI**, samples were **voided not catheterised**, and **PVR/incontinence unmeasured** → **not grounds to abandon cranberry in all women**.
    - Other meta-analyses: benefit, especially after **rUTI and gynaecological surgery**; **combinations with lactobacilli, D-mannose, propolis add nothing**.
    - **Warfarin: FDA warning removed 2011**; international normalised ratio (INR) interaction not substantiated → **safe with warfarin**.
    - **AUA supports offering cranberry**; authors recommend **≥36 mg PAC**.

- ***Oestrogen:***
    - Postmenopausal reinfection linked to **residual urine (prolapse)** and **oestrogen loss → atrophic epithelium, raised vaginal pH, loss of lactobacilli, *E. coli* colonisation**.
    - **Landmark trial — Raz and Stamm (1993), intravaginal oestriol vs placebo:** **UTI rate reduced about 90% (0.5 vs 5.9 episodes per patient-year)**; mechanism **return of lactobacilli and fall in vaginal pH**; **more dropouts on oestriol** (local burning, itching, irritation). **Oestradiol vaginal ring** also reduced UTIs.
    - **Cochrane 2008: vaginal oestrogen effective; oral oestrogen not** (AUA concurs). **Benefit takes ≥12 weeks.** Adverse: **breast tenderness, discharge, spotting, local irritation**. All forms (cream, tablet, ring) improve atrophy; recent meta-analysis and a multicentre placebo RCT confirm **vaginal (not oral) oestrogen reduces rUTI**.

<p class="tt ct">Vaginal versus oral oestrogen</p>

| **Vaginal oestrogen** | **Oral oestrogen** |
|---|---|
| **Reduces rUTI** (RCTs, meta-analyses) | **No reduction** |
| Restores lactobacilli, lowers pH | — |
| **Minimal systemic absorption** — levels within postmenopausal range | Systemic exposure |

- ***Oestrogen safety:***
    - **Boxed warnings apply to all routes** and are **not supported for vaginal use**; low-dose vaginal preparations keep plasma levels **within normal postmenopausal range**.
    - Observational data (incl. **Women's Health Initiative Observational Study**): **no increased fracture, breast cancer, other cancer or cardiovascular disease**.
    - **Breast cancer survivors** (incl. on tamoxifen): **no increased recurrence**; **American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO)** support use for GSM in selected cases → **consider for rUTI with oncologist endorsement**.
    - Yet **providers and patients remain hesitant**.
- ***Laser therapy for GSM*** (**fractional CO₂, erbium:YAG**; collagen stimulation): small observational studies; **efficacy and safety for GSM unestablished; no data for rUTI**.
- ***Probiotics:***
    - **Lactobacilli are the dominant vaginal/periurethral commensal**; some strains (e.g. **hydrogen peroxide producers**) interfere with uropathogens.
    - Early products (Lactobacillus GC drink; *L. casei* v *rhamnosus* suppository) **failed**; ***L. crispatus* vaginal suppositories and oral *L. rhamnosus* GR-1/*L. reuteri* RC-14 more promising**.
    - **Cochrane 2015 and later meta-analyses: not recommended** (small, flawed trials). Strain, dose and route unknown.
- ***Methenamine:***
    - Used for about a century; **70–90% renally excreted**; in **acid urine (pH <6)** converts to **ammonia and formaldehyde** (bactericidal/bacteriostatic by concentration). Optimal formaldehyde in vitro at **pH <5.85**; whether acidification (**vitamin C 1–4 g**) is needed in vivo is unknown.
    - **Two FDA-approved salts: hippurate (1 g twice daily) and mandelate.** Early placebo RCTs: **substantial (56–75%) reduction in rUTI**.
    - Acid salts are **non-specifically bacteriostatic → ineffective against urease producers** (which alkalinise urine).
    - Adverse: **GI upset, dysuria, rash, raised liver enzymes**. **Contraindicated: CrCl <50 mL/min, severe liver disease, hypersensitivity**; **avoid with sulfonamides, antacids, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors**.
    - **Cochrane 2012 (hippurate):** heterogeneous; **benefit in normal urinary tracts, especially short-term (≤1 week) therapy; no benefit with anatomical abnormalities**; adverse effects infrequent (nausea, diarrhoea).
    - Recent: high success in a tertiary rUTI cohort; **RCT: methenamine hippurate non-inferior to daily antibiotic prophylaxis over 12 months, cheaper and with less resistance**; another small RCT: **as effective as TMP**.
    - **Formaldehyde carcinogenicity** relates to **nasopharyngeal, sinonasal and lymphohaematopoietic** cancers, **not urothelial** → bladder cancer risk is **theoretical**.

## KIDNEY INFECTIONS

### Acute Pyelonephritis

- Defined as **inflammation of kidney and renal pelvis**, but **diagnosed clinically**; upper tract infection can be proven by **ureteric catheterisation or bladder washout**, which are **impractical and unnecessary** in most; **no non-invasive localisation test is fully reliable**.

#### Clinical Presentation

- Spectrum: **Gram-negative sepsis ↔ cystitis with mild flank pain**.
- **Classic: abrupt chills, fever ≥38 °C (100.4 °F), unilateral or bilateral flank/costovertebral angle pain or tenderness**; **lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) often absent**.
- **Symptoms correlate poorly with the true site of infection** (localisation studies by ureteric catheterisation and bladder washout).
- **Deep CVAT** on examination. May **mimic GI disease** (abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea).
- **Asymptomatic progression to chronic pyelonephritis** can occur in compromised hosts; **acute renal failure is rare**.

#### Laboratory Diagnosis

- **Neutrophil leucocytosis**; urinalysis — **numerous WBCs, often clumped**, bacterial rods or chains of cocci.
- **Granular or leucocyte casts suggest pyelonephritis**; a **cast containing bacteria in its matrix** is specific (Fig. 26.21).
- Blood: raised **erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP)**, creatinine (if renal failure), reduced CrCl; **blood cultures may be positive**.

#### Bacteriology

- **Urine cultures positive**, though a minority have **<10⁵ CFU/mL** and a negative urine Gram stain.
- ***E. coli* (a special virulent subgroup) causes most cases.** Without VUR, patients of **P blood-group phenotype** are susceptible to **recurrent pyelonephritis from P-piliated *E. coli*** binding P antigen receptors. Much community pyelonephritis comes from **a few resistant clonal groups**.
- **Suspect resistant species (*Proteus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, Serratia, Enterobacter, Citrobacter*)** with **rUTI, hospitalisation, catheters, recent instrumentation**.
- **Gram-positives rarely cause pyelonephritis except *E. faecalis, S. epidermidis, S. aureus*.**
- **Blood cultures** if **systemic toxicity, hospitalisation, or risk factors such as pregnancy**.

#### Renal Ultrasonography, Computed Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging

- **Ultrasound:** **focal swelling, increased or decreased echogenicity** (Fig. 26.22: echogenic, swollen upper pole).
- **CT/MRI:** focal swelling, **diminished, heterogeneous enhancement** (Fig. 26.23: patchy); unenhanced CT — reduced density; MRI — **restricted diffusion**.
- **Changes usually resolve**, though **chronic scarring** can follow.

#### Differential Diagnosis

- **Appendicitis, diverticulitis, pancreatitis** — similar pain intensity, **different location**, usually **normal urine**.
- **Herpes zoster** — superficial loin pain **without urinary symptoms**; obvious once the rash appears.

#### Management

- ***Initial management*** — three groups:

<p class="tt ct">Acute pyelonephritis: management groups</p>

| Group | Setting |
|---|---|
| **1. Uncomplicated, not needing admission** | **Oral outpatient**; imaging usually deferred (ultrasound if indicated) |
| **2. Uncomplicated, normal tract, ill enough for parenteral therapy** | Inpatient IV |
| **3. Complicated** (hospitalisation, catheter, urological surgery, tract abnormality) | Inpatient IV; **CT** to define tract and extent |

- **Outpatient first line: oral ciprofloxacin × 7 days where community fluoroquinolone resistance ≤10%** (regimens, including extended-release [XR] ciprofloxacin, in Table 26.11), ± an **initial one-off parenteral dose**; **TMP-SMX only if the organism is known to be sensitive**. Future guidance must address **cephalosporins and alternatives** given quinolone resistance and collateral damage.
- **Parenteral therapy** if **high fever, high WBC, vomiting, dehydration, sepsis**, complicated pyelonephritis, or **outpatient failure**. IDSA inpatient options: **fluoroquinolone; aminoglycoside ± ampicillin; extended-spectrum cephalosporin ± aminoglycoside; carbapenem** (doses in Table 26.11). **Gram-positive cocci → ampicillin–sulbactam ± aminoglycoside.** Plus **IV fluids and antipyretics**.
- **Obstruction:** unilateral obstruction **does not reduce overall renal function**, but a **solitary obstructed kidney causes acute renal failure** → **dose by glomerular filtration rate (GFR)**; **relieve substantial obstruction promptly by the simplest, safest route — ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy**.
- **Urine Gram stain** guides initial choice; therapy must reach **renal tissue and urine**.

Radionuclide imaging shows **reduced flow, delayed peak and excretion**, and **VUR-related cortical defects**.
- ***Follow-up:*** selected patients need **VCUG, cystoscopy, localisation studies** and correction of abnormalities. **DMSA 10–20 years after adult pyelonephritis shows scars in about half, but renal function change is minimal and unrelated to scarring.**

- **Pyelonephritis (fever, flank pain, leucocytosis)** → split by **sepsis**:
    - **No sepsis, nausea or vomiting** → urine culture; **optional imaging** to exclude obstruction/stones → **outpatient oral fluoroquinolone 7–10 days**.
    - **Sepsis** → **imaging for complicating factors**, blood and urine cultures → **inpatient parenteral antibiotics**.

- **No improvement or deterioration** → **admit outpatient**; review cultures; **drain obstruction or abscess**.

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> In pyelonephritis <strong>the 72-hour mark is the pivot</strong>: fever may lag behind a sterilised urine, but persistence beyond three days means an undrained collection or obstruction until CT proves otherwise — antibiotics treat the infection, drainage treats the complication.</p>

### Acute Focal or Multifocal Bacterial Nephritis

- **Uncommon, severe** renal infection with **dense leucocyte infiltrate in one lobe (focal) or several (multifocal)**.
- ***Clinical presentation:*** like pyelonephritis but **more severe**; **about half diabetic**; **sepsis common**; leucocytosis, **Gram-negative UTI**, **about half bacteraemic**. **AFBN lies midway between pyelonephritis and renal abscess.**
- ***Radiological findings:*** diagnosis is **radiological**; lesion slightly **less dense** than surrounding parenchyma.
    - **Ultrasound:** **poorly marginated, relatively sonolucent** mass with low-amplitude echoes **disrupting the corticomedullary junction** (the Fig. 26.25 example is slightly more echogenic than normal parenchyma; lesions **resolve after antibiotics**).
    - **CT requires contrast** (barely visible unenhanced): **wedge-shaped zones of reduced enhancement, no wall, no liquefaction**.
    - **Gallium** uptake larger than the mass. Multifocal disease — **same findings in several lobes**.

<p class="tt ct">AFBN versus renal abscess on imaging</p>

| **AFBN** | **Renal abscess** |
|---|---|
| **Wedge-shaped**, poorly marginated | **Round**, well defined |
| **No wall, no liquefaction** | **Liquid centre**; chronic → **enhancing ring** |
| **Seen only after contrast** | **Visible before and after contrast** |
| **Medical treatment** | Size-dependent: antibiotics or drainage |

- ***Management:*** AFBN is probably an **early phase of abscess formation**; progression to frank abscess despite appropriate therapy is documented. **Hydration + IV antibiotics ≥7 days, then 7 days oral.** Usually responds; follow-up shows **resolution of wedge zones**. **Non-response → exclude obstruction, renal/perirenal abscess, renal carcinoma, renal vein thrombosis.** Long-term multifocal cases may show **reduced renal size and calyceal deformity suggesting papillary necrosis**.

### Emphysematous Pyelonephritis

- **Urological emergency** — **acute necrotising parenchymal and perirenal infection by gas-forming organisms**.
- Pathogenesis unclear: **high tissue glucose fermented to CO₂** (e.g. by *E. coli*) is proposed, but does not explain **its rarity among the many diabetic Gram-negative UTIs** or **non-diabetic cases**.
- Besides DM, many have **obstruction (calculi, papillary necrosis)** and **significant renal impairment**. **Mortality about 19–43%** historically.

#### Clinical Presentation

- **Adults** (juvenile diabetics not at risk); **women more than men**.
- **Severe acute pyelonephritis**, sometimes after chronic infection; **classic triad: fever, vomiting, flank pain**.
- Occurs in **insulin-dependent and non-insulin-dependent diabetics, even without obstruction**; **non-diabetics usually have ureteric obstruction** and **less extensive disease**.
- **Pneumaturia absent unless the collecting system is involved.** **Urine culture invariably positive** — ***E. coli* commonest**; *Klebsiella*, *Proteus* less so.

#### Radiological Findings

- **Plain film:** **mottled gas over the kidney** (often **mistaken for bowel gas**); a **crescent of gas over the upper pole is more distinctive**; later gas extends to **perinephric space and retroperitoneum** (Fig. 26.26).
- **Ultrasound:** **strong focal echoes** of intraparenchymal gas.
- **CT is the imaging of choice** — defines extent, guides management (Fig. 26.27).

<p class="tt ct">Emphysematous pyelonephritis: CT pattern and prognosis</p>

| CT pattern | Prognosis |
|---|---|
| **No fluid; streaky or mottled gas** (± bubbly/loculated gas) | **Rapid parenchymal destruction; 50–60% mortality** |
| **Renal/perirenal fluid; bubbly or loculated gas, or gas in collecting system; no streaky/mottled gas** | **<20% mortality** |

- **Huang–Tseng classification (2000):** **four CT classes of progressively greater gas extent** in renal and adjacent tissue — used to **guide management**.
- **Obstruction in about a quarter.** **Nuclear renal scan** to assess function of both kidneys.

<p class="tt ct">Emphysematous pyelonephritis versus emphysematous pyelitis</p>

| **Emphysematous pyelonephritis** | **Emphysematous pyelitis** |
|---|---|
| Gas in **parenchyma** ± perinephric space | Gas in the **collecting system** |
| **Mostly diabetic** | **Often non-diabetic** |
| **Emergency; high mortality** | **Less serious; usually responds to antibiotics** |

#### Management

- **Most are septic → fluid resuscitation, glucose and electrolyte control, broad-spectrum antibiotics**; **relieve obstruction by nephrostomy or stent**.
- **Adverse prognostic factors:** **hypoalbuminaemia, shock at presentation, bacteraemia, need for haemodialysis, thrombocytopenia, altered mental status, polymicrobial infection**.
- **Evolution:** until the late 1980s **emergency nephrectomy ± drainage** (still 40–50% mortality) → now **percutaneous catheter drainage as definitive treatment**, with **nephrectomy for extensive diffuse gas with renal destruction**.
- **Early diagnosis and treatment of UTI in diabetics** may prevent progression and nephrectomy.

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> <strong>Fluid is a good sign and streaky gas a bad one</strong>: a contained, liquefied collection can be drained, whereas diffuse streaky gas without fluid means parenchyma is necrosing faster than the host can wall it off — the same principle that moved treatment from routine nephrectomy to drainage with nephrectomy reserved for diffuse destruction.</p>

### Renal Abscess

- **Collection of pus confined to the renal parenchyma.**

<p class="tt ct">Renal abscess: then versus now</p>

| **Pre-antibiotic era** | **Current era** |
|---|---|
| About **80% haematogenous staphylococcal** | **Mostly Gram-negative (since about 1970)**: *E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus, Pseudomonas* |
| **Young men, no prior renal disease** | **Prior renal disease or obstruction; no sex or side predilection** |
| Haematogenous seeding of normal kidney | **Ascending infection with tubular obstruction** (prior infection, calculi) |

- **Gram-negative haematogenous pyelonephritis is almost impossible to produce experimentally** unless the kidney is **traumatised or completely obstructed**; **normal and partially obstructed kidneys reject blood-borne Gram-negatives** → ascent is the main Gram-negative route (haematogenous Gram-negative seeding occurs with **IV drug use and skin infection**).
- **Risk factors: UTI, calculi, DM** (DM and **longer hospital stay** very significant); link with **VUR less clear** than for pyelonephritis.

#### Clinical Presentation

- **Fever, chills, abdominal or flank pain**, sometimes **weight loss and malaise**, ± cystitis symptoms; may be **vague**, delaying diagnosis (historically to exploration or autopsy).
- History may reveal a **Gram-positive source 1–8 weeks earlier** (**skin carbuncles, IV drug use**) or recent UTI/pyelonephritis. Complicated UTI (**stasis, calculi, pregnancy, NGB, DM**) predisposes.
- Even with **normal tracts**, nearly all have a predisposing factor: **DM, HIV, steroids, IV drug use, liver disease, UTI, pregnancy**.

#### Laboratory Diagnosis

- **Marked leucocytosis.** **Blood cultures positive in only about a quarter to a third**; **urine, blood and abscess isolates rarely match** (few concordant).
- **Pyuria and bacteriuria may be absent unless the abscess communicates with the collecting system.** **Haematogenous Gram-positive abscesses → urine sterile or a different organism.**
- **Ultrasound — quickest, cheapest:** **echo-free or low-echo space-occupying lesion with increased through-transmission** (Fig. 26.28); margins indistinct acutely with **oedematous surrounding parenchyma**, later **well-defined**; internal echoes vary with **debris**; **air → strong echo with shadow**. **Abscess vs tumour may be hard to separate.**
- **CT — diagnostic procedure of choice:** **well defined before and after contrast**. Early: **renal enlargement, rounded low-attenuation foci** (Fig. 26.29); after days, **thick fibrotic wall**; chronic: **obliterated tissue planes, thickened Gerota's fascia, low-attenuation mass with an enhancing "ring sign"** (vascular wall) (Fig. 26.30).

#### Management

- Classic treatment **percutaneous or open drainage**, but **IV antibiotics and observation are appropriate for small abscesses (≤3 cm) in stable patients**; early antibiotics may **obviate intervention**.
- **CT/ultrasound-guided aspiration** to distinguish **abscess from cystic tumour**; culture the aspirate.
- **Start IV antibiotics immediately**:

<p class="tt ct">Renal abscess: empirical therapy by source</p>

| Presumed source | Organism | Empirical agent |
|---|---|---|
| **Haematogenous** | **Penicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus*** | **Penicillinase-resistant penicillin**; **vancomycin if penicillin-allergic** |
| **Ascending (abnormal tract)** | **Gram-negatives** | **IV 3rd-generation cephalosporin, antipseudomonal penicillin, or aminoglycoside** |

- **Serial ultrasound or CT until resolution**; worsening → suspect **misdiagnosis, perinephric extension or resistant organism**.
- **Size governs management:**

<p class="tt ct">Renal abscess: management by size</p>

| Size | Management |
|---|---|
| **≤3 cm** | **Antibiotics alone** (also in obstructed/anomalous tracts if stable) |
| **3–5 cm** | **No consistent pathway** — antibiotics alone or drainage; **≤5 cm with normal tracts resolved on antibiotics alone** in one series |
| **>5 cm** | **Percutaneous drainage first line**; may need several drains, manipulations, surgical washout or nephrectomy |
| **Any size — immunocompromised or non-responding** | **Evaluate for percutaneous drainage** |

| Series (country, period) | <3 cm | 3–5 cm | >5 cm |
|---|---|---|---|

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> The modern renal abscess is <strong>an ascending Gram-negative complication of a damaged kidney</strong>, not a haematogenous staphylococcal lesion of a normal one — so the source dictates the antibiotic, and size dictates whether antibiotics alone can reach the pus.</p>

### Infected Hydronephrosis and Pyonephrosis

- **Infected hydronephrosis** = bacterial infection of a hydronephrotic kidney. **Pyonephrosis** = infected hydronephrosis with **suppurative parenchymal destruction and total or near-total loss of function** (Fig. 26.31: thinned cortex/medulla, pus-distended pelvicalyceal system, distal ureteric obstruction). The boundary is **clinically indistinct**; **rapid diagnosis and treatment prevent permanent loss of function and sepsis**.
- ***Clinical presentation:*** usually **very ill** — high fever, chills, flank pain and tenderness; occasionally only **fever and vague GI discomfort**. **Prior calculi, infection or surgery** common. **Bacteriuria may be absent if the ureter is completely obstructed.**
- ***Radiological findings:*** ultrasound — **internal echoes in the dependent part of a dilated collecting system; fluid–debris levels**; **focal hypoechoic areas in the parenchyma suggest pyonephrosis**. CT non-specific — **thick pelvis, perirenal stranding, striated nephrogram** (Fig. 26.32: echogenic septa, debris-filled calyces; antegrade pyelogram shows pus-filled calyces, scarred pelvis, stone — kidney did not recover).
- ***Management:*** **antibiotics + drainage of the infected pelvis by ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy**; once stable, **identify and treat the cause of obstruction**.

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> <strong>An infected, obstructed kidney is an abscess with a collecting system</strong> — antibiotics cannot sterilise it until it is drained, and a sterile urine may simply mean the ureter is completely blocked.</p>

### Perinephric Abscess

- **Beyond the renal capsule but within Gerota's fascia.** Sources: **rupture of a cortical abscess**, **extravasation of infected urine from obstruction**, **haematogenous seeding** (about a third, usually **skin**). **Pyonephrosis with calculus** predisposes; **about a third are diabetic**. **Infected perirenal haematoma** (haematogenous or direct).
- **Rupture through Gerota's fascia → paranephric abscess**; paranephric abscess also arises from **bowel, pancreas or pleura**; conversely perinephric/**psoas** abscess may follow **bowel perforation, Crohn disease, thoracolumbar osteomyelitis**.
- **Organisms: *E. coli*, *Proteus*, *S. aureus*.**

#### Clinical Presentation

- **Insidious; symptoms >5 days in most**; resembles pyelonephritis but **more than a third are afebrile**; **flank or abdominal mass in about half**; CVAT usual. **Limp with flexed, externally rotated hip → psoas abscess.**
- Labs: **leucocytosis, raised creatinine, pyuria**. **Urine culture predicts the abscess organism in only about a third; polymicrobial blood culture suggests perinephric abscess** but identifies all organisms in under half; another series found urine the most sensitive → **treat culture results with caution**.
- **Pyelonephritis responds in 4–5 days; perinephric abscess does not** → suspect with **UTI plus flank mass or fever persisting after 4 days** of antibiotics. Often **coexists with renal abscess**.
- **CT** shows the primary abscess, **extension to flank or psoas** (Fig. 26.33), **route of spread and obstruction** (Fig. 26.34: pararenal abscess involving adrenal) → plans drainage. **Ultrasound**: anechoic mass displacing kidney ↔ echogenic collection blending with perirenal fat; can delineate collections outside Gerota's fascia.
- **Mortality fell from 40–50% to about 12%** with imaging, yet diagnosis still lags by **about 3–4 days** and only about a third are **correctly diagnosed at presentation** — **delay contributes to nearly all deaths** → keep a **low threshold for imaging**.

#### Management

- **Immediate antibiotics**, guided by **Gram stain**: **aminoglycoside + antistaphylococcal penicillin (methicillin/oxacillin)**; **penicillin allergy → cephalosporin or vancomycin**.
- **Small (<3 cm) abscesses in immunocompetent patients may resolve with antibiotics alone**; **delay and size >3 cm predict failure and death** → **prompt diagnosis and drainage of collections >3 cm**.
- **Larger or non-responding → intervention**: classic **surgical drainage or nephrectomy** (non-functioning/severely infected kidney), now largely **CT/ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage**; the **threshold is lower than for renal abscess**.
- **Then treat the underlying cause** — **cortical abscess or enteric communication** promptly; **nephrectomy for pyonephrosis** at the same time if feasible, otherwise staged. A proportion of large drained abscesses **still need nephrectomy** for non-functioning kidneys — but **far fewer than historically**.

<p class="tt ct">Renal versus perinephric abscess</p>

| | **Renal abscess** | **Perinephric abscess** |
|---|---|---|
| Site | Parenchyma | **Beyond capsule, within Gerota's fascia** |
| Antibiotics alone | **≤3 cm** (up to 5 cm in some series) | **<3 cm, immunocompetent** |
| Drainage | **>5 cm** first line; 3–5 cm variable | **>3 cm — lower threshold** |
| Empirical cover | Haematogenous: antistaphylococcal; ascending: Gram-negative | **Aminoglycoside + antistaphylococcal penicillin** |

- ***Perinephric abscess versus acute pyelonephritis:*** delay in diagnosis is the main obstacle; commonest misdiagnosis is **pyelonephritis**. **Two discriminators:**

<p class="tt ct">Acute pyelonephritis versus perinephric abscess</p>

| | **Acute pyelonephritis** | **Perinephric abscess** |
|---|---|---|
| Symptoms before admission | **≤5 days** | **>5 days** |
| Fever after appropriate antibiotics | **Never >4 days** | **≥5 days (median 7)** |

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> <strong>Time is the diagnostic test for perinephric abscess</strong> — a pyelonephritis that has lasted too long before admission or will not defervesce by day four is a collection until imaged, and the mortality of this condition lies almost entirely in that delay.</p>

### Xanthogranulomatous Pyelonephritis

- **Rare, severe, chronic** infection → **diffuse renal destruction**; mostly **unilateral, enlarged, non-functioning kidney with obstruction from stones**; **lipid-laden foamy macrophages**; starts in **pelvis and calyces**, then destroys parenchyma and adjacent tissue.
- **Imitates almost every renal inflammatory disease and renal cell carcinoma (RCC)** radiologically; on **frozen section mimics clear cell carcinoma** → unnecessary radical nephrectomy.
- **Under 1% of histologically documented chronic pyelonephritis**, but **up to about a fifth of nephrectomies for pyelonephritis**.

#### Pathogenesis

- **Triad: nephrolithiasis, obstruction, infection.** Stones in **up to about 80%**, about **half staghorn**.
- **Obstruction then *E. coli* infection** reproduces tissue destruction and lipid collection by macrophages (**xanthoma cells**) — sheets around abscesses and calyces with **lymphocytes, giant cells, plasma cells**.
- **Bacteria of low virulence** (spontaneous bacteraemia rare). Other factors: **venous occlusion and haemorrhage, abnormal lipid metabolism, lymphatic blockage, failed antibiotic therapy, altered immunity, ischaemia**.
- **No single cause**: an **inadequate acute inflammatory response in an obstructed, ischaemic or necrotic kidney**.

#### Pathology

- **Massively enlarged kidney with normal contour.** **Diffuse (>80%)** or **segmental** (parenchyma around one or more calyces, or one pole of a duplex).
- Cut surface: **stones, peripelvic fibrosis**, **dilated pus-filled calyces**, **pelvis not dilated (encased by fibrosis)**, **papillary necrosis**; advanced — **multiple abscesses with viscous pus lined by yellow tissue**, thin cortex replaced by xanthogranulomatous tissue, **thickened capsule**, frequent **perinephric/paranephric extension** (Fig. 26.37A).
- Microscopy: yellow nodules = **sheets of foamy histiocytes (small dark nuclei, clear cytoplasm)** with lymphocytes, giant cells, plasma cells (Fig. 26.37B). **Xanthoma cells are not specific** — seen wherever inflammation and obstruction coexist. Lipid (cholesterol esters) may derive from **lysed red cells after haemorrhage**.

#### Clinical Presentation

- **Suspect with UTI + unilateral enlarged, non- or poorly functioning kidney + stone or mass indistinguishable from tumour.**
- **Flank pain, fever and chills (about two-thirds each), persistent bacteriuria (about half)**; malaise; **flank mass in about 60%**, **prior calculi in about a third**; less often **hypertension, haematuria, hepatomegaly**. History of **UTI and instrumentation**; **DM** a risk factor.
- **Peak fifth to seventh decades; women more often; no side predilection.**

#### Bacteriology and Laboratory Diagnosis

- ***Proteus* is the commonest organism** (linked to **stone formation**), ***E. coli*** also common; **tissue cultures almost always positive**; **anaerobes** occur; **about 10% mixed**. **About a third have sterile urine** (prior antibiotics) — organism may be found **only in operative tissue culture**.
- Urinalysis: **pus and protein**. Blood: **anaemia**; **hepatic dysfunction in up to half**.
- **Almost always unilateral → azotaemia/renal failure uncommon.**

#### Imaging

- **CT is the most useful** (Fig. 26.38): **classic triad in 50–80% — unilateral enlargement, little or no function, large renal pelvic calculus**.
    - **Reniform mass; pelvis tightly contracted around a central calcification, without pelvic dilatation**; parenchyma replaced by **water-density masses** (dilated calyces/abscesses).
    - **Cavity walls blush on contrast (vascular granulation tissue) but cavities do not enhance** — **tumours and other inflammatory lesions usually do**.
    - Shows **extent and involvement of adjacent organs/abdominal wall**.
- **Ultrasound:** global enlargement; **multiple hypoechoic fluid-filled masses** (debris-filled calyces, destruction); **focal (tumefactive) XGP** = solid segmental mass ± stone → **RCC in differential**.
- **⁹⁹ᵐTc-DMSA** quantifies lost function. **MRI**: better for **extrarenal extension**; lesions **intermediate on T1, hyperintense on T2**. **Imaging, though distinctive, often cannot separate XGP from RCC.**

#### Differential Diagnosis

<p class="tt ct">XGP: differential diagnosis</p>

| Entity | Distinguishing point |
|---|---|
| **Segmental XGP without calculi** | Difficult to diagnose |
| **XGP with massive pelvic dilatation** | **Indistinguishable from pyonephrosis** |
| **XGP in small contracted kidney** | Non-specific findings |
| **Renal parenchymal malakoplakia** | Enlargement and multiple inflammatory masses but **usually no calculi** |
| **Renal lymphoma** | Hypoechoic masses around a non-dilated pelvis; **clinically obvious, usually bilateral, no calculi** |

#### Management

- **Main obstacle is misdiagnosis**; with CT, **about 90% are diagnosed preoperatively**.
- Antibiotics to stabilise; **rarely, long-term antibiotics eradicate infection and restore function**.
- **Nephrectomy usually** (diffuse disease or cannot exclude tumour); **localised XGP → partial nephrectomy**.
- **Frozen section may mimic clear cell carcinoma**, and XGP coexists with **RCC, papillary urothelial carcinoma of pelvis or bladder, and squamous cell carcinoma of the pelvis** → **if malignancy cannot be excluded, perform nephrectomy**.
- **Extensive retroperitoneal disease → remove kidney and perinephric fat**; may require dissection from **diaphragm, great vessels and bowel**. **Remove the entire inflammatory mass** — the tissue is **infected in about three-quarters**; **incision and drainage alone → protracted illness and renocutaneous fistula**, making later nephrectomy harder.
- **Laparoscopic nephrectomy**: early series found no benefit; later, larger series deem it **reasonable (retroperitoneal, or transperitoneal hand-assisted)** but **difficult with high conversion rates**.

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> XGP is <strong>chronic obstruction plus low-grade infection in a host that cannot mount an acute response</strong> — hence the stone, the non-functioning kidney, the fat-laden macrophages and the resemblance to cancer; because the tissue itself is infected and may harbour or mimic carcinoma, the answer is excision of the whole mass, not drainage.</p>

### Malakoplakia

- **"Soft plaque"** — unusual inflammatory disease first described in the **bladder**, also in **GU and GI tracts, skin, lungs, bones, mesenteric nodes**. Described by **Michaelis and Gutmann (1902)**; characterised by **von Hansemann (1903)** as **soft yellow-brown plaques** with histiocytes containing **basophilic lysosomal inclusions (Michaelis–Gutmann bodies)**.
- **Abnormal macrophage function in response to bacterial (mostly *E. coli*) infection.**

#### Pathogenesis

- Bacteria or fragments form the **nidus for calcium phosphate crystals** that laminate Michaelis–Gutmann bodies. Leading theory: **defective phagocytic/degradative function of histiocytes** → chronic inflammation, often in **immunocompromised hosts**.

#### Pathology

- **Diagnosis by biopsy.** Grossly: **confluent tumour-like masses** and **firm plaque-like cortical lesions** (Fig. 26.39). **Large histiocytes (von Hansemann cells)** and **small basophilic intra- or extracytoplasmic calculospherules — Michaelis–Gutmann bodies, pathognomonic** (Fig. 26.39C: incompletely destroyed bacteria with lipoprotein membrane).
- Electron microscopy: **intact coliforms and fragments within phagolysosomes**.
- **Michaelis–Gutmann bodies may be absent early and are not required for diagnosis.**
- **Macrophages contain abundant α1-antitrypsin**, unchanged through early, granulomatous and fibrosing phases; lookalike processes lack it (**except a few macrophages in TB and XGP**) → **α1-antitrypsin immunostaining aids early diagnosis**.

#### Clinical Presentation

- **Most >50 years**; **urinary tract female:male 4:1** (not in other organs). Any part of the GU tract, **frequently bladder**.
- Patients often **debilitated, immunosuppressed, chronically ill**; notably after **solid organ (especially renal) transplantation** — graft kidney or ureter, GI and skin.
- **Bladder:** irritative LUTS, haematuria; cystoscopy — **mucosal plaques or nodules** → **fungating, sessile masses** causing filling defects; **distal ureteric stricture → obstruction or non-function**.
- **Renal parenchymal:** one or more masses with **chronic *E. coli* infection**; may cause **renal vein and inferior vena cava thrombosis**. **Testis → epididymo-orchitis.** **Prostate** — rare, **mimics carcinoma**.
- **Mortality can exceed 50%.**

#### Radiological Findings

- **Multifocal:** **enlarged kidneys with multiple filling defects; no calcification, lithiasis or hydronephrosis**; best seen on **ultrasound (confluent masses raise parenchymal echogenicity, distort central echo complex)**, CT (foci **less dense than enhanced parenchyma**) or arteriography.
- **Unifocal:** **non-calcified mass** indistinguishable from other inflammatory or neoplastic lesions; solid or cystic depending on necrosis. **CT best shows extrarenal extension.**

#### Differential Diagnosis

- **Consider malakoplakia with renal mass(es) ± collecting-system filling defects in women with recurrent *E. coli* UTI or altered immunity**, and in a **renal transplant recipient with persistent UTI** despite appropriate therapy.
- Exclude **cystic disease** (ultrasound/CT); **metastases/lymphoma** (late disease); **multifocal RCC** (von Hippel–Lindau context); **XGP** (**enlarged kidney but calculi and obstruction common**); **multiple abscesses** (haematogenous).

<p class="tt ct">XGP versus malakoplakia</p>

| | **XGP** | **Malakoplakia** |
|---|---|---|
| Cell | **Lipid-laden foamy macrophages (xanthoma cells)** | **Von Hansemann histiocytes with Michaelis–Gutmann bodies** |
| Stones / obstruction | **Usually present (staghorn)** | **Absent** |
| Organism | ***Proteus*, *E. coli*** | ***E. coli*** |
| Laterality | **Almost always unilateral** | Uni- or multifocal; may be bilateral |
| Sex | Women more | **Women 4:1 (urinary tract)** |
| Treatment | **Nephrectomy (or partial)** | **Control infection with intracellularly active antibiotics**; nephrectomy for symptomatic unilateral disease |

#### Management

- **Control the UTI** to stabilise disease. Agents with **intracellular bactericidal activity** preferred: **sulfonamides, rifampicin, doxycycline, TMP**; **fluoroquinolones (concentrated in macrophages) effective**.
- Bladder disease: **ascorbic acid and cholinergics (bethanechol)** with antibiotics — both **raise intracellular cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP)**, the postulated macrophage defect.
- **Reduce immunosuppression** where possible; **surgery if progression despite medical therapy**; **nephrectomy for symptomatic unilateral renal disease**.
- **Prognosis by extent: bilateral or transplant-kidney parenchymal disease → death usually within 6 months; unilateral disease → long-term survival after nephrectomy.**

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> Malakoplakia is <strong>a failure to digest what has been swallowed</strong> — macrophages ingest *E. coli* but cannot degrade it, leaving mineralised remnants (Michaelis–Gutmann bodies); treatment therefore uses antibiotics that work inside macrophages and agents that correct the degradative defect, not merely drugs that sterilise urine.</p>

**KEY POINTS: KIDNEY INFECTIONS**
