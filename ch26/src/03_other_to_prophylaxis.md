## OTHER INFECTIONS

### Fournier's Gangrene

- **Potentially life-threatening necrotising fasciitis of the external genitalia, perineum and/or perianal region.** Synonyms: **idiopathic gangrene of the scrotum, streptococcal scrotal gangrene, perineal phlegmon, spontaneous fulminant gangrene of the scrotum**.
- Described by **Baurienne (1764)** and **Fournier (1883)** as abrupt, **idiopathic** fulminant genital gangrene in **healthy young men**.

<p class="tt ct">Fournier's gangrene: original versus contemporary</p>

| **Fournier's original description** | **Contemporary disease** |
|---|---|
| **Idiopathic** | **A source is identified in about 95%** |
| Healthy young men | **Mostly 50–79 years; male:female about 10:1** |
| Abrupt, fulminant | **Broader age range; may be indolent** |

- **Sources: skin, urethra, rectum.** Well-documented link with **urethral stricture/obstruction with extravasation, and instrumentation**.
- **Predisposing factors: DM, local trauma, paraphimosis, periurethral urinary extravasation, perirectal/perianal infection, surgery (circumcision, herniorrhaphy).**
- **Fascial spread from a urethral source:** **Buck's fascia (penis) → dartos fascia (scrotum and penis) → Colles' fascia (perineum) → Scarpa's fascia (anterior abdominal wall)**.
- **Polymicrobial — aerobic–anaerobic synergy:** facultative organisms (***E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, enterococci**) with anaerobes (***Bacteroides*, *Fusobacterium*, *Clostridium*, microaerophilic streptococci**). Other necrotising fasciitis classes are defined by microbial composition.

#### Clinical Presentation

- History of **perineal trauma, instrumentation, sexually transmitted disease (STD)–related urethral stricture, urethrocutaneous fistula**.
- **Clues to source:** **rectal** — pain, rectal bleeding, anal fissures; **dermal** — acute/chronic scrotal infection, recurrent **hidradenitis suppurativa**, **balanitis**.
- **Sequence:** **cellulitis at the portal of entry** → swollen, erythematous, tender as deep fascia is involved; **pain prominent, fever and systemic toxicity marked** → **scrotal swelling and crepitus increase rapidly** → **dark-purple areas → extensive gangrene**. **Abdominal wall involvement in an obese diabetic → very rapid spread** (Fig. 26.41).
- GU symptoms: **dysuria, urethral discharge, obstructed voiding**. **Sepsis markers: altered mental state, tachypnoea, tachycardia, temperature >38.3 °C (101 °F) or <35.6 °C (96 °F).**

<p class="tt">Fig. 26.41 Fournier's gangrene of the scrotum (Hagedorn and Wessells, 2017)</p>

- **Necrotic skin along the median raphe**; **erythema spreading over both groins signals progressive disease deep to the skin** — the skin underestimates the fascial extent.

#### Laboratory Diagnosis and Radiological Findings

- **Anaemia** (thrombosis and ecchymosis reduce functioning red cell mass; sepsis reduces production); **raised creatinine, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia** — **bacterial lipases free fatty acids that chelate ionised calcium**.
- **Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotising Fasciitis (LRINEC) score** — **CRP, WBC, haemoglobin, sodium, creatinine, glucose**; **≥6 raises suspicion, ≥8 strongly predictive**; validated adjunct to clinical assessment.
- **Crepitus (subcutaneous gas) is early** → **plain film** may show air. **CT has the highest specificity** and delineates **soft tissue and fascial gas** for surgical planning (Fig. 26.42: fluid pocket with gas in scrotum tracking into perineum).
- **Biopsy of ulcer base:** **intact epidermis, dermal necrosis, vascular thrombosis, polymorphonuclear invasion, subcutaneous necrosis**.

#### Management

- **Prompt diagnosis is critical.** Early signs (pain, oedema, erythema) resemble cellulitis; **systemic toxicity out of proportion to local signs, and gas on imaging**, are the warnings.
- **Sequence:** **fluid resuscitation and electrolyte correction + broad-spectrum IV antibiotics as soon as suspected** → **immediate debridement** → **re-look at 24–48 h if debridement in doubt** → hyperbaric oxygen (adjunct) → reconstruction.
- **Antibiotics must cover aerobes, anaerobes, Gram-positives and Gram-negatives:**

<p class="tt ct">Fournier's gangrene: antibiotic regimens</p>

| Regimen | Note |
|---|---|
| **Piperacillin–tazobactam** | **Especially if *Pseudomonas* suspected** |
| **Ampicillin–sulbactam** | β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor |
| **Vancomycin or carbapenem + clindamycin or metronidazole** | — |

- **Clinically obvious disease** (**deep pain with patchy surface hypoaesthesia or crepitus, bullae, skin necrosis**) → **straight to theatre**.
- **Debridement:** extensive incision through skin and subcutaneous tissue **beyond the involved area until normal fascia**; excise necrotic fat and fascia; **leave open**.
- **Orchidectomy almost never needed** — **testes have an independent blood supply** from the compromised scrotal fascial/cutaneous circulation.
- **Suprapubic diversion** if urethral trauma or extravasation; **colostomy** if colonic or rectal perforation.
- **Hyperbaric oxygen** (with debridement and antibiotics) may **shorten stay, improve healing and limit spread**. **Myocutaneous flap reconstruction** after healing improves cosmesis.

#### Outcome

- **Mortality about 20–40%** (wide range); higher with **liver failure, chronic alcoholism, DM, advanced age, congestive heart failure, coagulopathy, renal failure**.
- **A true urological emergency**: early recognition, aggressive antibiotics and surgical debridement.

### Periurethral Abscess

- Named in the section's Key Points (secondary to **urethral stricture or catheterisation**; treatment **surgical debridement, suprapubic urinary drainage and antibiotics**); the body text of this section was not in the source provided.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: OTHER INFECTION**

- **Fournier's gangrene** = necrotising fasciitis of **external genitalia, perineum and/or perianal region**.
- **Emergent surgical debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics** are the essentials.
- **Periurethral abscess** may follow **urethral stricture or catheterisation**; treat with **debridement, suprapubic drainage and antibiotics**.
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> In Fournier's gangrene <strong>the skin is the last layer to show the disease</strong>: infection runs along continuous fascial planes with thrombosis of the vessels that feed the skin, so systemic toxicity outruns the visible signs, debridement must chase normal fascia rather than normal skin, and the testes — perfused independently — are spared.</p>

## BACTERIURIA IN PREGNANCY

### Pregnancy

- **Bacteriuria in pregnancy → pyelonephritis → adverse birth outcomes**; hence **pregnancy is one of the few indications to treat ASB** (IDSA). **American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG): screening urine culture at the first visit.**
- **Prevalence of ASB is not raised by pregnancy** (about 2–7% in the text; 1.9–9.5% in Table 26.2 — different source series). Risk rises with **low socioeconomic status, multiparity, sickle cell trait**.
- **Non-pregnant women often clear ASB; pregnant women tend to stay bacteriuric and become symptomatic.**
- **Pyelonephritis:** **1–4% of all pregnancies**; historically **15–45% of untreated bacteriuric pregnancies** (contemporary uncomplicated singleton data much lower); **60–75% in the third trimester** (peak hydronephrosis and stasis); **10–20% recur** before or soon after delivery.

<p class="tt ct">Bacteriuria: non-pregnant versus pregnant</p>

| | **Non-pregnant** | **Pregnant** |
|---|---|---|
| ASB prevalence | Similar | **Similar** |
| Natural history | **Often clears spontaneously** | **Persists; progresses to pyelonephritis** |
| Screen/treat ASB | **No** | **Yes** |

### Pathogenesis

- Anatomical and physiological changes of pregnancy **alter the natural history of bacteriuria** and may require altered therapy.

#### Anatomical and Physiological Changes During Pregnancy

- ***Increase in renal size:*** length **+ about 1 cm** — **vascular and interstitial volume, not true hypertrophy**; biopsies show no histological change.
- ***Smooth muscle atony of collecting system and bladder:*** **reduced ureteric peristalsis**; **most women have significant ureteric dilatation by the third trimester** — from **progesterone relaxation** and **uterine compression at the pelvic brim** (the mechanical effect dominating later); progesterone also **increases bladder capacity**.
- ***Bladder changes:*** uterus displaces bladder **superiorly and anteriorly**; bladder **hyperaemic/congested** endoscopically; oestrogen → **bladder hypertrophy and urethral squamous change**.
- ***Augmented renal function:*** **GFR rises 30–50%** (raised cardiac output) with more proteinuria → **"normal" non-pregnant creatinine may signify renal insufficiency in pregnancy**; **proteinuria abnormal only >300 mg/24 h**.
- These changes (from the **first trimester**) cause **stasis and mild hydroureteronephrosis** → pyelonephritis.

<p class="tt">Fig. 26.44 Progressive hydroureter and hydronephrosis on intravenous pyelogram during normal pregnancy (Hundley et al., 1935)</p>

- **Bilateral dilatation visible from 15 weeks**, increasing through 39 weeks; **predominantly right-sided**; **both tracts normal by 6 weeks postpartum**.

| | Non-pregnant women (mg/dL) | Pregnant women (mg/dL) |
|---|---|---|
| **Serum creatinine** | 0.7 | **0.5** |
| **Urea nitrogen** | 13.0 | **9.0** |

- Purpose: shows the **fall in creatinine and urea from raised GFR**. Trap: **a creatinine of 0.7–0.9 mg/dL, normal outside pregnancy, may indicate renal impairment in pregnancy**.

### Complications Associated With Bacteriuria During Pregnancy

#### Prematurity and Perinatal Mortality

- Pre-antibiotic era: symptomatic UTI/pyelonephritis → **prematurity, low birth weight, fetal death**.
- **Cochrane 2019: treating ASB in pregnancy reduces pyelonephritis, preterm delivery and low birth weight** — the basis for **screen-and-treat** protocols.

### Laboratory Diagnosis

- **Urinalysis and dipstick screening have significant false-negative rates** → **screening culture in the first trimester for all**. **USPSTF: culture at 12–16 weeks or first visit if later.**
- **Negative early culture → bacteriuria later is unlikely.** Women with **rUTI or VUR** may benefit from **prophylaxis**.

### Management

- Choose with **maternal and fetal toxicity** in mind; **expanded volume, fetal distribution, raised renal blood flow and GFR** may **lower serum drug levels**. **Uropathogens are the same as in non-pregnant women.**

- **Screen by urine culture at the first prenatal visit or 12–16 weeks.**
- Prevalence **unchanged by pregnancy**, but **spontaneous resolution is unlikely**.
- **Treat all bacteriuria in pregnancy.**
- Bacteriuria **progresses to pyelonephritis more often** and causes **adverse birth outcomes**.
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> Pregnancy does not make bacteriuria commoner — <strong>it makes it more dangerous</strong>: progesterone and uterine compression convert a harmless colonised bladder into a stagnant, dilated upper tract, which is why pregnancy is the one population in which ASB is screened for and treated.</p>

## BACTERIURIA AND URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS IN THE ELDERLY

- The **US population aged ≥65 is projected nearly to double by 2050** → growing public health importance.

### Epidemiology

- **Bacteriuria rises with age**; the **female excess narrows** in old age (still commoner in women); **most elderly bacteriuric patients are asymptomatic**.
- Prevalence rises with **age and comorbidity**, **exceeding 50% in selected groups** (figures in Table 26.2); **functional impairment doubles it**.

### Screening for Bacteriuria

- **Do not screen** community or long-term care elderly. **No link between ASB and deteriorating renal function**; **treating ASB does not improve incontinence**; the association of bacteriuria with **reduced survival is not shown to be causal**.

### Pathogenesis

- Multifactorial: **declining cell-mediated immunity, incomplete emptying, NGB, perineal soiling from faecal/urinary incontinence, more catheterisation, oestrogen depletion** (women).
- ***E. coli* remains commonest (50–75%)**, but **more *Proteus, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Serratia, Pseudomonas* and enterococci**. **Enterococci often resolve untreated (colonisation).** **Polymicrobial bacteriuria commoner.**
- The shift, polymicrobial pattern and resistance are driven by **institutionalisation, hospitalisation, catheterisation and antibiotic use**.

### Diagnosis

- **Subtle presentations** (abdominal discomfort, **delirium**); even upper tract infection may lack **fever or leucocytosis**.
- **New localised lower tract symptoms — especially dysuria — remain the key criteria.**
- **Bacteriuria + altered mental state ≠ UTI**; no data show antibiotics improve confusion in bacteriuric elderly.
- **Do not culture** for **non-specific symptoms (dizziness, confusion), chronic symptoms (incontinence, frequency) or asymptomatic patients.**
- **Confusion without urinary symptoms → hydrate and reassess**; test urine **only if altered mental state or change in urine persists after fluids**, or in dementia unresponsive to other measures.
- Meta-analysis: **delirium is associated with UTI but not with ASB**; strongest effect modifiers **retention, dehydration, catheters**; whether treating bacteriuria helps delirium is **uncertain**.
- **Never culture to document clearance.**
- **Thresholds: ≥10⁵ CFU/mL with symptoms; ≥10² CFU/mL in catheterised specimens.**
- **Dipstick is better at ruling out than ruling in** (moderate sensitivity, lower specificity). **Pyuria is common and a poor predictor of bacteriuria in the elderly** — many women with pyuria have no bacteriuria — and **pyuria alone is not an indication for treatment**; **absence of pyuria reliably predicts absence of bacteriuria**.
- **Catheterised specimen may be the only clean sample** in institutionalised or dexterity-limited patients.
- **Urological evaluation** (creatinine, CT, ultrasound, **urodynamic studies [UDS]**, cystoscopy) for **renal dysfunction, calculi, hydronephrosis, retention, NGB**; timing by clinical setting.
- **Prompt therapy needed** when bacteriuria → UTI with **structural abnormality (obstruction/hydronephrosis)**, **severe DM**, or **urea-splitters (*Proteus, Klebsiella*) → infection stones and renal damage**.
- **Sepsis increasingly common in the elderly**: **catheters and invasive devices, prostheses, chemotherapy, corticosteroids**, and longer survival with metabolic, neoplastic and immune disorders.

### Management

- **Randomised trials in nursing-home residents: treating ASB gives no benefit**, only **adverse effects, resistant reinfection and cost** → **do not treat ASB in long-term care**.
- **IDSA 2019 recommends against screening for or treating ASB in functionally impaired community-dwelling older people and in long-term care residents.**
- **McGeer surveillance criteria** (Table 26.16) standardise UTI diagnosis in aged-care residents; **limited in cognitive impairment**.
- **Mild LUTS → await culture**; start empirical antibiotics only if immediate treatment needed.
- **Duration:** traditionally 7 days; **Cochrane: short (3–6 days) = long (7–14 days) for uncomplicated symptomatic UTI**; short courses better than **single dose** for persistence; **fever or systemic infection → 10–14 days**. **Goal: eliminate symptoms, not sterilise urine.**
- **Greater drug toxicity**: impaired metabolism/excretion → high serum levels and renal damage; interactions; **narrow therapeutic margin** → dose and monitor carefully. **Fluoroquinolones: tendon rupture risk greatest over 60** (boxed warning) and **CNS effects**.

<p class="tt">TABLE 26.16 Surveillance Definitions for Urinary Tract Infections — revised McGeer criteria (Stone et al., 2012; Ryan et al., 2018)</p>

| | **No indwelling catheter** (criteria 1 AND 2) | **Indwelling catheter** (criteria 1 AND 2) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Clinical (≥1)** | **(a) Acute dysuria**, or acute pain/swelling/tenderness of **testis, epididymis or prostate**; **(b) fever or leucocytosis + ≥1** localising sign: acute CVA pain/tenderness, suprapubic pain, gross haematuria, new/marked increase in incontinence, urgency or frequency; **(c) no fever/leucocytosis → ≥2** of: suprapubic pain, gross haematuria, new/increased incontinence, urgency, frequency | **(a) Fever, rigors or new hypotension** without another source; **(b) acute mental or functional decline** without alternative diagnosis **+ leucocytosis**; **(c) new suprapubic or CVA pain/tenderness**; **(d) purulent pericatheter discharge**, or acute testicular/epididymal/prostatic pain, swelling or tenderness |

- Purpose: **stops bacteriuria alone being labelled UTI** in residents. Pattern: **non-catheterised residents need localising signs; catheterised residents can qualify on systemic signs**. <div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: BACTERIURIA IN THE ELDERLY**

- **Do not screen; do not treat ASB** — no link to uncomplicated UTI or renal deterioration.
- UTI may present **subtly**; a **high index of suspicion** is needed.
- Treatment must allow for **age-related physiology and comorbidity**.
- **Never culture to document clearance.**
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> In the elderly <strong>bacteriuria is the background, not the signal</strong>: because a positive culture is expected, diagnosis must rest on new localising symptoms (or, with a catheter, systemic signs without another source), and non-specific decline should be met with fluids and reassessment before antibiotics.</p>

## CATHETER-ASSOCIATED URINARY TRACT INFECTION

- **Definition (2015 guidelines): UTI after an indwelling catheter has been in place >2 days**, with **≥1 symptom** (suprapubic or CVA tenderness, frequency, urgency, dysuria, **fever >38 °C**) **and a single organism ≥10⁵ CFU/mL**.
- **Catheter-associated bacteriuria is the commonest hospital-acquired infection** and **inevitable** — **about 5–10% per catheter-day** (text gives about 10% in one place and 5–10% in another). **Intermittent catheterisation: 1–3% per catheterisation.**
- **Risk factors: duration, female sex, no systemic antibiotics, breaches of catheter care.**
- **Mostly asymptomatic**: short term — only a minority of bacteriuric episodes are symptomatic; long term — **about 1 febrile episode per 100 catheter-days**.
- **Cost** is substantial and **higher for resistant Gram-negatives** (parenteral therapy). **2008: US Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) stopped reimbursing CAUTI costs.**
- **Perioperative catheter indications: intraoperative output monitoring, prolonged surgery, urological surgery — and limit duration.**
- **Bundled "6-C" approach (Mayo Clinic; large fall in CAUTI):**

<p class="tt ct">The 6-C CAUTI bundle</p>

| C | Action |
|---|---|
| **Consider** | Appropriate placement; **daily review of need** |
| **Connect** | — |
| **Clean** | Catheter care |
| **Closed** | Maintain closed system |
| **Call** | Irrigation only when necessary |
| **Culture** | **Only when indicated** |

### Pathogenesis

- **Entry:** at insertion (**urethral bacteria pushed in**, poor technique); thereafter **periurethral** (extraluminal — **predominant in women**, most had prior urethral colonisation with the infecting strain) or **intraluminal** (from the **drainage bag** — commoner when **catheterised patients are clustered**).

<p class="tt ct">Routes of catheter-associated infection</p>

| **Extraluminal (periurethral)** | **Intraluminal** |
|---|---|
| Ascent along outer surface from meatus | Ascent from **contaminated drainage bag/tubing** |
| **Commonest in women** | **Cross-infection among clustered catheterised patients** |

- **Two populations: planktonic (urine) and biofilm (catheter surface).** **Biofilm** — organisms in matrix of bacterial and host products → **encrustation**; **shields bacteria from antibiotics → CAUTI and resistance**; **forms within minutes**, grows with dwell time.
- ***Pseudomonas* and *Proteus* are adept biofilm formers** → prevalent here. ***E. coli* still commonest**, with *Pseudomonas, Proteus, Enterococcus*. **Long-term (>30 days): polymicrobial, often 4–5 organisms; populations dynamic.**

### Clinical Presentation

- **Suprapubic discomfort, fever, chills or flank pain** suggest symptomatic UTI.

### Laboratory Diagnosis

- **≥10² CFU/mL is significant** — even low counts progress to ≥10⁵ in almost all. **Pyuria does not discriminate infection** in catheterised patients.

### Management

- **Aseptic insertion and a closed, dependent drainage system are essential.**
- **Clean the catheter–meatal junction daily with water; avoid antimicrobial agents** (select resistant organisms such as *Pseudomonas*).
- **Silicone = latex** for CAUTI rates.

<p class="tt ct">Catheter modifications</p>

| Catheter modification | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Silver oxide/alloy catheter; hydrogen peroxide in bag** | Mixed; **silver alloy reduces bacteriuria/CAUTI in short-term hospital catheterisation** (Cochrane) |
| **Nitrofurazone-impregnated** | **Small CAUTI reduction** but **more discomfort and cost** (Cochrane) |

- **Short-term catheter about to be removed (≤3 days) in an asymptomatic patient → dipstick can exclude bacteriuria.**
- **Systemic antibiotics during catheterisation**: delay bacteriuria only **3–4 days**, then rates equalise, with **resistance and side effects** → **not recommended**.
- **Bacterial interference in SCI**: colonisation with a **non-virulent strain reduced symptomatic UTI and improved QoL**.
- **Treat only symptomatic (e.g. febrile) CAUTI** → **culture first** → **stop antibiotics within 48 h of resolution** → **change the catheter** (encrustation shelters bacteria).

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: CATHETER-ASSOCIATED BACTERIURIA**

- **Aseptic insertion and closed, dependent drainage** minimise bacteriuria.
- **Catheter-associated bacteriuria is inevitable.**
- **Only symptomatic CAUTI needs treatment.**
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> <strong>The catheter, not the bladder, is the infected organ</strong>: a biofilm forms within minutes and is beyond the reach of antibiotics, so prevention is about minimising catheter-days and keeping the system closed, and treatment of a symptomatic episode includes removing the colonised surface by changing the catheter.</p>

## MANAGEMENT OF URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS IN PATIENTS WITH SPINAL CORD INJURY

- **All UTIs in SCI are complicated.** **No bacteriuric group compares in severity and morbidity.** Early catheterisation is near-universal (**overactivity or flaccidity**); many develop **ureterectasis, hydronephrosis, VUR, calculi**. Bacteriological and urodynamic advances have **greatly reduced morbidity and mortality**.

### Epidemiology

- **Before CIC and modern antibiotics, UTI and renal failure caused most SCI deaths**; both have fallen since.
- **UTI is among the commonest urological complications** — about a third bacteriuric at any time; **almost all eventually**; significant bacteriuria and febrile UTI recur several times per person-year on intermittent or condom drainage. **UTI is the commonest cause of fever in SCI.**
- **Risk factors (National Institute on Disability and Rehabilitation Research Consensus Conference [NIDRRCC], 1992):** **impaired voiding, overdistension, raised intravesical pressure, obstruction, VUR, instrumentation, stones**; also **low fluid intake, poor hygiene, perineal colonisation, pressure sores and local trauma, reduced host defence**.

### Pathogenesis

- **Bladder management method profoundly affects UTI incidence.**

<p class="tt ct">SCI: bladder management method and infection</p>

| Method | Bacteriuria / outcome |
|---|---|
| **Indwelling urethral catheter** | **Most likely to cause UTI**; **most bacteriuric by 30 days**; all cultures positive in one series |
| **Suprapubic catheter** | **Eventually equivalent** infection rate, but **onset of bacteriuria delayed** |
| **Condom drainage** | Bacteriuria in most to almost all |
| **Clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC)** (Lapides, 1972) | **Lowest risk of long-term complications** |

- **CIC** (never rigorously compared head-to-head) **keeps intravesical pressure low, reduces stones**, and reduces **UTI, fever, bacteraemia, epididymitis and prostatitis**; long-term follow-up shows **fewer complications than urethral catheterisation and no more than any other method**.
- **Sterile vs clean ("no-touch") CIC: conflicting**; an **introducer-tip** catheter bypassing the distal 1.5 cm of urethra reduced UTI. **Low-friction/hydrophilic catheters** may be **less traumatic** and **delay the first UTI**, but an effect on bacteriuria/UTI is **unproven**.

### Clinical Presentation

- **Most bacteriuric SCI patients are asymptomatic → do not treat ASB; do not screen** asymptomatic patients on indwelling, condom or intermittent catheters.
- **No standard UTI definition in SCI.** **Sensory loss → frequency, urgency, dysuria usually absent.** Instead: **flank, back or abdominal discomfort; leakage between catheterisations; increased spasticity; malaise, lethargy; cloudy, malodorous urine; fever** (commonest cause).

### Bacteriology and Laboratory Diagnosis

- **Bacteriuria and pyuria**; **pyuria is not diagnostic** (catheter irritation).

<p class="tt ct">NIDRRCC criteria for significant bacteriuria in SCI</p>

| Drainage | Significant count |
|---|---|
| **Indwelling urethral or suprapubic catheter** | **Any detectable bacteria** (low counts rise rapidly to ≥10⁵) |
| **Intermittent catheterisation** | **≥10² CFU/mL** |
| **Catheter-free men (clean voided)** | **≥10⁴ CFU/mL** |

- Organisms differ in **cause, complexity and susceptibility**, by catheter type and duration: *E. coli* frequent; **enterococci, *P. mirabilis*, *Pseudomonas* commoner than in intact cords**; also *Klebsiella, Serratia, Staphylococcus, Candida*. **Short-term catheter: single organism; >1 month: polymicrobial.**

### Management

- **Culture before empirical therapy** (diverse, resistant flora).
- **Afebrile:** **β-lactams, TMP-SMX or nitrofurantoin** pending culture; **oral fluoroquinolone second-line**. **Change an indwelling catheter** (drainage, remove encrusted foci).
- **Duration (no consensus; expert):**

<p class="tt ct">SCI: duration of therapy</p>

| UTI type | Duration |
|---|---|
| **New afebrile** | **5–7 days** |
| **Recurrent afebrile** | **7–10 days** |
| **Febrile** | **14 days** |

- **Fever or chills → admit; parenteral aminoglycoside + penicillin or 3rd-generation cephalosporin**; involve an antimicrobial expert, especially with recurrences.
- **No improvement in 24–48 h** → repeat culture, adjust therapy, **image for obstruction, stones, abscess**.
- **Post-treatment cultures unnecessary** (recolonisation common) — **except urea-splitters, which must be shown eradicated**.
- **Bladder irrigation** (saline/antibiotic) traditionally **not recommended**; a small series of **gentamicin instillations** in NGB showed **fewer symptomatic UTIs, fewer oral courses and less MDR**.
- **Methenamine ineffective in NGB** (Cochrane). **AP not supported in SCI/NGB** — no reduction in symptomatic UTI and **about a doubling of resistant bacteria**.
- **Recurrent symptomatic UTI → imaging, UDS, review of bladder management** (catheter drainage, CIC technique and frequency, voiding schedule); **lowering storage pressure may reduce symptomatic UTI**.
- **Bladder cancer: catheterisation >10 years raises risk**; in large SCI databases a small proportion developed bladder cancer, and **duration of catheterisation was the only significant predictor**; proposed carcinogens — **chronic infection/inflammation** and **nitrosamines in infected urine**.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: MANAGEMENT OF UTIS IN PATIENTS WITH SCI**

- Most bacteriuric SCI patients are **asymptomatic; treat only symptomatic** patients.
- Symptoms: **fever; flank, back or abdominal discomfort; leakage between catheterisations; spasticity; malaise, lethargy; cloudy, malodorous urine**.
- **Culture before empirical therapy** — diverse, resistant flora.
- **CIC carries the lowest risk of long-term complications.**
- **Chronic infection can be carcinogenic.**
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> In SCI <strong>the bladder-management method is the main modifiable determinant of infection</strong>: low-pressure, intermittent emptying (CIC) prevents the stasis, high pressure and reflux that make infection dangerous, whereas antibiotics can neither prevent colonisation nor safely eradicate it — so they are reserved for symptoms, which must be recognised through non-classical signs.</p>

## KIDNEY TRANSPLANT RECIPIENTS

- **No consensus on treating ASB after renal transplant.**

<p class="tt ct">Renal transplant: guidance on ASB</p>

| Source | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **IDSA 2005** | **No recommendation** (insufficient evidence) |
| **IDSA 2019** | **Do not screen or treat ASB >1 month after transplant** |
| **American Society of Transplantation (AST) 2019** | **Do not routinely culture or treat ASB (even MDR) >2 months after transplant**; if screened and positive → **repeat culture**; **observe if cleared or a different organism**; **consider treatment with unexplained creatinine rise**; **do not treat asymptomatic candiduria except before an invasive urological procedure** |
| **Cochrane 2018** | ASB in about a fifth to a half of recipients; **insufficient evidence for routine treatment**; unclear whether to screen |

- **Immunosuppression (especially early months) → recognising and treating UTI is paramount and may be critical for graft survival.** Recipients have **higher VUR risk**.
- **Risk factors: female sex, DM, cadaveric graft, two episodes of ASB, prolonged pre-transplant haemodialysis.**
- **Duration (early, first 6 months): uncomplicated UTI 5–10 days — not single-dose or 3-day; pyelonephritis/complicated UTI 14–21 days.**
- **Recurrent UTI — AST stepwise evaluation:** **PVR + upper tract imaging (ultrasound/CT) + evaluation for prostatitis** → **correct structural abnormality** → if none, **VCUG for VUR** (surgery or endoscopic injection if present) → if no VUR, **UDS** → **medical management of bladder dysfunction; surgery for outflow obstruction**.
- **Ureteric stents** at transplantation **reduce leak and obstruction** but **raise UTI risk by about half** (Cochrane 2013); **removal within 2 weeks lowers risk**. **AST: remove catheters and stents within 4 weeks; consider early stent removal in severe UTI.**

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> After transplantation the balance tips with time: <strong>early on, graft and host are vulnerable</strong> — longer courses, low threshold, early removal of hardware — <strong>but once the graft is established (beyond 1–2 months), ASB behaves as in other hosts</strong> and treating it only selects resistance.</p>

## ANTIMICROBIAL PROPHYLAXIS FOR COMMON UROLOGIC PROCEDURES

### Principles of Surgical Antimicrobial Prophylaxis

- **AUA Best Practice Statement (BPS)**: 2008 (validated 2011) → **2019** current.
- **Prophylaxis (prevent postprocedural local/systemic infection) vs treatment (eradicate suspected or documented infection).**
- **2019 BPS: consider periprocedural AP for all urological procedures that breach a tissue barrier.**

<p class="tt ct">Principles of surgical prophylaxis</p>

| Principle | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Timing** | **Within 60 min** of the procedure; **vancomycin and fluoroquinolones within 120 min** |
| **Duration** | **Single dose** for most uncomplicated procedures; maintain levels through the procedure; **≤24 h** only in special circumstances |
| **Agent** | Target likely local organisms; guided by **local antibiogram**, cost and safety |
| **Non-traditional prophylaxis** | Periprocedural treatment of the urinary tract before **manipulating colonised hardware (stents, catheters)** |

- Two key questions: **can the host respond to bacteriuria/bacteraemia**, and **what are the consequences of infection** — plus **likelihood of invasion at the surgical site**.
- **Fluoroquinolone boxed warning** must be weighed when choosing AP. **AP has its own morbidity** — rash, GI upset, **allergic nephritis, anaphylaxis**.

<p class="tt">BOX 26.13 Host Factors That Increase the Risk of Infection (Cruse, 1992; Mangram et al., 1999)</p>

- **Advanced age**; **anatomical anomalies**; **poor nutrition**; **smoking**; **chronic corticosteroids**; **immunodeficiency** (e.g. untreated HIV)
- **Chronic indwelling hardware**; **infected endogenous/exogenous material (e.g. stones)**; **distant coexistent infection**; **prolonged hospitalisation**
- The last four act by **raising local bacterial load or altering flora**; the first six by **impairing host response**.

<p class="tt ct">ASB and funguria before procedures: guideline bodies</p>

| Body | Position |
|---|---|
| **AUA BPS 2019** | **ASB and asymptomatic funguria need no treatment before procedures that do not breach the urothelium**; **low-risk** patients may not need AP for such procedures (**except pregnancy**); **single-dose antifungal prophylaxis for endoscopic, robotic or open surgery on the urinary tract**; **no repeat urinalysis to prove clearance** |
| **EAU 2017 (updated 2022)** | ASB **not a risk factor** for procedures **not entering** the urinary tract; **a definite risk factor for procedures breaching the mucosa (especially endoscopic) → culture and treat ASB preoperatively**; **do not treat before arthroplasty** |
| **IDSA 2016 (candidiasis)** | **Treat all candiduria before any urological procedure** (oral fluconazole or IV amphotericin B for several days before and after) |
| **AUA BPS (disagrees with IDSA)** | **Antifungal prophylaxis only for high-pressure-irrigant endoscopy** (transurethral resection of bladder tumour [TURBT] or prostate [TURP], ablative outlet procedures, therapeutic ureteroscopy) and **entry into the urinary tract**; **full treatment for symptomatic fungal UTI at stent/tube change**; **neutropenia + funguria + obstruction → longer course**; **fungal balls (mycetoma) → preoperative fungal culture and 5–7 days periprocedural antifungal** |

- **No benefit from treating ASB before arthroplasty or open-heart surgery.**
- **Consider AP at voiding trials and catheter, drain, stent or nephrostomy removal**, especially with risk factors — a meta-analysis showed **fewer symptomatic UTIs with AP at catheter removal**.

### Urethral Catheterisation and Removal

- AP depends on **health, sex, living circumstances and indication**. **One-off catheterisation: infection 1–2% in healthy domiciliary women, much higher in hospital** → with **risk factors (Box 26.13), oral TMP-SMX** lowers post-procedural infection.
- **Evidence on AP during short-term catheterisation conflicts:**

<p class="tt ct">Prophylaxis during short-term catheterisation: conflicting reviews</p>

| Review | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Cochrane 2013** (short-term ≤14 days, surgical patients; AP 3 days postoperatively or day 2 to removal) | **Less bacteriuria, pyuria, fever, Gram-negative cultures** — interpret with caution (limited evidence, adverse effects) |
| **Cochrane 2005** (AP until removal or first 3 postoperative days) | **No reduction in bacteriuria or infection** |

- **Indwelling catheter: bacteriuria 5–10% per catheter-day**; **routine AP during catheterisation not recommended** (rapid resistance).
- **Long-term intermittent catheterisation: no routine prophylaxis** (comparable UTI rates, low overall risk).
- **After removal:** a quarter of asymptomatic bacteriuric women catheterised 4–6 days developed UTI within 14 days; **1-day TMP-SMX = 10-day course**; no equivalent data in men.
- **Chronic catheter changes: AP not indicated** (occasional low-grade bacteraemia, no systemic sequelae).

### Urodynamics

- **Minimally traumatic** — low infection risk in normal hosts. **AUA: no AP for UDS in "index patients" (no risk factors).**
- Systematic review of RCTs: **AP reduced bacteriuria** (symptomatic UTI not assessed; only a small minority with ASB become symptomatic within a week). **Cochrane 2012: no significant reduction in UTI.**
- **Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine and Urogenital Reconstruction (SUFU) 2017 BPS (non-index patients):** **screen for UTI symptoms and dipstick all**; **suspected UTI → postpone and treat**.

<p class="tt ct">SUFU 2017: prophylaxis for urodynamics</p>

| **No AP needed** | **AP recommended** |
|---|---|
| No GU anomaly; **DM**; **prior GU surgery or prior UTI**; **postmenopausal**; recent hospitalisation; **cardiac valve disease**; nutritional deficiency; **obesity** | **Neurogenic lower urinary tract (LUT) dysfunction**; **raised PVR**; **ASB**; **immunosuppression (incl. renal transplant)**; **age >70**; **indwelling catheter, external collection device or intermittent catheterisation**; **total joint implant within 2 years or high joint-infection risk** |

- **Agent: single oral TMP-SMX**; alternatives **1st/2nd-gen cephalosporin, amoxicillin–clavulanate, fluoroquinolone, parenteral aminoglycoside + ampicillin**. The only nitrofurantoin study (UDS + cystoscopy) showed **no reduction in bacteriuria**; the authors nevertheless use **nitrofurantoin as the alternative to TMP-SMX** (fluoroquinolone warnings). Mostly **Level IV evidence**; judgement prevails.
- **EAU: no AP for cystoscopy or UDS.**
- Trap: **DM, prior UTI, valve disease and obesity do not warrant AP for UDS; age >70, raised PVR and ASB do.**

### Transrectal Ultrasound–Guided Prostate Biopsy

- **Infectious complications rising** (overall up to several per cent; **sepsis 0.3–3.1%**). **Commonest cause: fluoroquinolone-resistant *E. coli*.** **Commonest risk factor: antibiotics within 6 months.**
- **Fluoroquinolone AP reduces infection substantially vs placebo**, but **fluoroquinolone (FQ)-resistant rectal colonisation is common (up to about a fifth)**, most resistant strains are **more virulent**, yet **about 90% of urologists still use empirical fluoroquinolones**.

<p class="tt ct">Transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) biopsy prophylaxis strategies</p>

| Strategy | Evidence | Issues |
|---|---|---|
| **Empirical fluoroquinolone** | Standard; **single dose = 3 days** | Rising FQ-resistant infection |
| **Augmented (aminoglycoside + fluoroquinolone)** | **Meta-analysis of RCTs: fewer UTIs and admissions** | Resistance will evolve |
| **Targeted (rectal swab culture)** | **Meta-analysis: more effective than empirical**; one study **eliminated infections** and **saved cost** (about 38 swabs per infection prevented); a very large US series found **no difference in sepsis** | **Cost, extra visits, special media, laboratory** |
| **Fosfomycin** | **As effective as fluoroquinolone** | — |

- **Minimum 1 day of AP.**
- **AUA White Paper update (2017):** assess **risk factors for resistance**; **urine culture and treat infection in men with LUTS**; **fluoroquinolone within 1 h**; **rectal swab → culture-specific agent (gentamicin, ceftriaxone commonest)**; **use alternatives if local *E. coli* fluoroquinolone resistance >20%**.
- **EAU: AP plus povidone-iodine rectal cleansing.**

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> Transrectal biopsy <strong>inoculates the patient's own rectal flora into the prostate</strong>, so the prophylactic must match that flora — which, after decades of fluoroquinolone use, is increasingly resistant; hence the move from empirical to augmented or swab-targeted prophylaxis.</p>

### Shock Wave Lithotripsy

- **AUA Surgical Management of Stones: no AP if preoperative urine microscopy is benign.** Meta-analysis of RCTs: **no reduction in bacteriuria, fever or UTI**; prospective series: **very low ASB and UTI rates**.
- **Recent UTI or infection stones → full treatment course before SWL** (treatment, not prophylaxis). **EAU: no AP for SWL.**

### Endoscopic Procedures: Lower Urinary Tract

#### Simple Cystoscopy (Without Manipulation)

- Older trials (sterile urine, no AP): **UTI in a few per cent** (higher with **prior UTI**; one series much higher); **single-dose TMP or ciprofloxacin reduced infection to 1–5%**; **no serious systemic infections**.
- Recent evidence: **AP unnecessary for diagnostic cystoscopy** — large series without AP: **few febrile UTIs, all settled on oral antibiotics, none needing admission** (including patients with ASB); meta-analysis: **no difference**; **ciprofloxacin or fosfomycin not indicated for flexible cystoscopy**.
- **Risk factors: recent antibiotics or hospitalisation**; a **risk-plus-antibiogram strategy reduced AP without more UTIs**.
- **EAU 2017: strongly recommends against AP for cystourethroscopy (flexible or rigid)** — low absolute risk, huge volume, resistance risk.

#### Transurethral Resection of the Prostate and Bladder (Cystourethroscopy With Manipulation)

- **Higher risk than diagnostic cystoscopy** — **mucosal trauma, longer/harder procedures, pressurised irrigation, resection of infected material**.
- **TURP (most studied) — meta-analysis: AP markedly reduced postoperative bacteriuria and septicaemia**; most effective: **fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, cephalosporins, TMP-SMX**. **Short courses (2–5 days while the catheter remains) beat single doses**, and continuing the agent briefly with the catheter **does not increase resistance**.
- **TURBT:** no recent trials; TURP data suggest benefit, but **EAU (two RCTs) finds no benefit — AP only in high-risk TURBT**; a prospective study found **no benefit**.
- **Other manipulations (laser prostatectomy, bladder biopsy):** extrapolated from TURP/TURBT; **office biopsy without AP = theatre biopsy with AP** for UTI and febrile UTI — yet **CMS mandates AP for cystoscopy with biopsy in theatre**, and **IDSA and AUA concur**. **IDSA: fluoroquinolone, TMP-SMX or cefazolin for lower tract instrumentation with risk factors.**
- **Risk factors:** **obesity, DM, smoking, immunosuppression, malnutrition**; urological — **anomalies, obstruction, stones, indwelling or externalised catheters**.
- **Known preoperative UTI → eradicate first** (this is treatment); **operating on unsterilised urine → bacteraemia in about half**.
- **Pressurised-irrigant diagnostic/therapeutic upper tract studies → AP covering uropathogens.**

<p class="tt ct">Diagnostic cystoscopy versus TURP</p>

| **Diagnostic cystoscopy** | **Transurethral resection (TURP)** |
|---|---|
| **No AP** (EAU strongly against; recent AUA-aligned evidence) | **AP in all** (single dose or short course with catheter) |
| Minimal urothelial injury | Mucosal trauma, pressurised irrigant, infected tissue |

### Endoscopic Procedures: Upper Urinary Tract

#### Ureteroscopy

- **Higher risk than cystoscopy** (mucosal trauma, duration, **irrigant pressure**, infected material). **RCT: fluoroquinolone AP reduced post-ureteroscopy bacteriuria** in healthy patients with sterile urine.
- **Suspected infection → culture and full treatment before**; some use **furosemide diuresis** intraoperatively.
- Meta-analysis (ureteroscopic lithotripsy): **no difference in febrile UTI**, but **single preoperative dose lowers bacteriuria and pyuria** → **single oral preoperative dose recommended**.

#### Ureteral Stenting

- **Give AP at stent removal**; **culture the stent** in high-risk patients to guide later treatment.
- **Stent colonisation far exceeds urine colonisation**; **dwell time is the main determinant and is not reduced by AP**; **urine culture identifies the stent organism in only a small minority**.

<p class="tt ct">Urine versus stent colonisation</p>

| **Urine** | **Stent** |
|---|---|
| Colonised in a minority | **Colonised in about half or more** |
| **Poor surrogate for stent organism** | Culture it directly when risk is high |

#### Percutaneous Renal Surgery (Prophylaxis Indicated in All Patients)

- For **large stones, pelviureteric junction obstruction, urothelial carcinoma surveillance**. **Pyrexia and bacteraemia are frequent** — **parenchymal injury, pressurised irrigation, infected stones**; risk rises with **procedure duration and irrigant volume**. No RCTs.
- **Positive preoperative culture → treat first; negative → AP against common uropathogens.**

### Open, Laparoscopic and Robotic Surgery

- Wound classes (Table 26.18): **AP for clean-contaminated and contaminated; treatment for dirty**. **Laparoscopy lowers SSI** (general surgery data).
- **Clean** — e.g. **radical nephrectomy without entering the urinary tract**; **elective opening of the urinary tract = Class II**; **entering an infected tract = Class III** (higher SSI).
- **Give within 1 h; stop by 24 h** — longer courses show no benefit.
- **First-generation cephalosporins** standard for clean-contaminated (**few allergies, long half-life, cheap**); **β-lactam allergy → vancomycin or clindamycin** (National Surgical Infection Prevention Project [NSIPP] 2004).
- **Intestinal reconstruction → 1st-generation cephalosporin + metronidazole.**
- **Colon/appendix: oral neomycin + erythromycin or neomycin + metronidazole 18–24 h before + IV cefotetan or cefoxitin 30–60 min before incision** (NSIPP, WHO); **β-lactam allergy → clindamycin + gentamicin, aztreonam, or ciprofloxacin**.
- **Dirty wounds** (all abscesses, traumatic GU perforation) → **broad empirical cover + intraoperative cultures**, then tailor.

<p class="tt">TABLE 26.18 Wound Classifications (modified from Mangram et al., 1999)</p>

| Class | Definition | Urological examples |
|---|---|---|
| **I — clean** | Uninfected; **no entry into respiratory, GI or GU tract** | **Inguinal/scrotal procedures for non-infective indications; retroperitoneal lymph node dissection (RPLND)** |
| **II — clean-contaminated** | **Controlled entry** into respiratory, GI or GU tract; no other contamination | **Opening the urinary tract: nephrectomy, cystectomy, prostatectomy, endoscopic procedures** |
| **III — contaminated** | **Infected stone procedures; use of bowel segments** | **PCNL for struvite/infected stones; transrectal prostate biopsy** |
| **IV — dirty** | **Open trauma, abscesses** | **Debridement** — organisms present before surgery |
| **Prosthesis implantation** | — | **IPP, AUS**: cover **skin organisms**; **MRSA colonisation → many surgeons add vancomycin** |

- Purpose: **class dictates prophylaxis vs treatment**. Traps: **any endoscopic procedure is Class II**; **transrectal biopsy is Class III**; this table places **bowel segments in Class III**, whereas Table 26.17 labels bowel procedures clean-contaminated.

### Skin Preparation for Surgery

- **Pre-admission bathing, mechanical bowel preparation, hair removal and skin preparation** all help prevent SSI; details in the AUA BPS.

### Vaginal Surgery

- No urological RCTs; **vaginal hysterectomy data: AP markedly reduces pelvic infection**; **≤24 h as effective as longer courses**.
- **AUA BPS (2010, 2019): AP for all vaginal procedures, including midurethral sling (MUS)** — **expert opinion**. MUS studies (uncontrolled series, an RCT stopped early for low event rates, decision analysis) suggest **AP may be unnecessary because infection is rare**.

### Special Considerations

#### Patients With Risk of Endocarditis

- **Risk of infective endocarditis (IE) after urological procedures is low.**
- **American Heart Association (AHA) 2007: AP solely to prevent IE is not recommended** for GU procedures, though GU instrumentation can cause **transient enterococcal bacteraemia**. **American College of Cardiology (ACC)/AHA 2017: no benefit; IE is likelier from random bacteraemia** — **not even for high-risk patients** (prosthetic valve, previous IE, cardiac transplant with valvulopathy).
- **Exception (Class IIb): high-risk cardiac condition + active urinary infection or colonisation before GU manipulation (incl. elective cystoscopy) → treat to sterilise the urine** — **amoxicillin/ampicillin first line (enterococcus), vancomycin if intolerant**, or culture-directed.

#### Patients With Indwelling Orthopaedic Hardware

<p class="tt ct">Orthopaedic hardware: statements compared</p>

| Statement | Position |
|---|---|
| **AUA/American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS)/infectious disease specialists advisory 2003** | **No AP if joint replacement, pins, plates or screws ≥2 years old**; **<2 years → oral quinolone, or IV ampicillin 2 g + gentamicin 1.5 mg/kg (vancomycin if ampicillin-allergic) 30–60 min before** |
| **AAOS 2009** | **Consider AP for all total joint replacements** before any bacteraemia-risk procedure (cost and consequences of infected joints) |
| **AUA BPS 2019** | **AP to prevent hip/knee infection** for **high-bacteraemia-risk procedures, joints <2 years, high-risk patients** — **weak evidence, but potentially catastrophic harm** |

- **Procedures warranting AP for joint seeding:** **stone manipulation, transmural incision of the urinary tract, upper tract endoscopy, bowel segments, transrectal prostate biopsy**.
- **Patient factors:** **recent prosthesis or compromised host** with **urinary diversion, indwelling stent or catheter, recent retention, or UTI** → AP before urinary tract procedures.

<p class="tt">TABLE 26.19 Antimicrobial Regimens for Patients With Indwelling Orthopedic Hardware (AUA and AAOS, 2003)</p>

| Patient type | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Total joint >2 years ago; pins, plates, screws; no host risk factors** | **Not recommended empirically** |
| **Total joint <2 years ago, or aberrant host factor(s)** | **Oral quinolone, or ampicillin 2 g IV + gentamicin 1.5 mg/kg IV, 30–60 min before**; **vancomycin 1 g IV over 1–2 h** if ampicillin-allergic |

- Purpose: **time since implantation and host factors decide prophylaxis**. Trap: **pins, plates and screws alone never need AP**; the 2-year cut-off is the pivot.

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: ANTIMICROBIAL PROPHYLAXIS FOR COMMON UROLOGIC PROCEDURES**

- **AP = antimicrobial before and for a limited time after a procedure** to prevent local/systemic infection.
- **Procedure type and host defences** determine need.
- Special considerations: **TRUS biopsy, endocarditis risk with bacteriuria, orthopaedic hardware**.
</div>

<p class="concept"><strong>Concept —</strong> <strong>Prophylaxis is justified by the product of wound contamination and host vulnerability</strong>: where neither is raised (diagnostic cystoscopy, SWL with sterile urine, UDS in index patients) the ecological cost outweighs the benefit; where either is (bowel, infected stones, transrectal biopsy, prostheses, immunocompromise) a single, well-timed, flora-matched dose is the default, and anything longer is treatment, not prophylaxis.</p>
