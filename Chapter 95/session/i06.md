# Instalment 6 — Hyperoxaluria: Definition and Oxidative Injury, Primary Hyperoxaluria Types 1–3, SLC26A1, Enteric Hyperoxaluria (Short Bowel, Cystic Fibrosis, Bariatric Surgery), Dietary Hyperoxaluria, Role of the Microbiome, Idiopathic Hyperoxaluria; Hyperuricosuria and Hyperuricosuric Calcium Nephrolithiasis

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF UPPER URINARY TRACT CALCULI (continued)

### Calcium Stones (continued)

#### Hyperoxaluria

- **Definition: urinary oxalate > 40 mg/day.**
- **Effects**: **↑ calcium oxalate saturation**; also implicated in **crystal growth and retention via tubular cell injury** — **lipid peroxidation and oxygen free radicals** → membrane injury → **crystal fixation**.
  - **Antioxidants** prevent calcium oxalate precipitation in rat kidney and reduce oxalate excretion; **free-radical scavengers (phytic acid, mannitol)** prevent crystal deposition in vitro.
  - **But in humans**, large oxalate loads (up to 8 mmol) **did not raise oxidative-stress or renal-injury markers** — **the importance of oxalate-induced membrane damage is questioned**.
- **Three causes**: **primary (biosynthetic enzyme defect)**, **enteric (malabsorption — inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, intestinal resection)**, **dietary (excess intake or substrate)**.

##### Primary Hyperoxaluria

- **Rare autosomal recessive disorders of glyoxylate metabolism**: glyoxylate cannot be converted to **glycine**, so it is **oxidised to oxalate**, a metabolic end product.
- **Urinary oxalate markedly high (≥ 100 mg/day)** → calcium oxalate crystals in tubular lumens → some **attach to epithelium and aggregate into stones**, others are **internalised and extruded into the interstitium → marked nephrocalcinosis**.
- **Renal injury**: direct toxicity of oxalate or crystals (**reactive oxygen species**), **recurrent obstructing stones**, and **inflammation and interstitial fibrosis** from nephrocalcinosis.
- **Vicious circle**: falling renal function → oxalate elimination fails → **systemic oxalosis** (systemic calcium oxalate deposition).

<p class="tcap">Primary hyperoxaluria types 1–3 — comparison</p>

| Feature | **PH1** | **PH2** | **PH3** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Enzyme** | **Alanine-glyoxylate aminotransferase (AGT)** — **pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6)-dependent** | **Glyoxylate reductase/hydroxypyruvate reductase (GRHPR)** | **4-Hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate aldolase (HOGA)** |
| **Gene** | **AGXT** (≥ 253 mutations) | **GRHPR** (≈ 60 mutations) | HOGA1 |
| **Organelle** | **Liver peroxisome** | Liver | **Mitochondria** |
| **Urinary markers** | **Oxalate + glycolate** | **Oxalate + L-glyceric acid** | Oxalate; **severe hypercalciuria** |
| **Course** | **Most aggressive** — **ESRD in second to third decade**; **untreated → ESRD inevitable** (**88% by age 60** in the Rare Kidney Stone Consortium, RKSC) | **Less aggressive** renal course | Recurrent childhood stones that may become **clinically silent**; **only one reported case of ESRD** |
| **Specific therapy note** | **Pyridoxine-responsive** in Gly170Arg (with Pro11Leu) mistargeting | — | Mechanism of hyperoxaluria **not established** |
| **Transplant** | **Combined liver–kidney** for most severe cases | **Isolated kidney** transplant, needed less often | Rarely reaches ESRD |

- **PH1 mistargeting mutation**: commonest AGXT mutation **Gly170Arg**; with the **Pro11Leu minor-allele polymorphism**, AGT is **sent to mitochondria (inactive) instead of peroxisomes** → **pyridoxine responds** because its product **pyridoxal phosphate** is the AGT cofactor, raising catalytic activity and **restoring peroxisomal targeting**.
- **PH2 biochemistry**: ↓ GRHPR → ↑ hydroxypyruvate and glyoxylate → **lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)** converts them to **L-glyceric acid and oxalate**.
- **PH3 biochemistry**: HOGA converts hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate (from **hydroxyproline**) to pyruvate and glyoxylate.
- **Nephrocalcinosis on imaging → about 70% higher ESRD risk** (RKSC).
- **Why liver–kidney transplant in PH1**: **the liver is the only organ that detoxifies glyoxylate** — a kidney alone would be re-injured by continuing hepatic oxalate overproduction.
- **Genetic testing** (all three types): confirms biochemistry, allows **prenatal diagnosis**, and identifies affected relatives.
  - **All three types can stay asymptomatic until adulthood → screen family members.**
  - **Exclude PH in relatives before living-related kidney donation.**
  - **Caveat: not all carriers develop PH.**
- **SLC26A1 (SAT1 sulphate–oxalate transporter) mutations** — another inherited hyperoxaluria; consider when **PH1–3 are excluded**.

**Concept —** *All three primary hyperoxalurias block the **disposal of glyoxylate** in the liver, forcing it to oxalate; severity follows the enzyme (PH1 ≫ PH2 > PH3), and because the defect is hepatic, only replacing the liver cures PH1.*

##### Enteric Hyperoxaluria

- **The commonest cause of acquired hyperoxaluria.**
- **Mechanism (Fig. 95.9)** — a sequence:
  **fat malabsorption → luminal fatty acids saponify calcium and magnesium → less calcium available to complex oxalate → more free luminal oxalate → colonic hyperabsorption**; **malabsorbed fatty acids and bile salts also ↑ colonic permeability to oxalate**.
- **Fecal fat correlates strongly with urinary oxalate** in steatorrhoea (Fig. 95.10).
- **Co-existing risks in chronic diarrhoea**: **dehydration, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesiuria, hypocitraturia, low urine pH**.
- **Any malabsorption** — **small-bowel resection, intrinsic small-bowel disease, jejunoileal bypass** — can cause it.
- **Requires an intact colon** — **the colon is the principal site of oxalate hyperabsorption**; in **terminal-ileal Crohn disease** stone incidence **rises with length of bowel resected, provided the colon is intact**.
- **Cystic fibrosis**: increased stone risk (overall prevalence about 4.5%); proposed causes — **enteric hyperoxaluria from fat malabsorption, loss of O. formigenes, hyperuricosuria, hypocitraturia**.
- **Bariatric surgery**:
  - **Jejunoileal bypass was abandoned** partly for **renal failure and stones from severe hyperoxaluria**.
  - **Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB)** — initially considered safer, but **oxalate nephropathy with dialysis or transplantation** was reported; retrospective, cross-sectional and prospective studies confirm **↑ urinary oxalate after RYGB and other malabsorptive procedures**.
  - **Hyperoxaluria develops ≥ 6 months after surgery.**
  - **Falling urinary calcium and uric acid partially offset** the oxalate rise — **net effect on calcium oxalate saturation varies**.
  - Nonetheless **stone incidence is higher after RYGB than in obese controls** (claims data).
  - **Cause**: loss of O. formigenes is **unlikely** (colonisation is already low before surgery and does not differ from obese controls); an **exaggerated urinary response to oral oxalate load** points to **malabsorption / enteric hyperoxaluria**.

| Bariatric procedure | Type | Stone risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Long-limb Roux-en-Y gastric bypass; duodenal switch** | **Malabsorptive** | **Increased** (enteric hyperoxaluria) |
| **Gastric banding; sleeve gastrectomy** | **Restrictive** | **Not increased** |

| Governing contrast | Colon intact (short bowel, small-bowel disease) | Colon absent (ileostomy after colectomy) |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxalate** | **Hyperoxaluria** (colonic hyperabsorption) | **No hyperoxaluria** |
| **Typical stone** | **Calcium oxalate** | **Uric acid and ammonium acid urate** (low volume, low pH, low sodium) |

##### Dietary Hyperoxaluria

- **Oxalate-rich foods**: **nuts, chocolate, brewed tea, spinach, potatoes, beets, rhubarb** — overindulgence causes hyperoxaluria in otherwise normal people.
- **Dietary oxalate contributes 24–42% of urinary oxalate** (in this analysis).
- **Severe calcium restriction ↑ oxalate absorption** (less luminal binding) — **calcium restriction is counter-productive**.
- **Ascorbic acid supplements ↑ urinary oxalate** by **in vivo conversion**, but **clinical stone risk is not unequivocally increased**.

##### Role of the Microbiome

- **Humans lack oxalate-degrading enzymes**; some gut bacteria degrade oxalate.
- **O. formigenes uses oxalate as its sole carbon source** — a potential **modulator of intestinal oxalate**.
- **Stone formers have reduced or absent colonisation**, and **non-colonised individuals have higher urinary oxalate**.
- **Antibiotic exposure depletes O. formigenes**:
  - **Cystic fibrosis** (prolonged antibiotics) — **absent O. formigenes, raised urinary oxalate**.
  - **Helicobacter pylori eradication** — **lower colonisation** than untreated H. pylori controls.
- **Population case–control study (UK electronic records)**: exposure to **any of five antibiotic classes 3–12 months earlier ↑ stone risk** — yet O. formigenes is **resistant to some of those classes**, so the effect may reflect **reduced overall microbiome diversity**, not O. formigenes alone.

##### Idiopathic Hyperoxaluria

- **Mild hyperoxaluria may matter as much as hypercalciuria** in idiopathic calcium oxalate stones.
- **Arabian Peninsula**: calcium stone prevalence **far higher than in the West** despite **near-absent hypercalciuria** — **hyperoxaluria is the predominant risk factor**.
- **Abnormal oxalate metabolism and transport** may contribute.

**Concept —** *Outside the rare primary forms, hyperoxaluria is a problem of **free luminal oxalate reaching the colon** — raised by fat malabsorption, low calcium intake, high oxalate intake or loss of degrading bacteria — so colonic presence and luminal calcium are the levers.*

#### Hyperuricosuria

- **Definition: urinary uric acid > 600 mg/day.**
- **Isolated in up to 10% of calcium stone formers; combined with other abnormalities in up to 40%.**

<p class="tcap">How uric acid promotes calcium oxalate stones (Fig. 95.11) — three mechanisms</p>

| Phase | Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **I. Colloid** | **Colloidal sodium urate/uric acid adsorbs macromolecular inhibitors** | **Inhibitor removal** → calcium oxalate nucleation |
| **II. Solution** | **"Salting out"** — dissolved urate salt **lowers calcium oxalate solubility** | ↑ Calcium oxalate precipitation |
| **III. Crystalline** | **Monosodium urate or uric acid crystals act as nidi** | **Heterogeneous nucleation / epitaxial growth** of calcium oxalate |

- **Each mechanism has supporting evidence; which dominates is unclear.**
- **Commonest cause: dietary purine excess.**
- **Other causes**: **gout; myeloproliferative and lymphoproliferative disorders; multiple myeloma; secondary polycythaemia; pernicious anaemia; haemolytic disorders; haemoglobinopathies and thalassaemia; complete or partial hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT) deficiency; phosphoribosylpyrophosphate synthetase overactivity; hereditary renal hypouricaemia**.
- **URAT1** (urate–anion exchanger, **proximal tubule**, gene **SLC22A12**): **mutations → hyperuricosuric hypouricaemia (renal uric acid leak)**, **exercise-induced acute renal failure** and **high stone risk**.
- **Evidence against a role**: in three large cohorts with 24-hour urines, **urinary uric acid was inversely associated with incident stones in males**, marginally inversely in younger females and not associated in older females.
- **Evidence for — landmark trial, Ettinger (1986)**: randomised, placebo-controlled trial of **allopurinol** in **hyperuricosuric, normocalciuric calcium oxalate stone formers** — **more than twofold reduction in recurrence**. **But the benefit cannot be attributed with certainty to lowering urinary uric acid.**

**Concept —** *Uric acid drives calcium oxalate stones through **urate's physical chemistry** (inhibitor adsorption, salting out, epitaxy) rather than through uric acid stones themselves — and although epidemiology is conflicting, the allopurinol trial shows that in selected hyperuricosuric formers treatment works.*

## ▸ The numbers to lock in

- **Hyperoxaluria > 40 mg/day; primary hyperoxaluria ≥ 100 mg/day.**
- **PH1: AGT, peroxisome, B6-dependent, ESRD in 2nd–3rd decade; 88% ESRD by 60 (RKSC).**
- **Nephrocalcinosis → ~70% higher ESRD risk in PH.**
- **PH1 → combined liver–kidney transplant; PH2 → kidney alone (less often); PH3 → rarely ESRD.**
- **Pyridoxine-responsive PH1: Gly170Arg + Pro11Leu (mitochondrial mistargeting).**
- **Enteric hyperoxaluria requires intact colon; appears ≥ 6 months after RYGB.**
- **Dietary oxalate = 24–42% of urinary oxalate.**
- **Antibiotics 3–12 months before → ↑ stone risk.**
- **Hyperuricosuria > 600 mg/day; isolated in ≤ 10%, combined in ≤ 40% of calcium formers.**
- **Landmark: Ettinger 1986 allopurinol RCT — > twofold recurrence reduction in hyperuricosuric normocalciuric calcium oxalate formers.**
- Eponyms:
  - **Oxalobacter formigenes** — obligate oxalate-degrader (sole carbon source).
  - **Roux-en-Y** — malabsorptive bypass; stone risk ↑.
  - **Crohn disease** — stone risk scales with resected length if colon intact.
  - **Lesch-Nyhan / HGPRT deficiency** — hyperuricosuria.
- When to operate / transplant:
  - **PH1 with ESRD → combined liver–kidney transplantation.**
  - **Screen relatives for PH before living-related donation.**
- Classic traps:
  - **Ileostomy (no colon) → no hyperoxaluria**; uric acid / ammonium urate stones instead.
  - **Restrictive bariatric procedures (band, sleeve) do not raise stone risk.**
  - **Calcium restriction raises urinary oxalate.**
  - **Loss of O. formigenes does not explain post-bypass hyperoxaluria.**
  - **PH3 has severe hypercalciuria but benign renal course.**
  - **Allopurinol benefit ≠ proof that uric acid lowering is the mechanism.**
