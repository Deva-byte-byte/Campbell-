# Instalment 8 — Uric Acid Stones (Uricase Loss, Rising Incidence, pKa and Solubility, Three Determinants, Theobromine), Low Urine pH and Insulin Resistance (Ammoniagenesis, Renal Lipotoxicity, Animal Protein), Hyperuricosuria, Low Urinary Volume; Cystine Stones (Transporter Genetics, Solubility, ICC Classification, Renal Function)

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF UPPER URINARY TRACT CALCULI (continued)

### Uric Acid Stones

- **Humans and apes lack hepatic uricase** (which converts uric acid to **allantoin**) — the gene was **silenced (pseudogenised)** in evolution, leaving **higher blood and urine uric acid**.
- **Allantoin is 10–100 times more soluble than uric acid** — hence **human susceptibility to uric acid stones**.
- **Frequency**: **8–10% of US stones**, **up to 25% in parts of Germany**; **rising** — a US registry saw the proportion **double (≈ 7% → 14%) over 30 years**; a Norwegian series rose from **2% to 9% over 40 years**.
- **Uric acid is a weak acid, pKa 5.35 at 37 °C** — at that pH, **half is urate salt, half free acid**.
- **Sodium urate is about 20 times more soluble than free uric acid** → **the free-acid fraction (set by pH) determines risk**.
- **pH governs solubility** (Fig. 95.13):
  - **At pH 5, even modest uric acid exceeds solubility.**
  - **At pH 6.5, > 1200 mg/L remains soluble.**
  - **Solubility limit of undissociated uric acid ≈ 96–100 mg/L** — readily exceeded by normal daily excretion (**500–600 mg/day**), so urine is often supersaturated **below pH 6**.
- **Low pH also drives calcium oxalate and mixed stones** — uric acid and sodium urate act as **nidi (heterogeneous nucleation, epitaxy)**.
- **Uric acid stone formation after precipitation is poorly understood**; crystal adhesion and glycosaminoglycans are proposed but unproven — **no natural urinary inhibitor of uric acid is known**.
- **Theobromine** (dimethylxanthine in **cocoa and chocolate**) **inhibits uric acid nucleation and growth in vitro**; cocoa products **raised urinary theobromine and reduced uric acid crystallisation** in volunteers (in vitro urine assay).

<p class="tcap">The three determinants of uric acid stone formation (Fig. 95.14)</p>

| Determinant | Causes | Importance |
|---|---|---|
| **Low urine pH** | **High animal protein diet, chronic diarrhoea, primary gout, obesity ↔ insulin resistance** | **The single most important factor** — most uric acid formers have **normal uric acid excretion but persistently low pH** |
| **Low urine volume** | **Diarrhoeal states**, volume depletion, hot climate or occupation | Concentrates uric acid |
| **Hyperuricosuria** | **High animal protein diet, primary gout, myeloproliferative disorders, uricosuric drugs, congenital disorders** | Least often the driver in idiopathic cases |

- **Drosophila model**: knockout of an orthologue of human **Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger regulatory factor (NHERF1)** → more acidic urine and more uric acid crystals in **Malpighian tubules** — underlining pH.
- **Aetiological categories**:
  - **Congenital** — defects in **renal urate transport or uric acid metabolism** → hyperuricosuria.
  - **Acquired** — **chronic diarrhoea, volume depletion, myeloproliferative disorders, high animal protein intake, uricosuric drugs** — may act on any of the three determinants.
  - **Idiopathic** — **decreased fractional excretion of urate**, **no gout**.

| Governing contrast | Idiopathic uric acid nephrolithiasis | Hyperuricosuric calcium nephrolithiasis |
|---|---|---|
| **Urinary uric acid** | **Normal** | **High** |
| **Urine pH** | **Low (acidic)** | **Normal** |
| **Other urine features** | Low ammonium, high titratable acid | **High urinary sodium and calcium** → ↑ sodium urate and calcium oxalate saturation |
| **Stone** | **Uric acid** | **Calcium oxalate** |

#### Low Urine pH

- **Pathogenesis uncertain and probably multifactorial**; the leading model links it to **insulin resistance**:
  - **Normouricosuric pure uric acid formers are more often diabetic or glucose-intolerant.**
  - On a **controlled metabolic diet** their **urine pH is lower** than controls or calcium oxalate formers; they excrete **less acid as ammonium**, **more as titratable acid** and **less citrate** — **impaired ammonium excretion**.
  - **Uric acid stones are far commoner in type 2 diabetes** (about a third of stones in diabetics versus roughly a tenth or less in non-diabetics in two series).
  - Uric acid formers share **metabolic syndrome features** — **hypertriglyceridaemia, hyperglycaemia, obesity, hypertension**.
  - **Hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp**: **low urine pH correlates with insulin resistance** in normal subjects, and **uric acid formers are the most insulin-resistant**.
  - **Hyperglycaemia → lower urine pH and higher uric acid saturation.**
  - **Body weight is inversely related to urine pH**, **independent of urinary sulphate** (animal-protein marker); **BMI ≥ 27 kg/m² predicts urine pH ≤ 5.5**.
- **Mechanism (Fig. 95.15)**: **insulin promotes proximal tubular ammoniagenesis from glutamine** and **stimulates the Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger NHE3**, which **carries NH₄⁺ or supplies luminal H⁺ to trap NH₃** → **insulin resistance → ↓ ammonium production/excretion → unbuffered H⁺ → low urine pH**.
- **Diet-independent impairment**: higher BMI → **lower ammonium-to-sulphate ratio** (ammoniagenesis corrected for acid intake) and **higher uric acid supersaturation**.
- **Increased endogenous acid production**:
  - On a fixed **low acid-ash diet**, uric acid formers have **higher net acid excretion**.
  - **Controlled for urinary sulphate**, net acid excretion is higher in uric acid formers and in **non-forming type 2 diabetics**.
  - Matched for age, sex and BMI on a metabolic diet, uric acid formers show **lower pH, higher titratable acid and net acid excretion**, and **higher uric acid supersaturation despite equal uric acid excretion**.
- **Synthesis**: with **impaired ammonium excretion plus increased endogenous acid**, **titratable acids become the main urinary buffer**; acid–base balance is kept, **but at a lower urine pH** than ammonium (higher pKa) would allow.
- **Why ammoniagenesis fails — renal lipotoxicity**:
  - **Metabolic switch**: proximal tubule uses **free fatty acids instead of glutamine** → less ammoniagenesis.
  - **Lipotoxicity**: fat deposited in non-adipose tissue injures cells; in the **Zucker diabetic fatty (ZDF) rat**, **renal steatosis reduced NHE3 expression and activity**.
  - Uric acid stone matrix proteomics: proteins mainly of **inflammation and complement**; pathways chiefly **phospholipid and fatty acid** metabolism.
  - **Visceral fat on CT** is associated with **low urine pH (< 6)** and independently with **uric acid composition**.
- **Diet**: in a randomised crossover of **vegetable, vegetable-plus-egg and animal protein diets** (rising sulphate), **animal protein raised urinary calcium**, gave the **most undissociated uric acid and the least citrate** (lower pH); matched for electrolytes and protein, it **raised uric acid stone risk but — because of opposing factors — not calcium oxalate or calcium phosphate risk**.

**Concept —** *Uric acid stones are a **disease of urine pH, not of uric acid**: insulin resistance and renal fat impair **ammoniagenesis**, so the kidney buffers acid with titratable acid at a lower pH — making the uric acid stone a renal manifestation of metabolic syndrome.*

#### Hyperuricosuria

- **Definition: urinary uric acid > 600 mg/day** — causes supersaturation with undissociated uric acid.
- **Gout**: stones **markedly fewer** with urinary uric acid **< 600 mg/day** than with **> 1000 mg/day**.
- **Causes**: dietary, acquired and hereditary diseases, **urate-transporter defects** (as above).

#### Low Urinary Volume

- **Any cause of low volume raises uric acid supersaturation** — **hot-temperature workers** show high uric acid supersaturation; **warm climates (e.g. Israel)** have **high uric acid stone rates**.

### Cystine Stones

- **Normal handling**: amino acids are freely filtered and **almost completely reabsorbed proximally**.
- **Transporter**: cystine and the other **dibasic amino acids** cross the apical membrane of the **proximal tubule and jejunum** via a **sodium-independent heteromeric amino acid transporter**, exchanging for neutral amino acids.

| Subunit | Gene | Chromosome | ICC type |
|---|---|---|---|
| **rBAT (heavy)** | **SLC3A1** (≈ 247 mutations) | **2p (short arm of chromosome 2)** | **Type A** |
| **b⁰,⁺AT (light)** | **SLC7A9** (≈ 160 mutations) | **19q (long arm of chromosome 19)** | **Type B** |
| Both | — | 2 and 19 | **Type AB** |

- **Mutation in either subunit → cystinuria.**
- **Inheritance: autosomal recessive** (rarely **autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance**).
- **No nutritional consequence** of the identical jejunal defect — amino acids are absorbed as **oligopeptides and whole proteins**.
- **Fractional excretion of cystine: ≈ 0.4% normally, up to 100% in cystinuria.**
- **Only cystine forms stones** — other dibasic amino acids are soluble; **cystine = two cysteines joined by a disulphide bond**, **far less soluble than cysteine**; intracellular reduction to cysteine maintains the reabsorption gradient.
- **Epidemiology**: incidence **1 in 1000 to 1 in 17,000** (US and Europe); **up to 10% of paediatric stones**; **more frequent and more severe in males**.
- **Solubility determinants**: **cystine concentration, pH, ionic strength, urinary macromolecules**.
  - **Supersaturation is the main driver — there is no specific urinary inhibitor of cystine crystallisation.**
  - **Precipitates at physiological urine conditions.**
  - **Highly pH-dependent**: **≈ 300 mg/L at pH 5, ≈ 400 mg/L at pH 7, ≈ 1000 mg/L at pH 9** (Dent and Senior, 1955).
  - **Ionic strength** (0.005 → 0.3) dissolves up to **≈ 70 mg/L more**; **colloids** also increase solubility → **cystine is more soluble in urine than in synthetic solution** (Fig. 95.16).
- **Co-existing abnormalities** in cystine formers — **hypocitraturia (commonest), hyperuricosuria, hypercalciuria** — can produce **calcium or mixed calcium–cystine stones**.
- **Classification**:
  - **Historical** — by urinary cystine in **obligate heterozygote parents**; **correlated poorly with genotype**.
  - **International Cystinuria Consortium (ICC)** — by chromosome: **type A (chromosome 2), type B (chromosome 19), type AB (both)**.
- **ICC data**: homozygotes excrete **up to ≈ 2000 μmol cystine/g creatinine**; **mean age at first stone ≈ 12 years**; stone episodes **≈ 0.42/year in males versus 0.21/year in females**.
- **Heterozygotes**: **type B carriers excrete far more cystine** (≈ 475 versus ≈ 70 μmol/g creatinine in type A), **yet stone formation does not differ and is uncommon in type B carriers**.
- **Renal function**: stone formers generally have **higher CKD risk**, and **cystine formers have lower creatinine clearance than other formers**; in a French cohort about a quarter of adults had **eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²** and many had **hypertension**.
  - Explanation: **more open surgery, including nephrectomy**; histology shows **dilated ducts of Bellini plugged with cystine**, **glomerulosclerosis and interstitial fibrosis**.

**Concept —** *Cystinuria is a **pure supersaturation disease** — a single transport defect, no inhibitor, and a solubility that only rises meaningfully at alkaline pH — so only dilution and a markedly alkaline pH move it out of the precipitating range, and repeated surgery erodes renal function.*

## ▸ The numbers to lock in

- **Allantoin 10–100× more soluble than uric acid.**
- **Uric acid pKa 5.35; sodium urate ~20× more soluble than free acid.**
- **Undissociated uric acid solubility ≈ 96–100 mg/L; pH 6.5 → > 1200 mg/L soluble.**
- **Uric acid 8–10% of US stones; up to 25% (Germany); rising.**
- **BMI ≥ 27 kg/m² → urine pH ≤ 5.5.**
- **Hyperuricosuria > 600 mg/day; gout stones rise at > 1000 mg/day.**
- **Cystine: FE 0.4% → 100%; solubility 300 / 400 / 1000 mg/L at pH 5 / 7 / 9.**
- **Cystinuria incidence 1:1000–1:17,000; ≤ 10% of paediatric stones; male > female.**
- **ICC: A = SLC3A1 (chr 2), B = SLC7A9 (chr 19), AB = both.**
- **First cystine stone mean ≈ 12 years; homozygote excretion up to 2000 μmol/g creatinine.**
- Eponyms:
  - **Dent and Senior (1955)** — the classic cystine–pH solubility curve.
  - **Zucker diabetic fatty rat** — renal steatosis ↓ NHE3.
  - **Malpighian tubules** — Drosophila model of uric acid stones.
- Classic traps:
  - **Most uric acid stone formers have NORMAL urinary uric acid** — low pH is the problem.
  - **Idiopathic uric acid stones: normal uric acid, low pH; hyperuricosuric calcium stones: high uric acid, normal pH.**
  - **No natural inhibitor exists for uric acid or cystine.**
  - **Type B heterozygotes excrete more cystine but rarely form stones.**
  - **Cystine formers lose renal function more than other stone formers.**
  - **Animal protein increases uric acid stone risk, not calcium oxalate risk, in matched diets.**

## ▸ Source flags

- **Unit error**: "normal daily uric acid excretion, which averages 500 to 600 **mg/L**" — daily excretion is **500–600 mg/day**. Corrected.
- **Typographical error**: "the incidence of uric acid nephrolithiasis **in** increasing" = **is** increasing.
- **Internal inconsistency — solubility limit of undissociated uric acid**: "≈ **96 mg/L**" (text) versus "≈ **100 mg/L**" (Fig. 95.13 legend). Compatible approximations; both carried.
- **Statistical inconsistency** (visceral fat study): low urine pH reported as "OR 5.87, 95% CI 1.59–58.3, **P = 0.26**" — a CI excluding 1 is incompatible with P = 0.26; probably **P = 0.026**. Finding described without the figure.
- **Symbol corruption**: "BMI $27 kg/m2 … pH ,5.5" = **BMI ≥ 27 kg/m², pH ≤ 5.5**; "(!6)" = **< 6**; "bo,*AT" = **b⁰,⁺AT**; "-mol/g" = **μmol/g**; "eGFR !60" = **< 60**; "pK a" split = **pKa**; "Na*,H*" = **Na⁺/H⁺**; "’-ketoglutarate" = **α-ketoglutarate**.
- **Frequency discrepancy**: uric acid "8–10%" (text) versus 7% (Table 95.1) — tabulated in Instalment 4.
- **Cystine incidence**: "1 in 1000 to 1 in 17,000" — an unusually wide range as printed; reproduced as given.
