# Instalment 4 — Calcium Absorption and Vitamin D–Parathyroid Axis, Renal Calcium Handling (Claudins, CaSR, TRPV5), Phosphorus, Magnesium, Oxalate Absorption and Synthesis, Key Points: Mineral Metabolism, Classification of Nephrolithiasis (Tables 95.1 and 95.2)

## MINERAL METABOLISM

### Calcium

##### Intestinal absorption

- **30–40% of dietary calcium is absorbed**, mostly in the **small intestine**; only **about 10% in the colon**.
- **Intestinal adaptation**: **fractional absorption rises when intake is low and falls when intake is high**.

| Pathway | Transcellular | Paracellular |
|---|---|---|
| Saturable? | **Saturable** | **Non-saturable** |
| Regulation | **Vitamin D (calcitriol)-dependent** — calcitriol increases brush-border calcium permeability | Unregulated, concentration-driven |
| Dominates when | **Calcium intake is low**; **down-regulated by a calcium-replete diet** | **Calcium intake is high** |

- **Divided doses are absorbed better than one large dose** (because of the saturable component).
- **Net absorption**: a small amount of calcium is **secreted into the lumen**, so of a typical **600–1200 mg/day intake, 100–300 mg is absorbed net**.
- **Calcium is absorbed only in ionic form**; luminal **complexing agents reduce absorption** — **phosphate, citrate, oxalate, sulphate, fatty acids**.
  - **Calcium phosphate complexation is pH-dependent (pK ≈ 6.1)** — **high luminal pH favours it**.
  - **Calcium oxalate complexation is less pH-dependent and less reversible** — hence **an oxalate-rich diet reduces calcium absorption**.

##### The vitamin D–parathyroid axis

- **1,25(OH)2D3 (calcitriol) is the most potent stimulator of intestinal calcium absorption.**
- **Synthesis sequence**: **7-dehydrocholesterol (skin) → sunlight → previtamin D3 → liver 25-hydroxylation → 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 → proximal renal tubule 1α-hydroxylation → 1,25(OH)2D3**.
- **1α-hydroxylase** (mitochondrial, proximal tubule) is **stimulated by parathyroid hormone (PTH) and by hypophosphataemia**.
- **Loop**: **low serum calcium → PTH ↑ → 1α-hydroxylase ↑ → calcitriol → vitamin D receptor (VDR) in brush border → gut calcium absorption ↑**; calcitriol with PTH also **recruits osteoclasts**; rising serum calcium then **suppresses PTH and calcitriol**, and calcitriol **inhibits PTH synthesis** by up-regulating parathyroid **VDR and CaSR**.
- **PTH**:
  - **84-amino-acid** peptide cleaved from **prepro-PTH**; only mature PTH is secreted.
  - **Most potent secretory stimulus: fall in serum calcium**, sensed by the **G-protein-coupled CaSR**, which also regulates renal tubular calcium reabsorption.
  - Acts via **cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) and phospholipase C**.
  - **Bone**: osteoclastic calcium and phosphorus release. **Kidney**: **↑ calcium reabsorption, ↓ phosphate reabsorption, ↑ calcitriol**. **Gut: no direct effect** — only via calcitriol.

##### Renal calcium handling

| Segment | Share of filtered calcium reabsorbed | Route | Key molecules |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Proximal tubule** | **60–65%** (the largest share) | **Paracellular** | Tight-junction proteins **occludin, junctional adhesion molecules, claudins**; **claudin-2** mediates cation permeation |
| **Thick ascending limb (TAL)** | **25–30%** | **Paracellular**, driven by a **lumen-positive transepithelial voltage** (from apical potassium secretion, basolateral chloride exit and the transepithelial sodium chloride gradient) | **Claudin-16/claudin-19 complex** forms the cation channel; **CaSR → claudin-14**, which **blocks** the 16/19 channel |
| **Distal convoluted tubule (DCT)** | **8–10%** (fine regulation) | **Transcellular** | **Transient receptor potential vanilloid 5 (TRPV5)** apical entry (highly calcium-selective) → **calbindin-D28k** chaperone → basolateral exit |

- **Only 1–3% of filtered calcium is excreted.**
- **TRPV5 is regulated** by gene expression, feedback inhibition and membrane trafficking; **TRPV5 knockout mice develop severe hypercalciuria**, compensated by **calcitriol-driven intestinal hyperabsorption**.

**Concept —** *Calcium balance is a **three-organ loop (gut, bone, kidney)** closed by PTH and calcitriol: bulk renal reabsorption is **paracellular and passive (proximal tubule, TAL)**, fine control is **transcellular (TRPV5 in the DCT)** — and a defect at any organ can produce hypercalciuria, which is why hypercalciuria was later classified by organ.*

### Phosphorus

- **Absorption is saturable (transcellular) at low phosphate concentrations (1–3 mmol/L) and non-saturable (paracellular) at higher levels.**
- **About 60% of dietary phosphate is absorbed**; active uptake is **sodium-dependent and calcitriol-regulated**.
- **Strongly pH-dependent**: **low luminal pH reduces, high pH enhances** phosphate absorption.
- **About 65% of absorbed phosphate is excreted renally**, the rest by the gut.
- **Kidney reabsorbs 80–90% of filtered phosphate**, excreting **10–20%**.
- **Primary regulator: PTH — inhibits tubular phosphate reabsorption.**

### Magnesium

- Absorbed by **passive diffusion (predominant) and active transport**, in large and small intestine — **mainly the distal small intestine**.
- **Hormonal regulation mainly via vitamin D.**

### Oxalate

| Contrast | Calcium | Oxalate |
|---|---|---|
| **Fraction of intake absorbed** | **30–40%** | **6–14%** (range between individuals **10–72%**) |
| **Main site** | Small intestine (colon ~10%) | **Throughout the gut — roughly half small intestine, half colon** |
| **Intake–absorption relationship** | Adaptive (fractional absorption falls with high intake) | **Curvilinear** — **higher fractional absorption at low intake** |

- **Urinary oxalate as a surrogate for absorption** is valid only if ingestion–excretion is linear and absorbed oxalate is not taken up, metabolised or re-secreted — assumptions that **do not strictly hold**.
- **Whether stone formers absorb more oxalate than non-formers is unclear.**
- **Small-bowel disease or resection with an intact colon → markedly increased oxalate absorption.**
- **Transport**: **paracellular (passive)** and **transcellular (largely active, carrier-mediated)**.
  - **Solute-linked carrier family 26 (SLC26) anion exchangers** — **SLC26A1 and SLC26A6** mediate **intestinal oxalate *secretion*** (loss of function would therefore raise net absorption); shown in **rodents**, **not yet in humans**.
- **Modifiers of absorption**:
  - **Oxalate-binding cations (calcium, magnesium)** — **co-ingestion of calcium with oxalate forms insoluble calcium oxalate**, limiting free oxalate.
  - **Oxalate-degrading bacteria** — **Oxalobacter formigenes** uses oxalate as its **energy source**; in colonised rats it also **stimulates colonic secretion of endogenous oxalate**, lowering urinary oxalate by more than luminal degradation alone.
  - O. formigenes is the **most potent** degrader but **other species also degrade oxalate**; in healthy people it sits within a **cooperative bacterial network**. **Its net contribution to stone risk is not fully understood.**
- **Absorbed oxalate is almost entirely excreted in urine.**
- **Sources of urinary oxalate**: **about half dietary** (varying with calcium and oxalate intake), the rest **endogenous hepatic synthesis**.
- **Liver = primary site of synthesis**; the **primary hyperoxaluria (PH)** enzyme defects are hepatic and increase **glyoxylate, the direct precursor of oxalate**.
- **Endogenous sources**:
  - **Ascorbic acid — the largest endogenous source**, up to **40% of urinary oxalate**, via **non-enzymatic** conversion to **2,3-diketogulonic acid** → oxalate plus threosone or erythrulose.
  - **Hydroxyproline** (collagen breakdown; **gelatin**) — about **15% of endogenous synthesis**.
- **Renal handling**: **86–98% of plasma oxalate is ultrafilterable**; **tubular secretion (probably proximal)** is supported by animal and clinical data; **SLC26A6** is implicated; the candidate **sulphate anion transporter-1 (SAT1, SLC26A1)** showed **no correlation** with hyperoxaluria in rats. **Whether stone formers handle oxalate differently is unknown.**

**Concept —** *Calcium and oxalate are handled in mirror image: calcium is **well absorbed and tightly regulated**, oxalate is **poorly absorbed, unregulated and fully excreted** — so urinary oxalate simply tracks what the gut lets through (free luminal oxalate, colonic exposure, bacterial degradation) plus hepatic synthesis.*

<div class="kp" markdown="1">
**KEY POINTS: MINERAL METABOLISM**

- **Calcium absorption is primarily small-intestinal** and depends on intake.
- **1,25-Dihydroxyvitamin D3 is the most potent stimulator** of intestinal calcium absorption.
- **PTH stimulates 1α-hydroxylase** in the proximal tubule to convert 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 to 1,25(OH)2D3.
- **PTH enhances renal calcium reabsorption and phosphate excretion.**
- **Intestinal oxalate absorption is modulated by luminal calcium, magnesium and oxalate-degrading bacteria.**

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF UPPER URINARY TRACT CALCULI

### Classification of Nephrolithiasis

- **Drug-related stones** (triamterene, silica, indinavir, ephedrine) are **uncommon and usually preventable**.
- **Classification is by underlying metabolic or environmental abnormality** (Table 95.2); **calcium stones arise from hypercalciuria, hypocitraturia, hyperuricosuria and hyperoxaluria**, alone or combined.
- **Non-calcium stones form in unique settings**:
  - **Uric acid — only in acid urine.**
  - **Cystine — impaired renal cystine reabsorption.**
  - **Infection (struvite) — alkaline urine from urease-producing bacteria.**
- **Stone composition alone can direct therapy for some stones (e.g. cystine)**, but **calcium stones need metabolic and environmental evaluation** because causes are multiple. **Molecular and genetic insights may reshape treatment.**

- **Purpose**: the **metabolic work-up map** — each 24-hour urine abnormality points to a condition and a defect.

- **Trap**: **thiazides cause hypocitraturia via hypokalaemia** — the stone-preventing drug can itself lower citrate.
- **Trap**: **primary hyperparathyroidism explains only 3–5%** of calcium stone formers.

## ▸ The numbers to lock in

- **Calcium absorbed 30–40%**; colon ~10%; **net 100–300 mg of 600–1200 mg/day**.
- **Calcium phosphate complexation pK ≈ 6.1.**
- **Calcitriol = most potent stimulator of gut calcium absorption.**
- **1α-hydroxylase stimulated by PTH and hypophosphataemia.**
- **PTH: 84 amino acids; no direct gut effect.**
- **Renal calcium: excreted 1–3%; proximal 60–65%, TAL 25–30%, DCT 8–10% (transcellular).**
- **Phosphate: gut absorbs ~60%; kidney reabsorbs 80–90%; saturable at 1–3 mmol/L.**
- **Oxalate absorbed 6–14% (10–72% individual range)**; **≈ half of urinary oxalate dietary**.
- **Ascorbate: largest endogenous oxalate source (up to 40% urinary oxalate)**; **hydroxyproline ≈ 15%**.
- **Oxalate 86–98% ultrafilterable.**
- **Calcium in ≈ 80% of stones; calcium oxalate 60%, mixed calcium oxalate–apatite 20%, brushite 2%, uric acid 7%, struvite 7%, cystine 1–3%.**
- **Table 95.2 headline prevalences**: absorptive 20–40%; renal 5–8%; resorptive 3–5%; hyperuricosuric 10–40%; hypocitraturic 10–50%; hyperoxaluric 2–15%; hypomagnesiuric 5–10%; low pH 15–30%; low volume 10–50%; infection 1–5%; cystinuria < 1%; no abnormality < 3%.
- Classic traps:
  - **PTH does not act directly on the gut.**
  - **Proximal tubule reabsorbs the most calcium, but the DCT (TRPV5) does the fine tuning.**
  - **CaSR activation reduces TAL calcium reabsorption** (via claudin-14) — it raises urinary calcium.
  - **SLC26A6 mediates oxalate *secretion*** into the gut — loss increases net absorption.
  - **Uric acid stones need acid urine; struvite needs alkaline urine.**
