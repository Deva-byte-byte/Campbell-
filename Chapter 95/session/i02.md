## EPIDEMIOLOGY OF RENAL CALCULI (continued)

### Occupation

- **Heat exposure and dehydration** are occupational risk factors:
  - Royal Navy — **cooks and engine-room personnel** had the **highest stone rates**.
  - **Steelworkers in hot areas** — markedly higher stone incidence than those at normal temperatures, with **low urine volume and hypocitraturia**.
  - **Glass-plant workers exposed to heat** — **lower urine volume and pH**, **higher uric acid and specific gravity**, hence **high uric acid saturation**; stone formers among them had a **strikingly high proportion of uric acid stones**.
- **Sedentary occupations (managerial, professional)** carry **increased risk**, reason unclear:
  - consistent with higher risk in **affluent individuals and societies** (richer diet and lifestyle);
  - alternatively, **inactivity increases bone turnover and urinary calcium** — the same mechanism seen in **astronauts under weightlessness**, where **exercise** to limit bone demineralisation is the best prevention.
- **Restricted bathroom access** (**taxi drivers, operating-room staff**) increases risk.

| Occupational mechanism | Examples | Urinary consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Heat / dehydration** | Cooks, engine rooms, steel and glass workers | **Low volume, low pH, hypocitraturia → uric acid stones** |
| **Sedentary** | Managerial, professional; astronauts (weightlessness) | **Bone turnover → hypercalciuria** |
| **Restricted voiding / fluid access** | Taxi drivers, operating-room staff | Low volume |

### Obesity, Diabetes, and Metabolic Syndrome

- **Stone prevalence and incident risk rise with weight and body mass index (BMI)** in both sexes — **the association is stronger in females** (two large prospective cohorts).
- **Obesity and weight gain are independent risk factors**, **not explained by diet alone**; confirmed in further cohorts and claims data.
- **Women's Health Initiative Observational Study** (postmenopausal females without prior stones): **higher BMI, less physical activity and energy intake > 2200 kcal/day** each independently raised incident risk — supporting **diet and exercise components of weight-loss counselling** in prevention.
- **Metabolic syndrome** (visceral obesity with hyperlipidaemia, hypertriglyceridaemia, hyperglycaemia and/or hypertension) increases stone risk, with **risk rising with the number of metabolic traits**; it is also a precursor of **type 2 diabetes mellitus**.
- **Diabetes and stones — a bidirectional, sex-specific link**:
  - **Diabetes predicts incident stones in females but not males** (after adjusting for BMI, diet and thiazides).
  - **A stone history predicts incident diabetes in both sexes**; stone formers are about **1.3 times more likely** to be diagnosed with diabetes within 5 years.
  - In asymptomatic stones followed by CT, **fasting glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL predicted stone growth** — suggesting **glycaemic control may reduce recurrence**.
- **Unifying mechanism: insulin resistance**:
  - **Obesity and insulin resistance → low urine pH → uric acid stones.**
  - **Hyperinsulinaemia → hypercalciuria → calcium stones.**
  - **Overweight (BMI 25–30 kg/m²)** is already associated with **lower urine pH** and **higher urinary sodium, uric acid and calcium**.
  - **Abnormalities are harder to correct with rising BMI**: response to **potassium citrate** for low pH and hypocitraturia falls as BMI rises, requiring **more dose adjustment**.
- **Hepatic link to oxalate**: in a mouse model, **non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)** **down-regulated alanine-glyoxylate aminotransferase (AGXT)** — the enzyme deficient in **primary hyperoxaluria type 1 (PH1)** — increasing oxalate production; a proposed explanation for **calcium oxalate stones in diabetes, obesity and metabolic syndrome**.
- **Higher BMI → more urinary oxalate, uric acid, sodium and phosphorus.**

| Obesity-associated stone | Principal driver |
|---|---|
| **Calcium oxalate** | **Increased excretion of promoters** (oxalate, calcium, sodium, uric acid) |
| **Uric acid** | **Low urinary pH** |

**Concept —** *Obesity, diabetes and metabolic syndrome converge on **insulin resistance**, which lowers urine pH (uric acid stones) and raises promoter excretion (calcium oxalate stones) — so the stone is a urinary marker of a systemic metabolic state.*

### Cardiovascular Disease

- **Hypertension**: **meta-analysis** shows stone formers have a **significantly increased risk of hypertension**, **stronger in females**.
  - Proposed shared dietary exposures: **calcium, sodium, potassium**.
  - Hypertensive individuals have **higher urinary calcium, uric acid, oxalate and calcium oxalate supersaturation**; hypertensive stone formers excrete **about 25 mg/day more calcium** than normotensive stone formers.
- **Heart disease — association, causation unproven**:
  - Stone history associated with **higher incidence of myocardial infarction (MI)**, independent of **chronic kidney disease (CKD)**.
  - Stone history associated with **subclinical carotid atherosclerosis** in young people.
  - In three large prospective cohorts, stones predicted **heart disease in both female cohorts but not the male cohort**.
  - Large population cohort (> 3 million): stone formers had **higher risk of acute MI and stroke**, **greater in younger individuals (< 50 years) and in females**.
- **Candidate mechanisms**:
  - **Dyslipidaemia** (high total cholesterol, high triglycerides, low **high-density lipoprotein (HDL)**) alters urine chemistry towards stone formation.
  - **Calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR)** dysfunction — a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed in **kidney and vasculature** — a molecular bridge.
  - **Oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction and inflammation**.

### Chronic Kidney Disease

- **Stone history is associated with CKD** after controlling for confounders (case–control study).
- Stone formers, **especially with BMI ≥ 27 kg/m²**, have a **lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)** and are **nearly twice as likely to have stage III CKD**.
- **Prospective cohorts** (insured, predominantly White) confirm **higher incident CKD**.
- **Stones associated with CKD and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) requiring dialysis — accounted for largely by females**; **no association in males**.
- **Symptomatic stone formers have increased ESRD risk**, possibly from **repeated obstruction**.
- **Causal versus shared-risk-factor relationship remains unresolved.**

**Concept —** *Across hypertension, cardiovascular disease and CKD the pattern repeats: the **association is real, stronger in females, and not proven causal** — stones and these diseases probably share a metabolic soil.*

### Water

- **Fluid intake is inversely related to incident stones** (two large observational studies).
- **Landmark trial — Borghi (1996)**, randomised trial in **first-time idiopathic calcium stone formers**: high fluid intake raised urine volume and cut **recurrence to 12% versus 27%** in controls given no advice.
- **Water hardness** (calcium carbonate content):
  - Some studies found **fewer stones in "hard"-water regions**; others found **no difference**.
  - Hardness correlates with **urinary calcium and citrate**, but **not with stone episodes**.
  - **Systematic review (1989–2018)**: hard water and mineral water raise calcium intake (and potentially urinary calcium), yet the **net effect is reduced calcium stone formation**, putatively from **high magnesium and bicarbonate** — but studies were **heterogeneous, no meta-analysis**, so **interpret with caution**.

| Hard water — opposing effects | Direction |
|---|---|
| **High calcium content** | **Raises** urinary calcium (pro-stone) |
| **High magnesium and bicarbonate** | **Inhibit** / raise citrate (anti-stone) — **net effect probably protective** |

**Concept —** *Volume is the one universally effective modifier — proven by randomised trial — whereas water composition is a weak, conflicting and poorly evidenced influence.*

**KEY POINTS: EPIDEMIOLOGY**

- **Upper-tract stones are commoner in males**, but **the gap is narrowing**.
- **White individuals have the highest incidence** compared with Asian, Hispanic and Black individuals.
- **Geographic variability**, with **highest prevalence in the Southeast** (US).
- **Risk correlates with weight and BMI.**
- Stones are associated with **diabetes, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease and CKD**, **causation unproven**.

## ▸ The numbers to lock in

- **Energy intake > 2200 kcal/day** — independent stone risk (Women's Health Initiative).
- **Overweight BMI 25–30 kg/m²** already lowers urine pH.
- **BMI ≥ 27 kg/m²** — stone formers with lower eGFR, ~2× stage III CKD.
- **Fasting glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL** — stone growth.
- **Stone → diabetes ≈ 1.3-fold within 5 years.**
- **Hypertensive stone formers: +25 mg/day urinary calcium.**
- **Landmark: Borghi 1996 fluid RCT — recurrence 12% vs 27%.**
- **Diabetes → stones: females only; stones → diabetes: both sexes.**
- **Stones ↔ CKD/ESRD and ↔ heart disease: female-driven.**
- **CV risk greatest < 50 years and in females.**
- Classic traps:
  - **Association ≠ causation** for cardiovascular disease and CKD.
  - **Hard water is not proven harmful** — net effect probably protective.
  - **Obesity and calcium oxalate** = promoter excretion; **obesity and uric acid** = low pH.
  - **NASH → AGXT down-regulation** links fatty liver to oxalate (the PH1 enzyme).

Findings retained; attribution unclear.
