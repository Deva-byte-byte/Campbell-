## URINARY LITHIASIS — CHAPTER OPENING

- **Stone disease is ancient but has changed its site and its sex**: with Westernisation, stone formation has **migrated from the lower to the upper urinary tract**, and a disease that historically favoured males is becoming **increasingly sex-neutral**.
- **Minimally invasive and non-invasive techniques** have transformed stone *removal*, but **surgery does not alter the course of the disease** — the stone is removed, the tendency to form stones is not.
- **Cost**: annual United States (US) expenditure on insured patients with nephrolithiasis **exceeded $10 billion in 2012**, a **nearly fivefold rise since 2000**.
- Because **recurrence is frequent**, a **medical prophylactic programme** is desirable, and that requires understanding of cause, epidemiology and pathogenesis.

**Concept —** *Stone treatment is two separate problems: **removal is surgical, recurrence is metabolic**, and the rest of this chapter exists to explain the metabolic half.*

## EPIDEMIOLOGY OF RENAL CALCULI

### Prevalence and Incidence

- **Lifetime prevalence 1–15%**, varying with **age, sex, race and geography**.
- **Regional prevalence**: **North America 7–13%**, **Europe 5–9%**, **Asia 1–5%**.
- **National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)**: **linear rise in US adult prevalence 1976–2010**; a transient dip (2007–2008 to 2011–2012) was followed by renewed rise to **about 10% in 2015–2016**; **age-standardised prevalence 2007–2016 ≈ 9.3%**.
- **12-month incidence (2015–2018) ≈ 2.1%**, about **2000 stones per 100,000 adults**.
- **The rise is global** (Europe, Japan, US) and **partly a detection artefact** — more **computed tomography (CT)** finds more **asymptomatic calculi**.

### Sex

- **Historically male-predominant**: by inpatient, outpatient and emergency-department indicators, males were affected **two to three times** more often than females.
- **The gap is narrowing**:
  - US inpatient discharges 1997–2002: **female discharges rose, male discharges fell**; the **male-to-female ratio fell from 1.7 to 1.3**.
  - NHANES 2007–2018: **prevalence rose in females, unchanged in males** — persisting after adjustment for sociodemographic and dietary factors.
  - Population data 1997–2012: **female incidence rose about 15% per 5 years; male incidence stable**; the **steepest female rise was in 10- to 19-year-olds**.
- **Females carry more stone-related morbidity** (retrospective data): more **positive urine cultures, nephrostomy placement, intensive care admission and longer stays**.

### Race and Ethnicity

- **NHANES 2007–2016 prevalence by group** (highest to lowest): **non-Hispanic White > Hispanic > non-Hispanic Black > non-Hispanic Asian**.
- **Trend**: prevalence **rose in White and (slightly) Hispanic** groups; **stable in Black and Asian** groups.
- **Sex ratio varies by race** (table); rising incidence was **greater in Black females than Black males**.
- **Race is a social construct, not a genetic proxy**: differences reflect **genetics plus cultural and sociodemographic factors**.

### Age

- **Paediatric stones are rising at 5–10% per year**; historically uncommon under age 20.
- **Paediatric incidence peaks in female adolescents** — the **reverse of the adult sex pattern**; young females are also more often **hospitalised**.
- Population data (Olmsted County, 1984–2008): overall paediatric rise **about 4% per year**, **about 6% per year in 12- to 17-year-olds**.
- **Proposed drivers of the paediatric rise**: **obesity and metabolic syndrome** reaching younger generations, or changes in the **exposome** rather than genetics or comorbidity alone.
- **Adult incidence peaks in the fourth to sixth decades**; **prevalence rises with age in both sexes** — in males highest over 80 years.
- **Females have lower prevalence than males at every age except 20–39 years**, where female prevalence is higher.
- Recent NHANES: female prevalence **rose only in those under 60**; **no rise in females over 60**.
- **Bimodal female distribution**: a **second peak in the sixth decade** at **menopause**, attributed to **loss of oestrogen**.
  - **Oestrogen is protective** — it **enhances renal calcium reabsorption** and **reduces bone resorption**.
  - Females have **lower urinary calcium and calcium oxalate and brushite saturation** than males **until beyond age 50**; **oestrogen-treated postmenopausal females** have lower urinary calcium and saturation than untreated ones.

**Concept —** *Age and sex patterns are largely **hormonal and metabolic**: oestrogen holds urinary calcium down until menopause, while obesity and metabolic syndrome are pulling stone disease into younger, and increasingly female, populations.*

### Geography and Climate

- **Prevalence follows environmental risk**: highest in **hot, arid or dry climates** (mountains, desert, tropics), although **genetic and dietary factors may outweigh geography**.
- **US veteran data**: **calcium oxalate stones highest in the Southeast**, **uric acid stones highest in the East**.
- **Prevalence rises from north to south and from west to east**, **highest in the Southeast** (the "**stone belt**"); **ambient temperature and sunlight are independent predictors**.
- **Seasonal variation**: **peak in summer (July–September)**, **within 1–2 months of maximal mean temperature** — via **perspiration fluid losses** and possibly **sunlight-induced vitamin D**. Confirmed worldwide by **systematic review**.
- **Short latency**: once mean daily temperature exceeds **10 °C**, stone presentations rise **within the ensuing 10 days**.
- **Military translocation studies** (a natural experiment):
  - Soldiers in desert postings — **more renal colic in summer**.
  - Personnel deployed to Kuwait and Iraq — **mean time to stone formation ≈ 93 days**.
  - Soldiers transferred to the Persian Gulf **in summer** developed **raised urinary calcium within 10 days** (not in the cold season) — attributed to **sunlight-induced 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 [1,25(OH)2D3]**.
- **Heat response is mainly male**: prevalence rises more steeply per degree in males, and heat-related emergency presentation rises **more in males** and most at **40–69 years** (confounders: sunlight, occupation, hydration).
- **Global warming projections**: modelled temperature–prevalence relationships (**peaked/non-linear** in one data set, **linear** in another) predict **1–1.5 million additional lifetime cases by 2050** and a **northward expansion of the stone belt**; a further projection anticipates **about 10,000 additional emergency presentations between 2025 and 2089**.
- **Urban heat islands**: urbanisation, architecture and reduced vegetation make cities warmer — a proposed contributor, **not yet tested** against fluid intake, diet and socioeconomic status.

**Concept —** *Climate acts **indirectly** — heat drives **low urine volume** and sunlight drives **vitamin D-mediated hypercalciuria** — so geography is really a proxy for these two urinary effects, predominantly in males.*

## ▸ The numbers to lock in

- **Lifetime prevalence 1–15%**; North America 7–13%, Europe 5–9%, Asia 1–5%.
- **US prevalence ≈ 9–10%** (NHANES); **12-month incidence ≈ 2%**.
- **US cost > $10 billion (2012)**, ~fivefold since 2000.
- **Historical male-to-female ratio 2–3:1**; **inpatient ratio fell 1.7 → 1.3 (1997–2002)**.
- **White ≈ 2.3 male-to-female; Black ≈ 0.65** (female predominance).
- **Race prevalence order**: White > Hispanic > Black > Asian.
- **Paediatric incidence rising 5–10%/year; peak in adolescent females.**
- **Adult peak incidence: fourth to sixth decades.**
- **Female exception**: prevalence exceeds male only at **20–39 years**.
- **Female second peak: sixth decade (menopause, oestrogen loss).**
- **Seasonal peak July–September**, 1–2 months after peak temperature; **lag after hot day ≤ 10 days (threshold > 10 °C)**.
- **Deployment to desert → stones at mean ≈ 93 days.**
- **Highest US prevalence: Southeast ("stone belt")**; calcium oxalate Southeast, uric acid East.
- **Global warming: +1–1.5 million lifetime cases by 2050**; stone belt moves north.
- Classic traps:
  - "Stones are a male disease" — **the sex gap is narrowing**, and in **Black patients and adolescents females predominate**.
  - "Heat causes stones directly" — heat acts **indirectly via low urine volume and vitamin D**.
  - Rising prevalence is **partly a CT detection artefact**.
