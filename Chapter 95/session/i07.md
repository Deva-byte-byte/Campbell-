# Instalment 7 — Hypocitraturia (Actions of Citrate, Definition, Causes), Low Urine pH, Renal Tubular Acidosis (Normal Acidification, Types 1, 2 and 4, Genetics), Hypomagnesiuria

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF UPPER URINARY TRACT CALCULI (continued)

### Calcium Stones (continued)

#### Hypocitraturia

- **An important and correctable abnormality**: **isolated in up to 10%** of calcium stone formers; **combined with other abnormalities in 20–60%**.
- **Actions of citrate** (four):
  1. **Complexes calcium** → ↓ saturation of calcium salts.
  2. **Prevents spontaneous nucleation** of calcium oxalate.
  3. **Inhibits agglomeration and sedimentation** of calcium oxalate crystals, and **growth** of calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate crystals.
  4. **Enhances the inhibitory effect of Tamm-Horsfall glycoprotein** at normal levels.
- **Definition: urinary citrate < 320 mg/day.**
- **Acid–base status is the primary determinant of citrate excretion**: **metabolic acidosis → ↑ tubular citrate reabsorption and ↓ peritubular citrate synthesis → hypocitraturia**.
- **The defect is renal, not intestinal**:
  - Stone formers and controls have **similar serum citrate and filtered load**, but formers show **lower urinary citrate** and **higher tubular reabsorption**.
  - **Oral citrate is rapidly and almost completely absorbed** (96–98% within 3 hours) in both hypocitraturic formers and controls — so **absent overt bowel disease, gut malabsorption is not the cause**.

| Cause of hypocitraturia | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Distal RTA** | **Systemic acidosis** — **urine pH high (> 6.8 as quoted here)**, **high serum chloride, low serum bicarbonate and potassium**; confirmed by **failure to acidify after ammonium chloride load** |
| **Chronic diarrhoea** | **Intestinal alkali loss → systemic acidosis** |
| **Excess animal protein** | **Acid load**; high-protein, low-carbohydrate diets ↓ citrate and pH (low citrus, high animal protein) |
| **Thiazides** | **Hypokalaemia → intracellular acidosis** |
| **Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors** | Hypocitraturia **independent of systemic acidosis or hypokalaemia** — perhaps intracellular acidosis |
| **Strenuous exercise** | **Lactic acidosis** |
| **Isolated (idiopathic)** | Unrelated to acidosis |

- **Citrate rises with alkalosis** and with **PTH, oestrogen, magnesium, calcitonin and vitamin D**.

**Concept —** *Citrate excretion is a **readout of intracellular pH in the proximal tubule**: anything that acidifies the cell (systemic acidosis, hypokalaemia, acid diet) makes the tubule reclaim citrate — which is why alkali (potassium citrate) is the treatment.*

#### Low Urine pH

- **Below pH 5.5, undissociated uric acid predominates** → **uric acid and/or calcium stones**; calcium oxalate forms by **heterogeneous nucleation on uric acid crystals**.
- **Any disorder that lowers urine pH predisposes to stones.**
- **Chronic metabolic acidosis → low urine pH, hypercalciuria (↑ bone resorption and renal calcium leak) and hypocitraturia.**
- **Idiopathic low urine pH** (formerly **"gouty diathesis"**) — stone propensity with **low urine pH of unknown cause**, **with or without gouty arthritis**.

#### Renal Tubular Acidosis

- **RTA = metabolic acidosis from defective tubular hydrogen-ion secretion or bicarbonate reabsorption.** Three types: **1, 2 and 4**.
- **Type 1 (distal) matters most to urologists**: **commonest form** and **most stone-associated** (stones in **up to 70%**); **stone symptoms lead to the diagnosis in up to half** of cases.

##### Normal renal acidification

- **Bicarbonate is freely filtered**; the kidney must **reclaim ≈ 4500 mmol/day**, mainly **proximally**, and **excrete the daily acid load** (from carbohydrate, fat and protein metabolism and stool bicarbonate loss) **distally**.

| Governing contrast | Proximal tubule | Distal tubule / collecting duct |
|---|---|---|
| **Job** | **Reclaim filtered bicarbonate** (the bulk) | **Net acid excretion** (plus 5–10% of bicarbonate) |
| **Machinery** | **Carbonic anhydrase** generates H⁺ + HCO₃⁻; **apical Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger** (driven by **basolateral Na⁺,K⁺-ATPase**); **basolateral Na⁺/HCO₃⁻ co-transporter**; apical H⁺-ATPase; **luminal carbonic anhydrase** converts H₂CO₃ to H₂O + CO₂ | **α-Intercalated cells**: **apical H⁺-ATPase** and **H⁺,K⁺-ATPase**; **basolateral Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger (AE1)** |
| **Net effect** | **Bicarbonate absorbed without net H⁺ secretion or pH change** | **H⁺ excreted bound to buffers — titratable acid (mainly phosphate) and ammonia (as NH₄⁺)**; **1000:1 gradient → urine pH as low as 4.5** |
| **Luminal carbonic anhydrase** | **Present** | **Absent** — prevents rapid carbonic acid dissociation, helping maintain the gradient |

- **Both proximal and distal RTA produce hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis with inappropriately high urine pH**; the distinction is **acid excretion (distal, type 1)** versus **bicarbonate reabsorption (proximal, type 2)**.

##### Type 1 (Distal) Renal Tubular Acidosis

- **Collecting-duct failure to acidify despite systemic acidosis**: **hypokalaemic, hyperchloraemic, non-anion-gap metabolic acidosis**, **nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis**, **urine pH > 6.0**.
- **Incomplete distal RTA**: **cannot lower urine pH below 5.5 after an acid load**, but **no systemic acidosis** — **serum electrolytes normal**.
- **Stone type: calcium phosphate**, from **hypercalciuria, hypocitraturia and high urine pH**.
  - **Acidosis → bone demineralisation → secondary hyperparathyroidism → hypercalciuria.**
  - **Profound hypocitraturia — probably the most important factor** — from acidosis, abnormal tubular citrate transport, or citrate movement into mitochondria with intracellular acidosis.
- **Forms**: hereditary, idiopathic or acquired; **most sporadic**; **autosomal dominant and recessive** patterns exist.
  - **Inherited forms**: **growth retardation, nephrocalcinosis, calculi, hypokalaemic metabolic acidosis**.
  - **Autosomal recessive form**: **more severe, earlier onset**, with **learning disability and sensorineural hearing loss**.
  - **Children are one-third of cases** — vomiting, diarrhoea, failure to thrive, growth retardation.
  - **Secondary distal RTA**: **Sjögren syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus**, **obstructive uropathy, pyelonephritis, acute tubular necrosis, hyperparathyroidism, idiopathic hypercalciuria**.
- **Molecular basis — α-intercalated cell dysfunction** (Fig. 95.12):

| Gene | Protein | Inheritance | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SLC4A1** | **AE1 Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger** | **Mostly autosomal dominant**; complete or incomplete | **Hearing loss not usually a feature** |
| **ATP6V1B1** | **B1 subunit of H⁺-ATPase** | **Autosomal recessive** | Heterozygous B1 carriers may have a **hypofunctional pump → incomplete distal RTA** |
| **ATP6V0A4** | **A4 subunit of H⁺-ATPase** | **Autosomal recessive** | — |
| **CA2** | **Carbonic anhydrase II** (proximal tubule, loop, α-intercalated cells) | **Recessive** | **Mixed proximal–distal RTA**: bicarbonate wasting, **cannot acidify below pH 5.5, ↓ NH₄⁺ excretion** |

##### Type 2 (Proximal) Renal Tubular Acidosis

- **Defect in proximal bicarbonate reabsorption** — a **self-limiting** sequence:
  **proximal HCO₃⁻ loss → distal tubule overwhelmed → bicarbonaturia, high urine pH → plasma HCO₃⁻ falls → filtered HCO₃⁻ falls → distal capacity suffices → steady state**.
- **Usually part of a Fanconi-type generalised proximal defect** — loss of **glucose, protein (amino acids), uric acid and phosphate**.
- **Clinical**: **growth retardation and hypokalaemia in children**.

##### Type 4 Renal Tubular Acidosis

- **Chronic renal damage** — **interstitial disease, diabetic nephropathy**.
- **Low urine pH, decreased acid excretion**; **↓ GFR → hyperkalaemic, hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis** (urinary HCO₃⁻ loss, ↓ ammonium excretion).
- **Aldosterone resistance** is common → ↓ distal acidification and H⁺/K⁺ exchange → **↓ ammoniagenesis, worse hyperkalaemia**.
- **Can still acidify urine after an acid challenge.**
- **Stones uncommon** — **reduced calcium and uric acid excretion** from impaired renal function.
- **But low urine pH (from hyperkalaemia-impaired ammoniagenesis) predisposes to uric acid stones.**

| Feature | **Type 1 (distal)** | **Type 2 (proximal)** | **Type 4 (hyperkalaemic)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Defect** | **Distal H⁺ secretion** (α-intercalated cell) | **Proximal HCO₃⁻ reabsorption** | **Aldosterone deficiency/resistance**, ↓ ammoniagenesis |
| **Serum potassium** | **Low** | **Low** | **High** |
| **Urine pH** | **Always high (> 6.0); cannot go < 5.5** | **High initially, then < 5.5 at steady state** | **Low; can acidify** |
| **Serum HCO₃⁻** | Low (may be very low) | **15–18 mEq/L** | Low |
| **Urinary citrate** | **Profoundly low** | **Relatively normal** | — |
| **Stones** | **Common (up to 70%) — calcium phosphate**; nephrocalcinosis | **Uncommon** | **Uncommon — but uric acid if any** |
| **Associations** | Sjögren, lupus, obstruction, hypercalciuria; genetic (SLC4A1, ATP6V1B1, ATP6V0A4) | **Fanconi syndrome** | Diabetic nephropathy, interstitial disease |

**Concept —** *Stone risk in RTA follows **citrate and urine pH**: only distal (type 1) RTA combines **fixed alkaline urine with profound hypocitraturia and hypercalciuria**, the recipe for calcium phosphate stones; proximal RTA spares citrate, and type 4 lowers the stone load (except uric acid, via low pH).*

#### Hypomagnesiuria

- **Rare: isolated in < 1% of stone formers**; combined with other abnormalities in **6–11%**.
- **Mechanism**: magnesium **complexes oxalate and calcium salts**, so low magnesium means **less inhibition**; low urinary magnesium also tracks **low urinary citrate** (cause or effect unclear).
- **Causes**: **poor dietary intake**, **intestinal malabsorption from chronic diarrhoeal syndrome**.
- **Evidence is contradictory**:
  - Rat studies both **implicate and question** magnesium.
  - Hypomagnesiuric patients had **higher recurrence** in one study; others found **no difference** in magnesium excretion between formers and controls — possibly because **few formers are hypomagnesiuric**.
- **Magnesium raises urinary pH, citrate and magnesium and lowers calcium oxalate saturation** in vitro and in vivo, **but two randomised trials of magnesium oxide versus placebo or no treatment showed no clinical benefit**.

**Concept —** *Magnesium is a **physicochemically plausible but clinically unproven** inhibitor: favourable urine chemistry has not translated into fewer stones in randomised trials.*

## ▸ The numbers to lock in

- **Hypocitraturia < 320 mg/day; isolated ≤ 10%, combined 20–60%.**
- **Oral citrate absorption 96–98% within 3 hours.**
- **Uric acid predominates undissociated at pH < 5.5.**
- **Bicarbonate reclaimed ≈ 4500 mmol/day; distal tubule reclaims 5–10%.**
- **Distal acidification gradient 1000:1 → minimum urine pH 4.5.**
- **Distal RTA: stones up to 70%; stones reveal diagnosis in up to 50%; urine pH > 6.0; children one-third.**
- **Incomplete distal RTA: cannot reach pH < 5.5 after acid load; normal electrolytes.**
- **Proximal RTA steady state: HCO₃⁻ 15–18 mEq/L, urine pH < 5.5.**
- **Hypomagnesiuria isolated < 1%; combined 6–11%.**
- **Confirmatory test for distal RTA: failure to acidify after oral ammonium chloride (acid) load.**
- Eponyms:
  - **Fanconi syndrome** — generalised proximal defect accompanying type 2 RTA.
  - **Sjögren syndrome** — autoimmune cause of secondary distal RTA (with systemic lupus erythematosus).
- Classic traps:
  - **Type 1 = distal = stones (calcium phosphate); type 2 = proximal = no stones (citrate normal).**
  - **Type 4 is hyperkalaemic and can acidify urine.**
  - **Distal RTA: hypokalaemic, hyperchloraemic, NORMAL anion gap.**
  - **Thiazides and ACE inhibitors lower citrate.**
  - **Magnesium: good urine chemistry, negative randomised trials.**
  - **SLC4A1 distal RTA is dominant and spares hearing; H⁺-ATPase forms are recessive (hearing loss).**
