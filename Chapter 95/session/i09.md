## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF UPPER URINARY TRACT CALCULI (continued)

### Infection Stones

- **Composition**: **magnesium ammonium phosphate hexahydrate (struvite, MgNH₄PO₄·6H₂O)**, often with **carbonate apatite (Ca₁₀[PO₄]₆·CO₃)**.
- **History (sequence of discovery)**: a Swedish geologist found magnesium ammonium phosphate in **guano** and named it **struvite** after his mentor **H.C.G. von Struve** → bacteria proposed to **split urea** (early 20th century), with **Proteus vulgaris** isolated from a stone → a **bacterial enzyme** proposed to hydrolyse urea → **urease isolated** (from **Canavalia ensiformis**, the jack bean) → now established that **struvite forms only with urea-splitting infection**.

#### Pathophysiology

- **Urease is absent from sterile human urine — infection with a urease-producing organism is a prerequisite.**
- **Chemical cascade** (step → step):
  1. **Urea + H₂O → 2NH₃ + CO₂** (bacterial urease).
  2. **Alkaline urine (pH 7.2–8.0)** → **NH₃ + H₂O → NH₄⁺ + OH⁻** (pKa 9.0). Normally alkalinity would halt ammonium generation, but **urease keeps producing ammonia**, pushing pH higher.
  3. **CO₂ hydration → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (pKa 6.3) → H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ (pKa 10.2).**
  4. **Phosphate dissociation**: **H₂PO₄⁻ → H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻ (pKa 7.2) → H⁺ + PO₄³⁻ (pKa 12.4).**
  5. With physiological **magnesium** → **struvite**; with **calcium, phosphate and carbonate** → **carbonate apatite and hydroxyapatite** (Fig. 95.17).
- **Obstruction or stasis** may associate with or exacerbate infection stones → **growth can be rapid**.

#### Bacteriology

- **Enterobacteriaceae** make up most urease producers, but many **gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, some yeasts and Mycoplasma species** make urease (Table 95.3).
- **Commonest urease producers: Proteus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas and Staphylococcus**; **Proteus mirabilis is the organism most associated with infection stones**.
- **Escherichia coli** — a common uropathogen, but **only rare strains produce urease**.
- **Yet contemporary series culture E. coli (and sterile urine) from struvite patients surprisingly often**; E. coli matched Proteus as the commonest isolate in one international series; **higher struvite content predicts urease-producing organisms**.
- **Explanation: bladder urine and stone cultures are frequently discordant** → **voided urine culture is unreliable** for identifying the stone organism.

- **Purpose**: which organisms can make urease — i.e. which infections can build a struvite stone.
- **Pattern**: **Proteus is reliably urease-positive**; **Klebsiella and Pseudomonas only occasionally**; **Staphylococcus aureus usually, S. epidermidis occasionally**; **Ureaplasma** is a reliable, easily missed producer.
- **Trap**: **E. coli does not appear** — yet it is frequently cultured from struvite patients (discordant cultures, or rare urease-positive strains).

#### Pathogenesis

- **Beyond chemistry**, urease-producing bacteria **damage the urothelial mucosal layer** → ↑ bacterial colonisation and **crystal adherence**.
- **Ammonium from ureolysis alters the protective glycosaminoglycan layer** → ↑ bacterial adherence → **biofilm**.
- **Bacteria live within an exopolysaccharide matrix**; **struvite crystallises inside and around bacterial cells** → stone growth, **bacteria encased within the stone**.

#### Epidemiology

- **5–15% of all stones.**
- **Staghorn calculi are not mostly infection stones**: in a modern analysis **less than half were infection stones**; the majority were **metabolic**, most often **calcium phosphate**.
- **Incidence is falling worldwide** (Australia, Japan; in the US **in females**, little change in males) — attributed to **better diagnosis and treatment of urinary tract infection (UTI) in females** and **more effective stone surgery**.
- **Geography**: **highest in India and Pakistan** (poor sanitation → female UTI); **lowest in Canada, China, Argentina, Poland, Iraq and Italy**.
- **Female-to-male ratio ≈ 2:1.**
- **At-risk groups**: **elderly; premature infants and congenital urinary tract malformation; diabetes; urinary stasis from obstruction, urinary diversion or neurological disorders**.
- **Spinal cord injury** — risk of **infection and metabolic stones** (neurogenic dysfunction plus **immobilisation hypercalciuria**); **functionally complete cord transection → highest staghorn risk**.

**Concept —** *Infection stones need **one thing no human urine can supply — urease**; everything else (alkaline pH, ammonium, carbonate, phosphate) follows chemically, and biofilm then locks the bacteria inside the stone.*

### Miscellaneous Stones

#### Xanthine and Dihydroxyadenine Stones

- **Autosomal recessive purine disorders**: enzyme deficiency causes accumulation of the precursor **immediately proximal** to the block.

| Contrast | **Xanthine** | **2,8-Dihydroxyadenine (DHA)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Enzyme deficient** | **Xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH)** | **Adenine phosphoribosyltransferase (APRT)** |
| **Accumulating compound** | **Xanthine** | **Adenine → converted by XDH to DHA** |
| **Gene** | — | **APRT, chromosome 16q24**; > 40 mutations (**Met136Thr common in Japanese**; an intron-4 splice-site T insertion common in a French cohort) |
| **Radiology** | **Radiolucent** — mistaken for uric acid | **Radiolucent** — mistaken for uric acid |
| **Solubility** | **Highly insoluble** | **Extremely insoluble at any pH** |
| **Iatrogenic cause** | **High-dose allopurinol** (XDH inhibition) — e.g. **Lesch-Nyhan syndrome** (HGPRT deficiency) | — |
| **Treatment clue** | — | **Allopurinol prevents; febuxostat more effective at studied doses** |

- **DHA → stones, obstructive nephropathy or crystalline nephritis.**
- **Allopurinol rarely causes xanthine stones** — only **partial XDH inhibition**; serum uric acid rarely falls below **3 mg/dL**.
- **APRT deficiency can present in infancy** and may be confused with **HGPRT deficiency** because **DHA resembles uric acid**.
- **Rare Kidney Stone Consortium APRT-deficiency registry**: **allopurinol 400 mg/day and febuxostat 80 mg/day both lowered DHA excretion; febuxostat more effective**.

#### Ammonium Acid Urate Stones

- **≈ 1% of stones**; **endemic bladder stones in children in developing countries**.
- **Radiolucent.**
- **Associations**: **chronic diarrhoea, inflammatory bowel disease, ileostomy, laxative abuse, recurrent UTI, recurrent uric acid stones, morbid obesity**; a contemporary series added **prostate surgery with bladder neck contracture** and found **no laxative association**.
- **Ileostomy after colectomy**: **low urine volume, pH and sodium**, and **no hyperoxaluria** (no colon) → **ammonium acid urate and uric acid stones, not calcium oxalate**; inflammatory bowel disease with ileostomy gave the **highest ammonium urate content**.
- **Laxative abuse mechanism** (sequence): **gastrointestinal fluid loss → dehydration → intracellular acidosis → ↑ urinary ammonia**; **low urinary sodium** leaves urate to **complex with ammonium** → ammonium acid urate supersaturation.
- **Precipitation favoured at pH 6.2–6.3.**
- **Link with uric acid stones**: shared **low volume and low pH**; mixed uric acid–ammonium urate stones are usually **uric acid-predominant**; fluctuations in acidity, ammonium and sodium shift the balance between uric acid and sodium- or ammonium-bound urate.
- **Obesity (BMI ≥ 30)** was the **commonest feature** once bowel-disease patients were excluded; **BMI correlated with ammonium urate content** — consistent with the obesity–low urine pH link.

#### Matrix Stones

- **Protein suspensions can promote calcium stones**; **osteopontin and calprotectin** help build the matrix of calcium stones.
- **Predominantly matrix stones are rare**, **radiolucent**, and may be **mistaken for tumour or uric acid stones**; literature is **mostly case reports**.
- **Matrix is ≈ 2.5% of calcium stone dry weight**; **pure matrix stones contain > 65% protein**.
- **Composition ≈ two-thirds mucoprotein, one-third mucopolysaccharide**; matrix of crystalline stones closely resembles that of matrix stones.
- **Why they fail to calcify is unclear** — low urinary calcium was proposed, but **patients had normal urinary calcium**.
- **Dialysis patients**: proteinuria may contribute; matrix contains **microfibrillar protein and β₂-microglobulin**.
- **Proteus infection**: electron microscopy shows **fibrous net-like laminations with bacterial, cellular and crystalline material**; proteomics found proteins **mostly previously unreported**, the majority **inflammatory or defensive**.

#### Medication-Related Stones

- **Two routes**: **direct** (the drug or a metabolite **precipitates**) or **indirect** (the drug **alters urine chemistry** to favour metabolic stones).
- **Stones of the drug itself** (with excessive intake): **ephedrine, triamterene, guaifenesin, silicate, indinavir, ciprofloxacin**.
- **Calcium-stone promoters**: **loop diuretics** (furosemide, bumetanide) and **carbonic anhydrase inhibitors** (acetazolamide, topiramate, zonisamide).

##### Medications That Directly Promote Stone Formation

###### Antiretroviral Agents

- **Indinavir sulphate** — protease inhibitor that **raises CD4⁺ counts** and **lowers HIV RNA** in **human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)** infection and **acquired immunodeficiency syndrome**.
  - **Stone incidence 4–13%.**
  - **Pharmacokinetics**: peak plasma level **< 1 hour**; hepatic metabolism, mainly faecal elimination, but **about half excreted unchanged in urine**.
  - **pKa 5.5; solubility falls steeply with rising pH**: **≈ 0.300 mg/mL at pH 5, 0.035 at pH 6, 0.020 at pH 7** — **far more soluble below pH 5.5**.
  - **Standard dose → urinary concentration near solubility limit ≈ 3 hours after ingestion** at average volume and pH.
  - **Crystalluria in about two-thirds** of new users; stable at about a quarter of sediments after 2 weeks.
  - **Now infrequently used.**

<p class="tcap">Antiretroviral agents and stone formation — comparison</p>

| Agent | Class | Urinary excretion unchanged | Stone risk | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Indinavir** | Protease inhibitor | **≈ 50%** | **4–13% (highest)** | Least soluble at physiological pH; **acidification increases solubility**; now rarely used |
| **Ritonavir-boosted atazanavir** | Protease inhibitor | **≈ 7%** | **≈ 7% — highest of the newer agents** | Widely used |
| **Lopinavir–ritonavir** | Protease inhibitor | — | Reported | — |
| **Nelfinavir** | Protease inhibitor | **< 3%** | Lower | — |
| **Amprenavir** | Protease inhibitor | **< 3%** | Lower | — |

- **Rule: stone rate tracks urinary excretion of unchanged drug and its insolubility.**

###### Triamterene

- **Potassium-sparing diuretic** for hypertension; **≈ 0.4% of stones** in a large series, only a third largely triamterene.
- **Stone formers do not handle the drug differently** from controls; about half of all subjects exceed the solubility of the **sulphate metabolite**.
- **Tends to incorporate into existing stones or nidi rather than initiate stones** → rare in non-recurrent formers; **hospitalisation rates for stones were no different from hydrochlorothiazide users**.

###### Guaifenesin and Ephedrine

- **Large quantities → stones of their metabolites**; typically **over-the-counter cold remedies abused for ephedrine's stimulant effect**; drug-abuse history common.
- **Herbal ecstasy and ma huang** — ephedrine-containing preparations.
- **Tachyphylaxis → escalating doses**; toxicity includes **death, cardiomyopathy, stroke, hypertension, seizures**.

###### Silicate Stones

- **Silica** is ubiquitous (vegetables, grains, seafood, water) and easily excreted.
- **Silicate stones are extremely rare** — linked to **large intakes of silicate antacids (magnesium trisilicate)**.

##### Medications That Indirectly Promote Stone Formation

<p class="tcap">Drugs that alter urine chemistry — comparison</p>

| Drug / class | Urinary effect | Stone type |
|---|---|---|
| **Corticosteroids, vitamin D, phosphate-binding antacids** | **Hypercalciuria** | Calcium |
| **Thiazides** | **Intracellular acidosis → hypocitraturia** | Calcium |
| **Loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide)** | Inhibit **TAL sodium and calcium reabsorption → hypercalciuria** | **Calcium oxalate** — stones in **up to 64% of low-birth-weight infants on furosemide** |
| **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors** | **Metabolic acidosis with alkaline urine → hypocitraturia, hypercalciuria** | **Calcium phosphate** |
| **Laxatives (abuse)** | Diarrhoea, **low volume, low sodium, ammonia excretion, low pH** | **Ammonium acid urate** |
| **Cytotoxic agents** | **High cell turnover → uric acid excretion** | Uric acid |

<p class="tcap">Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors — comparison</p>

| Agent | Indication | Carbonic anhydrase inhibition | Stone evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acetazolamide** | Classic carbonic anhydrase inhibitor | **Strong**; blocks sodium bicarbonate reabsorption at multiple nephron sites | **Calcium phosphate** stones with chronic use |
| **Topiramate** | **Epilepsy, migraine prophylaxis**; off-label **obesity, neuropathic pain, alcoholism, type 2 diabetes, smoking and cocaine dependence** | **Several isoenzymes** | Label **1.2–1.5%** is an **underestimate**: about **one in ten** users reported stones and **one in five** had **asymptomatic stones on CT** (mean ≈ 43 months); **metabolic acidosis, high urine pH, low citrate, ↑ calcium phosphate saturation**; hypocitraturia **dose-dependent** and inversely related to duration |
| **Zonisamide** | Epilepsy (sulphonamide) | **Weak** | **Increased stone risk** |

**Concept —** *Drug stones follow two rules: for **direct** stones, risk equals **urinary excretion of an insoluble drug** (indinavir, atazanavir); for **indirect** stones, the drug simply **recreates a known metabolic phenotype** — carbonic anhydrase inhibitors mimic distal RTA (alkaline urine, low citrate, calcium phosphate), loop diuretics mimic hypercalciuria, laxatives mimic ileostomy.*

## ▸ The numbers to lock in

- **Struvite = MgNH₄PO₄·6H₂O; with carbonate apatite.**
- **Ureolysis urine pH 7.2–8.0.**
- **pKa: NH₄⁺ 9.0; H₂CO₃ 6.3; HCO₃⁻ 10.2; H₂PO₄⁻ 7.2; HPO₄²⁻ 12.4.**
- **Infection stones 5–15% of stones; female:male 2:1; incidence falling.**
- **Staghorn: majority metabolic (calcium phosphate), not infection.**
- **Commonest urease producers: Proteus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus; Proteus mirabilis commonest in stones.**
- **Highest staghorn risk: complete spinal cord transection.**
- **Ammonium acid urate ≈ 1%; precipitates at pH 6.2–6.3; obesity BMI ≥ 30 commonest feature.**
- **Matrix ≈ 2.5% of dry weight; pure matrix stones > 65% protein; two-thirds mucoprotein.**
- **Indinavir: stones 4–13%; ~50% excreted unchanged; pKa 5.5; solubility 0.300 / 0.035 / 0.020 mg/mL at pH 5 / 6 / 7.**
- **Atazanavir-ritonavir ≈ 7% stones; nelfinavir/amprenavir < 3% excreted.**
- **Triamterene ≈ 0.4% of stones.**
- **Furosemide: stones up to 64% of low-birth-weight infants (calcium oxalate).**
- **Topiramate: label 1.2–1.5% (underestimate); ~20% asymptomatic stones on CT.**
- **Allopurinol rarely drops serum urate < 3 mg/dL.**
- **DHA: APRT on 16q24; allopurinol 400 mg vs febuxostat 80 mg — febuxostat better.**
- Eponyms:
  - **Struvite (von Struve)** — magnesium ammonium phosphate.
  - **Proteus mirabilis** — archetypal urease producer.
  - **Lesch-Nyhan syndrome** — HGPRT deficiency; high-dose allopurinol → xanthine stones.
  - **Canavalia ensiformis** — source of the first isolated urease.
- Classic traps:
  - **E. coli is rarely urease-positive, yet often cultured** — voided urine and stone cultures are discordant.
  - **Most staghorns are metabolic, not struvite.**
  - **Radiolucent stones: uric acid, xanthine, DHA, ammonium acid urate, matrix, indinavir.**
  - **Ileostomy → ammonium urate / uric acid, not calcium oxalate.**
  - **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors give calcium phosphate stones in alkaline urine.**
  - **Triamterene incorporates into existing stones more than it initiates them.**
  - **Indinavir is more soluble in acid urine** — the reverse of the usual alkalinise-to-dissolve reflex.
