Chapter 95 — Urinary Lithiasis: Etiology, Epidemiology, and Pathogenesis

  • Stone disease is ancient but has changed its site and its sex: with Westernisation, stone formation has migrated from the lower to the upper urinary tract, and a disease that historically favoured males is becoming increasingly sex-neutral.
  • Minimally invasive and non-invasive techniques have transformed stone removal, but surgery does not alter the course of the disease — the stone is removed, the tendency to form stones is not.
  • Cost: annual United States (US) expenditure on insured patients with nephrolithiasis exceeded $10 billion in 2012, a nearly fivefold rise since 2000.
  • Because recurrence is frequent, a medical prophylactic programme is desirable, and that requires understanding of cause, epidemiology and pathogenesis.

Concept — Stone treatment is two separate problems: removal is surgical, recurrence is metabolic, and the rest of this chapter exists to explain the metabolic half.

EPIDEMIOLOGY OF RENAL CALCULI

Prevalence and Incidence

  • Lifetime prevalence 1–15%, varying with age, sex, race and geography.
  • Regional prevalence: North America 7–13%, Europe 5–9%, Asia 1–5%.
  • National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES): linear rise in US adult prevalence 1976–2010; a transient dip (2007–2008 to 2011–2012) was followed by renewed rise to about 10% in 2015–2016; age-standardised prevalence 2007–2016 ≈ 9.3%.
  • 12-month incidence (2015–2018) ≈ 2.1%, about 2000 stones per 100,000 adults.
  • The rise is global (Europe, Japan, US) and partly a detection artefact — more computed tomography (CT) finds more asymptomatic calculi.

Sex

  • Historically male-predominant: by inpatient, outpatient and emergency-department indicators, males were affected two to three times more often than females.
  • The gap is narrowing:
    • US inpatient discharges 1997–2002: female discharges rose, male discharges fell; the male-to-female ratio fell from 1.7 to 1.3.
    • NHANES 2007–2018: prevalence rose in females, unchanged in males — persisting after adjustment for sociodemographic and dietary factors.
    • Population data 1997–2012: female incidence rose about 15% per 5 years; male incidence stable; the steepest female rise was in 10- to 19-year-olds.
  • Females carry more stone-related morbidity (retrospective data): more positive urine cultures, nephrostomy placement, intensive care admission and longer stays.

Race and Ethnicity

  • NHANES 2007–2016 prevalence by group (highest to lowest): non-Hispanic White > Hispanic > non-Hispanic Black > non-Hispanic Asian.
  • Trend: prevalence rose in White and (slightly) Hispanic groups; stable in Black and Asian groups.
  • Sex ratio varies by race (table); rising incidence was greater in Black females than Black males.
  • Race is a social construct, not a genetic proxy: differences reflect genetics plus cultural and sociodemographic factors.

Age

  • Paediatric stones are rising at 5–10% per year; historically uncommon under age 20.
  • Paediatric incidence peaks in female adolescents — the reverse of the adult sex pattern; young females are also more often hospitalised.
  • Population data (Olmsted County, 1984–2008): overall paediatric rise about 4% per year, about 6% per year in 12- to 17-year-olds.
  • Proposed drivers of the paediatric rise: obesity and metabolic syndrome reaching younger generations, or changes in the exposome rather than genetics or comorbidity alone.
  • Adult incidence peaks in the fourth to sixth decades; prevalence rises with age in both sexes — in males highest over 80 years.
  • Females have lower prevalence than males at every age except 20–39 years, where female prevalence is higher.
  • Recent NHANES: female prevalence rose only in those under 60; no rise in females over 60.
  • Bimodal female distribution: a second peak in the sixth decade at menopause, attributed to loss of oestrogen.
    • Oestrogen is protective — it enhances renal calcium reabsorption and reduces bone resorption.
    • Females have lower urinary calcium and calcium oxalate and brushite saturation than males until beyond age 50; oestrogen-treated postmenopausal females have lower urinary calcium and saturation than untreated ones.

Concept — Age and sex patterns are largely hormonal and metabolic: oestrogen holds urinary calcium down until menopause, while obesity and metabolic syndrome are pulling stone disease into younger, and increasingly female, populations.

Geography and Climate

  • Prevalence follows environmental risk: highest in hot, arid or dry climates (mountains, desert, tropics), although genetic and dietary factors may outweigh geography.
  • US veteran data: calcium oxalate stones highest in the Southeast, uric acid stones highest in the East.
  • Prevalence rises from north to south and from west to east, highest in the Southeast (the "stone belt"); ambient temperature and sunlight are independent predictors.
  • Seasonal variation: peak in summer (July–September), within 1–2 months of maximal mean temperature — via perspiration fluid losses and possibly sunlight-induced vitamin D. Confirmed worldwide by systematic review.
  • Short latency: once mean daily temperature exceeds 10 °C, stone presentations rise within the ensuing 10 days.
  • Military translocation studies (a natural experiment):
    • Soldiers in desert postings — more renal colic in summer.
    • Personnel deployed to Kuwait and Iraq — mean time to stone formation ≈ 93 days.
    • Soldiers transferred to the Persian Gulf in summer developed raised urinary calcium within 10 days (not in the cold season) — attributed to sunlight-induced 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 [1,25(OH)2D3].
  • Heat response is mainly male: prevalence rises more steeply per degree in males, and heat-related emergency presentation rises more in males and most at 40–69 years (confounders: sunlight, occupation, hydration).
  • Global warming projections: modelled temperature–prevalence relationships (peaked/non-linear in one data set, linear in another) predict 1–1.5 million additional lifetime cases by 2050 and a northward expansion of the stone belt; a further projection anticipates about 10,000 additional emergency presentations between 2025 and 2089.
  • Urban heat islands: urbanisation, architecture and reduced vegetation make cities warmer — a proposed contributor, not yet tested against fluid intake, diet and socioeconomic status.

Concept — Climate acts indirectly — heat drives low urine volume and sunlight drives vitamin D-mediated hypercalciuria — so geography is really a proxy for these two urinary effects, predominantly in males.

Occupation

  • Heat exposure and dehydration are occupational risk factors:
    • Royal Navy — cooks and engine-room personnel had the highest stone rates.
    • Steelworkers in hot areas — markedly higher stone incidence than those at normal temperatures, with low urine volume and hypocitraturia.
    • Glass-plant workers exposed to heat — lower urine volume and pH, higher uric acid and specific gravity, hence high uric acid saturation; stone formers among them had a strikingly high proportion of uric acid stones.
  • Sedentary occupations (managerial, professional) carry increased risk, reason unclear:
    • consistent with higher risk in affluent individuals and societies (richer diet and lifestyle);
    • alternatively, inactivity increases bone turnover and urinary calcium — the same mechanism seen in astronauts under weightlessness, where exercise to limit bone demineralisation is the best prevention.
  • Restricted bathroom access (taxi drivers, operating-room staff) increases risk.

Obesity, Diabetes, and Metabolic Syndrome

  • Stone prevalence and incident risk rise with weight and body mass index (BMI) in both sexes — the association is stronger in females (two large prospective cohorts).
  • Obesity and weight gain are independent risk factors, not explained by diet alone; confirmed in further cohorts and claims data.
  • Women's Health Initiative Observational Study (postmenopausal females without prior stones): higher BMI, less physical activity and energy intake > 2200 kcal/day each independently raised incident risk — supporting diet and exercise components of weight-loss counselling in prevention.
  • Metabolic syndrome (visceral obesity with hyperlipidaemia, hypertriglyceridaemia, hyperglycaemia and/or hypertension) increases stone risk, with risk rising with the number of metabolic traits; it is also a precursor of type 2 diabetes mellitus.
  • Diabetes and stones — a bidirectional, sex-specific link:
    • Diabetes predicts incident stones in females but not males (after adjusting for BMI, diet and thiazides).
    • A stone history predicts incident diabetes in both sexes; stone formers are about 1.3 times more likely to be diagnosed with diabetes within 5 years.
    • In asymptomatic stones followed by CT, fasting glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL predicted stone growth — suggesting glycaemic control may reduce recurrence.
  • Unifying mechanism: insulin resistance:
    • Obesity and insulin resistance → low urine pH → uric acid stones.
    • Hyperinsulinaemia → hypercalciuria → calcium stones.
    • Overweight (BMI 25–30 kg/m²) is already associated with lower urine pH and higher urinary sodium, uric acid and calcium.
    • Abnormalities are harder to correct with rising BMI: response to potassium citrate for low pH and hypocitraturia falls as BMI rises, requiring more dose adjustment.
  • Hepatic link to oxalate: in a mouse model, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) down-regulated alanine-glyoxylate aminotransferase (AGXT) — the enzyme deficient in primary hyperoxaluria type 1 (PH1) — increasing oxalate production; a proposed explanation for calcium oxalate stones in diabetes, obesity and metabolic syndrome.
  • Higher BMI → more urinary oxalate, uric acid, sodium and phosphorus.

Cardiovascular Disease

  • Hypertension: meta-analysis shows stone formers have a significantly increased risk of hypertension, stronger in females.
    • Proposed shared dietary exposures: calcium, sodium, potassium.
    • Hypertensive individuals have higher urinary calcium, uric acid, oxalate and calcium oxalate supersaturation; hypertensive stone formers excrete about 25 mg/day more calcium than normotensive stone formers.
  • Heart disease — association, causation unproven:
    • Stone history associated with higher incidence of myocardial infarction (MI), independent of chronic kidney disease (CKD).
    • Stone history associated with subclinical carotid atherosclerosis in young people.
    • In three large prospective cohorts, stones predicted heart disease in both female cohorts but not the male cohort.
    • Large population cohort (> 3 million): stone formers had higher risk of acute MI and stroke, greater in younger individuals (< 50 years) and in females.
  • Candidate mechanisms:
    • Dyslipidaemia (high total cholesterol, high triglycerides, low high-density lipoprotein (HDL)) alters urine chemistry towards stone formation.
    • Calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) dysfunction — a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed in kidney and vasculature — a molecular bridge.
    • Oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction and inflammation.

Chronic Kidney Disease

  • Stone history is associated with CKD after controlling for confounders (case–control study).
  • Stone formers, especially with BMI ≥ 27 kg/m², have a lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and are nearly twice as likely to have stage III CKD.
  • Prospective cohorts (insured, predominantly White) confirm higher incident CKD.
  • Stones associated with CKD and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) requiring dialysis — accounted for largely by females; no association in males.
  • Symptomatic stone formers have increased ESRD risk, possibly from repeated obstruction.
  • Causal versus shared-risk-factor relationship remains unresolved.

Concept — Across hypertension, cardiovascular disease and CKD the pattern repeats: the association is real, stronger in females, and not proven causal — stones and these diseases probably share a metabolic soil.

Water

  • Fluid intake is inversely related to incident stones (two large observational studies).
  • Landmark trial — Borghi (1996), randomised trial in first-time idiopathic calcium stone formers: high fluid intake raised urine volume and cut recurrence to 12% versus 27% in controls given no advice.
  • Water hardness (calcium carbonate content):
    • Some studies found fewer stones in "hard"-water regions; others found no difference.
    • Hardness correlates with urinary calcium and citrate, but not with stone episodes.
    • Systematic review (1989–2018): hard water and mineral water raise calcium intake (and potentially urinary calcium), yet the net effect is reduced calcium stone formation, putatively from high magnesium and bicarbonate — but studies were heterogeneous, no meta-analysis, so interpret with caution.

KEY POINTS: EPIDEMIOLOGY

  • Upper-tract stones are commoner in males, but the gap is narrowing.
  • White individuals have the highest incidence compared with Asian, Hispanic and Black individuals.
  • Geographic variability, with highest prevalence in the Southeast (US).
  • Risk correlates with weight and BMI.
  • Stones are associated with diabetes, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease and CKD, causation unproven.

PHYSICOCHEMISTRY AND PATHOGENESIS

  • Stone formation is a cascade along the nephron: urine becomes supersaturated → ions precipitate as crystals or nuclei → crystals either pass in the urine or are retained at anchoring sites → growth and aggregation → stone.

State of Saturation

  • Concentration product (CP) — the product of the concentrations of a salt's pure chemical components (e.g. CP = [Na⁺][Cl⁻]).
  • Thermodynamic solubility product (Ksp) — the CP at saturation, where dissolved and crystalline phases are in equilibrium; adding crystals beyond this causes precipitation unless pH or temperature change.
  • Formation product (Kf) — the CP at which the salt can no longer be held in solution and crystals form spontaneously.
  • Urine holds salts above Ksp without precipitating because of inhibitors and other molecules — this supersaturated-but-stable state is metastable.
State (Fig. 95.3) Concentration product What happens
Undersaturated < Ksp Crystals never form; existing stones may dissolve
Metastable Ksp to Kf — where most urinary stone salts sit No spontaneous (homogeneous) nucleation; growth on existing crystals possible; heterogeneous nucleation may occur; inhibitors effective; matrix may be involved; the zone of therapeutic intervention
Unstable > Kf Spontaneous nucleation, growth and aggregation; inhibitors not generally effective
  • How crystals still form in metastable urine — three routes:
    1. Local CP exceeds Kf in parts of the nephron long enough for nucleation.
    2. Obstruction or stasis prolongs transit time.
    3. Heterogeneous nucleation — impurities adsorb crystal components in a lattice-like geometry, needing much less energy than homogeneous nucleation.
  • Estimating saturation:
    • Activity product ratio — accounts for urine pH and ionic activities of all major ion species and overall ionic strength.
    • EQUIL 2 — computer program, commonly used; gives the relative saturation ratio (RSR), also called concentration product ratio (CPR) = urine CP ÷ Ksp of the salt.
    • RSR < 1 → crystals dissolve; RSR > 1 → crystals form and grow.
  • Lowering RSR:
    • Reduce urinary concentration of stone components (calcium, oxalate), reduce filtered load, or increase tubular reabsorption.
    • Complexation — citrate binds calcium, cutting free ionic calcium.
    • pH manipulation works for phosphate (strongly pH-dependent) but not for oxalate — oxalic acid is a strong acid (pK ≈ 4), so physiological pH changes barely alter oxalate.

  • Calcium versus oxalate — which matters more?
    • Historical view: oxalate dominates — a rise in oxalate raised calcium oxalate saturation more than a rise in calcium, and calcium-driven saturation plateaued below Kf whereas oxalate-driven saturation did not.
    • Revised view: equal contributors — the relative effect depends on the stability constant chosen; with the accepted constant (2.746 × 10³, used in EQUIL 2) calcium and oxalate have equivalent effects (Fig. 95.4). Re-analysis with JESS confirmed equivalence, though dependence was less marked.
    • Therapeutic corollary: lowering either calcium or oxalate reduces calcium oxalate saturation.

Concept — Every prevention strategy is an attempt to push urine down out of the metastable zone — by dilution, by lowering calcium or oxalate (equally effective), by complexation with citrate, or (for phosphate and uric acid, never oxalate) by changing pH.

Nucleation and Crystal Growth, Aggregation, and Retention

  • Normal urine holds calcium oxalate at about four times its aqueous solubility.
  • Promoters of calcium oxalate supersaturation: low volume, low citrate; high calcium, oxalate, phosphate, uric acid.
  • With inhibitors present, calcium oxalate precipitates only at 7–11 times its solubility.
  • Homogeneous nucleation — nuclei form in pure solution; nuclei = earliest crystal structures that will not dissolve.
    • Small nuclei are unstable — below a critical size, dissolution is favoured.
    • Nuclei persist if driving force (supersaturation) and stability are favourable and lag time to nucleation is short relative to nephron transit.
    • Inhibitors (e.g. citrate) destabilise nuclei; promoters stabilise them by offering a binding surface.
  • In vivo, nucleation is usually heterogeneous — on epithelial cells, cell debris or other crystals.
  • Nephron transit ≈ 5–7 minutes — too short for a single crystal to grow large enough to occlude a tubule, but aggregation of many nuclei can produce occluding particles within minutes.
  • Inhibitors can block growth or aggregation at each step (see the inhibitor table).

Randall Plaque
  • Randall (1937) found subepithelial papillary plaque in 17% of autopsy kidneys; proposed an origin in the basement membrane, with erosion through the papillary surface creating a nidus.
  • Endoscopy: papillary plaques in 74% of stone formers versus 43% of controls.
  • Papillary biopsy at percutaneous nephrolithotomy in idiopathic calcium oxalate stone formers localised the sequence: basement membrane of the thin limbs of the loop of Henle → medullary interstitium → subepithelial papilla → erosion through urothelium → anchored surface → calcium oxalate stone grows attached.
  • Plaque mineral = calcium apatite (carbapatite) — by infrared microspectroscopy, electron diffraction and analysis of > 5000 plaque-associated stones.
  • Microstructure: laminated particles of alternating mineral and organic layers; osteopontin sits at the crystal–organic interface; apatite forms spherulites that grow, aggregate and fuse; in the basement membrane they lie in amorphous matrix and membrane-bound vesicles; migrating through the interstitium they propagate a mineralisation front on collagen and cell debris until they reach the papillary subepithelium.
  • Calcifying nanoparticles ("nanobacteria") — implicated in atherosclerotic calcification; associated (not proven causal) with Randall plaque.
  • Vascular hypothesis: injury to the vasa recta near the papilla → atherosclerosis-like repair and calcification → erosion into interstitium and collecting ducts → nidus.
    • Supported by osteogenic transformation: vascular smooth muscle becomes osteoblast-like with high calcium and phosphate; rat tubular cells show osteogenic activity with high oxalate or crystals, mediated by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase and reactive oxygen species.

  • Brushite pathogenesis (proposed): apatite crystallises in collecting ducts → duct injury, cell death, duct dilatation → interstitial inflammation → progressive involvement of adjacent tissue.
  • Calcium oxalate-to-brushite conversion hypothesis: an insult such as infection or shock-wave lithotripsy causes tubular dysfunction, alkaline environment, inflammation and intraductal hyaluronic acid, converting a calcium oxalate former into a brushite former.
  • Evidence that idiopathic stones start on plaque: plaque area correlates with stone number and 24-hour urine parameters; papillary mapping and micro-CT show stones originating on interstitial plaque.
  • The distinction has blurred (consecutive endoscopic series):
    • Randall plaque in almost all stone formers (99%) — including uric acid formers.
    • Ductal plugs in half of uric acid formers and in up to a third of idiopathic calcium oxalate formers (severe in about a tenth), correlating with urine risk parameters.
    • Plaque covered less papillary surface than earlier reports and did not correlate with urine parameters — possibly because this cohort was more metabolically diverse (not restricted to hypercalciuria).
    • Practical rule: ductal plugs should raise suspicion of a secondary cause.
  • "Unified" theory of plaques and plugs:
    • Plaque: oxidative stress from excess urinary calcium, oxalate and phosphate and/or deficient inhibitors → tubular cells de-differentiate into osteoblast-like cells → calcium phosphate crystals in membrane-bound vesicles → breach basement membrane → contact collagen and membrane debris → plaque spreads through interstitium with inflammation and fibrosis → matrix metalloproteinases breach papillary epithelium → plaque exposed to pelvic urine → stone growth.
    • Plugs: supersaturated tubular fluid and pelvic urine form plugs that slow outflow from ducts, promoting crystal formation and aggregation by retention.
  • Knowledge will remain incomplete without a reliable animal model.

Concept — Two routes to a stone: plaque (an interstitial apatite anchor on which calcium oxalate grows — the idiopathic pathway) and plug (intratubular crystal obstruction with tubular injury — the pathway of secondary, systemic disease); finding plugs should prompt a search for the secondary cause.

Inhibitors and Promoters of Crystal Formation

  • Urine is normally supersaturated for calcium, oxalate and phosphate; stones do not form routinely because inhibitors raise the supersaturation needed for nucleation or slow growth and aggregation.
  • Inhibitors exist for calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate — none is known for uric acid.
  • Blocking crystal adhesion to epithelium can also prevent retention and growth.
  • Whole urine raises the formation product of calcium phosphate; inorganic pyrophosphate accounts for a substantial share (25–50%) of this inhibitory activity by one method; by another, citrate, magnesium and pyrophosphate together account for a smaller share, citrate being the most important.

Matrix

  • Matrix = the non-crystalline component of stones — ≈ 2.5% of stone weight.
  • Matrix-dominant stones (a majority of the stone, up to about 65%) occur with chronic urinary tract infection.
  • Composition hard to define (only about a quarter is soluble): roughly two-thirds protein, with non-amino sugars, glucosamine, bound water and organic ash.
  • Proteins in matrix: Tamm-Horsfall protein, nephrocalcin, a γ-carboxyglutamic acid-rich protein, renal lithostathine, albumin, glycosaminoglycans, free carbohydrates and matrix substance A.
  • Matrix substance A — immunologically unique mucoprotein present in the matrix of all stone formers; its antigens are found in the urine of most stone formers and of no healthy individuals.
  • Proteomics:
    • Dozens of proteins in calcium oxalate stones, many inflammatory; inflammatory proteins predominate in both calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate stones — suggesting a shared inflammatory pathogenesis.
    • Later analysis added osteopontin, prothrombin F1 and Tamm-Horsfall protein plus leucocyte proteins (myeloperoxidase, lactotransferrin); variability between stones suggests diverse, partly hereditary aetiology.
  • Role of matrix — promoter, inhibitor or bystander — is undetermined.

KEY POINTS: PHYSICOCHEMISTRY AND PATHOGENESIS

  • Urine must be supersaturated for stones to form.
  • Supersaturation alone is not sufficient — urinary inhibitors prevent crystallisation.
  • Nephrocalcin, uropontin and Tamm-Horsfall protein are important inhibitors of nucleation, growth and aggregation.
  • Urinary calcium and oxalate contribute equally to calcium oxalate saturation.
  • Common calcium stones may originate on subepithelial calcium apatite plaques that anchor calcium oxalate growth.
  • Matrix — the non-crystalline component — comprises mucoproteins, proteins, carbohydrates and urinary inhibitors.

Calcium

Intestinal absorption
  • 30–40% of dietary calcium is absorbed, mostly in the small intestine; only about 10% in the colon.
  • Intestinal adaptation: fractional absorption rises when intake is low and falls when intake is high.

The vitamin D–parathyroid axis
  • 1,25(OH)2D3 (calcitriol) is the most potent stimulator of intestinal calcium absorption.
  • Synthesis sequence: 7-dehydrocholesterol (skin) → sunlight → previtamin D3 → liver 25-hydroxylation → 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 → proximal renal tubule 1α-hydroxylation → 1,25(OH)2D3.
  • 1α-hydroxylase (mitochondrial, proximal tubule) is stimulated by parathyroid hormone (PTH) and by hypophosphataemia.
  • Loop: low serum calcium → PTH ↑ → 1α-hydroxylase ↑ → calcitriol → vitamin D receptor (VDR) in brush border → gut calcium absorption ↑; calcitriol with PTH also recruits osteoclasts; rising serum calcium then suppresses PTH and calcitriol, and calcitriol inhibits PTH synthesis by up-regulating parathyroid VDR and CaSR.
  • PTH:
    • 84-amino-acid peptide cleaved from prepro-PTH; only mature PTH is secreted.
    • Most potent secretory stimulus: fall in serum calcium, sensed by the G-protein-coupled CaSR, which also regulates renal tubular calcium reabsorption.
    • Acts via cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) and phospholipase C.
    • Bone: osteoclastic calcium and phosphorus release. Kidney: ↑ calcium reabsorption, ↓ phosphate reabsorption, ↑ calcitriol. Gut: no direct effect — only via calcitriol.

Phosphorus

  • Absorption is saturable (transcellular) at low phosphate concentrations (1–3 mmol/L) and non-saturable (paracellular) at higher levels.
  • About 60% of dietary phosphate is absorbed; active uptake is sodium-dependent and calcitriol-regulated.
  • Strongly pH-dependent: low luminal pH reduces, high pH enhances phosphate absorption.
  • About 65% of absorbed phosphate is excreted renally, the rest by the gut.
  • Kidney reabsorbs 80–90% of filtered phosphate, excreting 10–20%.
  • Primary regulator: PTH — inhibits tubular phosphate reabsorption.

Magnesium

  • Absorbed by passive diffusion (predominant) and active transport, in large and small intestine — mainly the distal small intestine.
  • Hormonal regulation mainly via vitamin D.

  • Urinary oxalate as a surrogate for absorption is valid only if ingestion–excretion is linear and absorbed oxalate is not taken up, metabolised or re-secreted — assumptions that do not strictly hold.
  • Whether stone formers absorb more oxalate than non-formers is unclear.
  • Small-bowel disease or resection with an intact colon → markedly increased oxalate absorption.
  • Transport: paracellular (passive) and transcellular (largely active, carrier-mediated).
    • Solute-linked carrier family 26 (SLC26) anion exchangers — SLC26A1 and SLC26A6 mediate intestinal oxalate secretion (loss of function would therefore raise net absorption); shown in rodents, not yet in humans.
  • Modifiers of absorption:
    • Oxalate-binding cations (calcium, magnesium) — co-ingestion of calcium with oxalate forms insoluble calcium oxalate, limiting free oxalate.
    • Oxalate-degrading bacteria — Oxalobacter formigenes uses oxalate as its energy source; in colonised rats it also stimulates colonic secretion of endogenous oxalate, lowering urinary oxalate by more than luminal degradation alone.
    • O. formigenes is the most potent degrader but other species also degrade oxalate; in healthy people it sits within a cooperative bacterial network. Its net contribution to stone risk is not fully understood.
  • Absorbed oxalate is almost entirely excreted in urine.
  • Sources of urinary oxalate: about half dietary (varying with calcium and oxalate intake), the rest endogenous hepatic synthesis.
  • Liver = primary site of synthesis; the primary hyperoxaluria (PH) enzyme defects are hepatic and increase glyoxylate, the direct precursor of oxalate.
  • Endogenous sources:
    • Ascorbic acid — the largest endogenous source, up to 40% of urinary oxalate, via non-enzymatic conversion to 2,3-diketogulonic acid → oxalate plus threosone or erythrulose.
    • Hydroxyproline (collagen breakdown; gelatin) — about 15% of endogenous synthesis.
  • Renal handling: 86–98% of plasma oxalate is ultrafilterable; tubular secretion (probably proximal) is supported by animal and clinical data; SLC26A6 is implicated; the candidate sulphate anion transporter-1 (SAT1, SLC26A1) showed no correlation with hyperoxaluria in rats. Whether stone formers handle oxalate differently is unknown.

Concept — Calcium and oxalate are handled in mirror image: calcium is well absorbed and tightly regulated, oxalate is poorly absorbed, unregulated and fully excreted — so urinary oxalate simply tracks what the gut lets through (free luminal oxalate, colonic exposure, bacterial degradation) plus hepatic synthesis.

KEY POINTS: MINERAL METABOLISM

  • Calcium absorption is primarily small-intestinal and depends on intake.
  • 1,25-Dihydroxyvitamin D3 is the most potent stimulator of intestinal calcium absorption.
  • PTH stimulates 1α-hydroxylase in the proximal tubule to convert 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 to 1,25(OH)2D3.
  • PTH enhances renal calcium reabsorption and phosphate excretion.
  • Intestinal oxalate absorption is modulated by luminal calcium, magnesium and oxalate-degrading bacteria.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF UPPER URINARY TRACT CALCULI

Classification of Nephrolithiasis

  • Drug-related stones (triamterene, silica, indinavir, ephedrine) are uncommon and usually preventable.
  • Classification is by underlying metabolic or environmental abnormality (Table 95.2); calcium stones arise from hypercalciuria, hypocitraturia, hyperuricosuria and hyperoxaluria, alone or combined.
  • Non-calcium stones form in unique settings:
    • Uric acid — only in acid urine.
    • Cystine — impaired renal cystine reabsorption.
    • Infection (struvite) — alkaline urine from urease-producing bacteria.
  • Stone composition alone can direct therapy for some stones (e.g. cystine), but calcium stones need metabolic and environmental evaluation because causes are multiple. Molecular and genetic insights may reshape treatment.

Calcium Stones

Hypercalciuria

  • Hypercalciuria is the commonest abnormality in calcium stone formers (35–65%), though its role is debated because urinary calcium overlaps between formers and non-formers.
  • Evidence for a causal role:
    • Treatment that lowers urinary calcium reduces recurrence; persistent hypercalciuria predicts medical failure.
    • In three large cohorts with 24-hour urines, incident stone risk rises with urinary calcium after adjustment.
    • Randall plaque number correlates with urinary calcium and with the number of stone episodes.
  • Mechanism: high urinary calcium → ↑ saturation of calcium salts and ↓ inhibitor activity (calcium complexes negatively charged inhibitors such as citrate and chondroitin sulphate).
  • Normal load: kidney filters ≈ 270 mmol calcium/day and reabsorbs all but ≈ 4 mmol.
Definition of hypercalciuria Threshold
Strictest > 200 mg/day after 1 week of 400 mg calcium, 100 mg sodium diet

  • Pak classification (1974) — three subtypes by organ of dysregulation (intestine, kidney, bone): absorptive, renal and resorptive.

Absorptive Hypercalciuria
  • Definition and discriminators in the table; severe forms may also show fasting hypercalciuria.
  • Type I versus type II:
    • Type II — dietary calcium restriction normalises urinary calcium.
    • Type I — does not normalise with restriction.
  • Cause of hyperabsorption is unresolved: vitamin D-dependent and -independent processes and VDR up-regulation proposed; no mechanism explains all findings.
  • Genetic candidates:
    • CYP24A1 (encodes 1,25(OH)2D-24-hydroxylase, which inactivates 25(OH)D and 1,25(OH)2D3) — biallelic loss-of-function → elevated calcitriol, particularly with high vitamin D intake.
      • Causes idiopathic infantile hypercalcaemia (autosomal recessive; vitamin D supplement sensitivity).
      • In adults: hypercalcaemia, hypercalciuria, nephrocalcinosis and nephrolithiasis.
      • Carrier frequency 4–20%, but not all affected individuals manifest disease.
      • GWAS links CYP24A1 variants to serum vitamin D levels.
    • CYP27B1 — generates calcitriol by 1-hydroxylation of 25(OH)D (context for the above).
    • Vitamin D hypersensitivity increases absorption; VDR gene polymorphism and linkage to the VDR locus (chromosome 12q12–q14) reported in familial hypercalciuria — but not confirmed by others.
    • Chromosome 1q23.3–q24 locus for an inherited form of AH, with a putative gene homologous to rat soluble adenylate cyclase.
  • Renal phosphate leak (a subtype of AH):
    • Sequence: renal phosphate wasting → hypophosphataemia → ↑ calcitriol → ↑ gut calcium and phosphate absorption → hypercalciuria.
    • Hereditary hypophosphataemic rickets with hypercalciuria (HHRH) — autosomal recessive; candidate genes SLC34A1 and SLC34A3 (encoding NaPi-IIa and NaPi-IIc, apical proximal tubule sodium–phosphate co-transporters).
    • Rare — at most 2–4% of stone formers.

Resorptive Hypercalciuria
  • Infrequent; most commonly primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT), the cause of ≈ 5% of nephrolithiasis.
  • Normocalcaemic variant: normal serum calcium with inappropriately high PTH makes diagnosis harder.
  • Thiazide challenge: thiazide ↑ renal calcium reabsorption → exacerbates hypercalcaemia without suppressing PTH in PHPT — unmasks the diagnosis.
  • Nephrolithiasis occurs in < 5% of patients with PHPT.
  • Suspect PHPT when a stone former has serum calcium > 10.1 mg/dL.
  • Serum calcium varies by up to 5% and mild PHPT raises it only slightly → repeat serum calcium with intact PTH; ionised calcium helps in equivocal cases (may be high when total calcium is normal).
  • PTH also causes proximal bicarbonate and phosphate loss → phosphaturia and mild hyperchloraemic acidosis.
Hypercalcaemia-Induced Hypercalciuria
  • Rare causes: malignancy, sarcoidosis, thyrotoxicosis, vitamin D toxicity.

  • Malignancy-associated hypercalcaemia:
    • Tumour mix: lung and breast ≈ 60%; renal cell 10–15%; head and neck ≈ 10%; haematological (lymphoma, myeloma) ≈ 10%.

Hyperoxaluria

  • Definition: urinary oxalate > 40 mg/day.
  • Effects: ↑ calcium oxalate saturation; also implicated in crystal growth and retention via tubular cell injury — lipid peroxidation and oxygen free radicals → membrane injury → crystal fixation.
    • Antioxidants prevent calcium oxalate precipitation in rat kidney and reduce oxalate excretion; free-radical scavengers (phytic acid, mannitol) prevent crystal deposition in vitro.
    • But in humans, large oxalate loads (up to 8 mmol) did not raise oxidative-stress or renal-injury markers — the importance of oxalate-induced membrane damage is questioned.
  • Three causes: primary (biosynthetic enzyme defect), enteric (malabsorption — inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, intestinal resection), dietary (excess intake or substrate).
Primary Hyperoxaluria
  • Rare autosomal recessive disorders of glyoxylate metabolism: glyoxylate cannot be converted to glycine, so it is oxidised to oxalate, a metabolic end product.
  • Urinary oxalate markedly high (≥ 100 mg/day) → calcium oxalate crystals in tubular lumens → some attach to epithelium and aggregate into stones, others are internalised and extruded into the interstitium → marked nephrocalcinosis.
  • Renal injury: direct toxicity of oxalate or crystals (reactive oxygen species), recurrent obstructing stones, and inflammation and interstitial fibrosis from nephrocalcinosis.
  • Vicious circle: falling renal function → oxalate elimination fails → systemic oxalosis (systemic calcium oxalate deposition).

Enteric Hyperoxaluria
  • The commonest cause of acquired hyperoxaluria.
  • Mechanism (Fig. 95.9) — a sequence: fat malabsorption → luminal fatty acids saponify calcium and magnesium → less calcium available to complex oxalate → more free luminal oxalate → colonic hyperabsorption; malabsorbed fatty acids and bile salts also ↑ colonic permeability to oxalate.
  • Fecal fat correlates strongly with urinary oxalate in steatorrhoea (Fig. 95.10).
  • Co-existing risks in chronic diarrhoea: dehydration, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesiuria, hypocitraturia, low urine pH.
  • Any malabsorption — small-bowel resection, intrinsic small-bowel disease, jejunoileal bypass — can cause it.
  • Requires an intact colon — the colon is the principal site of oxalate hyperabsorption; in terminal-ileal Crohn disease stone incidence rises with length of bowel resected, provided the colon is intact.
  • Cystic fibrosis: increased stone risk (overall prevalence about 4.5%); proposed causes — enteric hyperoxaluria from fat malabsorption, loss of O. formigenes, hyperuricosuria, hypocitraturia.
  • Bariatric surgery:
    • Jejunoileal bypass was abandoned partly for renal failure and stones from severe hyperoxaluria.
    • Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) — initially considered safer, but oxalate nephropathy with dialysis or transplantation was reported; retrospective, cross-sectional and prospective studies confirm ↑ urinary oxalate after RYGB and other malabsorptive procedures.
    • Hyperoxaluria develops ≥ 6 months after surgery.
    • Falling urinary calcium and uric acid partially offset the oxalate rise — net effect on calcium oxalate saturation varies.
    • Nonetheless stone incidence is higher after RYGB than in obese controls (claims data).
    • Cause: loss of O. formigenes is unlikely (colonisation is already low before surgery and does not differ from obese controls); an exaggerated urinary response to oral oxalate load points to malabsorption / enteric hyperoxaluria.

Dietary Hyperoxaluria
  • Oxalate-rich foods: nuts, chocolate, brewed tea, spinach, potatoes, beets, rhubarb — overindulgence causes hyperoxaluria in otherwise normal people.
  • Dietary oxalate contributes 24–42% of urinary oxalate (in this analysis).
  • Severe calcium restriction ↑ oxalate absorption (less luminal binding) — calcium restriction is counter-productive.
  • Ascorbic acid supplements ↑ urinary oxalate by in vivo conversion, but clinical stone risk is not unequivocally increased.
Role of the Microbiome
  • Humans lack oxalate-degrading enzymes; some gut bacteria degrade oxalate.
  • O. formigenes uses oxalate as its sole carbon source — a potential modulator of intestinal oxalate.
  • Stone formers have reduced or absent colonisation, and non-colonised individuals have higher urinary oxalate.
  • Antibiotic exposure depletes O. formigenes:
    • Cystic fibrosis (prolonged antibiotics) — absent O. formigenes, raised urinary oxalate.
    • Helicobacter pylori eradication — lower colonisation than untreated H. pylori controls.
  • Population case–control study (UK electronic records): exposure to any of five antibiotic classes 3–12 months earlier ↑ stone risk — yet O. formigenes is resistant to some of those classes, so the effect may reflect reduced overall microbiome diversity, not O. formigenes alone.
Idiopathic Hyperoxaluria
  • Mild hyperoxaluria may matter as much as hypercalciuria in idiopathic calcium oxalate stones.
  • Arabian Peninsula: calcium stone prevalence far higher than in the West despite near-absent hypercalciuria — hyperoxaluria is the predominant risk factor.
  • Abnormal oxalate metabolism and transport may contribute.

Concept — Outside the rare primary forms, hyperoxaluria is a problem of free luminal oxalate reaching the colon — raised by fat malabsorption, low calcium intake, high oxalate intake or loss of degrading bacteria — so colonic presence and luminal calcium are the levers.

Hyperuricosuria

  • Definition: urinary uric acid > 600 mg/day.
  • Isolated in up to 10% of calcium stone formers; combined with other abnormalities in up to 40%.

  • Commonest cause: dietary purine excess.

Hypocitraturia

  • An important and correctable abnormality: isolated in up to 10% of calcium stone formers; combined with other abnormalities in 20–60%.
  • Actions of citrate (four):
    1. Complexes calcium → ↓ saturation of calcium salts.
    2. Prevents spontaneous nucleation of calcium oxalate.
    3. Inhibits agglomeration and sedimentation of calcium oxalate crystals, and growth of calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate crystals.
    4. Enhances the inhibitory effect of Tamm-Horsfall glycoprotein at normal levels.
  • Definition: urinary citrate < 320 mg/day.
  • Acid–base status is the primary determinant of citrate excretion: metabolic acidosis → ↑ tubular citrate reabsorption and ↓ peritubular citrate synthesis → hypocitraturia.
  • The defect is renal, not intestinal:
    • Stone formers and controls have similar serum citrate and filtered load, but formers show lower urinary citrate and higher tubular reabsorption.
    • Oral citrate is rapidly and almost completely absorbed (96–98% within 3 hours) in both hypocitraturic formers and controls — so absent overt bowel disease, gut malabsorption is not the cause.

Concept — Citrate excretion is a readout of intracellular pH in the proximal tubule: anything that acidifies the cell (systemic acidosis, hypokalaemia, acid diet) makes the tubule reclaim citrate — which is why alkali (potassium citrate) is the treatment.

Low Urine pH

  • Below pH 5.5, undissociated uric acid predominates → uric acid and/or calcium stones; calcium oxalate forms by heterogeneous nucleation on uric acid crystals.
  • Any disorder that lowers urine pH predisposes to stones.
  • Chronic metabolic acidosis → low urine pH, hypercalciuria (↑ bone resorption and renal calcium leak) and hypocitraturia.
  • Idiopathic low urine pH (formerly "gouty diathesis") — stone propensity with low urine pH of unknown cause, with or without gouty arthritis.

Hypomagnesiuria

  • Rare: isolated in < 1% of stone formers; combined with other abnormalities in 6–11%.
  • Mechanism: magnesium complexes oxalate and calcium salts, so low magnesium means less inhibition; low urinary magnesium also tracks low urinary citrate (cause or effect unclear).
  • Causes: poor dietary intake, intestinal malabsorption from chronic diarrhoeal syndrome.
  • Evidence is contradictory:
    • Rat studies both implicate and question magnesium.
    • Hypomagnesiuric patients had higher recurrence in one study; others found no difference in magnesium excretion between formers and controls — possibly because few formers are hypomagnesiuric.
  • Magnesium raises urinary pH, citrate and magnesium and lowers calcium oxalate saturation in vitro and in vivo, but two randomised trials of magnesium oxide versus placebo or no treatment showed no clinical benefit.

Concept — Magnesium is a physicochemically plausible but clinically unproven inhibitor: favourable urine chemistry has not translated into fewer stones in randomised trials.

Uric Acid Stones

  • Humans and apes lack hepatic uricase (which converts uric acid to allantoin) — the gene was silenced (pseudogenised) in evolution, leaving higher blood and urine uric acid.
  • Allantoin is 10–100 times more soluble than uric acid — hence human susceptibility to uric acid stones.
  • Frequency: 8–10% of US stones, up to 25% in parts of Germany; rising — a US registry saw the proportion double (≈ 7% → 14%) over 30 years; a Norwegian series rose from 2% to 9% over 40 years.
  • Uric acid is a weak acid, pKa 5.35 at 37 °C — at that pH, half is urate salt, half free acid.
  • Sodium urate is about 20 times more soluble than free uric acid → the free-acid fraction (set by pH) determines risk.
  • pH governs solubility (Fig. 95.13):
    • At pH 5, even modest uric acid exceeds solubility.
    • At pH 6.5, > 1200 mg/L remains soluble.
    • Solubility limit of undissociated uric acid ≈ 96–100 mg/L — readily exceeded by normal daily excretion (500–600 mg/day), so urine is often supersaturated below pH 6.
  • Low pH also drives calcium oxalate and mixed stones — uric acid and sodium urate act as nidi (heterogeneous nucleation, epitaxy).
  • Uric acid stone formation after precipitation is poorly understood; crystal adhesion and glycosaminoglycans are proposed but unproven — no natural urinary inhibitor of uric acid is known.
  • Theobromine (dimethylxanthine in cocoa and chocolate) inhibits uric acid nucleation and growth in vitro; cocoa products raised urinary theobromine and reduced uric acid crystallisation in volunteers (in vitro urine assay).

Low Urine pH

  • Pathogenesis uncertain and probably multifactorial; the leading model links it to insulin resistance:
    • Normouricosuric pure uric acid formers are more often diabetic or glucose-intolerant.
    • On a controlled metabolic diet their urine pH is lower than controls or calcium oxalate formers; they excrete less acid as ammonium, more as titratable acid and less citrate — impaired ammonium excretion.
    • Uric acid stones are far commoner in type 2 diabetes (about a third of stones in diabetics versus roughly a tenth or less in non-diabetics in two series).
    • Uric acid formers share metabolic syndrome features — hypertriglyceridaemia, hyperglycaemia, obesity, hypertension.
    • Hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp: low urine pH correlates with insulin resistance in normal subjects, and uric acid formers are the most insulin-resistant.
    • Hyperglycaemia → lower urine pH and higher uric acid saturation.
    • Body weight is inversely related to urine pH, independent of urinary sulphate (animal-protein marker); BMI ≥ 27 kg/m² predicts urine pH ≤ 5.5.
  • Mechanism (Fig. 95.15): insulin promotes proximal tubular ammoniagenesis from glutamine and stimulates the Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger NHE3, which carries NH₄⁺ or supplies luminal H⁺ to trap NH₃ → insulin resistance → ↓ ammonium production/excretion → unbuffered H⁺ → low urine pH.
  • Diet-independent impairment: higher BMI → lower ammonium-to-sulphate ratio (ammoniagenesis corrected for acid intake) and higher uric acid supersaturation.
  • Increased endogenous acid production:
    • On a fixed low acid-ash diet, uric acid formers have higher net acid excretion.
    • Controlled for urinary sulphate, net acid excretion is higher in uric acid formers and in non-forming type 2 diabetics.
    • Matched for age, sex and BMI on a metabolic diet, uric acid formers show lower pH, higher titratable acid and net acid excretion, and higher uric acid supersaturation despite equal uric acid excretion.
  • Synthesis: with impaired ammonium excretion plus increased endogenous acid, titratable acids become the main urinary buffer; acid–base balance is kept, but at a lower urine pH than ammonium (higher pKa) would allow.
  • Why ammoniagenesis fails — renal lipotoxicity:
    • Metabolic switch: proximal tubule uses free fatty acids instead of glutamine → less ammoniagenesis.
    • Lipotoxicity: fat deposited in non-adipose tissue injures cells; in the Zucker diabetic fatty (ZDF) rat, renal steatosis reduced NHE3 expression and activity.
    • Uric acid stone matrix proteomics: proteins mainly of inflammation and complement; pathways chiefly phospholipid and fatty acid metabolism.
    • Visceral fat on CT is associated with low urine pH (< 6) and independently with uric acid composition.
  • Diet: in a randomised crossover of vegetable, vegetable-plus-egg and animal protein diets (rising sulphate), animal protein raised urinary calcium, gave the most undissociated uric acid and the least citrate (lower pH); matched for electrolytes and protein, it raised uric acid stone risk but — because of opposing factors — not calcium oxalate or calcium phosphate risk.

Concept — Uric acid stones are a disease of urine pH, not of uric acid: insulin resistance and renal fat impair ammoniagenesis, so the kidney buffers acid with titratable acid at a lower pH — making the uric acid stone a renal manifestation of metabolic syndrome.

Hyperuricosuria

  • Definition: urinary uric acid > 600 mg/day — causes supersaturation with undissociated uric acid.
  • Gout: stones markedly fewer with urinary uric acid < 600 mg/day than with > 1000 mg/day.
  • Causes: dietary, acquired and hereditary diseases, urate-transporter defects (as above).

Low Urinary Volume

  • Any cause of low volume raises uric acid supersaturation — hot-temperature workers show high uric acid supersaturation; warm climates (e.g. Israel) have high uric acid stone rates.

Infection Stones

  • Composition: magnesium ammonium phosphate hexahydrate (struvite, MgNH₄PO₄·6H₂O), often with carbonate apatite (Ca₁₀[PO₄]₆·CO₃).
  • History (sequence of discovery): a Swedish geologist found magnesium ammonium phosphate in guano and named it struvite after his mentor H.C.G. von Struve → bacteria proposed to split urea (early 20th century), with Proteus vulgaris isolated from a stone → a bacterial enzyme proposed to hydrolyse urea → urease isolated (from Canavalia ensiformis, the jack bean) → now established that struvite forms only with urea-splitting infection.

Pathophysiology

  • Urease is absent from sterile human urine — infection with a urease-producing organism is a prerequisite.
  • Chemical cascade (step → step):
    1. Urea + H₂O → 2NH₃ + CO₂ (bacterial urease).
    2. Alkaline urine (pH 7.2–8.0) → NH₃ + H₂O → NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ (pKa 9.0). Normally alkalinity would halt ammonium generation, but urease keeps producing ammonia, pushing pH higher.
    3. CO₂ hydration → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (pKa 6.3) → H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ (pKa 10.2).
    4. Phosphate dissociation: H₂PO₄⁻ → H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻ (pKa 7.2) → H⁺ + PO₄³⁻ (pKa 12.4).
    5. With physiological magnesium → struvite; with calcium, phosphate and carbonate → carbonate apatite and hydroxyapatite (Fig. 95.17).
  • Obstruction or stasis may associate with or exacerbate infection stones → growth can be rapid.

Bacteriology

  • Enterobacteriaceae make up most urease producers, but many gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, some yeasts and Mycoplasma species make urease (Table 95.3).
  • Commonest urease producers: Proteus, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas and Staphylococcus; Proteus mirabilis is the organism most associated with infection stones.
  • Escherichia coli — a common uropathogen, but only rare strains produce urease.
  • Yet contemporary series culture E. coli (and sterile urine) from struvite patients surprisingly often; E. coli matched Proteus as the commonest isolate in one international series; higher struvite content predicts urease-producing organisms.
  • Explanation: bladder urine and stone cultures are frequently discordant → voided urine culture is unreliable for identifying the stone organism.

Pathogenesis

  • Beyond chemistry, urease-producing bacteria damage the urothelial mucosal layer → ↑ bacterial colonisation and crystal adherence.
  • Ammonium from ureolysis alters the protective glycosaminoglycan layer → ↑ bacterial adherence → biofilm.
  • Bacteria live within an exopolysaccharide matrix; struvite crystallises inside and around bacterial cells → stone growth, bacteria encased within the stone.

Epidemiology

  • 5–15% of all stones.
  • Staghorn calculi are not mostly infection stones: in a modern analysis less than half were infection stones; the majority were metabolic, most often calcium phosphate.
  • Incidence is falling worldwide (Australia, Japan; in the US in females, little change in males) — attributed to better diagnosis and treatment of urinary tract infection (UTI) in females and more effective stone surgery.
  • Geography: highest in India and Pakistan (poor sanitation → female UTI); lowest in Canada, China, Argentina, Poland, Iraq and Italy.
  • Female-to-male ratio ≈ 2:1.
  • At-risk groups: elderly; premature infants and congenital urinary tract malformation; diabetes; urinary stasis from obstruction, urinary diversion or neurological disorders.
  • Spinal cord injury — risk of infection and metabolic stones (neurogenic dysfunction plus immobilisation hypercalciuria); functionally complete cord transection → highest staghorn risk.

Concept — Infection stones need one thing no human urine can supply — urease; everything else (alkaline pH, ammonium, carbonate, phosphate) follows chemically, and biofilm then locks the bacteria inside the stone.

Ammonium Acid Urate Stones

  • ≈ 1% of stones; endemic bladder stones in children in developing countries.
  • Radiolucent.
  • Associations: chronic diarrhoea, inflammatory bowel disease, ileostomy, laxative abuse, recurrent UTI, recurrent uric acid stones, morbid obesity; a contemporary series added prostate surgery with bladder neck contracture and found no laxative association.
  • Ileostomy after colectomy: low urine volume, pH and sodium, and no hyperoxaluria (no colon) → ammonium acid urate and uric acid stones, not calcium oxalate; inflammatory bowel disease with ileostomy gave the highest ammonium urate content.
  • Laxative abuse mechanism (sequence): gastrointestinal fluid loss → dehydration → intracellular acidosis → ↑ urinary ammonia; low urinary sodium leaves urate to complex with ammonium → ammonium acid urate supersaturation.
  • Precipitation favoured at pH 6.2–6.3.
  • Link with uric acid stones: shared low volume and low pH; mixed uric acid–ammonium urate stones are usually uric acid-predominant; fluctuations in acidity, ammonium and sodium shift the balance between uric acid and sodium- or ammonium-bound urate.
  • Obesity (BMI ≥ 30) was the commonest feature once bowel-disease patients were excluded; BMI correlated with ammonium urate content — consistent with the obesity–low urine pH link.

Matrix Stones

  • Protein suspensions can promote calcium stones; osteopontin and calprotectin help build the matrix of calcium stones.
  • Predominantly matrix stones are rare, radiolucent, and may be mistaken for tumour or uric acid stones; literature is mostly case reports.
  • Matrix is ≈ 2.5% of calcium stone dry weight; pure matrix stones contain > 65% protein.
  • Composition ≈ two-thirds mucoprotein, one-third mucopolysaccharide; matrix of crystalline stones closely resembles that of matrix stones.
  • Why they fail to calcify is unclear — low urinary calcium was proposed, but patients had normal urinary calcium.
  • Dialysis patients: proteinuria may contribute; matrix contains microfibrillar protein and β₂-microglobulin.
  • Proteus infection: electron microscopy shows fibrous net-like laminations with bacterial, cellular and crystalline material; proteomics found proteins mostly previously unreported, the majority inflammatory or defensive.

Medication-Related Stones

  • Two routes: direct (the drug or a metabolite precipitates) or indirect (the drug alters urine chemistry to favour metabolic stones).
  • Stones of the drug itself (with excessive intake): ephedrine, triamterene, guaifenesin, silicate, indinavir, ciprofloxacin.
  • Calcium-stone promoters: loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide) and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide, topiramate, zonisamide).

Anatomic Predisposition to Stones

  • Anomalies causing obstruction or stasis carry high stone rates. The long-running question: stasis (delayed transit → crystal formation and retention) or the same metabolic abnormalities as other stone formers?

Ureteropelvic Junction Obstruction

  • Stones in nearly 20% of patients with ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO).
  • Evidence that metabolic risk persists after the obstruction is corrected (adult series):
    • Most recurred after UPJO repair, and a large share of recurrences were in the contralateral kidney.
    • Most patients with non-infectious stones had a metabolic abnormality — a rate comparable to other stone formers.
    • The spectrum mirrored the general stone population — hypercalciuria commonest, then hypocitraturia, hyperuricosuria, primary hyperparathyroidism, RTA.
    • Treating the metabolic abnormality substantially cut recurrence compared with conservative management.
  • Pre-repair metabolic evaluation: UPJO patients with stones had more metabolic abnormalities (higher urinary calcium, hypercalciuria and hyperuricosuria) than UPJO patients without stones.
  • Children:
    • Stone-forming children — with or without UPJO — differ in urinary citrate and oxalate from non-stone-forming children with UPJO (prospective comparison), supporting a metabolic contribution.
    • UPJO confers a markedly increased (about seventyfold) stone risk versus normal children.
    • Most children with non-struvite stones recurred after surgery, and most had a metabolic abnormality; those who did not recur mostly lacked one.

Horseshoe Kidneys

  • Pooled stone incidence (meta-analysis): ≈ 36% in adults, ≈ 3% in children.
  • High ureteral insertion → impaired drainage → UPJO tendency — risk traditionally attributed to stasis.
  • But metabolic evaluation found at least one abnormality in every patient tested; the distribution resembled normal-anatomy formers except hypocitraturia, which was over-represented.
  • Composition (meta-analysis): calcium-based ≈ 89%, struvite ≈ 4%, uric acid ≈ 4%, other ≈ 3%.

Calyceal Diverticula

  • Stones in up to 40%.
  • Conflicting evidence:
    • One series: metabolic abnormalities in half, and most had stones elsewhere (synchronous or metachronous) — supports metabolic risk.
    • Another: low metabolic abnormality rates in patients with and without diverticula — but pH and citrate were not measured, so abnormalities were under-counted (low pH and hypocitraturia affect about 10% and 28% of recurrent formers).
    • 24-hour urine of diverticular stone patients resembled calcium oxalate formers (hypercalciuria, ↑ calcium oxalate supersaturation) — yet urine aspirated from the diverticulum had lower calcium oxalate supersaturation than either renal pelvis.
  • Composition: mainly calcium oxalate monohydrate; struvite–carbonate apatite when infected; concomitant UTI in up to 40% (E. coli, Proteus, Pseudomonas).

Medullary Sponge Kidney

  • Medullary sponge kidney (MSK) = ectasia of the renal collecting ducts; nephrocalcinosis and calculi are frequent.
  • Candidate mechanisms: infection and stasis in ectatic ducts, plus tubular defects — hypercalciuria, impaired concentrating ability, defective acidification after ammonium chloride load.
  • Acidification defects: common on acid loading (mostly distal RTA, occasionally proximal); impaired concentrating ability in most; H⁺-ATPase gene mutations (ATP6V1B1, ATP6V0A4) found in MSK patients — supporting an MSK–distal RTA link.
  • Yet three studies of MSK patients with stones found no RTA.
  • Hypercalciuria is the commonest abnormality in some series (mostly absorptive), less frequent in others; sometimes renal leak from damaged tubules.
  • Hypocitraturia the commonest abnormality in other series.
  • Urine proteomics (Italian network) proposed biomarkers distinguishing MSK from idiopathic calcium formers — laminin α2, ficolin 1, mannan-binding lectin serine protease 2, complement component 4-binding protein, sphingomyelin, ephrins — role not yet established.

Stones in Pregnancy

  • Symptomatic stones: 1 in 204 to 1 in 3000 pregnancies; incidence has not risen (1991–2000 versus 2001–2011), unlike the general population.
  • Timing: mostly second and third trimesters, with flank pain or haematuria; odds rise in the second and third trimesters and peak 0–3 months post partum, returning to baseline by 1 year.
  • Misdiagnosis in up to 28% — appendicitis, diverticulitis, placental abruption.
  • Physiological hydronephrosis in up to 90%, persisting 4–6 weeks post partum:
    • Causes: progesterone and — at least contributory, perhaps primary — compression by the gravid uterus.
    • Right side greater — engorged uterine vein and dextrorotation of the uterus.
    • Dilatation may promote crystallisation through stasis; ↑ pelvic pressure may mobilise stones and provoke symptoms.

KEY POINTS: PATHOGENESIS

  • Absorptive hypercalciuria: normal serum calcium, normal or suppressed PTH, normal fasting urinary calcium, elevated urinary calcium.
  • Renal hypercalciuria: impaired renal calcium reabsorption stimulates PTH and causes fasting hypercalciuria.
  • Resorptive hypercalciuria: primarily primary hyperparathyroidism; also granulomatous diseases producing 1,25(OH)2D3.
  • Low urinary pH is the most important determinant of uric acid stones.
  • Low urine pH in uric acid formers probably reflects impaired ammoniagenesis from insulin resistance plus excess acid production.
  • In distal RTA, a defective H⁺-ATPase prevents excretion of excess acid into the distal tubule.
  • Infection stones require alkaline urine achievable only with urease-producing bacteria.
